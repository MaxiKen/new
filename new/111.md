# Sūrah al-Masad (Chapter 111) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Masad 111:1

> May the hands of Abu Lahab perish, and he ˹himself˺ perish!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb of Ruin Used Twice, Once as a Report and Once as a Prayer**

The sūrah opens on one word used in two different moods. *Tabbat* is a perfect verb — the hands of Abū Lahab *have perished*, the ruin is stated as accomplished. *Wa tabba* repeats the same root in a form that functions as supplication: *and may he perish*. So the first half of the verse reports and the second half prays, and the pairing is the whole method of the chapter. What God declares finished is finished, even while the man is walking around Mecca.

*T-b-b* carries the sense of ruin and loss rather than of death specifically. The Arabs used *tabbat yadāka* as an idiom of rebuke — "may your hands be ruined" — and the Qur'an takes the idiom up and returns it. The order of the two clauses is worth holding onto. The statement of fact comes first, the wish second; if the sequence were reversed the verse would be nothing but a curse. As it stands, the curse is the echo of a verdict already issued.

That structure explains why the sūrah could be recited publicly in Mecca at a moment when its subject was alive, armed with wealth, and closely related to the man reciting it. It is not a hope that an enemy might be destroyed. It is an announcement that he has been.

**Hands Stand for Power and for Everything They Have Done**

The ruin falls on the hands before it falls on the man. *Yad* in Arabic idiom is not merely the limb; it names agency, possession, and the record of what a person has done. To say that a man's hands have perished is to say that his capacity to act has been annulled, together with whatever that capacity produced.

The Qur'an uses the same idiom when it assigns responsibility: *“Whatever affliction befalls you is because of what your own hands have committed. And He pardons much.”* (42:30). The hands are the person as doer. Against Abū Lahab they are the hands that threw stones at his nephew's door, that counted out money to fund hostility, that struck and cursed the people who had begun to pray. The verse takes the instrument of all of it and declares it void.

The second clause then widens the target: *and he perish*. Not the hands alone but the whole man, since a man whose agency is annulled has nothing left that matters. Both halves are needed. The first is specific — it names the means by which he had opposed the message; the second is total. Read together they say that neither what he did nor what he is will survive him.

**The Only Contemporary Enemy the Qur'an Names**

Almost no one in the Prophet's own time is named in the Qur'an. Zayd ibn Ḥārithah is named in the passage about his marriage (33:37); the ancient figures — Pharaoh, Hāmān, Qārūn — are named by title rather than by personal name. Abū Lahab is the one contemporary opponent of the Prophet ﷺ to be named in the text, and he is named with a *kunya*, a nickname, which is how his city knew him.

The naming has consequences for how the chapter works. A curse against an unnamed type can be read as rhetoric. A curse against ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, identified in public, in a city where everyone knew him, is a claim that can be tested: it asserts that this man will not escape, will not benefit from his property, will burn, and will die outside the faith. Every one of those assertions was checkable by the people standing next to him.

That is why the sūrah has been treated in the tradition as an argument for the revelation's authenticity rather than as an outburst. It is also why it is uncomfortable. Nothing in the text softens the naming, adds a condition, or leaves room for a change of heart. The verse is a door that closes on a named individual while he can still hear it close.

**The Curse Came Back Word for Word From al-Ṣafā**

The occasion is reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4770, from Ibn ʿAbbās. When *“And warn ˹all, starting with˺ your closest relatives”* (26:214) was revealed, the Prophet ﷺ climbed al-Ṣafā and began calling out the clans of Quraysh — Banū Fihr, Banū ʿAdī — until they gathered, and those who could not come sent someone to see what was happening. Abū Lahab came with the rest. The Prophet ﷺ said: suppose I told you that cavalry were in the valley about to raid you — would you believe me? They said yes, since they had never found him saying anything but the truth. He said: then I am a warner to you before a tremendous punishment. Abū Lahab answered: *tabban laka sāʾira l-yawm* — may you perish the rest of this day; is this what you gathered us for? Then this sūrah came down.

The verbal echo is exact. He opened with *tabb*; the revelation opens with *tabbat*, the same root in the perfect, and transfers it to his own hands. The man who cursed his nephew's hands was answered with a curse on his. The scene also carries a detail that raises the stakes: the crowd had just testified that they had never known him to lie, and Abū Lahab contradicted the testimony in front of the witnesses who gave it.

**Why the Nickname Instead of the Given Name**

His name was ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā — servant of al-ʿUzzā, one of the three goddesses of Mecca. The Qur'an does not use it. Two explanations are offered for the substitution, and they are compatible. The first is practical: Abū Lahab was how the city knew him, and a text that named ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā would identify him less clearly to the people it was addressing. The second is theological: the given name is itself an act of idolatry, a declaration of servitude to a goddess, and the Qur'an does not repeat it.

Reports give two reasons for the nickname itself. Some say it referred to his appearance — a bright, ruddy complexion that made him conspicuous in a crowd. Others read it as descriptive of his end: the father of flame is the one who belongs to the fire. The second reading gains weight from the third verse of this sūrah, which sends him into *“a flaming Fire”* (111:3) — the word there, *lahab*, is the word in his name. Whether or not the Quraysh meant anything by the nickname when they gave it, the chapter that uses it makes it mean something: a man named for a blaze ends up inside one. The sūrah is also known by that word, *al-Lahab*, alongside the name it takes from its last verse.

**A Prediction That Stayed Open for More Than a Decade**

The sūrah was revealed in the early Meccan period, before the Hijrah; Abū Lahab died in the second year after it, in 624 CE, about a week after Badr. Between the recitation and the death lies more than ten years in which a single sentence would have falsified the chapter. Had he declared himself a Muslim — even, as polemicists have always pointed out, hypocritically — the sūrah's claim that he would burn in a flaming fire would have collapsed, and the Quraysh knew it.

He did not. He stayed out of Badr himself, sending a substitute in his place, and died in Mecca of a festering ulcer — the sources call the disease *ʿadasa* — after being struck by Umm al-Faḍl, the wife of his brother al-ʿAbbās, in retaliation for his beating of her slave Abū Rāfiʿ, who had rejoiced at the Muslim victory. Ibn Hishām records the sequence in his Sīrah. The manner of the death matters to the argument as much as the fact of it: the man the sūrah described as ruined died in the city he had defended, unmourned by his own family, of a wound that his kinship had produced.

**The Warning to the Closest Relatives Ends With the Closest Relative Cursed**

The verse that started the scene on al-Ṣafā was an instruction about family: *“And warn ˹all, starting with˺ your closest relatives”* (26:214). The sūrah that followed it is about one of those relatives. The order is not accidental, and the tradition kept the two together.

Kinship in the Qur'an transfers nothing. It does not make a person safe, and it does not make a warning softer. The uncle who mocked is named and condemned; two other uncles, Ḥamzah and al-ʿAbbās, entered Islam and are honoured in the same tradition; a fourth, Abū Ṭālib, protected the Prophet ﷺ for years and died without accepting the message. All of them came from the same father, ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, and the chapter that names one of them makes no allowance for blood. What is being asserted is that the message reaches inside the family first, and inside the family it produces the same division it produces everywhere else.

## Sūrah al-Masad 111:2

> Neither his wealth nor ˹worldly˺ gains will benefit him.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Has Already Been Answered**

The verse is built on a negative and a perfect verb: *mā aghnā ʿanhu māluhū*. Grammatically it can be taken two ways, and both were taken. As a statement it says his wealth did not avail him — the ruin described in the first verse is already in force. As a rhetorical question it says: what did his wealth avail him? The two readings agree on the content and differ only in tone; the question adds contempt, the statement adds finality.

The verb matters. *Aghnā ʿan* means to suffice in place of something, to shield a person from what was coming at him. It is the language of substitution: money stands between a man and his ruin. That is precisely what the Qur'an denies here, and it denies it in the past tense while the man in question is still alive and still rich. Abū Lahab was not poor when this verse was recited in Mecca. He was, by the accounts, the wealthiest of the Prophet's uncles. The sentence therefore does not describe a loss he had suffered; it describes a protection he had never possessed. His money had been unable to do the one thing he was relying on it to do.

**Two Categories of Property, Both Closed Off**

The verse names two things and refuses both. *Māl* is wealth as possession — what a man holds, whether he earned it or inherited it. *Mā kasab* is what he acquired, the product of effort, the result of trading, planning, and grasping. Between them the two cover every route by which property reaches a person's hands.

The pairing is deliberate, because the two categories answer two different kinds of confidence. Inherited wealth gives a man standing without his doing anything; acquired wealth gives him the sense that he built his own security. Abū Lahab had both. He was born into the house of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib at the centre of Quraysh, and he had added to that position through his own dealings. The verse takes the two supports away in a single clause and leaves nothing between them.

That is the argumentative shape of the whole chapter. The first verse removes the man's agency — his hands. This one removes his resources. The third will describe what is left when both are gone.

**What He Earned May Mean Whom He Fathered**

A well-attested reading takes *mā kasab* to mean his children. The grounds are two. First, the report of his own boast: he is said to have remarked that even if what his nephew claimed turned out to be true, he would ransom himself with his wealth and his children — a sentence that pairs exactly the two things this verse pairs. Second, Arabic usage itself; a child can be spoken of as a parent's acquisition, and the Qur'an elsewhere groups children with property as the two things people rely on: *“Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope.”* (18:46).

Against that, the plain lexical sense of *kasab* is earning, and the word's ordinary reference is gain rather than offspring. The reading is not thereby weakened. Both senses fall inside the phrase, and the Qur'an's parallel description of the Day makes the extension natural: *“The wicked will wish to ransom themselves from the punishment of that Day by their children, their spouses, their siblings, their clan that sheltered them, and everyone on earth altogether, just to save themselves.”* (70:11–14). Children are listed first among the things a person would hand over. Whether *mā kasab* means them or means his acquisitions, the verse says the same thing: none of it is accepted.

**The Ransom That Cannot Be Paid**

The idea this verse rejects has a settled place in the Qur'an. Payment on the Day is not a category that exists: *“Indeed, if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world, it would never be accepted from them. It is they who will suffer a painful punishment, and they will have no helpers.”* (3:91). The amount is deliberately absurd — the earth filled with gold — and the refusal is absolute.

What makes Abū Lahab's case sharper is that he is reported to have proposed exactly this transaction in advance. He knew the claim being made against him, understood that it involved a punishment, and answered with the commercial reflex of his class: everything has a price, including this. The verse closes the deal before it is offered. It does not say his wealth is small or that his gains are doubtful. It says they do not function in the sphere where he needs them to function.

There is a warning attached to the same theme for believers, and it is not less severe: *“O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who will be the ˹true˺ losers.”* (63:9). Property is neutral in that verse and useless in this one; between the two the Qur'an leaves no version of wealth that saves.

**Wealth Was His Argument, Not Only His Asset**

Money in Mecca did more than buy comfort. It bought clients, funded expeditions, and settled the costs of a campaign against a person. Abū Lahab used his position in that economy: he was one of the Qurayshī leaders most active against his nephew and against those who followed him, and the persecution that drove the Muslims out of the city was financed and directed by men of his standing.

The verse therefore strikes at something more specific than luxury. It denies the efficacy of his money in the exact use he made of it. What he spent on opposition produced nothing that reaches him now; what he kept for himself will not reach him either. Both directions of the same account are closed, and the closing is stated in a single negative.

This is why the chapter has been read as an attack on a way of thinking rather than on a person. The person is named, and the naming is not incidental; but the thing refuted is the assumption that resources translate into outcomes in the world to come. Every reader who has relied on an asset instead of on God is inside the scope of the verse, whether or not he has an uncle to be cursed.

**Why This Verse Comes Second**

The order of the chapter follows the order of a judgment. The first verse pronounces the verdict: ruin, already accomplished. The second removes the grounds of appeal: no property, no acquisition, no ransom. The third states the sentence: a flaming fire. Nothing is out of place, and nothing is added for emphasis.

That economy is what makes five verses enough. A longer treatment would describe the crime in detail; this one does not describe it at all. The chapter assumes its audience knows exactly what Abū Lahab did, because they watched him do it. What it supplies instead is the legal form — verdict, foreclosure, sentence — applied to a man standing in the crowd.

The effect on the original hearers is easy to reconstruct. A curse from an enemy is common; a judgment delivered in the language of settled law, by someone with no power to enforce it, is something else. The sūrah's claim is that the case has been heard by the only court whose decision cannot be bought, and that the decision has already been published.

**The Man Who Counted His Wealth**

Two short Meccan sūrahs sit near each other in the codex and make the same case from opposite ends. Al-Humazah opens: *“Woe to every backbiter, slanderer, who amasses wealth ˹greedily˺ and counts it ˹repeatedly˺, thinking that their wealth will make them immortal!”* (104:1–3), and answers itself at once: *“Not at all! Such a person will certainly be tossed into the Crusher.”* (104:4).

The parallel is close enough to be instructive. Both chapters describe a man whose property has been mistaken for permanence; both end in fire; both are addressed to a Meccan audience that measured standing in exactly these terms. The difference is that al-Humazah describes a type — every backbiter — while this sūrah names an individual. Together they show the two registers the Qur'an uses for one argument: the general warning and the particular case, with the particular case given a name and a nickname so that nobody in Mecca could pretend it was hypothetical.

## Sūrah al-Masad 111:3

> He will burn in a flaming Fire,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yaṣlā Means to Be Roasted, Not Merely to Arrive**

The verb is *yaṣlā*, from a root that means to be exposed to fire, to warm oneself at it or to be scorched by it. It is not the ordinary word for entering. Where the Qur'an wants to say that someone goes into the fire it can say so plainly; *ṣalā* adds the experience of the heat. A man who *yaṣlā* a fire is inside it and feeling it.

The Qur'an uses the same verb where the fire is described as exclusive: *“in which none will burn except the most wretched — who deny and turn away.”* (92:15–16). Abū Lahab is being placed in that category by a chapter that has spent two verses removing his defences. The specificity is the point. This is not a warning about a destination that many will reach; it is an assignment of one man to the part of the destination reserved for those who denied and turned away.

The tense completes the movement of the sūrah so far. Verse one declared his ruin in the perfect, as an accomplished fact. Verse two reported the failure of his wealth, also in the perfect. Now the future arrives: *he will burn*. What has been settled is about to happen, and the shift from "has perished" to "will burn" marks the difference between the verdict and its execution.

**Dhāt Lahab: A Fire Defined by Its Flame**

The object he enters is *nār dhāt lahab* — a fire possessing flame. *Lahab* is the blaze itself, the visible tongue of fire, as distinct from heat or burning. To describe a fire as having flame sounds redundant until the alternatives are considered: a fire can smoulder, it can be coals without light, it can be heat without a body. This one is defined by what it looks like.

The Qur'an's vocabulary of fire is precise about such distinctions. There is the fire *“kindled”* by God, *“which rages over the hearts”* (104:6–7); the scorching fire beside the scalding spring (88:4–5); the chains, shackles and blazing fire prepared for the disbelievers (76:4); the abyss that is *“a scorching Fire”* (101:9–11). Each phrase picks out a different aspect — intensity, location, instruments, depth. *Dhāt lahab* picks out visibility. The fire Abū Lahab is assigned to is one that can be seen at work, and the word chosen to describe it is the word in his own name.

That the phrase is short is itself significant. Three words of Arabic carry the entire sentence of the chapter: he will burn in a blazing fire. No duration is given, no crime is recited, no intercessor is mentioned or excluded. The terseness reads as impatience with the subject.

**The Nickname Fulfilled in Its Own Letters**

His *kunya* was Abū Lahab — father of flame. The fire he is sent into is *dhāt lahab*, possessing flame. The Qur'an takes the name the city gave him, apparently for his bright ruddy complexion, and reads it as a description of his end.

Wordplay of this kind is common in the Arabic tradition, but here it does more than decorate. A *kunya* is a claim about a person's identity, given by other people and worn publicly; Abū Lahab was called this in the markets of Mecca. The sūrah adopts the name and assigns it a referent he did not choose. The man who was known as the father of a blaze becomes the property of one. The chapter is even called by that word in the tradition — *al-Lahab* — alongside its more common title taken from the last verse.

The commentators noticed the economy of this. Only two readings of the nickname are recorded: it described his appearance, or it described his fate. The sūrah does not choose between them, because either way the name now belongs to the fire. What had been a comment on a complexion becomes a legal description.

**Three Moods in Three Verses**

The grammatical movement of this chapter is worth setting out, because it carries the argument. The first verse begins with a perfect verb used declaratively — *tabbat yadā Abī Lahab*, his hands have perished — and ends with an optative, *wa tabba*, may he perish. The second verse is negative and perfect: his wealth did not avail him. The third is future and affirmative: he will burn.

The sequence moves from what God has declared, to what has already proved true of his resources, to what will happen to him. Each step narrows the room available. After the first verse he might still have argued that his hands were his own; after the second that his money would cover him; after the third there is nothing left to argue with.

That progression is why the chapter works as a public recitation. A listener who heard it in Mecca heard not an insult but a case being closed in stages, with the stages marked by verb forms. The rhetorical force is in the grammar rather than in the vocabulary, which is why the sūrah is so short and so difficult to answer.

**A Sentence Addressed to One Man, and to Everyone Watching**

Most of the Qur'an's language about the fire is addressed to categories: the disbelievers, the wrongdoers, the hypocrites, those who deny and turn away. Groups are warned; individuals are usually left to be inferred. This verse does the opposite. It attaches a fire to a person with a name, a father, an address, and a wife.

The reversal has an effect on the audience. A warning about "the disbelievers" can be heard by a disbeliever as not quite applying to him, since categories are abstractions and he is concrete. A sentence on a named neighbour cannot. Everyone in the crowd on al-Ṣafā knew whose hands were being cursed, and everyone could see that those hands were still holding things.

So the chapter asks the audience to do a calculation about itself. If the fire is real enough to be assigned to a specific man while he stands there, then the categories are real too, and every hearer is inside one of them. The individual case is the proof of the general claim; that is the function of naming Abū Lahab at all.

**Fuel Is What He Becomes**

The Qur'an has a further way of describing the relation between the damned and the fire: they are its material. *“Certainly you ˹disbelievers˺ and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell. You are ˹all˺ bound to enter it.”* (21:98). The idols a man served are burned with him, and he is burned as fuel rather than merely as an occupant.

That image completes this chapter's account of Abū Lahab. His hands are void, his wealth does not avail, and he enters a blazing fire — where, on the Qur'an's own terms elsewhere, he is what keeps it burning. The goddess al-ʿUzzā, whose servant his name declared him to be, belongs in the same fire according to 21:98, together with the wealth and children he hoped to ransom himself with.

The next verse of this sūrah adds a person to the fuel, and the addition is the most shocking thing in the chapter.

## Sūrah al-Masad 111:4

> and ˹so will˺ his wife, the carrier of ˹thorny˺ kindling,

**Extended Commentary**

**A Wife Added to the Sentence**

The conjunction at the head of the verse attaches her to the verb that precedes it. He will burn — *and* his wife. She is not introduced with a new sentence, a new verdict, or a new charge; she is joined grammatically to his fate, as a co-subject of the same fire.

That joining is the most consequential thing the verse does. Arabic could have set her punishment apart in a separate clause, which would have required a separate account of what she did and why. Instead she is inside the same predicate. Whatever the fire is for him, it is for her, and the sūrah treats the two as a single case with two defendants.

The tradition holds that the couple acted as one unit against the Prophet ﷺ. He provided the standing and the money; she provided the energy and the presence in the streets. The verse records that partnership by refusing to separate them at the end. It is also the only place in the Qur'an where the punishment of a woman is stated in the same breath as that of a named man, and the reason is not her gender but her participation.

**Named by What She Did, Not by Whose Daughter She Was**

She was ʿArwā bint Ḥarb, sister of Abū Sufyān and therefore a woman of the leading house of Banū Umayyah. None of that appears. The Qur'an identifies her by a role: *ḥammālat al-ḥaṭab*, the carrier of kindling.

The choice of identifier is striking in a society where lineage was the primary way of naming a person. A Meccan audience hearing "the sister of Abū Sufyān" would have placed her instantly in a network of alliances; hearing "the carrier of brushwood" they would have placed her in a queue of labourers. The Qur'an gives her the second address, and gives it permanently, since it is the description by which she is remembered.

This is a different naming practice from the one used for her husband. He receives his *kunya*, the public nickname by which the city knew him. She receives an epithet derived from an activity — one that the sources say she actually performed, and one that the sūrah extends into her punishment. In both cases the name is a verdict rather than a label, but his is borrowed from Mecca and hers is minted here.

**What She Carried in This World**

Two accounts explain the title, and both were held. The literal one is recorded in the early commentaries: she gathered bundles of thorny brushwood and scattered them along the paths the Prophet ﷺ walked, and at his door, so that his feet would be cut. On this reading the verse is describing a campaign — a repeated, physical act of harm carried out by a woman of high station who did it with her own hands.

The second reading takes the phrase idiomatically. In Arabic, carrying firewood between people means carrying tales between them: supplying fuel to a quarrel. On that reading she is described as the sower of discord, the person who kept Mecca's hostility burning by what she said and repeated. The idiom is attested, and it fits a woman of her position, whose influence ran through her household and her clan rather than through the streets.

The two are not rivals so much as descriptions of the same activity at different altitudes. The literal reading has the advantage of explaining why the punishment in the next verse involves a rope on her neck — the tool of someone who carries bundles. The idiomatic reading explains why the Qur'an would fix on this of all her acts. Both were preserved by the exegetes for that reason.

**What She Will Carry in the Next**

The dominant interpretation places the carrying in Hell. She aided her husband in opposing the messenger, so part of her punishment is to aid him there: she will carry the firewood that feeds his fire. The correspondence is exact in form and inverted in value — the same service, the same partnership, now directed at his torment instead of his cause.

The grounds for this reading are structural. The verse follows immediately on "he will burn," and the noun *ḥammālah* is a continuous form: not one who carried but one whose function is carrying. A permanent fire requires a permanent supplier of fuel, and the sūrah supplies one.

The alternative is that the description refers only to this world, and that her punishment is contained in the phrase "and so will his wife." Nothing forbids that reading, and some commentators preferred it as more restrained. The stronger position holds both: what she did here is what she is made to do there, since the Qur'an's punishments consistently take their shape from the crime. She is given the same work forever, and the work now feeds the fire she is standing in.

**A Variant Reading Changes Her Into a Sentence of Her Own**

The transmitted readings differ in the vowel of the noun. On the common reading she stands in the same case as the subject of the preceding verb, so that the phrase means "and his wife, carrier of kindling, will burn." On the variant reading the noun is nominative and the construction becomes a fresh statement: "and his wife *is* a carrier of kindling."

The difference is not merely grammatical. The first reading keeps her inside her husband's sentence; the second makes an independent claim about her, which the following verse then elaborates with the rope. Both readings were transmitted, and both were accepted in the tradition.

A third feature of the common reading has been discussed since the earliest grammarians: the accusative can be read as the accusative of disparagement, a form Arabic uses to attach blame to a person in the middle of a sentence. On that reading the grammar itself is doing the work of condemnation — the case ending carries the insult, in a language where case endings are audible in recitation. Whether or not the disparagement is intended, the construction leaves her name attached to an act of labour rather than to her clan.

**The Work of the Lowest Station, Assigned to the Highest**

Carrying brushwood was not a metaphor in Mecca; it was the work of the poor, of slaves, and of women without households to command. A woman of Banū Umayyah did not gather thorns. She sent someone.

The sūrah reverses that precisely. She is described with the active participle of carrying — the form used of someone who does the work himself — and the following verse puts the rope of a woodcutter around her neck. The humiliation is in the substitution of roles: the lady of the clan performing the labour of the lowest person in it, and performing it forever.

The Qur'an frequently punishes by inversion rather than by intensity. The proud are made small, the rich are made bankrupt, the eloquent are silenced. Here the inversion is social and visible, and it is attached to a body part — the neck — that in her world carried jewellery. The verse does not need to add a description of suffering. The change of station is the suffering.

**Why a Woman Is Named in a Chapter About Her Husband**

The Qur'an names almost nobody, and it names fewer women than men. This chapter names no one at all by personal name; it identifies two people by nickname and by role, and one of them is a woman. The inclusion answers an objection that has been raised against the sūrah since the earliest centuries — that it is a private quarrel dressed up as revelation.

If it were a quarrel, one defendant would be enough. The chapter's argument requires two, because what it describes is a household that made a decision together and acted on it together with money, status, and hands. Her inclusion is also what makes the chapter general: it shows that opposition to the message was a choice available to every member of a family, and that the consequences attach to whoever made it. She is not condemned for being his wife. She is condemned for carrying.

## Sūrah al-Masad 111:5

> around her neck will be a rope of palm-fibre.

**Extended Commentary**

**Fī Jīdihā: The Place Where a Necklace Goes**

*Jīd* is not the general word for neck. It names the front of the neck, the part that is visible and that carries adornment; Arabic distinguishes it from *unuq* and *raqabah*, which are used for the neck as a limb or as a site of constraint. The Qur'an's choice of *jīd* puts the rope exactly where jewellery would have been.

That precision is what gives the image its force. A rope on the neck could be a tether, a halter, a means of dragging; on the *jīd* it is an ornament replaced. The verse describes a substitution rather than an addition: what she wore in Mecca is taken from her and something else is put in its place, in the same position, visible to everyone.

The substitution also answers the title in the previous verse. The carrier of kindling needs a rope; a bundle of brushwood is bound with one and slung over the shoulder or carried at the neck. The instrument of her labour and the ornament of her station arrive together in a single object, and the Qur'an leaves her wearing it.

**Masad: A Twisted Cord, and the Name of This Chapter**

*Masad* denotes a rope made of twisted fibre — palm fibre in the usual explanation, though the word is also applied to cords of other materials and, in some usages, to something plaited of iron. The root carries the sense of tight twisting and plaiting, of strands worked together until they hold.

The word occurs in the Qur'an here and nowhere else, and it gives the chapter its standard title, Sūrah al-Masad. That is the ordinary way the short Meccan sūrahs are named: not from a theme but from a striking word inside the text. The chapter is also called al-Lahab after the flame of verse three, and other titles are recorded in the tradition.

The lexical range matters for interpretation. If *masad* can mean a cable of iron, the phrase admits a harsher reading — a chain rather than a cord — and several commentators took it that way, describing an iron collar placed on her neck in the fire. The reading that follows the plain word keeps the material cheap: palm fibre, the stuff of bundles and of the poorest labour. Both readings are recorded, and the choice between them changes the emphasis of the punishment from weight to degradation.

**The Necklace She Vowed to Sell**

The interpretation with the most explanatory power connects the rope to a specific object she owned. It is reported that she possessed a precious necklace and vowed to sell it in order to spend the proceeds on the campaign against the Prophet ﷺ. On this account the rope of palm fibre is what takes its place: she committed her jewellery to the work of hostility, and the work returns to her as the cheapest cord in Arabia.

The reading explains details that would otherwise be arbitrary. Why a rope, why fibre, why the neck, why this woman — each of those questions is answered by the vow. It also fits the chapter's method, which is to take the resources a person relied on and show them converted into their opposite: his hands void, his wealth useless, her adornment replaced.

Against it stands the simpler punitive reading: an iron chain or heavy collar, the standard equipment of the fire elsewhere in the Qur'an. That reading has the advantage of consistency with other descriptions and the disadvantage of explaining nothing specific about her. The exegetes kept both. The stronger account treats the report of the necklace as explaining the image and the iron reading as describing the reality of the punishment, since a rope of fibre in this world and a collar of iron in the next are not competing claims about the same object.

**The Punishment Has the Shape of the Crime**

Correspondence is the Qur'an's ordinary way of constructing punishment, and this chapter is a compressed example. The hands that acted are ruined. The wealth that was trusted does not avail. The man enters the fire his name promised. The woman who carried fuel carries fuel, and the rope that bound her bundles binds her.

Nothing in the verse is invented for the sake of horror. Every element is drawn from the life being judged: her station, her labour, her adornment, her partnership with her husband. The punishment is the life continued under different management, and that is what makes it legible to the people who watched her live it.

The principle extends to the material as well as the activity. She chose the cheapest instrument available to harm the Prophet ﷺ — thorns, brushwood, a rope. The sūrah hands her the same instrument forever. What she was willing to use becomes what she is unable to put down, and the economy of her own contempt is what determines the terms.

**Degradation Rather Than Intensity**

Fire, chains, boiling water: the Qur'an has all of these, and it does not need another of them here. What this verse adds is a material. Palm fibre is not a torture instrument; it is what a woodcutter uses, what a market porter ties his load with, what the poorest household in Mecca owned.

Assigning it to a woman of Banū Umayyah is a statement about rank rather than about pain. The punishment works by putting her permanently in the position she despised. A reader who asks why the chapter ends on a rope instead of on a flame has missed the direction of the sentence: the flame was given to her husband in verse three, and what she receives is the loss of everything that distinguished her from the people who carried things.

This is a recognisable pattern. The arrogant are described in the Qur'an as gathered small and mute, dragged on their faces, sealed over, braced in. The reduction of scale and station does more work in these descriptions than the addition of suffering, and this verse is the clearest instance of it in the short sūrahs.

**Sealed, Braced, and Bound at the Neck**

Three other passages belong with this one, and each fixes a different part of the body. Al-Humazah, the neighbouring chapter, closes with an image of containment: *“It will be sealed over them, ˹tightly secured˺ with long braces.”* (104:8–9). The fire there is a closed structure, and the inhabitants are inside something fastened shut.

Sūrah al-Insān gives the instruments: *“Indeed, We have prepared for the disbelievers chains, shackles, and a blazing Fire.”* (76:4). And Ghāfir describes the neck specifically — *“when shackles will be around their necks and chains ˹on their legs˺. They will be dragged through boiling water, then burned in the Fire ˹as fuel˺.”* (40:71–72).

Set beside these, the rope of palm fibre is the oddest and the most precise of the four. Chains are generic; braces are architectural; the fibre rope belongs to one woman and to one bundle of thorns. The Qur'an's general vocabulary of constraint is here narrowed to a single household's history, and the narrowing is the point: the fire is not an abstraction into which categories disappear, but a place where particular people keep the particular things they chose.
