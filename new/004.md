# Sūrah al-Nisāʾ (Chapter 4) — Original Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The chapter of protected relationships**

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ, “The Women,” is a Madinan chapter of 176 verses. Its name points to its prominent concern, but not to its full range. The chapter speaks about women because a community’s claims to justice are tested most clearly in the household: in the treatment of wives, widows, daughters, mothers, stepdaughters, female orphans, and women whose property can be controlled by men. Yet the same chapter also deals with orphans, inheritance, prayer under danger, migration, public authority, war and treaty, hypocrisy, the People of the Book, Jesus, economic exploitation, testimony, and the unity of revelation. Its subject is therefore not “women” in isolation. Its subject is the moral ordering of relationships in which some people possess more power than others.

The chapter’s opening places all of humanity under one Lord and traces men and women to a single origin. It then moves immediately to the property of orphans. That sequence is decisive. The Qur’an does not begin family law by asking how the strong can preserve custom; it begins by asking whether the vulnerable will receive what belongs to them. A guardian may be physically stronger than an orphan, a husband may control the household’s income, a male relative may dominate an inheritance meeting, and a ruler may command an army. In every case the question is the same: will power be treated as a trust or as ownership?

**A Madinan setting of reconstruction**

The chapter belongs to the period in which the Muslim community was becoming a society rather than remaining a persecuted minority. Migration had brought families together, but it had also displaced people and unsettled property. Battles had left widows, orphans, injured men, and complicated questions of allegiance. A community that had once been sustained mainly by preaching now required rules for estates, marriage, testimony, public administration, military defence, and relations with neighbouring groups.

This setting explains why the chapter combines tenderness and severity. It speaks gently to the orphan and the repentant, but sharply to the person who consumes a child’s wealth, fabricates a charge, or uses religion to protect a fraud. It permits concessions in travel and danger, yet insists that prayer remain anchored to its appointed times. It recognizes human weakness in marriage and desire, yet does not allow weakness to become an excuse for coercion. Its realism is not moral compromise; it is the realism of a law that knows the circumstances in which people actually fail.

**The architecture of the chapter**

The opening section (1–14) establishes human kinship, the protection of orphans, the wife’s dowry, the guardianship of dependent property, and the revealed shares of inheritance. Verses 15–18 address sexual wrongdoing and the urgency of repentance. Verses 19–35 regulate marriage, prohibited relationships, chastity, household responsibility, marital discord, and arbitration. Verse 36 then widens the circle from worship and parents to relatives, orphans, neighbours, companions, travellers, and dependants.

The middle of the chapter moves between belief and social consequence. Verses 41–57 describe the coming testimony, ritual purity, the conduct of some People of the Book, the danger of shirk, and the different ends of rejection and faith. Verses 58–70 provide a political and judicial centre: return trusts, judge fairly, obey Allah and the Messenger, refer disputes to revelation, and seek the company of the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. Verses 71–104 address precaution, fighting, protection of the oppressed, migration, the sanctity of life, and prayer during travel and danger.

Verses 105–126 expose the attempt to hide wrongdoing behind legal advocacy, affirm the possibility of repentance, condemn scapegoating, and diagnose Satan’s false promises. Verses 127–152 return to women, marital settlement, emotional justice, divine ownership, testimony, faith, hypocrisy, and speech about revelation. The final section (153–175) addresses the People of the Book, the prophetic history of revelation, the status of Jesus, the unity of the messengers, and the invitation to hold fast to the clear light. The last verse returns once more to inheritance, closing the chapter where it began: with kinship translated into justice.

**Law and taqwā**

A striking feature of al-Nisāʾ is that its legal rules are continually surrounded by spiritual accountability. The guardian is told that Allah is Watchful. The heir is told that inheritance shares are divine limits. The judge is reminded that Allah hears and sees. The witness is warned that Allah knows whether testimony is distorted or withheld. The one who wants to postpone repentance is told that the moral opportunity does not last forever. These are not decorative religious phrases attached to secular regulation. They explain how law can survive when no court is present.

Human institutions remain necessary. The chapter calls for witnesses, arbiters, authorities, records, and fair judgment. But institutions cannot observe every hidden decision. Taqwā is the inner recognition that a private act remains public before God. It restrains the guardian who could manipulate an account, the spouse who could exploit a dowry, the official who could sell a decision, and the soldier who could turn a just defence into revenge.

**Women as legal persons**

The chapter’s treatment of women is especially important because it repeatedly separates a woman’s rights from the preferences of male relatives. She receives a dowry that belongs to her. She cannot be inherited against her will. Her property cannot be reclaimed through slander or harsh treatment. She has a share in inheritance. A wife may seek a fair settlement when a husband neglects her, and she cannot be left indefinitely suspended by deliberate partiality. The chapter’s language does not reduce her to a symbol of family honour; it addresses her as a person with property, consent, and a claim before God.

This does not mean that every modern legal question is settled by a slogan. The verses operate within a family system containing duties of maintenance, marriage, divorce, and kinship, and jurists have differed over many applications. A responsible reading must distinguish the text’s principles, the historical institutions it regulated, the Prophetic explanations, and later interpretations. It must also refuse both the denial of the text’s gendered responsibilities and the use of those responsibilities to justify abuse. The chapter’s own standard is *maʿrūf*: decent, recognized, honourable conduct.

**Inheritance as an ethics of grief**

The inheritance verses are often approached as arithmetic, but their moral purpose is visible throughout the chapter. Inheritance is distributed at the moment when a family is most vulnerable to manipulation. Grief can make people confused; old rivalries can become financial claims; a widow or daughter may be pressured to renounce what is hers; a guardian may hide assets; relatives may appeal to custom against revelation. Fixed shares prevent the strongest voice from writing the weakest person out of the estate.

The arithmetic also teaches that legal equality and identical shares are not the same claim. The Qur’an gives women and men legal standing as heirs, then assigns different portions in some relationships within a structure that also places financial obligations on men. Whatever one’s debate about contemporary applications, the text itself does not justify depriving women of their shares or treating their property as household property by default. A sound distribution begins with a complete family tree, debts, valid bequests, and independent knowledge of each heir’s right.

**Justice in conflict**

The chapter’s war passages must be read as a connected legal discourse, not as isolated sentences. Believers are told to take precautions, protect oppressed people, verify whom they fight, honour treaty relationships, accept offers of peace, and avoid killing a believer. The passages distinguish active aggression from mere difference of faith and make the permission to fight conditional on conduct. They do not authorize individuals to declare war, kill civilians, or treat every outsider as an enemy.

The same realism appears in the fear prayer. Security does not cancel worship, and worship does not require pretending that an enemy is absent. One group prays while another guards; weapons may be set aside when illness or rain makes them dangerous; prayer may be shortened during travel. The law accommodates danger without surrendering either spiritual duty or practical intelligence.

**Hypocrisy and the divided self**

Hypocrisy in this chapter is not simply private uncertainty. It is the deliberate use of belief-language to obtain the protection of one side while serving another, the outward performance of prayer for reputation, the refusal to let revelation judge one’s disputes, and the habit of taking credit after success while disowning the community in hardship. These behaviours damage trust because they make the sacred appear to be a costume.

Yet the chapter does not close the door on the hypocrite. Repentance, reform, holding fast to Allah, and sincere devotion lead back to the believers. That sequence is important: repentance must become a changed pattern, not only a statement. A community should protect itself from betrayal while leaving room for a person who genuinely repairs what he has damaged.

**The unity of revelation**

The People of the Book passages correct distortion without erasing the prophetic history shared by Jews, Christians, and Muslims. The Qur’an names Noah, Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, David, Jesus, and other messengers as recipients of one stream of revelation. It praises those who are grounded in knowledge and believe in what was revealed to Muhammad and what came before. Its criticism falls on rejection, manipulation, slander, idolatry, and the obstruction of guidance—not on an ethnic category treated as uniform.

The discussion of Jesus is similarly precise. Jesus is Messiah, son of Mary, messenger of Allah, His Word directed to Mary, and a spirit created by His command. These titles honour his miraculous birth and prophetic rank while denying that he shares in God’s divinity. He is a servant, and the angels nearest to Allah are servants. In this chapter, servanthood is not an insult but the truth that protects every created being from being elevated into a rival to God.

**How to read the commentary**

Each verse below is treated separately. The translation is reproduced from the repository’s translation file, while the commentary explains the verse through its context, language, legal implications, theology, and practical consequences. Where a hadith is mentioned, the collection and reference are given only when the report is sufficiently established; no claim about a report should be made more precise than the evidence allows. Qur’anic cross-references are used to illuminate the argument rather than to decorate it.

The chapter should be read neither as a collection of detached commands nor as a historical document with no present significance. Its recurring questions remain immediate: Who owns the property in our care? Whose testimony do we believe? Do our alliances protect justice or merely power? Do we return to revelation when it restrains us? Do we speak of God with truth rather than excess? And when a relationship gives us authority over another person, do we remember that Allah is Watchful over us all?

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:1

> O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you.

### Expanded Commentary

**The human family before its divisions**

The sūrah opens with an address wider than the community of believers: every human being is summoned to mindfulness of the Lord. That breadth matters because the laws that follow concern marriage, property, inheritance, war, and public trust. They are not rules for a religious elite detached from human nature; they are guidance for creatures who share one origin and therefore owe one another more than convenience or tribal loyalty. The verse puts descent before difference. Men and women, nations and families, all emerge from the same created source.

**“A single soul” and the dignity of persons**

The classical exegetes generally identify the single soul with Adam and the mate with Eve, while also noting that the Arabic expression can emphasize common nature rather than describe a physical process. The Qur’an itself does not require the rib narrative in order to establish its point. Whether “from it” is read as origin from Adam or as likeness in kind, the moral conclusion is the same: woman is not a lesser species and humanity is not divided into superior bloodlines. The passage moves from one soul, to a pair, to innumerable men and women; difference is real, but it is downstream from shared creation.

This is why the verse undermines claims of inherited human superiority. A person may possess a particular language, ancestry, or social office, but none of those can make the other descendants of Adam alien to him. The Prophet’s Farewell Sermon expressed the same principle by returning all people to Adam and Adam to dust. The point is not to erase family identity; it is to prevent identity from becoming a warrant for oppression.

**Taqwā and the rights created by kinship**

The repeated command to be mindful of Allah joins the Creator’s right to the rights of relatives. The Arabic *arḥām* means wombs or kinship ties, and its association with mercy is not accidental: the womb is the first place in which a human being receives care without having earned it. To honour kin is therefore not merely to maintain agreeable relationships. It includes visiting, supporting, reconciling, and refusing to exploit dependence. The following verses apply this principle to orphans and women, whose vulnerability made family both a possible refuge and a possible instrument of abuse.

**The divine Witness over private dealings**

The verse ends with Allah as Watchful. Human law can require witnesses, records, and judges, but none of these sees every concealed bargain. The guardian who quietly diminishes an orphan’s inheritance, the relative who uses a family claim to intimidate another, and the spouse who hides a betrayal may evade human inspection. The Watcher is not an argument for surveillance by people; it is the moral foundation of accountability when no person is looking. Al-Nisāʾ begins by making family ethics a form of worship: reverence for God must become protection of those whom our relationships place within our power.

**A foundation for public ethics**

The opening principle reaches beyond biological relatives. It asks a society to see every person as connected to the same created family, while giving special attention to relationships in which dependence makes abuse easy. Policies for foster care, inheritance, elder care, and family mediation should therefore begin with dignity rather than suspicion. Kinship is not a private sentiment; it creates duties that God will question.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:2

> Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own. For this would indeed be a great sin.

### Expanded Commentary

**A trust is not an inheritance for the guardian**

The command is direct: orphaned children are to receive their own wealth. In the setting of early Arabian society this was a social revolution. Property commonly followed male strength and tribal protection, while a child who lost his father could be surrounded by relatives who treated guardianship as ownership. The Qur’an reverses that assumption. The guardian is an administrator appointed for the child’s welfare; he does not acquire title merely by holding the keys.

The legal meaning is clarified by verse 6: the wealth remains protected until the orphan reaches maturity and demonstrates sound judgment, at which point it must be handed over. The verse therefore contains both a prohibition and a positive duty. It is not enough for a guardian to avoid stealing; he must preserve, manage, and eventually deliver the property in a usable form.

**Replacing the good with the inferior**

“Do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables” describes a subtle form of fraud. A guardian might retain good animals, land, or goods belonging to the orphan while substituting something defective and declaring the accounts balanced. The crime lies not only in the price difference but in abusing a position in which the child cannot effectively object. A second early interpretation understands the phrase more broadly: do not replace the good practice of preserving the trust with the corrupt practice of consuming it. Both readings illuminate the same ethical danger—self-interest disguising itself as administration.

Modern equivalents include transferring a child’s sound assets into a failing business, charging personal expenses to a trust, lending the money without security, or using complicated fees that a child could never understand. The material form changes; the moral pattern does not. A fiduciary must ask whether the transaction would appear reasonable if the beneficiary were present and fully informed.

**Mixing wealth and mixing liability**

The warning against consuming the orphan’s property together with one’s own addresses the ease with which ownership becomes blurred. Shared cupboards and accounts are not automatically unlawful, and the Qur’an elsewhere permits beneficial cooperation with orphans. But mingling creates a duty of accurate accounting. A guardian who cannot distinguish the child’s money from his own cannot reliably tell whether he is spending for the child or for himself.

**Why the sin is “great”**

The verse calls the abuse a grave sin because it combines financial wrong, betrayal of dependence, and suppression of a future adult’s rights. The Prophet included consuming an orphan’s wealth among the seven destructive sins in the report of Abū Hurayrah (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2766; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 89). The severity is also protective: a child cannot negotiate with the adult who controls food, shelter, documents, and inheritance. Divine law therefore places the guardian’s private appetite under public and ultimate judgment. The command is a charter for every modern trustee, executor, foster parent, and institution that manages the resources of minors.

**The guardian’s accounting**

A trustworthy guardian can make the child’s property more secure than it was at the moment of bereavement. That requires separation of records, prudent investment, and a willingness to let independent people review the accounts. The verse’s severity is aimed at the adult who mistakes silence for consent. An orphan’s inability to complain is precisely why the guardian must be more scrupulous, not less.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:3

> If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. This way you are less likely to commit injustice.

### Expanded Commentary

**The verse begins with the vulnerable, not with desire**

This passage is often quoted as though it were an isolated permission concerning marriage, but its grammar and context begin elsewhere: with fear of failing orphan women in one’s care. In pre-Islamic practice, a guardian could marry a wealthy orphan girl himself, diminish her bridal gift, or prevent her from marrying someone who would take her property outside his control. The reported explanation from ʿĀʾishah, preserved in the canonical hadith collections, identifies the remedy: if a guardian cannot deal fairly with a girl under his authority, he must marry someone else rather than turn guardianship into a route to exploitation.

**Limitation rather than an unlimited innovation**

The permission to marry two, three, or four is bounded by the immediate condition of justice and by the final instruction to marry only one when justice is feared. It therefore limited an existing and sometimes unrestricted practice rather than introducing an unqualified ideal. The number is a ceiling, not a target. The order of the verse is ethically significant: the text does not first celebrate plurality and add justice as a footnote; it places the protection of vulnerable women at the entrance and justice at the exit.

Classical law understood justice between wives to include maintenance, housing, time, and the outward rights that can be measured. It does not require a man to manufacture identical feelings, since verse 129 states that complete emotional equality is beyond human capacity. That limitation does not excuse unequal treatment. It distinguishes involuntary inclination from deliberate favoritism and demands that the latter be controlled.

**The social burden of marriage**

Marriage is represented here as a responsibility with financial and legal consequences. A man who takes several wives while unable to feed, house, or protect them may be tempted to consume the estates of orphans or other dependants. The verse therefore connects polygyny to fiduciary ethics: a choice is not responsible merely because it is formally permitted; it must be supported by the rights of the people affected by it.

The phrase concerning those “in your possession” belongs to the legal world of slavery in which the revelation first addressed its audience. It cannot be detached from the Qur’an’s wider regulation, emancipation incentives, and insistence on humane treatment. Modern readers should not turn a historical category into a casual recommendation, nor should they use it to evade the verse’s controlling principle: no dependent person may be treated unjustly.

**Justice as the governing test**

The concluding rationale—being less likely to commit injustice—makes the verse a moral examination, not a license for appetite. Where justice cannot be credibly maintained, monogamy is the commanded safeguard. The passage also shows how Qur’anic legal guidance works: it engages an existing institution, restricts it, attaches duties to it, and directs the conscience toward the protection of those with less power. Any reading that makes the wealthy guardian’s preference more important than the orphan girl’s rights has inverted the verse’s purpose.

**The justice test in practice**

A marriage proposal involving a woman whose property is controlled by a guardian should be examined for conflict of interest. Does she have independent advice? Is her dowry fair? Can she refuse without losing shelter or inheritance? These questions give practical form to the verse’s concern. The legal maximum cannot turn an exploitative arrangement into a righteous one; justice remains the condition that governs every permission.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:4

> Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience.

### Expanded Commentary

**The bridewealth belongs to the woman**

The command to give women their dowries establishes the bridewealth as the wife’s own entitlement. It is not a payment to her father, clan, guardian, or husband’s relatives, and it is not a fee that purchases a woman. The Arabic imperative presents it as a gift offered in good faith, while the legal effect is an enforceable financial right arising from the marriage contract. In a society where women’s property could be appropriated by male relatives, that clarification was foundational.

The word commonly translated as dowry, *ṣaduqāt*, is related to truthfulness. A marriage gift thus signifies the seriousness of the covenant and the sincerity expected of the husband; it cannot be replaced by a symbolic gesture while the real wealth is secretly retained by someone else. The amount may be modest or substantial, but the right is not modest merely because the amount is.

**Consent distinguishes gift from extraction**

The second part permits the husband to accept some of the dowry only when the wife gives it willingly. The condition is more demanding than a spoken “yes” obtained under pressure. A wife must possess the legal capacity to dispose of her property, and the surrounding circumstances must not make refusal dangerous or humiliating. A demand followed by emotional threats, family pressure, or fear of divorce is not the gracious remission described by the verse.

This principle also protects against a common reversal: treating the wife’s generosity as if it were the husband’s entitlement. Once the property has been delivered, she may keep it, invest it, donate it, or return part of it. Her husband may benefit only through a genuine act of her choice.

**A material right with spiritual meaning**

The verse does not reduce marriage to economics. Rather, it shows that affection and law reinforce one another. A contract is more likely to be humane when the vulnerable party enters it with recognized property and an acknowledged voice. The dowry gives practical shape to respect; it also exposes the false piety of a man who speaks tenderly while withholding what the law has assigned to his wife.

**Guidance for contemporary contracts**

The rule applies to delayed and immediate dowries alike. A delayed amount remains a debt, not a ceremonial promise that disappears when the marriage becomes difficult. Families may help negotiate an amount, but they cannot take ownership of it. Communities that record the obligation clearly, distinguish it from wedding expenses, and ensure that the bride understands her rights are acting in the spirit of the verse. The final phrase—permitting the husband to enjoy what she freely remits—preserves generosity while placing consent, not custom or coercion, at its centre.

**The wife’s financial independence**

Families may celebrate a marriage by contributing to its expenses, but custom cannot silently redirect the bride’s dowry. The woman should know what is due, whether it is immediate or deferred, and how she may use it. A husband who accepts a voluntary gift should be able to show that it was truly voluntary. This is how gratitude remains generosity instead of becoming a second claim upon her property.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:5

> Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly.

### Expanded Commentary

**Property as a means of communal support**

The verse forbids handing wealth to those unable to manage it while still requiring that their needs be met from it. The term *sufahāʾ* can include minors, people lacking financial judgment, and dependants whose incapacity makes immediate control of assets dangerous. The wealth is described as a means by which Allah sustains the community, not as a badge of personal prestige. Its purpose is nourishment, clothing, shelter, and the maintenance of human dignity.

This is not a command to deprive an incapable person forever. It is a rule of protection. A child or cognitively impaired adult may be the lawful owner of property while another person administers it. Ownership and management are therefore distinguished: the guardian protects the asset without treating the beneficiary as disposable.

**Needs must be supplied from the trust**

The instruction to feed and clothe them from the property prevents two opposite abuses. One guardian might release a large sum prematurely and expose the dependent to manipulation; another might hoard the estate while forcing the dependent to beg. The balanced duty is to spend reasonably for the person’s welfare and preserve the principal as far as possible. Medical care, education, housing, and ordinary support fall within the same logic in contemporary circumstances.

**Honourable speech is part of guardianship**

The verse joins financial administration to speech. A person who cannot be given unrestricted control must not therefore be insulted, infantilized, or treated as a burden. “Speak to them kindly” requires explanation, patience, and language that preserves self-respect. The dependent should be told the truth in a form he or she can understand, rather than lied to for the administrator’s convenience.

This pairing is significant. A family can technically preserve a trust while morally destroying its beneficiary through contempt. The Qur’anic standard measures both the ledger and the relationship. A guardian who pays for food but humiliates the person has not exhausted the duty.

**A principle for institutions**

The verse extends naturally to schools, charities, care homes, public funds, and anyone responsible for another’s resources. The administrator’s skill must serve the beneficiary’s life, not the institution’s image or the administrator’s comfort. Transparent records, periodic review, and qualified advice are modern means of fulfilling the ancient rule. Wealth is placed in human hands as stewardship; the person with less capacity is not a lesser person, and the guardian’s greater capacity is a reason for restraint, not domination.

**Capacity and supported decision-making**

The rule has an important distinction for modern care: protecting a person from financial exploitation does not justify erasing the person’s choices wherever he or she can make them. A guardian should support understanding, use the least restrictive arrangement, and review capacity over time. The Qur’anic aim is welfare with honour, not permanent control by the person who happens to manage the account.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:6

> Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age. Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them. And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺. If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation; but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision. When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner.

### Expanded Commentary

**Testing before transfer**

The verse sets out a process for restoring an orphan’s wealth: test competence, wait until an appropriate age, and hand over the property when sound judgment is evident. Physical maturity alone is not enough. A young person may be old enough to marry or work but still unable to understand contracts, resist manipulation, or distinguish a profitable investment from a ruinous one. The Qur’an therefore joins age with *rushd*—practical soundness and responsible judgment.

Testing does not mean inventing humiliating examinations. It means observing real decisions under safe supervision: whether the person can budget, recognize deception, care for property, and seek advice when necessary. A guardian who deliberately keeps a competent adult dependent in order to retain control violates the purpose of the test just as surely as one who releases the wealth recklessly.

**No premature consumption**

The warning not to consume the property hastily before the orphan grows up addresses the guardian who sees approaching maturity as a deadline for spending what remains. The estate cannot be treated as a temporary salary. Nor may a guardian justify extravagance by saying that the child will never know the difference. The child’s future is precisely the reason for careful preservation.

The wealthy guardian must not take compensation from the trust. A poor guardian may take what is reasonable for necessary service, according to the juristic discussions, but reasonableness excludes luxury, hidden commissions, and expenses unrelated to care. The permission recognizes that guardianship can impose real costs; it does not turn poverty into a license to consume the principal.

**Witnesses at the handover**

Calling witnesses when the property is returned protects both parties. It records that the transfer occurred, its amount, and the condition of the accounts. The witnesses are not a substitute for honesty, but they reduce later disputes and protect a young adult from a guardian’s denial. Documentation today—bank statements, inventories, independent valuation, and signed acknowledgment—serves the same objective.

**Allah as the final reckoner**

The closing reference to Allah as a sufficient Reckoner gives the administrative procedure its moral seriousness. Human witnesses can confirm what was handed over, but they cannot know every concealed transaction or motive. The verse combines due process with divine accountability: use safeguards in the world, and remember that no safeguard hides a betrayal from God. Inheritance law thus trains the community to make mercy verifiable. Compassion is not vague goodwill; it is a disciplined transfer of another person’s right.

**The handover as a rite of adulthood**

Returning an inheritance should be treated as a serious transition into adult responsibility. The young person can be taught budgeting, contracts, charity, and the dangers of predatory offers before the transfer. A guardian who prepares the beneficiary and then steps back fulfils the spirit of the command better than one who keeps control indefinitely while claiming to be protective.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:7

> For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave—whether it is little or much. ˹These are˺ obligatory shares.

### Expanded Commentary

**A share is a right, not a favour**

This verse states a principle before supplying the detailed fractions that follow: men and women both possess a recognized share in the property left by parents and close relatives. In the customary order of the first audience, inheritance was often attached to adult male strength, military usefulness, or tribal capacity. Children and women could be excluded precisely because they were thought unable to defend the estate. Revelation makes legal entitlement precede such calculations. The share belongs because the person belongs to the family and to the law, not because he or she can fight.

**Little and much are equally protected**

The wording covers both a small estate and a great one. A poor family may be tempted to say that a tiny amount is not worth dividing, while a wealthy family may try to use its influence to suppress a daughter’s or widow’s claim. The verse rejects both excuses. A small inheritance can pay for food, debt, education, or a new beginning; its amount does not determine the reality of the right.

**The setting of revelation**

Reports connect the verse with a widow and daughters whose husband and father died, leaving male relatives prepared to take the estate. Whether the individual report is applied exactly or as an illustration, it captures the social question the verse resolves: can those without public power be written out of the family’s property? The Qur’anic answer is no. Verses 11 and 12 later identify shares, but this verse first dismantles the assumption that only men inherit.

**From custom to an accountable system**

The word “obligatory” places these entitlements within a revealed order rather than a family negotiation. A testator cannot cancel a fixed share because of resentment, and heirs cannot agree among themselves to silence a person who is under pressure. Voluntary settlement after rights are known is different from coercive renunciation before distribution. The distinction protects family peace without allowing peace to be purchased by the weakest heir.

The verse also teaches a wider legal lesson. Justice is not achieved only by praising generosity; it often requires naming a right that generosity cannot withdraw. By placing women and men in parallel grammatical positions, the Qur’an makes visible an equality of legal personhood even where later shares differ for reasons tied to financial obligations. The chapter’s inheritance law begins, therefore, with recognition: every surviving heir must be seen.

**Recognition before calculation**

Before anyone asks how much an heir receives, the family must ask who counts as an heir. The verse’s first achievement is visibility. A daughter, wife, mother, or minor child cannot be made invisible by an old custom or by the fact that someone else managed the property. Accurate calculation becomes possible only after every entitled person has been named and heard.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:8

> If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it and speak to them kindly.

### Expanded Commentary

**The distribution is a public moral moment**

After identifying the lawful heirs, the verse turns to people who may be present at the division but have no fixed legal share: relatives outside the prescribed classes, orphans, and the needy. Their presence tests whether the family regards inheritance as an occasion for gratitude and humane generosity or as a scramble for private gain. The instruction is to offer them something and address them with kindness.

The precise legal status of the gift was discussed by the jurists. Some regarded it as an enforceable duty, others as a strongly encouraged practice, and some connected it with the portion a person may bequeath. Those differences should not erase the ethical core: the completion of legal shares does not exhaust the heirs’ moral responsibility toward vulnerable people standing before them.

**Fixed shares and voluntary provision**

The verse cannot be used to override the fractions established later in the chapter. A non-heir cannot be promised the property of a person who is legally entitled to it, and the heirs cannot give away another heir’s share. The appropriate provision comes from what the recipients may lawfully offer—before distribution through a valid bequest, or afterward from their own portions. Law supplies the floor; charity raises the community above mere compliance.

**Speech can either prevent or deepen injury**

The command to speak kindly is as important as the material gift. A small amount offered with contempt may wound; a modest provision accompanied by clear, respectful words can preserve dignity. Conversely, when there is little to give, gracious speech remains possible. This principle echoes the Qur’an’s wider teaching that a kind word and forgiveness are better than charity followed by harm (2:263).

**A lesson for estate practice**

Inheritance meetings can expose old grievances, poverty, and unequal power. A just process should therefore identify all claimants, explain the shares, provide a private avenue for questions, and ensure that widows, minors, and estranged relatives are not silenced. The verse asks the family to see those whom the arithmetic does not automatically include. The estate is not only an inventory of things; it is a final opportunity to turn loss into care.

**Dignity at the distribution table**

An estate meeting should not become a public ranking of whose life was valuable. Offering provision to non-heirs and speaking kindly can prevent a bereaved gathering from turning into humiliation. The heirs who give from their own shares are not losing the law; they are showing that the law has made them trustees of a wider social mercy.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:9

> Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind. So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably.

### Expanded Commentary

**The guardian must imagine his own children**

This verse asks people who supervise an orphan’s inheritance to picture themselves leaving behind children who are small, exposed, and dependent on strangers. The appeal is not sentimental manipulation. It is a method of moral knowledge: personal vulnerability can reveal what abstract calculation hides. A guardian who would insist that his own children receive food, safety, and an intact inheritance should not accept less for another family’s children.

The verse may also address those who make bequests or advise a dying person. They must not encourage arrangements that leave their own children helpless, nor should they use another person’s death to enrich themselves. Other interpretations extend the image to any orphan present in the community. These readings converge in the rule that power over a vulnerable estate must be exercised as if one’s own beloved dependants were placed in that position.

**Taqwā before legal cleverness**

The command to be mindful of Allah prevents the verse from becoming merely an appeal to sympathy. Empathy can be selective; people may feel for children who resemble their own while neglecting strangers. Taqwā supplies a stable obligation. The orphan’s right remains protected even when the guardian feels no emotional connection to the family.

**Just speech in a moment of succession**

“Speak equitably” applies to testimony, advice, and the language used around the deathbed. A dying person may be pressured, confused, or eager to please a powerful relative. Relatives and advisers should explain the consequences of a bequest honestly and resist requests designed to harm a lawful heir. Justice also requires not flattering the wealthy heir or dismissing the widow because she is less forceful.

The verse’s conditional form—if they left behind helpless children—does not limit care to parents. It teaches a moral analogy available to everyone. We know what we fear for those we love; the Qur’an asks us to let that knowledge govern conduct toward those whom we do not know. In this way the inheritance law is built on a disciplined imagination: see the orphan not as an obstacle to your share, but as the child you would pray others would protect if your own life ended today.

**The moral imagination of succession**

People who advise on wills should imagine not only their own children but also the child whose parent is dying at that very moment. This makes manipulation harder. It encourages balanced advice, transparent documentation, and refusal to exploit a dying person’s confusion. The verse turns private fear into a universal standard of care.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:10

> Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell!

### Expanded Commentary

**Fire hidden inside an ordinary transaction**

The verse changes the imagery of wealth. What appears to be food, coins, land, or an account is in reality fire when it has been taken from an orphan unjustly. The Qur’an does not present this as a metaphor that makes the act less real; it reveals the transaction’s ultimate character. A guardian may consume the property with pleasure in this world, but the moral substance of that consumption is burning.

The wording “into their bellies” emphasizes intimate incorporation. The wrong is not external to the person who commits it. He carries it within himself, and what he has swallowed becomes evidence against him. This reverses the usual logic of greed: the consumer thinks that property has entered his safety, while revelation tells him that the crime has entered his ruin.

**Why orphan wealth is singled out**

An orphan cannot normally enforce a contract against the adult who controls his home and documents. The offence therefore joins theft to betrayal of guardianship. It also steals time: by the time the child becomes an adult, records may be lost, assets depleted, and the confidence to challenge the guardian destroyed. The Qur’anic threat protects a future claim before the beneficiary has the power to defend it.

The Prophet’s inclusion of consuming orphan property among the seven destructive sins (al-Bukhārī 2766; Muslim 89) confirms that the warning is not rhetorical exaggeration. Yet the verse is not meant to make guardians despair. It tells them the danger so that they will separate funds, seek accountability, and return every right. Repentance for an offence involving another person requires more than private regret; it requires restoration where restoration remains possible.

**Blazing punishment and moral proportion**

The final image is severe because the social consequences are severe. If powerful relatives can consume the assets of children without fear, the family becomes a mechanism for predation. The fire is therefore both an individual judgment and a declaration that society may not normalize the practice.

Contemporary guardians should read the verse with practical precision: keep records, use independent oversight, avoid conflicts of interest, invest conservatively, and transfer the assets when competence is established. The Qur’an’s threat is not opposed to administration; it demands administration that remembers every figure in the ledger belongs to a person whose Lord sees the hand that takes it.

**Restoration is part of repentance**

If an orphan’s assets were taken, repentance should be concrete: reconstruct the account, return the principal and legitimate gains where possible, disclose the loss, and accept lawful consequences. A guardian may feel embarrassed by such disclosure, but protecting reputation cannot take precedence over the child’s right. The Fire image warns against the fantasy that hidden wealth stays hidden forever.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:11

> Allah commands you regarding your children: the share of the male will be twice that of the female. If you leave only two ˹or more˺ females, their share is two-thirds of the estate. But if there is only one female, her share will be one-half. Each parent is entitled to one-sixth if you leave offspring. But if you are childless and your parents are the only heirs, then your mother will receive one-third. But if you leave siblings, then your mother will receive one-sixth—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. ˹Be fair to˺ your parents and children, as you do not ˹fully˺ know who is more beneficial to you. ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**A revealed alternative to family power**

This is the first detailed inheritance ordinance in the sūrah, and it begins by placing children under divine protection. The pre-Islamic rule that inheritance belonged to adult male relatives is displaced by a schedule that recognizes daughters, sons, and parents. The verse does not leave the distribution to the preferences of the deceased or the strength of the surviving clan. It assigns portions through revelation because the people who benefit from a person’s affection are not always the people whom his power would protect.

**The shares of children**

When sons and daughters inherit together, the son’s portion is twice the daughter’s. This ratio is not a general statement that a son is twice a daughter’s worth. It is part of a larger financial structure in which the male heir bears obligatory maintenance for wives, children, and needy relatives, while a daughter’s inheritance remains her own property and is not a fund from which she must maintain a household. The distinction is a distribution of financial duties, not a ranking of human dignity.

If there are only daughters, the verse gives two or more a collective two-thirds and one daughter one-half. The remaining questions—such as residuary heirs and particular combinations—are treated through Prophetic practice and juristic reasoning. The apparent simplicity of the language should not encourage a person to calculate a complicated estate without qualified advice; debts, valid bequests, parents, spouses, and other heirs interact in ways that require the science of *farāʾiḍ*.

**The parents’ claims**

Each parent receives a sixth when the deceased leaves children. If there are no children and the parents are the only heirs, the mother receives a third, with the father taking the remainder under the established rules. When siblings are present, the mother’s share is reduced to a sixth. These provisions recognize that the deceased’s relationship to parents is not erased by marriage or adulthood, while also making room for the needs of a surviving household.

The statement that people do not know which parents or children are more beneficial to them corrects human attempts to measure affection. A child who seems dependent may become a source of good; a powerful heir may become a source of harm. God assigns because He knows the future and the hidden obligations better than the heirs do. The final words—All-Knowing, All-Wise—turn arithmetic into worship: the believer accepts a rule whose complete wisdom may exceed his immediate preference.

**Why the law is detailed**

The precision of the shares should itself be read as mercy toward families. Vague exhortations to be generous leave the weaker heir dependent on the stronger heir’s character. Fixed portions do not remove all complexity, but they set a floor below which affection, anger, or custom may not push a person. Learning the law is therefore a form of protecting kinship.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:12

> You will inherit half of what your wives leave if they are childless. But if they have children, then ˹your share is˺ one-fourth of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless. But if you have children, then your wives will receive one-eighth of your estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but only a brother or a sister ˹from their mother’s side˺, they will each inherit one-sixth, but if they are more than one, they ˹all˺ will share one-third of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm ˹to the heirs˺. ˹This is˺ a commandment from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing.

### Expanded Commentary

**Spouses remain heirs across household boundaries**

The ordinance in this verse protects the marital bond without allowing it to erase blood relations. A husband receives half of his wife’s estate when she leaves no child and a quarter when she does; a wife receives a quarter of her husband’s estate when he leaves no child and an eighth when he does. The difference reflects the deceased person’s surviving financial responsibilities, while the basic principle is mutual: marriage creates an enforceable claim in both directions.

If there are several wives, they share the collective portion assigned to wives. The text speaks in fractions because justice here must be calculable. A family’s grief cannot be allowed to become an excuse for uncertainty, and a widow should not have to bargain with her husband’s relatives for what revelation has already made hers.

**The maternal siblings and the meaning of *kalālah***

The brother or sister mentioned in this part is understood by the exegetes to be a maternal sibling, in the case described here, when the deceased leaves neither parents nor children. Each receives a sixth if there is one; several share a third equally. This equal division differs from the ratio for certain other siblings in verse 176 and illustrates why inheritance cannot be reduced to slogans. The identity of the heir and the relationship to the deceased matter.

The classical commentators discussed the scope of *kalālah*—one who dies without direct descendants and, in many interpretations, without a surviving father or grandfather. The Prophetic and early legal traditions clarify cases not apparent from a quick reading. The wise practice is to begin with a complete family tree, not with the portion one hopes to receive.

**No bequest designed to injure**

Both the priority of debts and bequests and the prohibition of harm are repeated. A valid bequest cannot be a device for depriving fixed heirs, favouring one child secretly, or creating a burden that consumes the estate. The Prophet warned that a bequest should not injure the heirs, and the jurists therefore distinguish a lawful bequest within its limits from an abusive arrangement disguised as charity.

The conclusion joins knowledge to forbearance. God knows the needs behind the shares, and He is forbearing with human grief and confusion. The law is firm, but people applying it must not be harsh, hasty, or vindictive. Proper succession begins with obedience and is carried out with patience.

**A complete estate, not a preferred heir**

The surviving spouse should be identified at the beginning of the calculation rather than treated as an outsider to the deceased’s birth family. Likewise, maternal siblings should not be forgotten because their relationship is less visible in patriarchal customs. A careful executor inventories debts and bequests before distributing anything, since generosity with property that belongs to another is not generosity.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:13

> These ˹entitlements˺ are the limits set by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger will be admitted into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. That is the ultimate triumph!

### Expanded Commentary

**Limits are protective boundaries**

After detailing shares, the Qur’an calls them the limits set by Allah. A limit is not merely a restriction on desire; it marks a protected boundary within which vulnerable rights are safe. The inheritance fractions prevent the powerful heir from absorbing the estate, the grieving testator from favouring one child, and custom from excluding women. To treat the shares as optional advice is therefore to remove the fence that protects the weak.

**Obedience includes practical distribution**

The verse connects obedience to Allah and His Messenger with admission to gardens. This connection corrects a false division between spiritual devotion and legal conduct. Prayer, fasting, and charitable feeling cannot compensate for deliberately withholding a widow’s share. At the same time, accurate distribution is not mere bureaucracy: it becomes an act of obedience when the heirs submit their preferences to revealed justice.

Prophetic practice is necessary for application. The Qur’an gives the central portions, while the Messenger explained the order of debts and bequests, the treatment of residuary heirs, and the details of competing claims. The believer follows both the principle and the authoritative explanation rather than choosing whichever calculation produces the desired result.

**The great success**

The word “triumph” redirects the family’s measure of success. A person may think he has triumphed by obtaining a larger portion, but if the gain rests on disobedience it is a loss that extends beyond the grave. Conversely, an heir who relinquishes an unlawful advantage or ensures that a weaker relative receives her due may appear to lose materially while attaining the success the verse names. Paradise is promised not to the best negotiator but to those who honour the boundary.

**Limits preserve peace**

The divine limits also protect the heirs from endless negotiation. Once the shares are known, a family can choose voluntary gifts from its own property without forcing a weaker person to bargain for recognition. Obedience thus makes reconciliation easier: people can disagree about feelings while submitting to a rule that is not controlled by the loudest relative.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:14

> But whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and exceeds their limits will be cast into Hell, to stay there forever. And they will suffer a humiliating punishment.

### Expanded Commentary

**The opposite boundary**

The warning follows immediately so that no reader can reduce the preceding rules to recommendations. To disobey Allah and His Messenger and to cross the limits is presented as a grave spiritual act. Inheritance is one of the clearest places where greed attempts to borrow the language of family affection: “we have always done it this way,” “she does not need it,” or “the estate should stay with the men.” The verse identifies such rationalizations as transgression when they contradict the revealed shares.

**Humiliation is part of the judgment**

The punishment is described as humiliating, not only painful. The wrongdoer sought honour through control of property, status within the clan, or public appearance. The final judgment exposes that apparent honour as disgrace. The orphan, widow, or daughter whom he treated as insignificant is not forgotten, and the inheritance he seized cannot purchase dignity before God.

The language of permanence is read by theologians in light of the Qur’an’s broader treatment of disbelief and grave sin. It certainly establishes a terrifying warning and the seriousness of knowingly violating divine limits. It should not be used to close the door of repentance that God has opened elsewhere, but repentance must include restoration of rights. A person who returns to Allah while still retaining another heir’s property has not completed the repair.

**Law guarded by conscience**

The verse also protects against the opposite error: treating legal compliance as a licence for cruelty. Giving a lawful portion while humiliating a grieving relative, concealing assets, or forcing a renunciation still contradicts the chapter’s justice. The limits include the manner in which rights are delivered. The family that fears this warning will use transparent accounts, impartial advice, and respectful speech, because it understands that the estate is being divided under the sight of the One who revealed it.

**The danger of calling injustice tradition**

Many violations of inheritance begin as apparently respectable customs. “This is how our family has always done it” can conceal a history of excluding daughters or pressuring widows. The warning against transgressing God’s limits gives believers a criterion stronger than ancestral prestige. A custom deserves preservation only when it can stand before justice.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:15

> ˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse—call four witnesses from among yourselves. If they testify, confine the offenders to their homes until they die or Allah ordains a ˹different˺ way for them.

### Expanded Commentary

**A high evidentiary threshold**

This verse addresses an accusation of unlawful sexual intercourse and requires four witnesses from the believing community. The condition is deliberately difficult to satisfy. It prevents rumour, jealousy, political revenge, and private suspicion from becoming a criminal judgment. A person who makes the accusation must do more than repeat what was heard or infer guilt from association; the law requires direct, reliable testimony of the act.

The early rule here prescribed confinement in the home until Allah revealed a further way, which later came through the punishment and procedural rules of the Qur’an and Sunnah. The verse should therefore be read within the sequence of revelation and not detached from the later legal system. It does not authorize vigilante imprisonment, family violence, or public humiliation. Criminal penalties belong to legitimate judicial authority and require the evidentiary protections established by revelation.

**Protecting reputation and the public order**

The four-witness requirement recognizes that sexual accusation can destroy a person even when no conviction follows. The law places the burden on the accuser because the alleged victim should not have to prove innocence against an invisible rumour. Later verses explicitly punish those who accuse chaste women without producing the required witnesses (24:4). Together these provisions make honour a protected interest.

This protection does not mean that abuse can never be reported or that a victim must produce four eyewitnesses to seek help. The legal category of *zinā* has a particular proof standard; evidence concerning assault, coercion, harm, or safeguarding is considered under other legal frameworks. Confusing these categories can silence victims and also permit reckless accusation. The verse demands both seriousness about sexual wrongdoing and seriousness about the damage of false claims.

**A temporary ruling and a permanent principle**

Exegetes discuss whether “women” refers generally to female offenders or to a particular category in the first Muslim community. Whatever the historical scope, the enduring principle is procedural restraint. A community that cares for justice must refuse to turn suspicion into certainty. The dignity of the vulnerable is guarded not by ignoring sin but by ensuring that judgment is based on proof rather than appetite.

**Evidence protects both sides**

The four-witness rule also protects a community from the destructive certainty of crowds. A rumour can be morally false even when many people repeat it. Responsible safeguarding still requires listening to victims and investigating harm, but it does not permit a public accusation to become a conviction without a lawful process. The verse teaches seriousness without sensationalism.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:16

> And the two among you who commit this sin—discipline them. If they repent and mend their ways, relieve them. Surely Allah is ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful.

### Expanded Commentary

**“The two among you” and the early communal response**

The verse refers to two people who commit the sexual offence and instructs the community to discipline them, then release the disciplinary response when they repent and reform. Early exegetes differed over whether the wording refers to a man and a woman, two men, or the two parties to the act in a general sense. The later legal verses refine the punishments, but this passage makes clear that public response must have a moral and judicial framework rather than becoming private revenge.

**Repentance is demonstrated by reform**

The verse does not treat an apology as a magic eraser. Repentance is joined to amendment: the wrongdoing must stop, its causes must be confronted, and any person harmed must receive restitution where possible. For a community, this means that discipline can have a rehabilitative purpose. It is not permitted to enjoy another person’s disgrace after the person has genuinely returned and repaired what can be repaired.

**Mercy within accountability**

The release from discipline is tied to Allah’s acceptance of repentance and mercy. That pairing prevents two distortions. One is permissiveness that calls every consequence oppression; the other is punitive cruelty that refuses to recognize moral change. Legitimate authority must assess evidence, repentance, and public safety. Individuals must not take the verse as permission to expose sins that Allah has concealed or to appoint themselves as executioners of justice.

The verse’s place beside 4:17–18 is important. The chapter will define when repentance is likely to be accepted and when a person has postponed it until the moment of death. Here, the emphasis is on a living reformer who can turn conduct around. The community’s task is to stop the harm, preserve justice, and leave a genuine path back to God.

**From discipline to restoration**

A response to sexual wrongdoing should ask how further harm will be prevented, how the parties can return to lawful conduct, and how private repentance can occur without public spectacle. The verse’s instruction to relieve them after reform resists the human appetite to keep punishing someone after the stated purpose of discipline has been fulfilled.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:17

> Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after—Allah will pardon them. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**Repentance is received while the moral capacity remains**

Allah’s acceptance of repentance is described as applying to those who commit evil in ignorance and then turn soon afterward. Ignorance here is not limited to having no information. It can include recklessness, impulsiveness, the clouding of judgment by passion, or acting without considering the divine consequence. It does not make the sin permissible; it explains the condition of a person who has not made defiance into a settled identity.

“Soon” does not set a stopwatch that humans can use to declare another person hopeless. Rather, it contrasts living responsiveness with deliberate postponement. A sinner who recognizes the wrong, stops it, regrets it, and begins repair should not be told that mercy is closed merely because days or years have passed. The urgency lies in not making delay a strategy.

**The elements of genuine return**

The jurists generally describe repentance as regret, cessation of the sin, resolve not to return, and restoration of other people’s rights when the offence involved them. A person who stole must return or compensate; a person who defamed must correct the harm in a wise manner; a person who abandoned an obligation must resume it according to its rules. Mere verbal regret while the benefit of the wrongdoing is retained does not match the verse’s moral movement.

**Knowledge and wisdom**

The divine names at the end are precise. Allah knows the inward truth of the turning person—the fear, motives, and resolve that human observers cannot fully measure—and He is wise in judging both the sin and its remedy. Human beings may need safeguards and consequences even after repentance, but they must not claim access to the final judgment of hearts.

The verse keeps hope and urgency together. No one should despair because the past is ugly; no one should feel secure because repentance can be pronounced later. The door is open now, and the wise person walks through it while choice, strength, and the possibility of repair are still present.

**No despair, no delay**

A person who has sinned should begin with the next available act of obedience rather than waiting to feel worthy. At the same time, “I will repent later” is not a harmless plan. The heart becomes accustomed to the sin it repeatedly postpones. The verse’s mercy is an invitation to turn today, when the turning can still shape the rest of a life.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:18

> However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, “Now I repent!” nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.

### Expanded Commentary

**The postponed confession**

The verse distinguishes repentance from a declaration made when death has removed the possibility of continued obedience. A person may knowingly persist in wrongdoing, treating warnings as distant, and then announce repentance when the unseen becomes visible. That statement may be emotionally sincere, but it is not the tested transformation described in the previous verse. The difference is not that God lacks mercy at the deathbed; it is that the person has reached the end of the arena in which repentance can become a life.

Pharaoh’s confession as he drowned is the Qur’anic example: recognition came only when punishment was undeniable, and it could no longer produce faithful action. The same principle appears in the Qur’an’s account of people who ask to return after death. A confession that costs no surrender of desire and cannot restore a harmed person is not equivalent to the turning of the living heart.

**A warning against spiritual procrastination**

The verse also addresses those who die as disbelievers. It does not encourage human beings to declare another person’s final state in individual cases; that judgment belongs to Allah. It warns the believer not to build a plan around delaying faith until certainty is forced upon him. Faith is a willing response to signs, not a word extracted by the arrival of the final consequence.

**Repentance and the rights of others**

The two verses together make a practical point about injustice. A guardian who consumed an orphan’s property cannot wait until death and assume that a sentence of regret cancels the child’s claim. Restoration must begin while the assets, victims, and opportunities for repair remain accessible. Likewise, a person who has harmed a spouse, employee, or neighbour should make amends now rather than convert fear of judgment into a last-minute performance.

The verse is severe because delay is a form of choice. Yet its severity is also mercy: while the reader is alive, the prohibition has not become the description of him. The proper response is immediate return, not despair.

**The ethics of timely repair**

The verse is especially urgent where another person has been harmed. A delayed apology may leave evidence lost and the victim unsupported. Return the property, correct the allegation, seek treatment, and ask forgiveness while these steps can still change the outcome. Deathbed regret may be sincere, but it cannot give back every opportunity that delay destroyed.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:19

> O believers! It is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will or mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry ˹as a ransom for divorce˺—unless they are found guilty of adultery. Treat them fairly. If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing.

### Expanded Commentary

**Women are persons, not inheritable property**

The verse abolishes a practice in which male relatives treated a deceased man’s widow as part of the estate. They might marry her, compel her to marry someone else, confine her until she surrendered property, or simply prevent her from choosing a new life. Revelation addresses the men who exercised this power and declares that a woman cannot be inherited against her will. She is herself a legal subject and an inheritor, not an asset to be transferred.

The prohibition also covers using harsh treatment to force a wife to return part of her dowry. A husband who wants release from a marriage may not manufacture misery so that the wife purchases freedom with property that was already hers. The exception concerning flagrant sexual misconduct must be interpreted through due process and the rest of the Qur’anic legal framework; it is not a licence for accusation, violence, or unilateral confiscation.

**Living together with *maʿrūf***

The command to consort with wives honourably includes good speech, adequate provision, respect for privacy, fulfilment of marital rights, and restraint during disagreement. *Maʿrūf* means what is recognized as decent and sound, not whatever a powerful husband can persuade himself is acceptable. Custom matters only when it is compatible with justice and revelation.

**Dislike is not a verdict**

The verse acknowledges that a spouse may dislike something in the other, but warns that human perception is partial. A quality that seems burdensome may conceal patience, loyalty, or goodness; a period of difficulty may be transformed by effort and counsel. This is not an instruction to remain in every harmful relationship. Abuse, danger, and persistent injustice must be addressed, and separation can be lawful and merciful. It is a warning against treating ordinary disappointment as permission to strip a spouse of rights.

The order is telling: first prohibit coercion, then command good treatment, then educate the heart about its limited knowledge. Marriage cannot be sustained by feeling alone, but it can be protected when law prevents exploitation and character refuses to turn irritation into oppression.

**Honourable association has limits**

Kind treatment does not require remaining in a relationship that is unsafe or persistently destructive. The verse’s encouragement to look for hidden good belongs with its prohibitions on coercion and the chapter’s arbitration procedures. It teaches patience where patience can repair, while the wider law protects a spouse from being trapped by a husband’s dislike, anger, or financial power.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:20

> If you desire to replace a wife with another and you have given the former ˹even˺ a stack of gold ˹as a dowry˺, do not take any of it back. Would you ˹still˺ take it unjustly and very sinfully?

### Expanded Commentary

**The dowry cannot be reclaimed through replacement**

A husband who wishes to marry another woman is prohibited from taking back the dowry he gave to the first, even when it was a large amount. The image of a “stack of gold” emphasizes that the size of the gift does not change its legal character. Once given, it belongs to the wife. Divorce does not retroactively turn her property into a loan.

This rule prevents a powerful form of financial pressure. A husband could otherwise threaten to replace his wife and demand repayment, leaving her with the choice between surrendering wealth and enduring a marriage in which she is unwanted. The Qur’an refuses to let a new desire rewrite an old obligation.

**Calumny as a tool of confiscation**

The verse asks whether the husband would take the property through slander and manifest sin. Accusing the wife of sexual misconduct, inventing defects, or publicly shaming her in order to make her relinquish the dowry is doubly wrong: it harms her reputation and steals her asset. The preceding verse’s exception cannot be activated by a private allegation; evidence and legitimate adjudication are required.

**Marriage, wealth, and moral memory**

The ordinance applies even when the husband has spent heavily on the marriage or feels that the relationship has failed. Financial disappointment does not erase the wife’s right. If she freely chooses to return some of it in a lawful separation arrangement, that is a different transaction; coercion is the dividing line.

For contemporary practice, the verse calls for clear contracts, independent access to the bride’s property, and a divorce process that does not make the woman bargain under fear. A new relationship may be permissible, but it cannot be financed by the unjust recovery of an old wife’s entitlement. God’s question—would you take it through falsehood and open sin?—turns an apparently private financial decision into a matter of conscience.

**Divorce must not become financial punishment**

A fair separation process tells each spouse what is owed, protects the waiting period, and prevents relatives from using shame to recover the dowry. The husband’s wish for a new marriage does not make the former wife responsible for financing it. The question in the verse should be asked before any demand is made: is this a lawful settlement, or is fear being used to disguise theft?

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:21

> And how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately and she has taken from you a firm commitment?

### Expanded Commentary

**A covenant, not a disposable arrangement**

The verse asks how a husband could take back the dowry after intimacy and after the spouses have entered a solemn covenant. The question is a moral rebuke: the private union that made the dowry due also reveals the gravity of the promise. Marriage is not a consumer transaction in which one party may demand a refund when preference changes. Intimacy creates vulnerability, and the covenant exists to protect that vulnerability with duties of care, maintenance, fidelity, and dignified separation.

**The indirect language of intimacy**

The expression translated as “you have been intimate with one another” uses an indirect Qur’anic idiom. Its modesty does not make the legal point vague. Classical jurists discussed whether the phrase refers to sexual intercourse, seclusion in circumstances that make intimacy possible, or both for particular legal consequences. Those distinctions affect the amount of dowry due in different situations, but none permits the husband to exploit uncertainty in order to seize the wife’s property.

**A solemn covenant before God**

Marriage is called *mīthāq*, a covenant. The Qur’an uses this word for weighty agreements and commitments, including the covenant taken from prophets. The Prophet’s Farewell address described women as a trust from God and marriage as made lawful through His word. A man therefore answers not only to his wife’s family or to a court but to the One who made the covenant meaningful.

This also explains why lawful divorce does not make the marriage insignificant. A covenant may be ended under rules without being treated as though it never existed. The husband must release his wife honourably, settle financial obligations, and avoid converting the history of intimacy into a weapon against her reputation.

**The ethical force of the question**

The verse appeals to memory: how can you take it back after you have shared what spouses share and pledged what spouses pledge? The believer is asked to let the closeness that once made a demand possible now restrain the demand. A successful legal system can compel payment, but the verse seeks something deeper—the conscience that finds confiscation shameful even before a judge forbids it.

**The covenant remembers consequences**

A marriage contract should make the parties more careful with one another precisely because intimacy has made them vulnerable. Clear terms, timely payment of dowry, and respectful divorce procedures are not signs that affection is absent; they are protections for the days when affection is strained. The solemn covenant gives legal form to the moral memory of shared life.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:22

> Do not marry former wives of your fathers—except what was done previously. It was indeed a shameful, despicable, and evil practice.

### Expanded Commentary

**Ending a pre-Islamic marriage custom**

The verse prohibits marrying women whom one’s fathers married. In the society addressed by the Qur’an, a deceased man’s widow could be treated as available to his male heir or stepson, sometimes as part of the property of the household. The ban is absolute for the future, while “except what has passed” recognizes that people had acted before the ruling and are not condemned for an act whose prohibition had not yet reached them.

**Marriage is not inheritance**

The placement after the prohibition on inheriting women is significant. A widow’s connection to a deceased husband does not make her transferable to his sons or brothers. She has her own will and dignity. A family may feel that preserving property or alliances justifies the arrangement, but revelation separates lineage from ownership. The widow is not a bridge by which property remains in the male line.

The rule also protects the integrity of family roles. The woman who was a father’s wife cannot become a son’s wife; what one generation treated as marriage cannot be repackaged by another generation as an ordinary contract. The prohibition is about the structure of kinship as well as about consent.

**Three moral descriptions**

The act is called indecent, hateful, and an evil way. These terms address different dimensions: its violation of modesty, its moral repugnance, and its consequences as a path that deforms family life. The Qur’an does not treat every inherited custom as sacred merely because ancestors practiced it. A custom is tested against justice, revelation, and the protection of persons.

The verse illustrates a broader method of reform. It names the practice clearly, closes the door for the future, and does not turn pre-revelation history into a pretext for endless guilt. Communities today likewise need the courage to end customs that make widows, divorced women, or dependants into objects of family control, even when those customs carry the prestige of age.

**Reform begins by naming the object**

The widow who was once treated as part of a household inventory becomes visible as a person with a decision. Communities often need this same act of naming when harmful customs are defended through euphemism. Once a practice is described as coercion rather than tradition, its moral evaluation becomes harder to evade.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:23

> ˹Also˺ forbidden to you for marriage are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal and maternal aunts, your brother’s daughters, your sister’s daughters, your foster-mothers, your foster-sisters, your mothers-inlaw, your stepdaughters under your guardianship if you have consummated marriage with their mothers—but if you have not, then you can marry them —nor the wives of your own sons, nor two sisters together at the same time —except what was done previously. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

### Expanded Commentary

**A map of permanent family boundaries**

This verse lists women whom a man may never marry because of blood, nursing, or affinity. The list begins with mothers and daughters, sisters, paternal and maternal aunts, nieces, foster mothers and sisters, mothers-in-law, stepdaughters under the specified marital condition, sons’ wives, and the wives of fathers. It closes by forbidding marriage to two sisters at the same time. The verse turns family relationships into protected boundaries rather than matters left to competing custom.

**Blood and nursing**

The inclusion of nursing relationships shows that kinship in Islamic law is not limited to genetic descent. Breastfeeding in the legally recognized manner creates a family relationship with marriage consequences. This protects the social meaning of nursing and prevents a child’s intimate early dependence from being ignored once adulthood arrives. The juristic schools discuss the number and circumstances of feeds that establish the prohibition; families should therefore record such relationships rather than rely on fading memory.

**Affinity and the condition of consummation**

A mother-in-law becomes permanently prohibited upon the marriage contract, while the stepdaughter mentioned in the verse becomes prohibited when the marriage with her mother has been consummated, according to the dominant legal reading. The distinction reflects the different forms of affinity created by a contract and by an established marital household. A person should not make a consequential decision from a partial list; marriage law requires attention to the details of the relationship.

**No simultaneous marriage to close sisters**

The ban on combining two sisters protects the original kinship from being turned into a field of rivalry. It also shows that a prohibition may concern simultaneity rather than the women individually: each sister could, after a lawful end to the first marriage and completion of the waiting period, marry the man, but both cannot be wives at once. The same principle is discussed concerning a woman and her paternal or maternal aunt.

The verse ends by recognizing past cases and by describing God as forgiving and merciful. Mercy does not preserve the custom; it allows people to stop at the point of guidance and rebuild their households within the new boundary.

**Boundaries make trust possible**

The prohibited degrees of marriage preserve spaces in which family care can remain free from sexual competition. They also help people identify obligations that arise through nursing and affinity. Healthy communities need clear boundaries because affection without boundaries can be manipulated by those who have authority over children and dependants.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:24

> Also ˹forbidden are˺ married women—except ˹female˺ captives in your possession. This is Allah’s commandment to you. ˹But˺ lawful to you are all beyond these—as long as you seek them with your wealth in a legal marriage, not in fornication. Give those you have consummated marriage with their due dowries. It is permissible to be mutually gracious regarding the set dowry. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**Marriage and the protection of existing bonds**

The verse first prohibits married women, with the historical exception of captives governed by the law of war at the time of revelation. The basic rule is that a woman already bound by marriage is not available for another contract. The exception belonged to a legal institution that the revelation regulated through processes of separation and waiting, not to private trafficking or modern exploitation. Reading the verse responsibly requires preserving both its historical context and its governing concern: marriage cannot be invaded by arbitrary power.

**Beyond the named list**

The verse continues the list of prohibited relationships and then distinguishes what is lawful. The Qur’an’s wording makes clear that lawful sexual relations must arise through property and contract rules recognized by revelation, not through secret arrangements or coercion. Classical law elaborated conditions concerning consent, guardianship, dowry, witnesses, and waiting periods to ensure that the contract was real rather than a disguise for abuse.

**Seeking chastity, not possession**

The purpose of marriage is described as seeking chastity and avoiding unlawful intimacy. This reframes the contract as a discipline for desire and a site of mutual rights. A person who enters marriage only to use or humiliate the other has violated its purpose even if the external paperwork is present. Conversely, the verse acknowledges human sexual needs without treating them as an excuse to disregard commitment.

**The financial obligation**

The dowry is made obligatory for the lawful enjoyment of marriage, though the spouses may agree on its amount and timing. The wife owns it, and any subsequent adjustment must be voluntary. The phrase “without blame” concerning what is mutually agreed recognizes that contracts can contain different arrangements, provided they do not cancel fixed rights or conceal coercion.

The verse’s legal detail sits within a moral declaration: God knows what is permissible and wise, and believers are not free to rebuild the family on appetite alone. The rules protect the existing wife, the future wife, and the integrity of the contract by requiring that desire travel through responsibility.

**Desire under covenant**

The verse’s rules do not deny sexual desire; they give it a lawful shape that protects those who bear its consequences. A marriage is credible when the woman’s property, status, and safety remain protected even if the husband’s desire changes. The law thus treats chastity as a shared social responsibility, not a burden placed only on women.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:25

> But if any of you cannot afford to marry a free believing woman, then ˹let him marry˺ a believing bondwoman possessed by one of you. Allah knows best ˹the state of˺ your faith ˹and theirs˺. You are from one another. So marry them with the permission of their owners, giving them their dowry in fairness, if they are chaste, neither promiscuous nor having secret affairs. If they commit indecency after marriage, they receive half the punishment of free women. This is for those of you who fear falling into sin. But if you are patient, it is better for you. And Allah is AllForgiving, Most Merciful.

### Expanded Commentary

**A dispensation tied to real constraints**

The verse addresses a man unable to marry a free believing woman and permits marriage to a believing bondwoman with her owners’ permission. This belongs to the historical institution of slavery and to a world in which emancipation was being encouraged but not completed at once. The passage does not invite the powerful to acquire women for pleasure; it places marriage, faith, consent through the lawful authority of the time, and a dowry within the arrangement, while describing the woman as belonging to another household in a way modern readers must treat as a historical legal category rather than a moral ideal.

**Equal spiritual worth**

The verse makes the crucial point that believing bondwomen are of the same human community as free women. Social status is not the measure of acceptance with Allah. Their Lord knows their faith, and the marriage is not a second-class spiritual union. The Prophet’s instruction to care for servants and feed and clothe them as one does oneself expresses the wider direction in which this law operates.

**A protected marriage**

The woman receives a dowry and enters marriage in chastity, not as a secret concubine. If she commits the specified offence, the legal penalty was half that of a free woman in the classical law because of her status, but the verse’s legal mechanics should never be detached from the prohibition of sexual exploitation and the possibilities of emancipation. It also warns that patience and chastity are better than treating the dispensation casually.

**Reading a historical text without erasing its limits**

The verse is neither a licence to revive trafficking nor a reason to deny the Qur’an’s historical legal address. It regulated a pervasive institution, recognized the faith and rights of enslaved people, and directed conduct toward lawful marriage and eventual freedom. Contemporary law has abolished the institution in the societies to which Muslims belong; the category therefore has no legitimate modern application. The lasting principle is that sexual access must not be built on coercion, concealment, or contempt for a person’s faith and dignity.

**The direction of the law**

The historical category addressed here should be studied with the Qur’an’s emancipation measures, the Prophetic commands of humane treatment, and the contemporary abolition of slavery. It cannot be extracted to validate ownership of persons today. What remains applicable is the insistence that vulnerability never makes a person available for secret or coercive intimacy.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:26

> It is Allah’s Will to make things clear to you, guide you to the ˹noble˺ ways of those before you, and turn to you in mercy. For Allah is AllKnowing, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**Law as clarification**

The verse explains why these marriage regulations have been revealed: Allah wishes to make the lawful and unlawful clear, guide believers through the patterns of earlier peoples, and turn toward them in mercy. This answer matters because legal detail can feel restrictive when isolated from purpose. Revelation clarifies in order to protect life, lineage, consent, property, and worship—not to multiply burdens for their own sake.

**Earlier communities as moral evidence**

The phrase about the ways of those before refers to the continuity of divine guidance and to the lessons found in earlier religious communities. It does not mean that every historical practice is automatically authoritative. Earlier revelation and prophetic example show how God’s servants sought to organize marriage and family, while the Qur’an confirms, corrects, and completes the rule for its own community.

**Repentance is built into legislation**

The promise that Allah turns to believers in mercy prevents the law from being read as a closed door for people who have lived in confusion. A person may have inherited a harmful custom, entered an unlawful relationship, or failed to understand a right. Once the guidance becomes clear, repentance means stopping the wrong, regularizing what can be regularized, and repairing harm. Mercy does not abolish accountability; it makes reform possible.

The verse is also an interpretive guide: when a legal command is difficult, seek the protected good it serves. That search does not allow a person to invent exceptions, but it does guard against a mechanical application that defeats the very welfare the law was meant to secure. Clarity and mercy are presented together because divine law is both intelligible and compassionate.

**Clarity is a mercy to the confused**

When a community explains its family law honestly, people can leave inherited abuse without guessing whether reform is allowed. Scholars should therefore distinguish settled principles from disputed applications and state the evidence for both. Clarity prevents the powerful from using complexity as a fog around another person’s rights.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:27

> And it is Allah’s Will to turn to you in grace, but those who follow their desires wish to see you deviate entirely ˹from Allah’s Way˺.

### Expanded Commentary

**The difference between guidance and appetite**

Allah wishes to turn toward believers in mercy, while those who follow desires want them to deviate greatly. The contrast is not between people who have no desires and people who have them. Desire is part of human nature. The question is whether desire is disciplined by revealed guidance or whether it recruits interpretation, custom, and power to make its own path appear lawful.

In the context of marriage, the warning includes those who want vulnerable women, orphans, and dependants to remain available to their appetites. A guardian may call exploitation protection; a husband may call confiscation divorce; a family may call coercion tradition. The verse exposes the underlying movement: desire seeks not merely a concession but a major deviation from the boundary.

**Why the deviation is “great”**

Small compromises can accumulate into an entire social order. One family hides a dowry, another excludes a daughter, another treats a widow as property, and soon the exception becomes the custom. The Qur’an describes the desired deviation as great because it would move the community away from the central direction of mercy and justice, not merely because one technical rule has been broken.

**Mercy as a social force**

Allah’s turning toward believers is not sentimental softness. It appears as clarification, protection, and a route back from inherited wrongdoing. Believers imitate that mercy by making rights intelligible, helping people leave harmful arrangements, and refusing to use law as camouflage for domination. A community should be especially alert when those with power insist that their desires define what is reasonable.

**Desire can recruit interpretation**

The verse is a warning for readers of every age: a person may search for a legal opinion only after deciding what he wants, then call the preferred answer guidance. The cure is to disclose the interest at stake, consult someone independent, and accept that revelation may correct the desire rather than bless it.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:28

> And it is Allah’s Will to lighten your burdens, for humankind was created weak.

### Expanded Commentary

**Human weakness and realistic law**

Allah wishes to lighten the burden because the human being was created weak. In this setting, the weakness includes sexual desire, emotional attachment, financial pressure, ignorance, and the difficulty of maintaining a household without guidance. The statement does not insult humanity; it explains why people need law, reminders, repentance, and institutions that prevent a moment of weakness from becoming permanent injustice.

**Lightening is not abandonment**

Divine facilitation does not mean every preference becomes lawful. It means the law recognizes capacity and gives legitimate routes: marriage instead of secret intimacy, defined dowry instead of vague entitlement, repentance instead of despair, and arbitration instead of endless domestic conflict. The route may require discipline, but it does not demand that human beings pretend they have no needs.

**A balance between ideal and reality**

The Qur’an’s family legislation often places a high ideal beside an acknowledgment of limitation. Justice must be pursued even though complete emotional equality is difficult; chastity is required even though desire is strong; wealth must be protected even though poverty tempts the guardian. The admission of weakness is therefore not permission to surrender. It is an argument for honest safeguards and for mercy toward people who are struggling to obey.

The verse also corrects moral arrogance. A person who has avoided one temptation should not assume that another person’s struggle proves hypocrisy. The proper response is to support lawful choices, restrain exploitation, and remember one’s own dependence on divine help. Law is lighter when the community does not add humiliation to the demands Allah has made.

**Mercy knows capacity**

A lawful concession should be used neither with embarrassment nor with carelessness. The traveller who shortens prayer, the sick person who uses purification by earth, and the spouse who seeks mediation are not failures for needing the route Allah provided. The failure is to make obedience impossible through harsh customs that revelation did not require.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:29

> O believers! Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally, but rather trade by mutual consent. And do not kill ˹each other or˺ yourselves. Surely Allah is ever Merciful to you.

### Expanded Commentary

**Property requires mutual consent**

The verse prohibits consuming one another’s wealth through false means, while permitting trade conducted by mutual consent. The prohibition encompasses theft, fraud, bribery, deception in measurement, gambling-like transactions, usurpation, and any arrangement in which apparent agreement is produced by coercion or concealed information. Wealth is not purified merely because money changes hands; the manner of transfer matters.

The permission for trade is qualified by mutual consent. A contract may be signed and still be morally defective if a party was misled, threatened, or unable to understand the material terms. In contemporary markets, this principle reaches predatory lending, hidden fees, exploitative employment, manipulation of vulnerable consumers, and digital designs that trick a person into payment.

**Do not destroy yourselves**

The prohibition on killing yourselves has both a direct and a wider reading. It forbids suicide and self-destructive violence, and it also warns against a social order in which people consume one another’s wealth until life becomes unbearable. A person who bankrupts himself through reckless gambling or ruins another through fraud has participated in the destruction the verse condemns.

The command must be taught with compassion to those facing suicidal thoughts. It is not a weapon for shaming the suffering; it is a protection of life and a call to immediate support, safety, and mercy. Human judgment about the final state of someone who dies by suicide belongs to Allah, who knows distress and capacity better than people do.

**Mercy and the limits of ownership**

The verse ends by invoking Allah’s mercy. Property rights are real, but they do not make the owner morally independent. Trade serves life when it is honest; it becomes a form of mutual injury when one person’s gain depends on another’s ignorance or desperation. The believer asks not only “Can I obtain this?” but “Was it transferred justly, and will it preserve rather than destroy the people involved?”

**Consent needs truthful information**

A buyer cannot consent to what the seller deliberately conceals, and an employee cannot freely accept terms when the employer has made refusal impossible through deception. The verse’s economic ethic therefore demands transparency, fair bargaining power, and remedies for fraud. Profit is wholesome when the other party’s informed choice remains real.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:30

> And whoever does this sinfully and unjustly, We will burn them in the Fire. That is easy for Allah.

### Expanded Commentary

**A short legal warning with a long reach**

Allah warns that whoever commits the prohibited acts unjustly will be entered into the Fire. The word “that” gathers the preceding offences: taking wealth through falsehood, consuming another’s property, and participating in self-destruction. The warning does not make every financial mistake an eternal sentence; it emphasizes deliberate injustice and the seriousness of treating another person’s right as an opportunity.

**Why material wrongdoing is spiritual wrongdoing**

The verse prevents a modern misconception that business is morally neutral while worship is sacred. A fraudulent contract can be an act of disobedience just as real as neglecting prayer. Wealth shapes food, housing, education, health, and family security; corrupting its circulation harms bodies and futures. The Fire image makes visible the hidden human cost behind a transaction that may look profitable on a ledger.

**Mercy does not erase restitution**

Allah’s mercy appears in the surrounding chapter through repentance and repair. The person who has taken another’s wealth must stop, regret, return, and seek reconciliation. A private prayer for forgiveness cannot substitute for restoring a stolen right when restoration is possible. At the same time, victims are not required to deny their injury in order to appear forgiving; justice and mercy can coexist.

The final description of Allah as Merciful keeps the threat from becoming despair. It calls the wrongdoer to act before the consequence becomes final and calls the community to create markets in which consent is real, records are reliable, and the vulnerable can obtain redress.

**The social cost of private greed**

Financial injustice can appear to concern only two parties, but it spreads through families and institutions. Debt, fraud, and self-destruction consume trust and make ordinary cooperation expensive. The verse’s warning calls communities to create channels for advice, relief, and redress before a private wrong becomes a public collapse.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:31

> If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds and admit you into a place of honour.

### Expanded Commentary

**Major sins are not all the same in appearance**

The promise that Allah will remove major sins if lesser sins are avoided and the person enters the great sins calls attention to moral proportion. It does not trivialize lesser wrongdoing; persistent small sins can harden the heart and become major through contempt or repetition. Nor does it tell a person that one good deed mechanically cancels any deliberate crime. It teaches that the believer should recognize levels of danger and actively avoid the acts that destroy faith, life, property, and honour.

**The principle of serious avoidance**

The hadith of the seven destructive sins includes associating partners with Allah, magic, unjust killing, usury, consuming an orphan’s wealth, fleeing lawful battle, and accusing chaste believing women. The list is not exhaustive; the Qur’an and Sunnah identify many major sins. Its value is diagnostic: each item combines grave harm with a willingness to violate a protected boundary.

**A condition, not a bargain**

Avoiding major sins is joined to faith and righteous action. It is not a contract by which a person deliberately commits minor sins while counting on automatic pardon. Sincere vigilance includes seeking forgiveness, repairing harm, and refusing to normalize what one has been warned against. Allah’s promise is an encouragement to persevere, not a licence for calculation.

**“We will admit you to an honourable entrance”**

The closing promise gives the moral life a positive destination. The believer does not merely crawl between prohibitions; he or she is led into a dignified entry—into forgiveness, safety, and nearness to God. The verse therefore combines fear of destructive conduct with hope that disciplined obedience produces a better path. Its language is especially consoling for people who have left a major sin and are learning to live faithfully afterward.

**Major sins need active avoidance**

The believer should learn the signs of a major sin, avoid environments that normalize it, and seek help before temptation becomes action. The promise of forgiveness encourages disciplined hope, while the wording “if you avoid” requires a deliberate moral strategy. Reliance on mercy is not the same as relying on repeated exposure to danger.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:32

> And do not crave what Allah has given some of you over others. Men will be rewarded according to their deeds and women ˹equally˺ according to theirs. Rather, ask Allah for His bounties. Surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

### Expanded Commentary

**Do not turn gifts into resentments**

The verse forbids coveting what Allah has given some people over others. It does not prohibit ambition, planning, or asking Allah for increase. It prohibits the resentful desire that another person lose a blessing simply because one did not receive it. Such envy damages gratitude, relationships, and judgment: the envier sees the visible advantage and ignores the hidden obligations attached to it.

**Different shares, one moral worth**

The verse recognizes differences in income, physical capacity, opportunity, family role, and responsibility without making them measures of honour before God. In the inheritance laws, different shares are connected to different financial duties; in the wider social order, people have different tests. A person cannot infer from a greater portion that God has declared him more virtuous, nor from a smaller portion that she is less valued.

**Women and men have earned shares**

The verse explicitly affirms that women have a share of what they earn and men a share of what they earn. This protects women’s independent property and rejects the idea that a wife’s income automatically belongs to the husband or male relatives. It also warns men not to appropriate women’s earnings under the cover of household authority. Each person’s lawful work, gift, and contribution carries a corresponding right.

**Ask for bounty, not another’s removal**

The cure for envy is directed supplication and constructive effort. Ask Allah for bounty, seek lawful means, and be willing to receive a blessing in the form He chooses. This attitude does not prevent criticism of injustice or advocacy for fair opportunity. It distinguishes envy from a principled demand that people not be deprived by discrimination or corruption. The conclusion—Allah knows all things—reminds the reader that the visible distribution is not the whole account; wisdom includes realities no observer can calculate.

**Envy and structural fairness**

The prohibition of envy does not tell people to accept discrimination silently. A woman denied her lawful inheritance may seek justice without envying the person who took it. The verse distinguishes a principled claim to one’s right from resentment of another’s lawful blessing. Gratitude and reform can therefore exist together.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:33

> And We have appointed heirs to what has been left by parents and next of kin. As for those you have made a pledge to, give them their share. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things.

### Expanded Commentary

**Every person has a place in the inheritance network**

Allah appoints heirs to what parents and close relatives leave, and He also recognizes those with whom a person has made binding agreements. The verse acknowledges two kinds of relationship: natural kinship and deliberate covenant. Families are not the only bonds through which support and obligation arise, even though fixed inheritance shares are primarily governed by kinship and marriage.

**Allies and contractual obligations**

Early Muslim society included pacts of mutual support and emancipation arrangements. The verse was relevant to such relationships, while later inheritance rules and Prophetic explanations clarified how fixed heirs interact with contractual claims. The enduring lesson is that promises can carry material consequences. A person must not invite service, protection, or partnership and then pretend that the relationship has no economic responsibility when death arrives.

**Why the verse follows the shares**

The chapter has just specified portions for children, parents, spouses, and maternal siblings. This verse broadens the moral field without dissolving the detailed law. It tells heirs that the estate is part of a web of obligations, not simply a contest among the nearest relatives. Wills, debts, emancipatory agreements, and legitimate claims must be identified before anyone announces a personal entitlement.

The final statement that Allah witnesses everything returns the reader to accountability. A family may hide a promise, deny a servant’s claim, or use a technical argument to avoid an obligation. Divine witnessing sees both the written contract and the power imbalance under which it was made. The responsible heir gathers the documents, consults the law, and honours what was promised rather than asking how little can be paid.

**Promises survive the emotions of heirs**

Executors should collect evidence of contracts, emancipation arrangements, debts, and promises before relatives begin dividing the estate. A deceased person’s family may dislike a contractual beneficiary, but dislike cannot erase a valid obligation. Allah witnesses the promises that were made when no inheritance meeting was in view.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:34

> Men are the caretakers of women, as men have been provisioned by Allah over women and tasked with supporting them financially. And righteous women are devoutly obedient and, when alone, protective of what Allah has entrusted them with. And if you sense ill-conduct from your women, advise them ˹first˺, ˹if they persist,˺ do not share their beds, ˹but if they still persist,˺ then discipline them ˹gently˺. But if they change their ways, do not be unjust to them. Surely Allah is Most High, All-Great.

### Expanded Commentary

**Responsibility, not superiority**

The verse describes men as maintainers and protectors of women because Allah has given some people different capacities and because men spend from their wealth. The statement belongs to a household system in which the husband bears material maintenance. It establishes responsibility and accountability, not a licence for domination. A person cannot claim authority while refusing provision, safety, consultation, and good treatment.

The verse praises women who are devout and protect what should be protected in the husband’s absence. The phrase includes fidelity, property, privacy, and the trust of the marriage. It does not erase the wife’s independent ownership or make her a servant. Both spouses have duties; the verse identifies the husband’s financial and protective burden as the basis for a particular household responsibility.

**Nushūz and a graduated response**

The passage addresses serious marital discord, not ordinary irritation or a husband’s desire for obedience in every preference. The steps—admonition, separation in beds, and the final term often translated as striking—have been the subject of extensive legal and ethical discussion. They cannot be read apart from the Prophet’s prohibition of harming women, his statement that the best believers are best to their families, his own practice of never striking a wife, and the following verse’s command to seek arbitration.

Many contemporary scholars and Muslim legal authorities emphasize that any act causing injury, humiliation, or fear violates the Qur’anic requirement of honourable association; domestic violence is a crime and cannot be justified by this verse. The Arabic term has a range of uses, and the Prophetic model, the general ban on harm, and the purposes of marriage constrain any interpretation. A reading that turns the verse into permission for abuse contradicts the chapter’s protection of women.

**The conclusion is mercy and knowledge**

Allah is Most High and Great: the husband who has a limited household responsibility remains under the authority of God. He must not imitate divine sovereignty. If the wife reforms, the husband has no right to continue searching for punishment or leverage. The verse ends by closing the door to retaliation and reminding the powerful that the One above them knows every hidden act.

**Authority is measured by care**

Any reading of household responsibility that produces fear, injury, or humiliation has failed the verse’s own standard of honourable conduct. A husband’s responsibility should make him more accountable to provide and protect. It cannot make him less accountable to the wife, the law, or Allah, who is higher than every household head.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:35

> If you anticipate a split between them, appoint a mediator from his family and another from hers. If they desire reconciliation, Allah will restore harmony between them. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware.

### Expanded Commentary

**Arbitration protects a marriage from private escalation**

When a serious breach is feared between spouses, the Qur’an directs the community to appoint one arbiter from each family. This arrangement recognizes that marital conflict is rarely only a private emotion; it affects children, property, safety, and kinship. A trusted representative can listen to both sides, identify the actual dispute, and seek reconciliation without allowing either spouse to control the entire narrative.

**The arbiters need integrity and competence**

Family representatives should not be chosen merely because they will defend their relative regardless of facts. They need discretion, fairness, knowledge of the marriage’s rights, and the courage to recommend separation if reconciliation would perpetuate harm. Confidentiality is important, but secrecy must not be used to conceal violence or endanger a spouse. Contemporary mediation can include qualified counselors, legal advocates, and safeguarding professionals while preserving the verse’s aim.

**Reconciliation is not forced coexistence**

“If they both desire reconciliation” recognizes that repair requires more than an external settlement. The spouses must be willing to change patterns, fulfil rights, and rebuild trust. Where abuse or danger continues, compelling a victim to return is not reconciliation. The verse’s final assurance that Allah is Aware and Informed promises that hidden suffering is not lost simply because a family refuses to speak about it.

**A communal ethic of repair**

The verse moves the response to discord away from spectacle and revenge. Relatives are not invited to form factions; they are asked to become instruments of justice. The process can preserve a household when the conflict is curable, and it can provide an orderly path to separation when it is not. In both outcomes, the goal is not the appearance of family unity but the restoration of rights and the prevention of continuing injury.

**Mediation needs a safety exception**

Arbitration is valuable when both spouses can speak freely, but a victim of violence may need independent protection before a joint meeting. Qualified mediators must assess danger, avoid forced reconciliation, and ensure that settlement does not purchase quiet with the victim’s rights. The verse seeks reconciliation through justice, not peace through silence.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:36

> Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him. And be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, near and distant neighbours, close friends, ˹needy˺ travellers, and those ˹bondspeople˺ in your possession. Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful—

### Expanded Commentary

**Worship and social conduct are one command**

The verse lists worship of Allah, kindness to parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, neighbours near and far, companions, travellers, and those under one’s authority. The sequence refutes the idea that devotion can be measured only by private ritual. Prayer and zakat stand beside family care, neighbourly responsibility, hospitality, and humane treatment of dependants because the same God is worshipped in all of them.

**The circles of obligation**

Parents and relatives have claims strengthened by kinship, but the list widens beyond family. The “near” neighbour may be close in residence, relation, or faith; the “far” neighbour may lack all those forms of proximity. A Muslim cannot use good treatment of his own group as a reason for indifference to outsiders. The companion at one’s side includes the spouse, colleague, or travelling partner whose rights arise from shared presence.

The traveller is vulnerable because he is separated from familiar support. The person under one’s authority is vulnerable because the power relation can make refusal difficult. The verse therefore places proximity and power under a common rule: give people their due and do not turn dependence into an opportunity for contempt.

**The arrogance that destroys good deeds**

The verse condemns those who are arrogant and boastful. Boasting can accompany charity, office, ancestry, religious practice, or wealth. A person may perform the outward act while using it to announce superiority over the one helped. Arrogance corrupts the social meaning of the deed because it turns service into self-worship.

The list is practical theology. Before asking whether worship has produced an elevated spiritual feeling, ask whether parents are safe, relatives are supported, neighbours are not harmed, guests are treated well, and workers or dependants are respected. The command begins with Allah and returns to Him through the people placed near us.

**The neighbour is a test of worship**

A person may know the rules of ritual purity while making the neighbour’s home unsafe, or give charity while humiliating a worker. The verse refuses that compartmentalization. Worship becomes credible when it improves the relationships in which a person has daily power and proximity, including relationships with people outside his own religious community.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:37

> those who are stingy, promote stinginess among people, and withhold Allah’s bounties. We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment.

### Expanded Commentary

**Miserliness and concealment of bounty**

The verse condemns those who are miserly, urge others to be miserly, and hide what Allah has given them. Miserliness is more than failing to donate beyond one’s means. It includes withholding obligatory support, refusing a right because the recipient is weak, and cultivating a culture in which generosity is mocked. The person who hoards may also hide the source of his privilege so that he is never asked to use it for good.

**Bounty carries responsibility**

Wealth, knowledge, influence, health, and time can all be gifts that ought to be used responsibly. The verse’s wording is broad enough to expose the scholar who hides beneficial knowledge, the employer who conceals profits while pleading poverty, and the official who monopolizes access. Gratitude is shown by making a blessing useful, not merely by saying that it came from God.

**The “painful punishment”**

The punishment corresponds to the social injury. Hoarding does not remain private when the hoarder controls resources needed by others or teaches a family to fear every act of giving. A community shaped by such behaviour experiences distrust, class division, and the collapse of mutual aid. The warning is therefore both about the Hereafter and about the moral poverty produced here.

The verse does not condemn prudent saving or responsible planning. Islam recognizes ownership and the need to provide for dependants. It condemns the heart that treats every resource as a fortress against obligation and then recruits others into the same fear. The cure is to identify rights, fulfil them first, and practice voluntary generosity until wealth becomes a means rather than a master.

**Prudence is not miserliness**

Saving for dependants, paying debts, and avoiding waste are responsible uses of wealth. Miserliness begins when legitimate responsibility becomes an excuse to withhold a right or when fear of losing control makes generosity impossible. The verse asks whether a resource is being managed wisely or worshipped through hoarding.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:38

> Likewise for those who spend their wealth to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. And whoever takes Satan as an associate —what an evil associate they have!

### Expanded Commentary

**Spending for display is not generosity**

The verse joins miserliness to spending wealth for the sake of being seen. The contrast is deliberate: one person refuses to spend, another spends lavishly, but both may be oriented toward human approval rather than Allah. Public generosity can become a performance in which the recipient is a prop and the giver is the subject of admiration.

**Faith and the Last Day discipline motive**

The person who believes in Allah and the Last Day knows that reputation is unstable while divine knowledge is complete. The same act can be charity when it seeks God’s pleasure and self-advertisement when it seeks applause. This does not require every charitable project to be anonymous; public giving can encourage others and support accountability. The question is whether praise is the purpose and whether the recipient’s dignity is sacrificed for it.

**The companionship of evil**

The verse calls Satan a companion for those who spend to be seen. This is not a claim that every visible donation is satanic. It describes the spiritual mechanism by which an outward good is made empty: the ego is invited to take credit, comparison replaces mercy, and the giver becomes angry when recognition is withheld. An act that feeds arrogance can leave the poor no better respected even when money changes hands.

The remedy is intention, quiet service, and attention to outcomes. Give what is useful, protect the beneficiary’s privacy, and accept that Allah may conceal a deed from people while recording it fully. The unseen account is the one that remains.

**The recipient must not be made a billboard**

Public charity can be useful, but the beneficiary should not be displayed as proof of the donor’s virtue. Ask whether the publicity informs supporters, protects accountability, or merely collects praise. Sincerity is strengthened when the giver remembers that the person receiving help is a neighbour in dignity, not a prop in an advertisement.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:39

> What harm could have come to them if they had believed in Allah and the Last Day and donated from what Allah has provided for them? And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of them.

### Expanded Commentary

**A question that exposes spiritual irrationality**

The verse asks what harm would have come to these people if they had believed in Allah and the Last Day and spent from what He provided. The question is not seeking information; it reveals the absurdity of refusing faith and generosity while claiming to pursue benefit. Belief would not impoverish them, and spending a portion of provision would not destroy their security. What prevents them is attachment, suspicion, and the desire to retain moral independence.

**Provision is entrusted, not self-created**

The verse attributes wealth to Allah’s provision without denying human labour. Work, skill, inheritance, and trade are real means, but none creates existence, opportunity, or the capacity to benefit from wealth. This attribution breaks the illusion that a wealthy person owes nothing to anyone. If provision is received, then gratitude and responsible circulation are rational responses.

**Faith and spending meet the hidden future**

Belief in the Last Day changes the calculation of cost and reward. A person who sees only the present may call charity a loss; a person who sees accountability recognizes that what is spent justly is preserved in a more lasting account. This does not authorize careless generosity or neglect of dependants. It orders priorities: fulfil obligations, avoid extravagance, and do not make fear of scarcity an excuse for indifference.

The closing statement that Allah knows them gives the question its force. He knows whether refusal comes from genuine hardship or a heart that hides behind hardship. He also knows the quiet gift that people overlook. The believer need not prove generosity to an audience; he must answer the Provider.

**The rationality of generosity**

Spending from provision does not deny the need to budget. It denies the belief that security is created by closing every hand. A sound household fulfils obligations, keeps reasonable reserves, and still leaves room for generosity. The verse challenges the fear that treats every act of giving as an attack on survival.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:40

> Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom’s weight. And if it is a good deed, He will multiply it many times over and will give a great reward out of His grace.

### Expanded Commentary

**Absolute justice in the smallest amount**

Allah does not wrong even the weight of a particle. The image makes the principle concrete: no deed, however tiny, is omitted or undervalued. Human courts overlook evidence, memories fail, and social status bends judgment. Divine justice has none of those weaknesses. The smallest injury to a dependent and the smallest act of kindness are both present in the final account.

**Increase is grace, not a wage owed to God**

When Allah multiplies a good deed, the increase is described as coming from His bounty. This preserves two truths: the good act genuinely matters, and the reward exceeds what the person could claim by merit alone. The believer therefore acts carefully without becoming proud. One kind word, one protected inheritance, or one refusal to join a rumour may be enlarged beyond public measurement.

**A foundation for law and hope**

The verse comforts victims who think their loss is too small to matter and restrains wrongdoers who think a minor appropriation is invisible. It also protects the poor person’s contribution. Charity is not valued by its market price alone; a small gift given from limited means may carry an immense moral weight.

Justice and mercy meet here. Allah does not diminish the good and does not overlook the wrong, yet He may multiply good beyond exact equivalence. The person who fears that the scale is against him should repent and repair, while the person who thinks himself righteous should remember that every hidden motive is also weighed. The chapter’s family laws rest on this confidence: no right entrusted to human beings disappears from the knowledge of God.

**The smallest right still belongs to someone**

A tiny sum taken from a child, an uncredited hour of labour, or a kind act ignored by the public may seem insignificant. The verse makes such estimates unsafe. Justice counts the scale of the amount, but God also knows the vulnerability of the person affected and the motive of the person who acted.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:41

> So how will it be when We bring a witness from every faith-community and bring you ˹O Prophet˺ as a witness against yours?

### Expanded Commentary

**A witness from every community**

The verse moves the reader from earthly testimony to the scene of resurrection. Every community will have a witness, understood by the exegetes as its messenger or one who conveys the truth, and Muhammad will be brought as a witness concerning his community. The arrangement establishes that revelation was not an abstract event without public responsibility. A message was delivered, heard, embodied, and answered; the final court gathers the evidence from those entrusted with conveying it.

**The Prophet’s witness**

The Prophet’s testimony is not a licence for believers to assume themselves safe because they carry his name. It intensifies responsibility. He witnessed whether a community received the guidance, lived by it, and transmitted it faithfully. The Qur’an elsewhere describes him as a witness, bearer of good news, and warner. His witness includes the clarity of his delivery and the reality of the response.

**A court beyond partial evidence**

Earthly courts depend on incomplete witnesses, documents, and memory. The divine court knows what was accessible to a person, what obstacles distorted the message, and what the heart did with the evidence. This does not cancel public accountability; it shows that final judgment will be more exact than any human process. The verse should make a believer careful about claiming certainty regarding another person’s ultimate fate.

It also gives dignity to testimony in this world. To speak truthfully about one’s community, family, and leaders is to imitate the seriousness of the coming court. A witness must not flatter his group or hide its wrongdoing, because the Messenger who taught the community will be its witness before the One who hears every account.

**Testimony and accountability**

The coming witness should make both speaker and listener careful with religious claims. A community that asks others to answer for truth must also answer for how it treated the truth. Testimony is not a spectacle reserved for the end of time; it begins whenever a person explains a command, reports an event, or speaks about another community.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:42

> On that Day, those who denied ˹Allah˺ and disobeyed the Messenger will wish they were reduced to dust. And they will never be able to hide anything from Allah.

### Expanded Commentary

**The wish to become dust**

Those who denied Allah and disobeyed the Messenger will wish to be reduced to dust. The wish expresses total escape from moral personhood: if they were dust, they would not have to answer, remember, or endure the consequences of chosen denial. But the wish cannot be fulfilled. The human being cannot hide from the identity in which deeds were performed.

The phrase “they will not be able to hide anything” closes the possibility of courtroom evasion. In this world a person can conceal a motive, recruit a witness, delete a record, or persuade himself that a wrong was harmless. In the Hereafter the concealed and the public are one account. The tongue may be silent or may attempt a defence, but no hidden fact is absent from Allah.

**Why the verse follows testimony**

The preceding verse established witnesses. This one shows why witness matters: judgment will not rely on a vague impression of humanity but on a complete record of message and response. The denial that seemed clever in life becomes exposed as an attempt to outrun truth. The wish for dust is the final recognition that excuses have no power when knowledge is perfect.

The verse is a warning but also a source of confidence for the oppressed. A victim whose testimony was dismissed, an orphan whose account was denied, or a believer mocked for speaking truth is not dependent on the completeness of human memory. Allah’s court does not lose evidence. The proper response is neither despair nor revenge, but truthful action while the account remains open.

**No hidden record is lost**

The wish to become dust also expresses the desire to escape responsibility by ceasing to be a person. The verse denies that escape. Its practical comfort is that forgotten injuries and concealed acts remain known to God, while its warning is that a person should not postpone repair in the hope that time will erase the account.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:43

> O believers! Do not approach prayer while intoxicated until you are aware of what you say, nor in a state of ˹full˺ impurity—unless you merely pass through ˹the mosque˺—until you have bathed. But if you are ill, on a journey, or have relieved yourselves, or been intimate with your wives and cannot find water, then purify yourselves with clean earth, wiping your faces and hands. And Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving.

### Expanded Commentary

**Prayer requires conscious presence**

The prohibition on approaching prayer while intoxicated until one knows what one is saying protects prayer as meaningful speech, not bodily performance. The verse belongs to the gradual Qur’anic prohibition of intoxicants. It names the immediate harm—loss of awareness in worship—while later revelation closes the practice more broadly. The principle extends to any condition that makes a person unable to understand, intend, or carry out the prayer responsibly.

**Purification and access to worship**

The verse also addresses major ritual impurity and the need for bathing, with an exception for someone merely passing through a mosque. Ritual purity is not a statement that the human body is morally filthy. It is a form of preparation that distinguishes sacred worship and teaches attentiveness. The law takes physical states seriously without confusing them with the worth of the person.

**Tayammum: concession without abandonment**

Illness, travel, the absence of water, and certain bodily circumstances open the route of *tayammum*: clean earth is used for the face and hands. The concession shows that prayer is not cancelled by hardship. Nor is it an invitation to avoid water when water is available and safe. Jurists discuss the details of what counts as illness, travel, impurity, and clean earth, but the governing principle is clear: preserve worship while removing impossible or harmful demands.

**Pardon and forgiveness**

The verse closes with Allah’s pardon and forgiveness. The ritual rule is firm, yet the Lord of the rule knows weakness, sickness, forgetfulness, and circumstance. Believers should therefore avoid both negligence and obsessive difficulty. Prepare for prayer, use the normal means when able, and accept the divinely granted concession without shame when genuinely needed. Worship is disciplined, but it is not designed to crush the worshipper.

**The ease within purification**

Ritual purity trains the body to approach worship attentively, but the concessions prevent ritual from becoming an obstacle to worship. Communities should teach the rule with clarity and compassion, especially to the ill, travellers, and people with recurring bodily difficulties. Excessive doubt can itself become a burden that the verse did not intend.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:44

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures yet trade it for misguidance and wish to see you deviate from the ˹Right˺ Path?

### Expanded Commentary

**Knowledge can be traded for misguidance**

The verse rebukes people given a portion of Scripture who exchange guidance for error and wish the believers to deviate. The phrase “a portion” is sobering: possession of scripture does not guarantee obedience to its whole message. A person may quote revelation, hold religious office, or inherit a learned tradition while selling its guidance for status, fear, or political advantage.

**The danger of wishing others to fall**

The verse identifies not only private error but an active desire to see the Prophet and his community leave the right path. This can occur when religious authority becomes invested in monopoly. If another community recognizes truth, the recognition threatens influence, so the authority reshapes evidence rather than revising itself. The Qur’an warns that the desire to control guidance can be more dangerous than simple ignorance.

**A lesson for believers**

The warning is not confined to the People of the Book. Muslims who inherit scripture can likewise trade it for faction, nationalism, money, or approval. A commentator who hides an inconvenient command, a leader who weaponizes a verse, and a student who seeks arguments only to defeat opponents repeat the same pattern. Knowledge is a trust whose value is measured by submission to truth.

The verse asks the reader to distinguish criticism of a distortion from hatred of a people. Its target is conduct and motive, not ethnicity. A believer can defend revelation firmly while acknowledging sincere seekers and learning from those who possess knowledge. The question is always whether scripture is being followed or made into currency.

**Religious knowledge and moral courage**

The person who knows scripture but sells its direction for influence is more dangerous than the uninformed person, because others trust the credential. Students should therefore learn to ask what a teaching requires of the teacher. Knowledge that never corrects power or desire may be information, but it has not yet become guidance.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:45

> Allah knows best who your enemies are! And Allah is sufficient as a Guardian, and He is sufficient as a Helper.

### Expanded Commentary

**Allah knows the real adversary**

The verse tells the Prophet that Allah knows best who the enemies are. Human beings can identify open hostility, but they often miss concealed motives, changing alliances, and the enemy within the self. A person may speak like an ally while undermining a community; another may disagree publicly without wishing harm. Divine knowledge is more exact than social suspicion.

**Guardian and helper**

Allah’s sufficiency as Guardian means the Prophet and believers are not dependent on winning every argument or controlling every opponent. A guardian protects, manages, and preserves; a helper supports a person when his own means are insufficient. The two descriptions answer two fears: “Who will keep us safe?” and “Who will give us strength?”

This does not authorize passivity. The chapter commands justice, consultation, preparation, and truthful speech. Trust in God releases the believer from paranoia and despair, not from responsibility. One may take precautions without making fear the master of judgment.

**A rule for conflict**

When communities face religious hostility, they should identify specific harmful acts rather than demonize entire populations. Allah alone knows hearts, while humans judge observable conduct through evidence. The verse provides confidence for the persecuted and restraint for the powerful: no enemy is hidden from God, and no accusation is made true merely by repeating it.

**Security without paranoia**

The believer may identify threats, protect a community, and still refuse to imagine that every disagreement is enmity. Allah’s knowledge is a reason to use evidence rather than suspicion. Human beings see only behaviour and circumstance; they should judge those carefully while leaving hidden motives to the divine Guardian.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:46

> Some Jews take words out of context and say, “We listen and we disobey,” “Hear! May you never hear,” and “Râ’ina!” [Herd us!]—playing with words and discrediting the faith. Had they said ˹courteously˺, “We hear and obey,” “Listen to us,” and “Unẓurna,” [Tend to us!] it would have been better for them and more proper. Allah has condemned them for their disbelief, so they do not believe except for a few.

### Expanded Commentary

**Words can be used as weapons**

The verse describes people who distort words, use ambiguous expressions as insults, and speak in ways designed to discredit the faith. The examples concern phrases that could sound like obedience while carrying mockery or malediction. Language is not morally neutral when a speaker deliberately chooses a form that humiliates, confuses, or undermines another person while preserving deniability.

**Respectful speech and honest disagreement**

The Qur’an contrasts the manipulative expressions with plain speech: “we hear and obey,” “listen to us,” and “attend to us.” The correction is not a ban on questions or criticism. It is a demand that disagreement be expressed without hidden contempt. Believers too can fail this standard when they use technical religious terms to insult opponents or hide a refusal to engage honestly.

**A warning against generalization**

The verse refers to some among the Jews, not to every Jew. It is part of a sustained Qur’anic method that distinguishes people by conduct: some distort, some believe, some act justly. The passage should therefore never be recruited to justify ethnic hatred or collective punishment. Its target is the abuse of language and rejection of truth.

The final description of disbelief shows that deliberate verbal sabotage is spiritually serious when it expresses settled opposition. Yet the gate of belief is not closed to anyone who abandons that conduct. The believer should guard the tongue, ask whether a phrase is truthful and fair, and prefer clarity to clever cruelty.

**Language and dignity**

A community’s verbal habits reveal whether it values truth. Sarcasm that humiliates, ambiguous insults, and religious mockery can damage trust even when a speaker later claims he meant no harm. The verse recommends plain courtesy because honest language makes repentance, correction, and coexistence possible.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:47

> O you who were given the Book! Believe in what We have revealed— confirming your own Scriptures—before We wipe out ˹your˺ faces, turning them backwards, or We condemn the defiant as We did to the Sabbathbreakers. And Allah’s command is always executed!

### Expanded Commentary

**The People of the Book are invited through continuity**

The call to believe in what has been revealed to Muhammad is supported by the fact that it confirms the Scriptures they already possess. The Qur’an does not present itself as a religion with no relationship to earlier revelation. It calls people back to the God of Abraham, Moses, and the prophets, while correcting distortions and rejecting claims that contradict divine unity.

**The severe imagery of reversal**

The warning that faces could be erased and turned backwards, or that people could be condemned as the Sabbath-breakers were, communicates the danger of refusing clear truth after recognition. The image can be read as a physical judgment and as a moral reversal: the person who turns away from guidance risks being returned to the condition of those who denied it. It is not a licence for believers to threaten minorities; it is a divine warning addressed through the Prophet.

**A command that will be executed**

The final statement underscores divine sovereignty. Human beings can delay, reinterpret, or mock a command, but they cannot cancel it. The invitation therefore carries urgency. Recognition of the continuity between Qur’an and previous revelation should lead to faith rather than to a defensive struggle for institutional ownership.

The verse also teaches Muslims to honour the scriptures’ original source while being careful about their present textual and interpretive forms. Confirmation is neither uncritical approval of every claim nor denial of the prophetic heritage. It is a call to return all communities to worship of the One God and to obedience to His final guidance.

**Recognition before resistance**

The invitation to the People of the Book is not based on erasing their tradition but on asking them to recognize continuity. Muslims can learn to speak of earlier revelation with reverence while explaining the Qur’an’s claims clearly. A defensive community may demand ever more signs; a truthful community responds to the evidence already before it.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:48

> Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating others with Him ˹in worship˺, but forgives anything else of whoever He wills. And whoever associates others with Allah has indeed committed a grave sin.

### Expanded Commentary

**The gravity of associating partners with Allah**

The verse places associating others with Allah in a unique category: Allah does not forgive it for one who meets Him persisting in it, while other sins remain subject to His will. This is not a restriction on divine power. It identifies the theological rupture of treating a created being as a partner in worship, lordship, or ultimate authority. If a person repents from shirk before death, the Qur’an’s universal call to repentance remains open; the warning concerns dying without abandoning it.

**Why the distinction matters**

Every other sin, however serious, can be confessed as a violation of God’s command. Shirk changes the relationship to the Commander by placing another at the centre of devotion. It therefore attacks the foundation from which forgiveness is sought. The verse does not imply that non-shirk sins are harmless. It says that forgiveness is possible for them according to God’s wisdom, while shirk without repentance is a final refusal of the source of forgiveness.

**Avoiding careless judgments**

The warning should make believers serious about pure worship, not eager to label individuals. Questions of ignorance, compulsion, misunderstanding, and final state require knowledge and humility. Allah knows what reached a person, what he intended, and how he died. The verse is first a mirror: examine the subtle partners of reputation, wealth, tribe, and desire that may compete for the obedience owed to God.

The chapter’s social commands arise from this theological centre. If God alone is ultimate, no family, ruler, scholar, or market may receive unquestioned sovereignty. Tawḥīd protects both worship and human rights.

**Tawḥīd and human freedom**

If no created power shares God’s ultimacy, no human being can legitimately demand absolute obedience from another. The doctrine that protects worship also limits rulers, family elders, scholars, and markets. Every created authority is judged by the One authority it cannot replace.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:49

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who ˹falsely˺ elevate themselves? It is Allah who elevates whoever He wills. And none will be wronged ˹even by the width of˺ the thread of a date stone.

### Expanded Commentary

**Self-praise is not purification**

The verse challenges those who declare themselves pure or superior. Religious identity, lineage, learning, and public reputation can all become tools of self-certification. But elevation belongs to Allah, and He raises whom He wills according to truth that may not be visible to the crowd. A person who announces his own righteousness has already made the announcement part of the test.

**The date-stone thread**

The image of the thin thread on a date stone means that divine justice omits nothing. It also restrains the self-exaltation described in the first sentence. No person can safely claim that God owes him honour, and no person should assume that another’s apparently small act is beneath divine notice. Human beings see surfaces; Allah knows sincerity, repentance, and hidden endurance.

**Humility is not self-hatred**

To reject self-praise does not require denying a real skill or refusing a legitimate qualification. A judge, teacher, or worker may describe competence when responsibility requires it. The difference is between accurate testimony offered for a useful purpose and a heart that treats its own virtue as an entitlement. Humility allows gratitude for a gift while returning the gift’s source to God.

The verse is especially relevant to religious communities, where titles and visible practices can create hierarchies of holiness. The believer should seek purification rather than advertise it, correct wrongdoing without claiming infallibility, and leave the final elevation to the One who knows every deed.

**Purity is received, not advertised**

People who need to present themselves as pure often become unable to admit a mistake. The Qur’anic alternative is hopeful humility: seek cleansing through repentance and good action, but let God decide what has been accepted. This protects the community from both self-righteous leaders and despairing sinners.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:50

> See how they fabricate lies against Allah—this alone is a blatant sin.

### Expanded Commentary

**Fabricating a lie against Allah**

The verse identifies self-purification claims as a form of fabrication against Allah. To say “God has declared me pure,” or to assign divine approval to a faction without evidence, is not merely boastful speech. It puts words in the mouth of the Lawgiver. Religious falsehood is especially dangerous because it recruits the hearer’s trust in God to protect the speaker’s ego.

**The difference between hope and certification**

A believer may hope for Allah’s mercy, speak of a good deed, and encourage others toward righteousness. What is prohibited is certainty about one’s own sanctity or about another person’s guaranteed standing without revelation. The early Muslims feared hypocrisy in themselves even while striving for faith; that fear was not despair but protection against complacency.

**Public consequences**

Fabricated holiness can justify abuse. A leader who declares his group pure may exclude critics, seize property, or excuse violence. A family that claims divine preference for its lineage may deny a woman her inheritance. The verse places such claims in the category of blatant sin because they corrupt both theology and social order.

The correction is to describe deeds honestly, cite revelation carefully, and leave hidden judgments to Allah. A community should honour knowledge and virtue without turning admiration into infallibility. Divine approval is sought through obedience, not manufactured through slogans.

**The responsibility of religious speech**

A fabricated attribution to God can change a family’s rights, a court’s judgment, or a community’s treatment of an outsider. Religious teachers should mark uncertainty honestly and distinguish scripture from commentary. Caution is not weakness; it is reverence for the One whose words are being represented.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:51

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures yet believe in idols and false gods and reassure the disbelievers that they are better guided than the believers?

### Expanded Commentary

**When scripture is joined to false worship**

The verse expresses astonishment at people given a portion of Scripture who nevertheless believe in idols and false authorities, then tell disbelievers that their path is better guided than that of the believers. The contradiction is not that a person knows something and still sins—human beings are weak—but that religious knowledge is used to validate a rejection of the truth it should have prepared them to recognize.

**The historical dispute and its wider application**

Exegetes connect the passage with figures who supported the pagan opponents of the Prophet, perhaps because of rivalry with the new Muslim community. The historical setting matters, but the moral pattern is broader. A religious authority can prefer political alliance, ethnic loyalty, or institutional advantage to fidelity to revelation. When that happens, scripture becomes a credential rather than a guide.

**False gods include false standards**

Idols are not only carved images. Anything treated as an ultimate source of allegiance and moral permission can function as a false god: status, nation, party, wealth, or the approval of a powerful patron. A person who invokes scripture while letting such a standard determine truth repeats the contradiction in the verse.

The passage is a warning to Muslims as much as to those it first addressed. Do not assume that possessing a Book protects against betrayal of its guidance. Test claims by tawḥīd, justice, and obedience, and do not call a path well guided merely because its advocates possess religious language.

**The allure of political religion**

Religious language can be attached to a powerful ally because the alliance promises security. The verse asks whether that security has made truth negotiable. The test is not which group offers the greatest advantage but whether the relationship permits justice, worship, and truthful speech to survive.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:52

> It is they who have been condemned by Allah. And whoever is condemned by Allah will have no helper.

### Expanded Commentary

**Condemnation follows deliberate alliance with falsehood**

The verse declares that those people are condemned by Allah and that the one whom Allah condemns has no helper. The condemnation follows the conduct described: using revealed knowledge to support false worship and to mislead others. It is not a blanket sentence on an ethnicity or a religious population; the Qur’an repeatedly distinguishes among people of earlier scripture and praises those who believe and act uprightly.

**The loss of every substitute**

A person may seek protection through status, a patron, a majority, or a religious institution. Yet when the final judgment exposes the false alliance, none can serve as a helper. The verse echoes a central Qur’anic theme: created powers may provide temporary worldly support, but they cannot rescue a person from accountability.

This is also a warning to communities that make compromise with injustice their security strategy. A group may gain influence by flattering a tyrant or endorsing his falsehood, but the alliance does not make falsehood true. It only transfers the moral cost to those who helped circulate it.

The appropriate response is not to despair of anyone’s guidance but to refuse the alliance ourselves. Ask Allah for protection from the kind of knowledge that becomes a weapon against truth, and measure support by justice rather than by the apparent strength of the supporter.

**No patron replaces God**

The person who chooses a corrupt patron may appear protected for a season. The verse looks beyond that season to the absence of any helper who can answer for the patronage. A believer can accept legitimate worldly help without treating the helper as an ultimate protector or obeying him in injustice.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:53

> Do they have control over shares of the kingdom? If so, they would not have given anyone so much as the speck on a date stone.

### Expanded Commentary

**The kingdom is not theirs to distribute**

The verse asks whether the opponents possess a share of the kingdom. If they did, their miserliness would be so extreme that they would not give people even the smallest speck. The question exposes the absurdity of their envy and authority claims. They resent the favour Allah has granted the Prophet and the believers, yet they themselves have no ownership of the divine treasury.

**The date-stone speck**

The image is intentionally tiny. Even a person who refuses generous sharing cannot pretend that he has the right to withhold the smallest particle of God’s bounty from those whom He chooses to favour. The example also exposes the psychology of envy: it is not always a desire to receive; sometimes it is a desire to ensure that another receives nothing.

**Authority and generosity**

Leadership is tested by how it distributes opportunities, knowledge, wealth, and recognition. A ruler or scholar who treats every benefit as private property imitates the imagined kingdom-holders in the verse. True stewardship recognizes that authority is delegated and that giving does not diminish the Giver.

The passage also consoles the Prophet and believers who were mocked for their apparent lack of power. The opponents’ social influence could not cancel divine choice. The believer should seek lawful means but not measure truth by the generosity or hostility of those who temporarily control resources.

**The envy that refuses even a speck**

Some people would rather block another person’s opportunity than share a small benefit. Institutions should therefore examine not only how much they give but which groups they systematically prevent from receiving. The verse condemns a culture of scarcity created by jealousy, not reasonable stewardship of limited resources.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:54

> Or do they envy the people for Allah’s bounties? Indeed, We have given the descendants of Abraham the Book and wisdom, along with great authority.

### Expanded Commentary

**Envy of a divine bounty**

The verse asks whether the opponents envy people for what Allah has given them. Envy here is connected to the previous passage: they do not merely disagree; they resent the distribution of guidance and authority. The descendants of Abraham had already received Scripture, wisdom, and a great kingdom, yet divine bounty was not exhausted by that history. God may grant revelation and honour to another branch of Abraham’s family without injustice to the first.

**Bounty cannot be monopolized by ancestry**

The reference to Abraham’s descendants confronts claims that prophethood or divine favour belongs permanently to one lineage. Scripture is a gift, not a hereditary estate whose borders humans can enforce against God. Likewise, Muslims must not assume that ancestry, ethnicity, or historical achievement guarantees continued guidance.

**Envy and gratitude**

A grateful person can recognize another’s blessing without feeling diminished by it. Envy interprets another’s success as one’s own loss and then searches for arguments to make the blessing illegitimate. Religious envy is especially corrosive because it turns the language of doctrine into a mask for wounded status.

The verse does not deny that communities may criticize injustice or ask for fair distribution. It asks them to distinguish principled concern from resentment of a blessing simply because it was given to someone else. Allah’s generosity is wider than human permission, and His choice is not subject to the jealous gatekeeping of creation.

**Bounty is not an ethnic possession**

The history of Abraham’s descendants shows that divine favour can appear in more than one branch and more than one age. No community can convert a past blessing into a permanent monopoly. Gratitude for inherited revelation should lead to recognition when God sends guidance through another people, not to resentment.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:55

> Yet some believed in him while others turned away from him. Hell is sufficient as a torment!

### Expanded Commentary

**Response divides people, not revelation alone**

Some believed in the one sent with truth and others turned away. The verse presents both reactions within the same historical community, reminding the reader that an inherited religious identity does not determine every individual’s response. People must answer for what they do with evidence, not receive a collective verdict based solely on belonging.

**Hell as a sufficient consequence**

The statement that Hell is sufficient as a punishment is brief and severe. It answers the arrogance of those who thought social standing or scriptural affiliation would shield them from accountability. Knowledge without submission cannot rescue, and opposition to truth has consequences even when the opponent possesses eloquence and influence.

The verse should be read alongside the Qur’an’s recognition of righteous People of the Book elsewhere. It cannot be used to erase those passages or to declare every Jew or Christian condemned by identity. Its subject is the people who responded to the specific truth before them with rejection and hostility.

For believers, the warning is personal. One may stand near a sacred tradition, recite scripture, and still turn away from a command because it threatens one’s status. The decisive question is not what name is inherited but whether the heart responds to God with faith and obedience.

**Identity does not complete faith**

A person may inherit a scripture, a name, or an institution and still turn away from the truth. Conversely, someone from a community criticized in one passage may respond faithfully. The verse encourages individual moral judgment and warns religious communities against assuming that history itself guarantees their future.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:56

> Surely those who reject Our signs, We will cast them into the Fire. Whenever their skin is burnt completely, We will replace it so they will ˹constantly˺ taste the punishment. Indeed, Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**The Fire and the continuity of sensation**

The verse depicts the punishment of those who reject Allah’s signs: the skin is replaced whenever it is burned so that the punishment continues to be tasted. The image is bodily because rejection is not merely an abstract opinion; it is enacted through a person’s faculties, choices, and treatment of others. The Qur’an makes the consequence perceptible to the imagination without asking the reader to reduce the unseen to a laboratory model.

**Divine power and wisdom together**

The conclusion names Allah as Almighty and Wise. His power makes resurrection and punishment possible; His wisdom ensures that judgment is not arbitrary. The verse is not saying that pain is inflicted without moral meaning. It addresses persistent rejection after signs, placing consequence within a just account of response.

**A warning against desensitization**

The repeated replacement of skin also warns against the false security that wrongdoing becomes harmless through repetition. In worldly life the conscience can become numb; a person can hear a warning so often that he feels nothing. The Hereafter reverses that numbness. The reality hidden by habit becomes continuously evident.

The proper use of the verse is moral awakening, not fascination with torment. It should make a believer take signs seriously, repent from denial and oppression, and refuse to use divine punishment as a tool for threatening people personally. Judgment belongs to Allah; responsibility belongs to us.

**The unseen consequence of denial**

The description of punishment is meant to make the hidden seriousness of rejection perceptible. It should lead to repentance and moral attention rather than to voyeurism about pain. Believers who recite the verse should ask what signs they themselves ignore in daily conduct and relationships.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:57

> As for those who believe and do good, We will admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. There they will have pure spouses, and We will place them under a vast shade.

### Expanded Commentary

**Gardens as the opposite of denial**

The verse sets the fate of believers who do good against the punishment just described. Faith is paired with action because a claim without conduct does not express submission. The promised gardens, flowing water, lasting residence, pure spouses, and expansive shade translate security into images of abundance and rest for people who lived under hardship, conflict, and exposure.

**Purity without the world’s injuries**

The spouses of Paradise are described as pure. The purity includes freedom from betrayal, illness, resentment, and the social harms that can corrupt earthly relationships. The Qur’anic promise does not reduce the reward to physical pleasure; it presents companionship within a purified environment where no one’s dignity is threatened.

**Shade after exposure**

The “vast shade” answers the fatigue and heat of earthly struggle. It is also a symbol of divine protection. The believer who protected orphans, spouses, neighbours, and travellers under the sun of worldly responsibility is promised shelter under a greater mercy. The images are concrete because the body matters, but their moral atmosphere is deeper than comfort.

The verse’s condition remains “believe and do good.” Good deeds do not purchase God’s grace as though Paradise were a commercial wage, yet they are the evidence of living faith. The final reward is a gift that fulfills, rather than cancels, the believer’s effort.

**Reward as protected life**

The garden imagery gathers the needs exposed throughout the chapter: safety after danger, shade after exposure, companionship without betrayal, and abundance without exploitation. The reward does not detach the body from faith; it promises that bodily and relational goods will finally be free from the injustices that corrupt them here.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:58

> Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners; and when you judge between people, judge with fairness. What a noble commandment from Allah to you! Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.

### Expanded Commentary

**Trusts include every responsibility**

Allah commands the return of trusts to their rightful owners. The command covers property, confidential information, public office, professional competence, promises, and the rights of people placed under one’s care. A trust is defined by another person’s legitimate claim, not by whether the custodian finds it convenient to return it. The orphan’s inheritance in the opening of the sūrah is one example; government and judicial authority are others.

**Judging with fairness**

The second command addresses those who decide between people. Fair judgment requires hearing both sides, examining evidence, avoiding conflicts of interest, and resisting wealth, kinship, fear, and affection. It applies to judges, leaders, employers, parents, mediators, and anyone whose decision affects another’s rights. A person need not hold a courtroom title to exercise judgment.

**The Prophetic context of the key**

Classical reports connect the verse with the Prophet’s return of the key of the Kaʿbah to its custodians after the conquest of Makkah, even though political victory might have made confiscation appear possible. Whether one focuses on that report or on the verse’s general wording, the lesson is clear: triumph does not cancel a trust. The enemy’s former conduct does not give the victor permission to take what belongs to others.

Allah hears the claims and sees the decisions. This protects the person whose trust is returned quietly and the litigant whose case is judged without public recognition. The verse is a charter for ethical administration: competence receives responsibility, responsibility returns what it holds, and authority is measured by justice rather than possession.

**Office is a trust**

A public appointment, a professional license, or a leadership role belongs to the people served before it belongs to the office-holder. Returning the trust means using competence for its proper purpose and handing authority over when the term ends. Fair judgment then becomes the test by which stewardship is known.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:59

> O believers! Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. Should you disagree on anything, then refer it to Allah and His Messenger, if you ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day. This is the best and fairest resolution.

### Expanded Commentary

**A hierarchy ordered by revelation**

Believers are commanded to obey Allah, the Messenger, and those in authority among them. The repeated command to obey Allah and the Messenger, but not repeated in exactly the same way for rulers, indicates that obedience to human authority is conditional. It is due in lawful matters and within the limits of justice; no creature may be obeyed in disobedience to the Creator.

“Those in authority” has been interpreted as rulers, commanders, judges, and people of knowledge. The varieties are not mutually exclusive. A community needs both political administration and informed guidance, but each authority remains accountable to revelation. Leadership is therefore a trust under the command of verse 58, not a private entitlement.

**Returning disputes to the sources**

When believers disagree, they are told to refer the matter to Allah and the Messenger—meaning the Qur’an and the established Prophetic guidance. This does not eliminate scholarship or require every person to interpret complex evidence alone. It establishes the highest reference that scholarship must serve. A dispute is not resolved by the loudest faction, ancestral custom, or the ruler’s preference.

The final description—best and fairest—links truth to social reconciliation. Returning to revelation should not be a slogan for humiliating opponents; it should be a shared method that makes correction possible. The verse makes faith visible in procedure: obey responsibly, consult authority, and when authority conflicts, let revelation judge the authority.

**Obedience with moral limits**

The verse supports orderly authority while preventing authoritarianism. A ruler, commander, or scholar deserves obedience in lawful matters, but the community must return disputes to revelation when a human command conflicts with justice. Consultation and principled disagreement are forms of loyalty to the higher order, not automatic rebellion.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:60

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who claim they believe in what has been revealed to you and what was revealed before you? They seek the judgment of false judges, which they were commanded to reject. And Satan ˹only˺ desires to lead them farther away.

### Expanded Commentary

**A claim of faith tested by arbitration**

The verse exposes people who claim belief in revelation but seek judgment from false authorities. Their problem is not a lack of religious vocabulary; it is that they want a decision that will protect their interest. When revelation and conscience may limit them, they look for a judge, custom, or power willing to authorize the desired result.

**False judges in every age**

The term *ṭāghūt* includes idols and satanic powers but can also describe a human or institution that is given authority to rival God’s guidance. Courts themselves are not false merely because they are human; justice requires human judges. The danger arises when a decision-maker is treated as entitled to legalize oppression, or when a believer deliberately bypasses a fair process for a corrupt one.

**Satan’s gradual redirection**

The verse says Satan wants to lead them far astray. The movement usually begins with a small exception: “this case is special,” “everyone does it,” or “the result matters more than the rule.” Once the principle is surrendered, the person can be moved farther without noticing. Returning disputes to Allah and His Messenger interrupts that gradual slide.

The verse also calls for intellectual honesty. If a person seeks advice, he should be willing to accept an inconvenient answer; if he goes to court, he should not manipulate the process through bribery or influence. Faith is tested less by the ruling one quotes when it serves one’s interest than by the ruling one accepts when it restrains it.

**Choosing the judge who flatters us**

A person may seek a corrupt authority not because he doubts the truth but because he knows the truth will restrain him. The cure is to choose counsel before desire hardens, disclose one’s interest, and accept an answer that could be inconvenient. The integrity of a dispute is tested by the judge one is willing to hear.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:61

> When it is said to them, “Come to Allah’s revelations and to the Messenger,” you see the hypocrites turn away from you stubbornly.

### Expanded Commentary

**The revealed standard exposes hypocrisy**

When the hypocrites are invited to the Qur’an and the Messenger, they turn away stubbornly. Their problem is not simply intellectual disagreement; they refuse the authority that would judge their motives and restrain their interests. A person may attend religious gatherings, repeat the vocabulary of belief, and still reveal his allegiance when obedience becomes inconvenient.

**Turning away as a deliberate act**

The verse depicts movement: a summons comes, and the hypocrite withdraws. This does not condemn every person who needs time to understand a difficult ruling. It addresses the stubborn refusal to let revelation adjudicate. The believer can ask questions and seek clarification while remaining open to correction; hypocrisy closes the door before the answer is heard.

The warning is communal. A society cannot preserve justice if its members invoke scripture for identity but reject it whenever property, power, or reputation is at stake. The remedy is not to spy on hearts, which belong to Allah, but to judge visible conduct fairly and to keep inviting people toward honest submission.

**Honest disagreement is better than false assent**

A community can survive a sincere question or a principled objection more easily than a promise designed to conceal sabotage. Leaders should make room for consultation so that obedience is not confused with silence. Members should speak before a decision, then honour the decision once they have accepted the process.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:62

> How ˹horrible˺ will it be if a disaster strikes them because of what their hands have done, then they come to you swearing by Allah, “We intended nothing but goodwill and reconciliation.”

### Expanded Commentary

**The excuse after consequences arrive**

The verse imagines the hypocrites after a disaster caused by their own choices. They return swearing that they intended only goodwill and reconciliation. The timing exposes the weakness of the claim: when the cost was distant, they resisted; when the consequence was visible, they offered a pious explanation.

**Good intentions cannot sanctify betrayal**

Islam values intention, but an intention cannot be proven by a slogan after harm has occurred. A person who undermines a just process and later says he meant peace must examine whether “peace” was being used to protect status or avoid accountability. Reconciliation requires truth about what happened, repair, and changed conduct.

**A warning for crisis leadership**

Communities often hear claims of good will from those who opposed necessary action until success became likely. The verse teaches leaders to assess patterns, evidence, and responsibility rather than accepting every late declaration. It also warns the accused not to become cynical: Allah knows the heart, and a sincere person may still repent. The final judgment does not belong to public suspicion.

**Late explanations cannot erase early choices**

When consequences reveal a plan’s harm, the person who claims good intentions should be asked to repair the damage, not merely repeat the claim. Reconciliation is proved by changed behaviour and restitution. The verse warns against the habit of using pious vocabulary only after an alliance has failed.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:63

> ˹Only˺ Allah knows what is in their hearts. So turn away from them, caution them, and give them advice that will shake their very souls.

### Expanded Commentary

**Advice that reaches the conscience**

Allah knows what is in their hearts, so the Prophet is instructed to turn away from their immediate performance, caution them, and address them with words that penetrate their souls. The three movements are balanced. Turning away avoids rewarding manipulation; warning makes the moral danger clear; penetrating advice leaves a door open for repentance.

**The limits of human diagnosis**

Because God alone knows the heart, believers must not claim certainty about hidden hypocrisy. They may identify public contradiction and protect the community from harm, but they should avoid declaring an individual’s final spiritual state. The Prophet was given revelation about particular cases; ordinary people do not possess that access.

**Speech with moral force**

Advice that “reaches” a person is not necessarily loud or humiliating. It names the wrong accurately, explains its consequence, and speaks in a way capable of awakening responsibility. Public shaming often hardens the heart; private counsel can create the space in which truth is admitted. The verse’s aim is reform and protection together.

**Advice should preserve a route back**

A warning can be firm without making repentance socially impossible. Correct the conduct, protect those at risk, and speak words that allow the person to recognize himself honestly. Public humiliation may satisfy anger while private truth has a better chance of producing reform.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:64

> We only sent messengers to be obeyed by Allah’s Will. If only those ˹hypocrites˺ came to you ˹O Prophet˺—after wronging themselves—seeking Allah’s forgiveness and the Messenger prayed for their forgiveness, they would have certainly found Allah ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful.

### Expanded Commentary

**Messengers are sent to be obeyed**

The verse defines the Messenger’s role: obedience to him is part of obedience to Allah, by Allah’s permission. Prophethood is not a delivery service in which people receive information and then retain sovereignty over whether divine guidance applies. The messenger explains, embodies, and judges by revelation.

**The route back after wrongdoing**

The verse offers a path for those who have wronged themselves: come to the Messenger, seek Allah’s forgiveness, and let the Messenger pray for them. The movement includes confession, return to the source of guidance, and a communal expression of repentance. It does not make the Prophet an independent owner of forgiveness; Allah is the One who accepts repentance, while the Messenger’s prayer is an honoured means.

After the Prophet’s death, the enduring lesson is to return to Qur’an and Sunnah, seek forgiveness, and repair harm rather than defend the ego. The verse’s mercy is active: the person must come, admit, and change. A community should imitate the Prophetic response by making repentance possible rather than making public disgrace permanent.

**Repentance has a communal form**

The person who returns to revelation should not be forced to repair alone. A just community can provide counsel, mediation, and a path to restore rights. The Prophet’s prayer for forgiveness models an authority that helps the wrongdoer return without denying the wrong that occurred.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:65

> But no! By your Lord, they will never be ˹true˺ believers until they accept you ˹O Prophet˺ as the judge in their disputes, and find no resistance within themselves against your decision and submit wholeheartedly.

### Expanded Commentary

**Submission in the place of dispute**

The verse swears that true belief requires accepting the Messenger as judge in disputes, feeling no inward resistance to his decision, and submitting wholeheartedly. This is a demanding definition because outward compliance can conceal resentment. Faith is tested when the ruling limits one’s own interest or favours a weaker claimant.

**The three stages of acceptance**

First comes referral: the dispute is brought to the revealed standard rather than settled by power. Then comes inward acceptance, the struggle against the feeling that one deserves an exception. Finally comes submission in action. The stages do not mean that a believer experiences no emotion; they mean that emotion does not become rebellion. One can ask about evidence and application without refusing truth once it is clear.

**The law protects equality**

Prophetic arbitration mattered because it placed influential and ordinary people under one rule. The famous reports of the Prophet rejecting intercession for a noble offender illustrate that justice cannot be adjusted according to lineage. The verse therefore supports rule of law, but with a sacred source and a moral demand that the heart also accept the outcome.

After the Prophet, disputes are referred to the sources and qualified scholarship that faithfully interprets them. No later authority is infallible. The verse calls for humility before truth, not blind submission to every person who claims to represent it.

**Submission is tested by self-interest**

It is easy to praise a judgment that benefits us. The deeper test is whether we accept the same standard when it limits our property, status, or family. This does not prohibit asking for evidence or seeking review; it prohibits making personal preference the final court.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:66

> If We had commanded them to sacrifice themselves or abandon their homes, none would have obeyed except for a few. Had they done what they were advised to do, it would have certainly been far better for them and more reassuring,

### Expanded Commentary

**The test of costly obedience**

Allah says that if believers had been commanded to sacrifice themselves or leave their homes, few would have obeyed. The statement exposes the gap between verbal allegiance and costly commitment. Most people imagine themselves brave until obedience threatens family, wealth, comfort, or status.

**“It would have been better”**

The verse does not glorify suffering for its own sake. It says that doing what they were advised would have been better and more strengthening. Obedience builds moral confidence: a person who acts on a difficult command discovers that fear did not deserve the authority he gave it. Communities also become more secure when members keep commitments rather than negotiating every duty around convenience.

The verse must not be used by unauthorized people to demand dangerous acts from others. The command discussed is divine and carried through legitimate prophetic authority. In contemporary life, costly obedience includes truth-telling, leaving unlawful income, protecting an abused dependent, and accepting a fair ruling against oneself.

The concluding assurance is that Allah knows capacity and does not command beyond it. The problem addressed is not an impossible burden but the tendency to resist even manageable duties because comfort has become a hidden idol.

**Cost reveals the seriousness of a command**

A person need not seek extraordinary suffering to prove faith. Ordinary costly obedience may include leaving an unlawful job, telling the truth about a relative, or protecting someone unpopular. The verse asks whether comfort has become so authoritative that even a reasonable duty feels intolerable.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:67

> and We would have granted them a great reward by Our grace

### Expanded Commentary

**Reward is grace, not a purchase**

Had the hypocrites obeyed, Allah would have given them a great reward by His grace. The wording keeps the balance between effort and gift. Obedience matters, but it does not make God indebted to the servant. The reward is generosity from the One who enabled the act and accepted it.

**Why the promise is placed after sacrifice**

The previous verse described obedience that might cost life or homeland. This one tells the hesitant heart that what is surrendered for Allah is not lost. The world measures sacrifice by what leaves the hand; divine grace measures it by what it opens in the soul and the everlasting account.

The verse also corrects the fear that obedience will make a person poor in every sense. Sometimes a duty does bring worldly loss, and the Qur’an does not promise that every sacrifice returns as money. It promises a greater reward, which may include guidance, honour, companionship, forgiveness, and the Hereafter. The believer acts for God, not as a bargain for a predictable earthly outcome.

**Sacrifice is not wasted**

The great reward may be hidden from the world’s accounting. A person who loses status by refusing corruption may gain integrity; a family that leaves oppression may gain safety; a soldier who protects rather than plunders may gain no headline but remain honoured by God. Grace measures what publicity cannot.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:68

> and guided them to the Straight Path.

### Expanded Commentary

**Guidance grows through obedience**

The reward is followed by guidance to the straight path. This order teaches that guidance is not only information received before action. When a person obeys sincerely, the path becomes clearer and the capacity to continue increases. Conversely, repeated resistance can make obvious truth appear burdensome.

**The straight path as direction**

The path is straight because it joins belief, worship, justice, and social duty without the distortions of excess or neglect. The hypocrites wanted to keep religious language while avoiding the path’s demands. The verse offers an alternative: obedience aligns the inner and outer life.

Guidance does not mean the disappearance of every question. It means a reliable orientation through questions. A believer may make a mistake, seek counsel, and return, but does not surrender the compass to appetite or public pressure. The verse encourages action as a form of learning: begin with the duty known, and ask Allah to open what is not yet clear.

**Action can deepen understanding**

Some guidance becomes clear only when a person begins to obey the part already understood. This is not blind action; it is the practical testing of a truth. Prayer, honest accounting, and fair speech make later questions easier to approach because the heart is no longer negotiating from pure self-interest.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:69

> And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company!

### Expanded Commentary

**The fellowship of the obedient**

Those who obey Allah and the Messenger are promised the company of prophets, the truthful, martyrs, and righteous. The verse does not say that every believer becomes a prophet or receives the identical rank of a martyr. It promises companionship and belonging in the community of the honoured. Obedience links ordinary lives to the highest models of faith.

**Four forms of excellence**

Prophets conveyed revelation; the truthful embodied unwavering honesty; martyrs gave their lives in witness; the righteous sustained obedience in ordinary conduct. The categories show that spiritual greatness has different expressions. A person need not imitate another’s historical role to share the same direction of truthfulness and submission.

**The beauty of “company”**

The reward is described relationally. Paradise is not only a place but a fellowship free from betrayal and envy. The believer who seeks good companionship now—people who remind him of God, keep trusts, and correct him gently—is preparing for that greater company. The verse also dignifies hidden righteousness that no public office recognizes.

The closing phrase calls it an excellent company. It is an invitation as much as a promise: choose today whom you follow and whose habits you normalize, for obedience is a road toward people whose presence is itself a reward.

**Good company is a form of education**

The people named in the promise embody different virtues, so companionship in Paradise begins with companionship in moral direction now. Choose friends who make truth easier, not merely those who confirm every preference. A community of truthful people can carry a person through weakness without turning weakness into an identity.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:70

> This is Allah’s favour, and Allah fully knows ˹who deserves it˺.

### Expanded Commentary

**The whole gift belongs to Allah**

“This is Allah’s favour” refers to the honour of obedience and its company. The verse prevents the obedient from turning the promise into a basis for pride. Faith, opportunity, perseverance, and reward all depend on divine favour, even though the servant must choose and strive.

**Knowledge of deserving**

Allah fully knows who deserves the reward. Human beings see public acts and cannot measure intention, obstacles, sincerity, or concealed repentance. This is a comfort to the unknown servant and a warning to the celebrated one. Recognition by people is neither proof of acceptance nor evidence of rejection.

The verse also guards against despair. A person may think that a long history of failure disqualifies him from noble company; Allah knows the truth of a final turning and the effort hidden from others. The correct response is to seek favour through obedience without claiming ownership of the outcome.

**Hidden deserving is known to God**

The promise is safe in divine knowledge even when people misjudge it. Someone whose work is unseen may be closer to the honoured than a celebrated figure, while a public reputation may hide serious betrayal. This should produce gratitude rather than anxiety: strive sincerely and leave the ranking to Allah.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:71

> O believers! Take your precautions and go forth either in groups or together.

### Expanded Commentary

**Precaution is part of reliance**

Believers are told to take precautions and to go forth in groups or together. The command concerns military danger, but its principle is wider: trust in Allah does not require abandoning means. Planning, intelligence, communication, protective equipment, and coordinated movement are forms of responsible reliance.

**Groups and collective discipline**

The options of going in small groups or as a whole respond to changing circumstances. There is no single tactical form detached from context. A commander must consider the threat, the mission, and the protection of people. The verse therefore supports flexibility without chaos.

In contemporary application, precautions include safeguarding civilians, verifying information, protecting aid workers, and refusing impulsive violence. The command is not permission for aggression; it is instruction for a community already facing danger. The believer prepares carefully, then entrusts the result to Allah rather than treating preparation as a guarantee.

**Preparation is not a lack of trust**

The command to take precautions corrects a spirituality that calls planning unbelief. Medical supplies, communication plans, safe routes, and trained leadership all serve the preservation of life. Reliance begins after responsible means have been taken, not before them.

### Expanded Commentary

**The opportunist who avoids every cost**

Some people lag behind so that they will not share danger. When disaster strikes the believers, they call their absence a blessing. Their words reveal a distorted understanding of safety: they measure blessing by personal escape even when others suffer for the common cause.

**Private comfort and public obligation**

The verse does not condemn every person who stays behind. Illness, necessary care, or a legitimate exemption can be part of obedience. It addresses those who evade a duty without excuse and then celebrate the hardship of those who fulfilled it. A community cannot survive if every member claims the benefits of collective action while refusing its burdens.

The opportunist’s statement also wounds morale. Instead of praying for the afflicted and helping their families, he converts tragedy into evidence for his own prudence. The believer should avoid that cruelty, assess duties honestly, and support those who bear risks for justice.

**Exemption is not opportunism**

A person who remains behind for a real duty or incapacity may still belong fully to the community. The problem is celebrating the suffering of those who served while claiming innocence from every cost. Communities should assess excuses fairly and refuse both suspicion of the genuinely unable and admiration for the cynical avoider.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:73

### Expanded Commentary

**Wanting the reward without the bond**

If victory and spoils come, the same laggards wish they had been present to share the gain. The verse depicts them saying it as though no bond had connected them to the believers. Their opportunism is exposed by the contrast: disaster makes them outsiders; success makes them relatives.

**The test of solidarity**

A genuine community is not defined by who appears at the distribution of benefit. It is revealed when members support one another during danger and loss. The verse does not prohibit rewarding those who were legitimately absent; it condemns the heart that claims belonging only when belonging is profitable.

The warning reaches beyond battle. Employees, citizens, relatives, and religious groups all face the temptation to distance themselves from difficult decisions and then demand equal credit. Honest participation includes accepting appropriate responsibility for outcomes, acknowledging those who carried the burden, and not rewriting the past when success arrives.

**Credit belongs with responsibility**

Success should not allow a person who abandoned a shared burden to rewrite his role. The ethical response is to honour those who served, offer fair support to their families, and welcome late participation without pretending that absence was sacrifice. Belonging is more than arriving at the distribution of gain.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:74

### Expanded Commentary

**Trading the lower life for the lasting one**

The verse calls those who would give the life of this world for the Hereafter to fight in Allah’s cause. The phrase is not a romantic invitation to seek death; it is a summons to value justice and obedience above survival as the highest good. Martyrdom and victory are both mentioned, preventing the believer from defining success as dying rather than fulfilling a lawful mission.

**The limits of fighting**

“Allah’s cause” excludes tribal revenge, conquest for wealth, coercion of faith, and personal rage. Legitimate authority, just cause, proportional conduct, and protection of noncombatants remain essential.

**Reward in either outcome**

The believer’s honour does not depend on worldly headlines. Victory may bring responsibility and testing; martyrdom may bring the direct reward of the Hereafter. In both cases Allah promises a great reward to the person who acts sincerely within His limits. The verse therefore trains courage without worshipping military power.

**Victory is not the only measure**

A lawful struggle may end in victory, stalemate, or the death of those who served. The verse places all outcomes under a reward beyond the battlefield. This preserves courage while preventing commanders from treating casualties as proof of spiritual success or victory as proof of moral correctness.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:75

### Expanded Commentary

**The cry of the oppressed**

The verse asks why believers do not fight in Allah’s cause and for oppressed men, women, and children. Their cry is a prayer for rescue from a land of oppressors and for a protector and helper. The text gives the oppressed a voice before describing the duty of those with capacity to help; their humanity is not abstracted into a political slogan.

**Protection, not domination**

The cause is framed by deliverance from oppression, not acquisition of territory or revenge. The presence of women and children also emphasizes the duty to protect civilians and the innocent. Any military claim that ignores their safety while invoking this verse has contradicted its moral centre.

The verse challenges comfortable believers to ask whether distance has become an excuse for indifference. Help can include diplomacy, refuge, financial support, advocacy, documentation, and lawful defence, according to capacity and consequence. Not every person has the same means, but every person can refuse to normalize oppression.

**The prayer in the verse**

The oppressed ask Allah to appoint a guardian and helper, teaching that human aid is a form of divine mercy rather than a replacement for God. Those who help must therefore act with humility, not turn rescue into ownership. The person freed from oppression remains a person with rights, voice, and agency.

**The oppressed are subjects, not symbols**

Those who help an oppressed population must listen to its people, protect their agency, and avoid replacing one domination with another. The prayer for a helper is not a demand for a new owner. Rescue becomes faithful when it returns people to safety and the ability to govern their own lives.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:76

### Expanded Commentary

**Two causes, two moral orientations**

Believers fight for Allah’s cause while disbelievers fight for the cause of Satan. The question is what a force serves: justice, protection, and obedience, or oppression, vanity, and domination.

**The weakness of Satan’s schemes**

Satan can make falsehood appear powerful, but his schemes are weak in the final sense. They depend on fear, fragmentation, exaggeration, and the willingness of people to cooperate with their own corruption. A believer should not be paralysed by the size of an unjust power. Yet confidence in Satan’s weakness does not eliminate planning; the previous verse commanded precautions.

The verse also prevents a simplistic geography of good and evil. A community calling itself Muslim can fight for Satan if it massacres innocents, steals, or pursues pride. The label of the cause must be tested by its means and ends. The real struggle begins with the self’s willingness to obey God rather than anger.

**The cause must be examined**

A slogan about God does not purify a violent cause. Examine whether the action protects the vulnerable, whether peaceful alternatives were considered, and whether means remain within divine limits. Satan’s scheme often persuades people that their anger is already a sacred mandate.

### Expanded Commentary

**From restraint to commanded defence**

The verse recalls people who were initially told not to fight, but to establish prayer and pay zakat. When fighting was later commanded, some became afraid of human opponents and asked for delay. The sequence shows that courage is not produced by excitement alone. A community is first formed through worship, charity, and discipline before it carries the burdens of armed defence.

**Fear exposes misplaced calculation**

Their fear is described as fearing people as Allah should be feared, or more. Fear of danger is natural; the problem is allowing it to outweigh obedience and justice. The request for a little more time reveals the endless postponement by which a person hopes the duty will disappear.

**Worldly enjoyment and the thread**

The world’s enjoyment is little compared with the Hereafter, and no one will be wronged even by the smallest amount. The verse does not deny the value of life or excuse reckless conduct. **Training precedes defence**

The sequence of prayer, zakat, and later fighting shows that a community is not prepared merely because it possesses weapons. It must learn discipline, generosity, restraint, and concern for the weak. Without that moral formation, power magnifies fear and revenge rather than justice.

**Death reaches every fortress**

Wherever people are, death overtakes them, even in fortified towers. The image addresses those who imagine that political power, architecture, medical wealth, or physical distance can cancel mortality. Precautions are commanded, but precautions do not create sovereignty over the appointed term.

**Attributing good and evil**

The hypocrites attribute good to Allah and evil to the Messenger. The final question about their lack of understanding is a call to intellectual honesty. Tragedy is not automatically evidence against truth, and success is not automatically evidence for it. The believer evaluates events by revelation, responsibility, and the final account.

**Events are not simple verdicts**

Success may test a person more dangerously than failure, and disaster may expose a weakness without proving that the entire cause was false. The verse asks believers to read events with revelation and responsibility rather than with immediate emotional attribution. This protects both gratitude and humility.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:79

> Whatever good befalls you is from Allah and whatever evil befalls you is from yourself. We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a messenger to ˹all˺ people. And Allah is sufficient as a Witness.

### Expanded Commentary

**Good as grace, evil as consequence**

The verse attributes whatever good befalls a person to Allah and whatever evil befalls him to himself. Good ultimately comes as provision, opportunity, guidance, and enabling grace from God. Evil is “from yourself” because people introduce injustice, negligence, and disobedience into the world, even though nothing escapes Allah’s decree.

This distinction protects two truths. Gratitude prevents success from becoming self-worship, while responsibility prevents suffering from being used as an excuse for wrongdoing. It does not mean that every illness or disaster is a direct punishment for a particular sin. The verse addresses moral accountability, not permission to blame victims for every hardship.

**The Messenger’s universal mission**

The Prophet is sent to all people, and Allah is sufficient as Witness. The statement establishes the scope of the message and the certainty of its testimony. The Messenger conveys; Allah judges how people respond. A believer who follows him should therefore carry the message through justice and mercy rather than through coercion or contempt.

The verse is also a correction to the idea that prophetic guidance is responsible for the evil people choose after rejecting it. Revelation offers a path; people remain answerable for walking away. Allah’s witness is sufficient even when the public record is distorted.

**Do not blame victims for every hardship**

The statement that evil is from oneself must not become a tool for telling the sick, displaced, or abused that they caused their suffering. It addresses culpable choices and moral consequences. Compassion requires distinguishing personal wrongdoing from trials that befall a person without his choice.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:80

### Expanded Commentary

**Obeying the Messenger is obeying Allah**

The verse makes Prophetic obedience a theological consequence of revelation. Muhammad is not an independent source competing with Allah; he conveys and embodies what Allah authorizes. To obey him in his revealed guidance is therefore to obey the One who sent him.

**Turning away and the limits of Prophetic office**

The Messenger is not sent as a keeper over people. He conveys clearly but does not control hearts or force faith. This distinction protects the Prophet from both accusations of failure when people reject him and from later claims that religious leadership means domination of consciences. Accountability belongs to those who hear and choose.

For Muslims, the verse demands careful study of authentic Sunnah rather than reducing obedience to slogans. A report must be established, understood in context, and reconciled with the Qur’an and the body of Prophetic teaching. Following the Messenger includes his justice, mercy, humility, and protection of rights, not only isolated commands quoted without his example.

**Prophetic example interprets command**

Obedience to the Messenger includes learning how he applied revelation: with justice, mercy, consultation, and protection of the weak. An isolated quotation that produces cruelty cannot represent the whole Prophetic guidance. The messenger’s authority is understood through the messenger’s character as well as his words.

**Public agreement, private sabotage**

**Turning away from manipulation**

The Prophet is told to turn away from them and trust Allah. This is not a command to ignore danger; it is a refusal to let every hidden plot control the heart. Institutions must still protect themselves through evidence and accountability, but leaders should not become consumed by suspicion. Trust in Allah allows firm action without vindictiveness.

The verse is also a warning to believers who say yes to a duty they have already decided to undermine. Honest disagreement is better than false assent. A community can consult, revise, and correct; it cannot build solidarity on promises that are merely tactical.

**Trust after the meeting**

The verse values consistency between public speech and private action. People who lead teams, families, or institutions should record decisions and responsibilities so that hidden contradiction can be corrected early. Trust is not built by demanding that others believe an attractive promise; it is built by making conduct match the promise over time.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:82

> Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies.

### Expanded Commentary

**Reflection on the Qur’an**

The verse calls people to reflect deeply on the Qur’an. The Qur’an invites active thought rather than passive possession.

**Coherence as a sign**

If the Qur’an were from someone other than Allah, the verse says, its many internal contradictions would be exposed. The argument does not claim that every reader will instantly resolve every difficulty. Apparent tension should therefore lead to study, context, language, and qualified interpretation—not to a hurried accusation. The reader must also ask whether the contradiction lies in the text or in an assumption brought to it. Reflection is a form of humility before a Book whose unity exceeds the reader’s first impression.

**Reflection is disciplined reading**

A reader should compare passages, examine context, learn Arabic terms through reliable scholarship, and notice the way a command operates within the whole chapter. The absence of contradiction becomes more evident through patient study. Difficulty is an invitation to understanding, not automatic evidence against the text.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:83

### Expanded Commentary

**News can become a public danger**

The verse criticizes those who broadcast reports of security or fear as soon as they hear them. Information can protect a community, but unverified information can create panic, expose operations, inflame suspicion, or encourage an enemy. The command is therefore about responsible circulation, not a blanket ban on communication.

**Refer matters to qualified authority**

The Messenger and those in authority are named as people to whom such reports should be referred. Those with sound judgment can assess authenticity, context, likely consequences, and whether publication serves the public interest. Authority here is not permission for rulers to conceal every inconvenient truth; it is a reminder that expertise and accountability matter in crises.

**A modern information ethic**

Social media makes the verse immediately recognizable. Sharing an alarming headline, private allegation, or fabricated emergency may feel like vigilance while actually serving Satan’s confusion. Verify before forwarding, distinguish fact from inference, and consider who may be harmed by disclosure. Divine grace saves a community from the consequences of every rumor; gratitude requires not manufacturing new ones.

**Verify before amplifying**

The modern equivalent of spreading news of security or fear may be forwarding an unverified message to thousands. Pause, identify the source, consider the risk, and let competent people assess it. A community protects itself not only with guards but with responsible information habits.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:84

**Curbing power, not seeking domination**

The aim is to curb the strength of those who attack the community. It is not a call to pursue war for its own sake. The surrounding passages on treaties, peace, and protection set boundaries. Military force is a means of stopping aggression and protecting the vulnerable, not a spiritual identity.

**Leadership begins with personal duty**

A leader cannot force sincerity in followers, but can make the moral standard visible through his own conduct. He must also encourage without manipulating, explain the cause, and protect those who cannot fight. Strength is measured by the justice it restrains itself to serve.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:85

> Whoever intercedes for a good cause will have a share in the reward, and whoever intercedes for an evil cause will have a share in the burden. And Allah is Watchful over all things.

### Expanded Commentary

**Intercession participates in the consequence**

Whoever intercedes for a good cause receives a share of its reward, while whoever intercedes for an evil cause shares its burden. A recommendation for a qualified worker, a mediation that prevents violence, or an appeal for a prisoner’s lawful rights can be good intercession. **Recommendation is moral participation**

Before lending a name or influence, ask whether the person or cause deserves support. The same recommendation can open a job to a qualified applicant or help an unqualified favourite displace one. Allah’s Watchfulness makes social influence part of the accounting of deeds.

**Greeting establishes a public ethic**

A community that answers courtesy with courtesy makes later truth-telling and negotiation easier because the other person has not been reduced to an enemy before the conversation begins.

**The final gathering dignifies the present**

Because every person will be gathered, no life is disposable and no deed is irrelevant. The belief can motivate care for people whom history overlooks and discourage the powerful from treating temporary victory as a final verdict. Allah’s word makes the moral future certain even when the present is confused.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:88

**Why believers differ about hypocrites**

**Judgment and protection**

**Protect without stereotyping**

A community may respond to demonstrated betrayal while refusing to punish an entire population for the actions of some. Evidence, fair procedure, and an opportunity for repentance are necessary. The verse’s warning about hypocrisy should create vigilance against conduct, not a license for suspicion based on appearance or identity.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:89

> They wish you would disbelieve as they have disbelieved, so you may all be alike. So do not take them as allies unless they emigrate in the cause of Allah. But if they turn away, then seize them and kill them wherever you find them, and do not take any of them as allies or helpers,

### Expanded Commentary

**A wartime command, not a license for indiscriminate violence**

The verse addresses people who wanted believers to abandon faith so that all would be alike, and it discusses hostile opponents in the context of the early Muslim polity. Its severe command must be read with verses 90–91, the treaty rules, the distinction between combatants and those who offer peace, and the Qur’an’s prohibition of transgression. It is not a general order to kill people who disagree with Islam.

**Alliance and active hostility**

The concern is not mere difference of belief but organized hostility that seeks to make the community abandon its faith and supports attack. The command to withhold alliance refers to military and political loyalty in an existential conflict, not to ordinary kindness, trade, neighbourliness, or justice toward non-Muslims.

The condition concerning emigration in Allah’s cause belongs to the historical obligation of joining and strengthening the persecuted community. The verse’s purpose is protection of a community under attack, and its boundaries in the next passages prove that purpose. Removing those boundaries turns a regulated wartime judgment into the very transgression the Qur’an condemns.

**Context is a moral safeguard**

The severe wartime language must be interpreted with its treaty exceptions and with the general prohibition of transgression. Removing context is not strictness; it is distortion. A faithful reader asks who is addressed, what conflict exists, what peace terms were offered, and which limits the chapter itself places on force.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:90

> except those who are allies of a people you are bound with in a treaty or those wholeheartedly opposed to fighting either you or their own people. If Allah had willed, He would have empowered them to fight you. So if they refrain from fighting you and offer you peace, then Allah does not permit you to harm them.

### Expanded Commentary

**Treaties limit force**

The verse makes an explicit exception for people allied to a treaty community and for those who withdraw from fighting both the Muslims and their own people. If Allah had willed, He could have given them power to fight, but they refrain and offer peace. In that case, the believers have no permission to harm them.

**Peace is a legal fact**

A treaty is not a decorative promise that can be broken when circumstances change. It creates obligations, protects persons, and restrains the use of force. The same applies to a person who communicates a genuine cessation of hostilities. The verse makes non-aggression morally relevant even when the person’s religious or political identity is not shared.

The passage is a powerful safeguard against reading 4:89 alone. It requires evaluation of conduct and declared peace. Muslim authorities must uphold agreements, investigate violations, and avoid treating uncertainty as proof of attack. The command not to harm peaceful people is as much part of the verse’s wartime ethic as the permission to fight aggressors.

**Peaceful conduct has legal weight**

A person who withdraws from fighting and offers peace is protected even if his community is politically opposed. This principle can govern ceasefires, asylum, and the treatment of civilians. Trust is not naïveté when it is supported by clear agreements and careful verification; it is obedience to a stated limit on harm.

**The door away from violence**

The verse names withdrawal, peace, and non-attack as grounds for restraint. These are not minor details but routes the community must actively seek. A commander who prefers escalation must answer why peaceful alternatives were rejected, not merely assert that force was permitted.

**The sanctity of believing life**

The verse begins by declaring that a believer may not kill another believer except by mistake. Modern systems may use different forms of compensation and expiation, but the principle remains: accidental death is not ignored. Families deserve truth, support, and a process that acknowledges their loss. The perpetrator’s remorse becomes meaningful when it produces responsibility rather than a demand that everyone forget.

**The exceptional severity of intentional killing**

The verse gives one of the Qur’an’s strongest warnings for intentionally killing a believer: Hell, divine anger, condemnation, and tremendous punishment. The accumulation of terms reflects the destruction of a protected life and the betrayal of the community’s covenant. Intentional murder cannot be treated as an ordinary dispute that a fine automatically resolves.

**Theological discussions and practical duty**

A murderer must repent, submit to lawful judgment, and seek restoration where possible.

The verse does not authorize families to exact private vengeance. Its severity supports due process because only a competent authority can establish intention, evidence, defences, and punishment. The sanctity of life is served by law, not by cycles of retaliation.

**No private blood vengeance**

The severity of the warning supports the need for lawful investigation, not personal retaliation. Intent, evidence, and circumstances must be established by competent authority. Families may seek justice and may forgive, but they should not be pushed into cycles of revenge by those who confuse anger with the sacred protection of life.

The mention of forgiveness and mercy keeps striving within worship. A person who serves must continue to repent, protect life, and resist the ego’s desire to be celebrated as a hero. The greatest rank can be lost if sacrifice becomes a reason to despise those who were not in the same position.

**Escape from oppression requires means**

Migration may involve legal planning, family coordination, money, and the help of those who already live in safety. The verse does not romanticize displacement. **Privacy is not immunity**

People need private spaces for counsel and repentance, but secrecy does not convert a plot into a right. When conduct threatens others, lawful investigation may be necessary. **The limits of worldly defence**

**Every sin returns to its doer**

Social conditions can influence behaviour and may create institutional responsibility, but personal accountability remains. The verse helps communities discuss causes without erasing the agent who must repent and repair.

**Slander adds a second crime**

The innocent person may lose property, trust, employment, or safety while the real offender appears respectable.

**The social mechanics of scapegoating**

Scapegoating often succeeds when the accused is weak, foreign, female, poor, or absent. The verse therefore stands with those who cannot easily defend themselves. Before repeating an allegation, ask who benefits, what evidence exists, and whether the accused has a genuine opportunity to respond.

Repentance requires public correction proportionate to public harm. A private apology cannot fully repair a lie broadcast to a community. Allah’s law protects truth by making the liar responsible for the entire chain of injury he set in motion.

**The damage of scapegoating**

False blame often appears when an institution wants to preserve its reputation. The Qur’anic response is to investigate who acted, who benefited, and who was made vulnerable by the lie. Protecting an innocent person may require correcting a respected figure publicly, because quiet internal knowledge does not undo public accusation.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:113

> Had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy, a group of them would have sought to deceive you ˹O Prophet˺. Yet they would deceive none but themselves, nor can they harm you in the least. Allah has revealed to you the Book and wisdom and taught you what you never knew. Great ˹indeed˺ is Allah’s favour upon you!

### Expanded Commentary

**Divine protection of the Messenger**

Allah’s grace and mercy prevented a group from successfully deceiving the Prophet. They intended to manipulate judgment, but the revelation exposed the scheme and preserved justice. The verse reassures the Prophet that human cunning cannot defeat divine guidance.

**Knowledge as a gift**

The Book, wisdom, and teaching are named as great favour. Wisdom includes sound judgment, understanding of contexts, and the ability to apply revelation without being trapped by appearances. It is not enough to possess text; a judge must perceive how truth bears on a real dispute.

The verse is also a warning to religious leaders not to overestimate their own insight. If the Prophet’s knowledge is described as favour, every later scholar remains dependent. Gratitude for knowledge is shown by fairness, humility, and resistance to the flattering story that one already knows enough.

**Grace protects truth from cleverness**

The Prophet was taught what he did not know, showing that sound judgment is a gift that must be received with gratitude. Later scholars should therefore state the limits of their knowledge and consult others. The more influence a person has, the more dangerous it is for him to treat intuition as revelation.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:114

> There is no good in most of their secret talks—except those encouraging charity, kindness, or reconciliation between people. And whoever does this seeking Allah’s pleasure, We will grant them a great reward.

### Expanded Commentary

**Not every private conversation is harmless**

Most secret talks contain no good, the verse says, except those that encourage charity, recognized kindness, or reconciliation. Secrecy can protect a vulnerable person and allow mediation without humiliation, but it can also shelter plotting, gossip, and manipulation. The moral value lies in what the conversation serves.

**Three constructive exceptions**

Charity includes urging material support and sharing resources. *Maʿrūf* includes every sound act recognized by revelation and decent custom. Reconciliation involves restoring relationships without concealing serious danger or forcing a victim back into harm. These are examples of speech that may need privacy precisely because public exposure would make them harder.

Whoever does this seeking Allah’s pleasure receives a great reward. The intention directs the conversation away from reputation. A mediator should not create drama so that others praise his peacemaking; he should quietly make justice and repair possible.

**Private speech can heal**

A quiet conversation that arranges food for a neighbour, reconciles spouses, or encourages a debt relief may be more valuable than public moral commentary. The verse gives such speech a high place because it serves people rather than the speaker’s reputation. Its reward belongs to the one who seeks Allah’s pleasure, not the one who seeks to be seen as kind.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:115

> And whoever defies the Messenger after guidance has become clear to them and follows a path other than that of the believers, We will let them pursue what they have chosen, then burn them in Hell—what an evil end!

### Expanded Commentary

**The path of the believers**

The verse warns whoever opposes the Messenger after guidance has become clear and follows a path other than that of the believers. The issue is not an honest question or a person who has not yet received clarity. It is defiance after evidence and deliberate departure from the community of faith.

**Consensus and its limits**

The phrase “path of the believers” supports the authority of the established community and the importance of scholarly agreement. It does not mean that every majority opinion is automatically revelation or that a ruler may silence criticism by claiming consensus. The believers’ path is measured by the Qur’an, authentic Sunnah, justice, and the inherited work of qualified scholarship.

Allah lets the defiant pursue what they have chosen, then warns of Hell. This is a form of abandonment: the person is not physically prevented from error, but his choice becomes his road. The verse calls for humility before clear guidance and careful discernment before declaring a matter settled.

**The believers’ path is accountable**

Following the community does not mean surrendering conscience to a crowd. The believers’ path is a path of evidence, worship, justice, and correction. A person who claims consensus should be able to show the scholarship behind the claim and accept that honest disagreement can exist before a matter becomes clear.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:116

> Surely Allah does not forgive associating ˹others˺ with Him ˹in worship˺, but forgives anything else of whoever He wills. Indeed, whoever associates ˹others˺ with Allah has clearly gone far astray.

### Expanded Commentary

**The repeated warning about shirk**

This verse repeats that Allah does not forgive associating partners with Him for one who dies persisting in it, while other sins remain under His will. Repetition signals centrality. The chapter has moved through law, politics, family, and judgment, and now returns to the foundation: no social order is sound if worship is divided between God and rivals.

The phrase “far astray” describes more than a wrong turn. Shirk misdirects the entire journey because the person’s ultimate loyalty is attached to a created object. Yet the repetition also preserves hope for the living: the call to exclusive worship remains available as long as the person can repent.

**Repentance remains the dividing line**

The warning about shirk is a warning about the end-state of a person who dies without turning back. It should lead to inviting people toward pure worship rather than declaring living neighbours beyond hope. The believer fears hidden forms of idolatry in status and desire while trusting Allah’s promise to the repentant.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:117

> Instead of Allah, they only invoke female gods and they ˹actually˺ invoke none but a rebellious Satan—

### Expanded Commentary

**False gods and the rebellious impulse**

The verse says that those who invoke female deities in place of Allah are actually invoking Satan. The immediate reference is to pagan idols associated with goddesses, but the principle is broader: a false object of worship is part of a rebellious system that redirects devotion from the Creator.

The description of Satan as rebellious emphasizes choice. Evil is not an equal cosmic power competing with God; it is a created will in revolt. People who follow it are not discovering freedom but entering a pattern of dependency on false promises and appetites.

The verse should not be used to insult people individually or to erase the complexity of their beliefs. It calls believers to examine their own invocations: whose approval do they seek as ultimate, whose command do they treat as unquestionable, and what desire has become an idol?

**False objects of devotion**

Modern idols may be institutions, personalities, markets, or ideologies that demand unquestioned allegiance. The verse teaches believers to examine what receives their deepest fear and hope. If a created system can command a wrong and still be obeyed, it has begun to occupy the place that belongs to God.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:118

> cursed by Allah—who said, “I will surely take hold of a certain number of Your servants.

### Expanded Commentary

**Satan’s declared strategy**

Satan says he will take a portion of Allah’s servants. The language presents temptation as selective and organized, not as an irresistible force imposed on everyone equally. He seeks willing participation, targeting weakness, pride, fear, and the desire for easy certainty.

The statement is also a divine exposure of the enemy’s method. Believers are told the plan so that secrecy loses its power. A temptation named can be resisted: ask whose interest a suggestion serves, what it makes you forget, and whether it turns a lawful gift into an object of worship.

The verse’s “portion” warns communities not to assume that religious numbers guarantee purity. Satan’s influence can enter through respected people, institutions, and apparently pious habits. Vigilance begins with humility.

**Temptation seeks a portion**

No one should assume that a community’s majority protects every individual from temptation. Watch for the places where status, anger, or fear are rewarded, because those are the doors through which rebellion becomes attractive. Naming the strategy makes vigilance a shared practice rather than a private panic.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:119

> I will certainly mislead them and delude them with empty hopes. Also, I will order them and they will slit the ears of cattle and alter Allah’s creation.” And whoever takes Satan as a guardian instead of Allah has certainly suffered a tremendous loss.

### Expanded Commentary

**Empty hopes and the alteration of creation**

Satan promises misguidance and fills people with false hopes. He commands practices that mark animals and alters Allah’s creation. Classical exegetes connected the language to pagan customs, while later scholars discussed how it applies to deliberate distortion of the natural order and to harmful manipulation.

The essential point is not that every change to the body or environment is forbidden. Healing, cultivation, clothing, and beneficial technology are part of human stewardship. The warning concerns alteration governed by idolatry, vanity, cruelty, or rejection of the limits God has set. Motive, harm, and purpose matter.

The person who takes Satan as a guardian suffers tremendous loss because he exchanges a real Lord for a deceiver. False hope promises control without responsibility; divine guidance offers responsibility with mercy. The believer tests every cultural command by truth, benefit, and worship rather than by novelty or ancestral habit.

**Creation and responsible change**

The verse does not prohibit beneficial medicine, cultivation, or adaptation. It warns against changes rooted in idolatry, cruelty, vanity, or a desire to reject the Creator’s moral order. Wisdom asks what is being changed, why, who bears the harm, and whether the act expresses stewardship or domination.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:120

> Satan only makes them ˹false˺ promises and deludes them with ˹empty˺ hopes. Truly Satan promises them nothing but delusion.

### Expanded Commentary

**Promises without substance**

Satan makes promises and generates hopes, but the verse calls them delusion. The promise may be immediate pleasure, guaranteed status, effortless repentance, or the belief that consequences apply only to others. Its common feature is that it gives the desire a future without requiring the person to face reality.

This does not mean hope itself is false. Hope in Allah motivates repentance and action; Satanic hope asks a person to wait, rationalize, and consume. The believer distinguishes the two by whether hope produces obedience and repair or excuses delay.

The verse is a psychological warning as much as a theological one. Repeated self-talk can make a destructive path feel inevitable or harmless. Counter it with truthful remembrance, good companions, and concrete habits that make obedience easier than the fantasy of obedience.

**Hope that produces action**

A promise is suspect when it invites a person to postpone every repair while assuring him that consequences will never arrive. Qur’anic hope is different: it directs the person toward forgiveness, truthful effort, and a new pattern. Test hope by its fruit. Does it make obedience more possible, or merely make delay more comfortable?

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:121

> It is they who will have Hell as their home, and they will find no escape from it!

### Expanded Commentary

**The end of delusion**

Those who follow Satan have Hell as their home and no escape. The language answers the false promises of the preceding verses. Satan offered a path that appeared open and liberating, but it ends in confinement. His “guardian” cannot rescue the people who trusted him because he never possessed the authority he pretended to offer.

The warning is not a reason to imagine that every sinner is already condemned. It describes the final home of those who persist in the satanic orientation and die without returning. The living reader is addressed before the door closes: abandon the false promise, repair what can be repaired, and seek the One whose mercy is real.

**The false guardian’s end**

Satan’s promises are exposed by their destination. A person who has followed every appetite may discover that the freedom promised by rebellion has become captivity. The warning is directed to the living, who can still replace a false guardian with worship, truthful companionship, and repentance.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:122

> And those who believe and do good, We will soon admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. Allah’s promise is ˹always˺ true. And whose word is more truthful than Allah’s?

### Expanded Commentary

**A promise anchored in truth**

Believers who do good will enter gardens eternally, and Allah’s promise is true. The verse follows the description of Satan’s delusion with a promise that can be trusted. Good action is not pointless effort; it participates in a future that God guarantees.

The rhetorical question about whose word is truer than Allah’s answers the uncertainty created by competing promises. Wealth, security, and status can fail, but divine promise cannot. The believer therefore acts without demanding immediate proof that every sacrifice has paid off.

The phrase “believe and do good” keeps hope connected to conduct. Faith is not an identity badge that excuses injustice, and deeds are not independent from trust in God. Together they form a life directed toward the gardens.

**The certainty of divine promise**

The promise of the gardens is stronger than any promise made by status or wealth. It does not remove work from the believer; it gives work a reliable horizon. When a good act appears to produce no worldly return, the believer remembers that Allah’s word is not measured by immediate visibility.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:123

> ˹Divine grace is˺ neither by your wishes nor those of the People of the Book! Whoever commits evil will be rewarded accordingly, and they will find no protector or helper besides Allah.

### Expanded Commentary

**Salvation is not a slogan of belonging**

Neither the wishes of Muslims nor those of the People of the Book determine divine grace. The verse rejects the claim that a community name automatically guarantees safety. Whoever commits evil will be recompensed accordingly and will find no protector besides Allah.

The statement is not denial of mercy or intercession; it is denial of entitlement. No group can bargain with God by saying its ancestry, label, or religious history exempts its members from accountability. The verse should make believers more humble, not more eager to condemn others.

Justice and grace remain together. Evil has a consequence, while repentance and divine pardon remain possible before death. The person who relies on a name is exposed; the person who combines faith, repair, and hope has a sounder relationship to mercy.

**No community owns forgiveness**

The verse makes religious identity a beginning of responsibility, not a substitute for it. A Muslim cannot demand safety merely by name, and a People of the Book cannot rely on ancestry. The question is how each person responds to truth, wrongdoing, repentance, and the rights of others.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:124

> But those who do good— whether male or female—and have faith will enter Paradise and will never be wronged ˹even as much as˺ the speck on a date stone.

### Expanded Commentary

**No gendered exclusion from reward**

The verse places male and female believers side by side: whoever does good and has faith enters Paradise. In a chapter dealing with women’s legal rights, this affirmation is especially important. Spiritual reward does not depend on being male, and women’s good deeds are not merely counted through the men of their families.

The promise that no one is wronged even by the speck of a date stone echoes the chapter’s insistence on precise justice. A quiet act of worship, care, courage, or patience is fully known. The believer should not measure worth by public recognition or by the scale of worldly power.

The verse also prevents a false opposition between faith and good work. Faith gives the action its orientation; good action gives faith public and practical form. Both men and women are addressed as responsible servants.

**Equal accountability and reward**

The parallel mention of men and women also guards against measuring a woman’s piety only through a male relative. Her faith and good work stand before Allah directly. The smallness of an action in public eyes is no reason to neglect it when the divine scale does not omit even a particle.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:125

> And who is better in faith than those who ˹fully˺ submit themselves to Allah, do good, and follow the Way of Abraham, the upright? Allah chose Abraham as a close friend.

### Expanded Commentary

**The best faith has an embodied direction**

The verse asks who is better in faith than one who submits himself to Allah, does good, and follows Abraham’s upright way. Submission is not passive resignation; it is turning the self toward God in worship, ethics, and action. The person does good because the submission has reached conduct.

**Abraham as the model of integrity**

Abraham’s *ḥanīf* path is a turning away from idolatry and inherited falsehood toward pure devotion. He combined theological clarity with hospitality, sacrifice, migration, and courage. Following him does not mean copying every historical circumstance; it means sharing his orientation: truth before custom and God before social pressure.

Allah chose Abraham as a close friend, an honour associated in the tradition with extraordinary sincerity. The believer should read the title as an invitation to intimacy with God through loyalty, not as permission to claim spiritual rank. The verse offers a complete definition of faith: surrender, beneficence, and upright monotheism.

**The Abrahamic pattern**

Abraham’s uprightness combines intellectual rejection of false gods with practical hospitality and sacrifice. Following him therefore cannot mean making monotheism a theory while neglecting the stranger, the hungry, or the displaced. Submission is tested in the ordinary acts by which a person makes room for another.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:126

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is Fully Aware of everything.

### Expanded Commentary

**God’s ownership encompasses every realm**

Everything in the heavens and earth belongs to Allah. The statement follows the praise of Abraham and prevents any lineage from becoming a rival source of authority. Prophets, scholars, families, and rulers are honoured only as servants within the divine kingdom.

Allah is fully aware of everything, including what ownership conceals: the origin of wealth, the use of authority, and the motive behind a charitable act. A person may own property legally while remaining morally accountable for how it was acquired and spent. A ruler may govern temporarily while being answerable for the people placed under his care.

The verse’s universality also creates a foundation for environmental and social responsibility. Creation is not an ownerless warehouse. Humans hold it under God’s knowledge and must not treat other creatures, land, or people as disposable.

**Stewardship under ownership**

Human ownership is real in law but dependent in theology. The owner must ask how property was acquired, whether a right was hidden, and what obligations accompany possession. Seeing everything as belonging to Allah makes stewardship more careful without making lawful work or personal responsibility meaningless.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:127

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding women. Say, “It is Allah Who instructs you regarding them. Instruction has ˹already˺ been revealed in the Book concerning the orphan women you deprive of their due rights but still wish to marry, also helpless children, as well as standing up for orphans’ rights. And whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah.”

### Expanded Commentary

**The Prophet is asked again about women**

The answer returns to the orphan women and vulnerable children at the centre of the opening legislation. People ask about women, and revelation says that Allah gives the instruction. The repetition shows that the earlier rules did not exhaust the problem: guardians continued to need correction about dowry, marriage, inheritance, and the protection of those unable to defend themselves.

The verse specifically mentions orphan girls whose due rights are withheld while guardians still desire to marry them. This exposes the conflict of interest. A guardian may want the girl as a wife precisely because control of her property makes the arrangement profitable. Revelation separates care from desire and demands that her legal rights be honoured.

The command to stand up for orphans’ rights broadens the duty beyond avoiding theft. It calls for advocacy, fair valuation, education, and protection from coercion. Whatever good people do is known to Allah, including work that receives no public thanks. The verse makes the treatment of vulnerable women a revealed test of goodness.

**Advocacy for orphan girls**

The chapter returns to the orphan because legal reform must be defended after the first command is heard. A girl’s guardian may know the rule and still seek a profitable marriage. Advocacy requires independent voices, access to information, and people willing to challenge a respected guardian when the girl’s consent or property is threatened.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:128

> If a woman fears indifference or neglect from her husband, there is no blame on either of them if they seek ˹fair˺ settlement, which is best. Humans are ever inclined to selfishness. But if you are gracious and mindful ˹of Allah˺, surely Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

### Expanded Commentary

**Settlement in the face of marital neglect**

If a woman fears indifference or neglect from her husband, the verse allows the spouses to seek a fair settlement. The problem may involve maintenance, time, companionship, or the emotional abandonment that leaves a wife suspended between marriage and freedom. A negotiated arrangement can preserve dignity when both parties choose it without coercion.

**Selfishness is a human tendency**

The verse acknowledges that each spouse may want the full benefit of marriage while giving less. This realism prevents the law from assuming that goodwill will automatically overcome conflict. Settlement must therefore be explicit, fair, and monitored against pressure. A wife should not be forced to surrender rights merely because the husband is powerful or because relatives fear social embarrassment.

“Being gracious and mindful” includes generosity beyond the minimum, truthful disclosure, and awareness that Allah sees what a private agreement hides. The verse’s preference for settlement is not a command to preserve every marriage at any price. If negotiation perpetuates abuse, justice may require separation and protection.

**Settlement must be fair**

A settlement is not fair merely because it is signed. Its fairness depends on information, freedom from fear, and preservation of basic rights. Counselors should ask what the woman would choose if she had secure housing and independent advice. Generosity is valuable, but a concession extracted by insecurity is not generosity.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:129

> You will never be able to maintain ˹emotional˺ justice between your wives—no matter how keen you are. So do not totally incline towards one leaving the other in suspense. And if you do what is right and are mindful ˹of Allah˺, surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

### Expanded Commentary

**Outward equality and inward feeling**

The verse states that complete emotional justice between multiple wives is beyond human capacity, even for one who desires it. Feelings cannot be divided with mathematical precision. The admission is realistic, but it is not permission to abandon outward justice.

The warning not to incline entirely toward one wife so that another is left suspended demands equal provision, time, housing, and recognized marital rights. A husband cannot defend deliberate neglect by saying that love is involuntary. The law controls what can be controlled and asks mercy regarding what cannot.

The verse is also a reason for caution before entering a plural marriage. If the person cannot maintain the practical balance, he must not use the impossibility of perfect feeling as an excuse. The closing promise of forgiveness and mercy encourages those who notice their failures to correct them rather than become defensive.

**The practical limit of plurality**

The admission that emotional equality is impossible should make a person more cautious, not more confident. Control the outward harms that feeling can produce, seek counsel before neglect becomes habitual, and do not leave a wife suspended. The verse’s mercy is available to the person who notices bias and works to correct it.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:130

> But if they choose to separate, Allah will enrich both of them from His bounties. And Allah is Ever-Bountiful, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**Separation can be a form of mercy**

If the spouses choose to separate, Allah will enrich both from His bounty. The verse refuses the idea that divorce necessarily leaves one party ruined or that a marriage must be prolonged because poverty makes leaving frightening. God can provide new support, companionship, stability, or spiritual healing.

The promise does not make separation casual. Rights, waiting periods, child welfare, debts, and housing must be handled justly. Nor does it guarantee a particular worldly outcome. It gives courage to leave an arrangement that has become a site of sustained harm and discourages the fear that no future is possible.

Allah is Ever-Bountiful and All-Wise. The first name inspires hope; the second prevents reckless assumptions. Seek counsel, protect the vulnerable, and separate with dignity when reconciliation has genuinely failed.

**A future after separation**

The promise of enrichment protects people from the fear that an unhappy marriage is their only source of survival. Families should help separated spouses with housing, employment, childcare, and emotional support. Trust in divine provision does not excuse society from building the means through which provision arrives.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:131

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Indeed, We have commanded those given the Scripture before you, as well as you, to be mindful of Allah. But if you disobey, then ˹know that˺ to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.

### Expanded Commentary

**One command across revelations**

Everything in the heavens and earth belongs to Allah, and all communities are commanded to be mindful of Him. The verse places Muslims in continuity with earlier People of the Book: divine ownership and moral accountability are not new inventions of one nation.

**Disobedience does not diminish God**

If people disobey, Allah remains the owner. His sovereignty does not depend on human compliance, and disobedience cannot make the universe ownerless. The statement can free the believer from panic about the success of falsehood while warning against thinking that divine patience means approval.

Allah is Self-Sufficient and Praiseworthy. Worship benefits the servant; God does not need the servant’s obedience. This is why the command is simultaneously serious and merciful: it directs people toward the good that their Lord knows they need.

**Mindfulness across communities**

The command to earlier scripture communities and to Muslims establishes a shared moral vocabulary. Different revelations and legal histories do not make God’s ownership local. Each community should therefore examine whether its claim to divine service produces humility and justice rather than entitlement.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:132

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is sufficient as a Trustee of Affairs.

### Expanded Commentary

**Sufficiency in stewardship**

The repeated declaration of divine ownership leads to Allah as a sufficient Trustee. A trustee manages affairs with knowledge and care, while the servant remains responsible for acting within the trust. Reliance therefore does not mean abandoning work, planning, or legal rights. It means refusing to imagine that the final outcome rests entirely on one’s limited control.

This is particularly important in family disputes, inheritance, and political uncertainty. A person should use lawful means to protect a claim and seek a just decision, then entrust what lies beyond power to Allah. Trust can make action calmer and less manipulative.

**Trusting the Trustee**

A person who entrusts outcomes to Allah can still document a debt, consult a lawyer, or protect a child’s property. These means are not rivals to reliance. They are forms of responsible action carried out by a servant who knows that success, protection, and final judgment remain beyond personal control.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:133

> If it is His Will, He can remove you altogether, O humanity, and replace you with others. And Allah is Most Capable to do so.

### Expanded Commentary

**No community is indispensable**

If Allah wills, He can remove a people and replace them with others. The warning addresses collective pride: a community that imagines its religious status, history, or power makes it immune to correction is mistaken. Divine service is an honour that can be lost through betrayal.

The verse is not an invitation to despair about the survival of a community. It is a call to humility and renewal. Institutions should ask whether they are fulfilling the trust or merely demanding loyalty to their name. New people may be raised to carry truth when the old refuse it.

Allah is capable of replacement, but human beings remain responsible for repentance now. The best response is gratitude, reform, and service rather than anxiety about being indispensable.

**Renewal instead of complacency**

The warning of replacement should make institutions welcome sincere correction. A community that treats every critique as betrayal may force the next generation to carry its trust elsewhere. Continuity is preserved not by refusing change but by returning continually to the principles that make the community worthy of being preserved.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:134

> Whoever desires the reward of this world, then ˹let them know that˺ with Allah are the rewards of this world and the Hereafter. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.

### Expanded Commentary

**Two kinds of reward are with Allah**

Whoever desires the reward of this world should know that Allah possesses both worldly and Hereafter reward. The verse does not condemn lawful worldly benefit. It corrects the narrowness of wanting only immediate return. Provision, success, health, and honour are gifts within God’s power, but they are not the final measure of a good life.

A person may perform religion for status and still fail to seek the Hereafter. Another may work for lawful livelihood while intending to support family and serve society, turning worldly effort into worship. Allah hears the intention and sees the deed, so outward similarity does not mean equal value.

The verse encourages a wider request: ask for good in this world and the next, without making either a rival to God. The believer uses worldly means but keeps the lasting reward as the governing horizon.

**Ask for both worlds wisely**

The verse does not require a false choice between livelihood and the Hereafter. A person can seek work, health, and family stability while intending that these goods support worship and service. The danger is allowing immediate reward to become the only reward one can imagine, because that narrows both prayer and moral judgment.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:135

> O believers! Stand firm for justice as witnesses for Allah even if it is against yourselves, your parents, or close relatives. Be they rich or poor, Allah is best to ensure their interests. So do not let your desires cause you to deviate ˹from justice˺. If you distort the testimony or refuse to give it, then ˹know that˺ Allah is certainly All-Aware of what you do.

### Expanded Commentary

**Justice even against the self**

This is one of the Qur’an’s strongest commands concerning testimony. Believers must stand firmly for justice as witnesses for Allah, even when the truth harms themselves, parents, or close relatives. Loyalty to family, tribe, or personal advantage cannot override the claim of justice.

**Rich and poor before the law**

The verse prevents both forms of bias: favouring the rich because their interests appear important and favouring the poor merely because poverty evokes sympathy. Allah is better able to ensure their interests than the witness is. Justice is not a charitable mood; it is accurate treatment of the person’s actual right.

Desire can distort testimony through fear, love, resentment, or the wish to avoid inconvenience. The warning against twisting words or refusing testimony covers both falsification and silent evasion. Courts, workplaces, family mediations, and public reporting all require this discipline.

Allah is aware of what the witness does. A truthful statement may be costly in the moment, but it prevents the greater harm of teaching society that truth belongs to whoever has the stronger relationship or wallet.

**Testimony before family loyalty**

A relative’s poverty may call for support, but it does not justify changing the facts. A wealthy relative may need protection from false accusation, but wealth does not make his version true. The witness who keeps these distinctions honours both compassion and justice, rather than sacrificing one to the other.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:136

> O believers! Have faith in Allah, His Messenger, the Book He has revealed to His Messenger, and the Scriptures He revealed before. Indeed, whoever denies Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, and the Last Day has clearly gone far astray.

### Expanded Commentary

**Faith is an integrated whole**

Believers are commanded to have faith in Allah, the Messenger, the Qur’an, and the scriptures revealed before it. The call is not addressed only to outsiders; people who already say they believe are reminded to make faith complete and active.

The verse lists Allah, angels, Books, messengers, and the Last Day. Denying one essential element fragments the revealed worldview. A person cannot claim respect for God while rejecting His messengers, or claim loyalty to Muhammad while dismissing the earlier prophets whom the Qur’an confirms.

The warning against far straying is not a demand for superficial uniformity. It requires informed commitment, honest questions, and acceptance of what God has revealed. The believer studies the tradition as a connected chain rather than selecting only the parts that serve a prior ideology.

**Faith and intellectual wholeness**

The list of articles of faith keeps believers from reducing religion to ethics without revelation or doctrine without ethics. Angels, Books, messengers, and the Last Day make human life part of a larger unseen order. Denial of one essential element can distort how a person understands responsibility, history, and hope.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:137

### Expanded Commentary

**Reversal as a settled pattern**

The verse describes people who believe, disbelieve, believe again, and then increase in disbelief. The sequence is not a condemnation of sincere struggle or a person who seeks answers and returns honestly. It describes repeated manipulation of faith—using belief when useful, abandoning it when inconvenient, and allowing each reversal to harden into greater denial.

Allah neither forgives nor guides them in this state because they have made instability a strategy rather than a struggle. The wording should make believers cautious about treating sacred commitment as a political costume. Faith may grow through questions, but it cannot survive deliberate cynicism forever.

The final judgment of a particular person remains with Allah, who knows the last state and the inward cause. The practical warning is to return quickly whenever doubt or sin appears instead of feeding it until return becomes unwanted.

**Doubt is not hypocrisy by itself**

A sincere believer may struggle, ask questions, and return. The condemned pattern is calculated alternation that uses belief as a tool and treats truth as disposable. Teachers should create spaces for honest questions so that uncertainty can move toward knowledge instead of being driven into concealment and resentment.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:138

### Expanded Commentary

**A painful “good news”**

The command to give hypocrites good news of painful punishment is an ironic reversal. They used pleasant words and religious appearance to conceal betrayal; revelation announces the consequence beneath the appearance. Irony reveals that worldly comfort was never the true news.

Hypocrisy in the chapter is a public danger because it exploits the trust reserved for believers. Yet the verse should not become a weapon for ordinary people to label opponents. The warning concerns a settled pattern of contradiction and treachery known in its full reality to Allah. Communities should address observable acts and leave ultimate hearts to God.

The phrase also comforts those who suffered from manipulation. Hidden betrayal is not hidden from the final judge, and the person who performed sincerity as a costume cannot rely on the costume forever.

**Irony exposes false comfort**

The “good news” of punishment overturns the hypocrite’s expectation that outward respectability will always protect him. It also reminds victims that a polished public image is not the final account. Communities should resist both gullibility and obsession with exposing every person; truthful evidence and fair procedure remain necessary.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:139

### Expanded Commentary

**Seeking honour through the wrong alliance**

The hypocrites take disbelievers as allies instead of believers and seek honour through that connection. The question exposes a mistaken calculation: power borrowed from hostile forces may bring temporary status, but true honour belongs to Allah. A person who abandons justice to be accepted by the powerful has traded dignity for permission.

Alliance here concerns loyalty and support in a conflict, not ordinary commerce, neighbourliness, or kindness. The Qur’an’s own treaty provisions make that distinction essential. Muslims may cooperate in good and justice with people of different faiths while refusing to support persecution of believers or anyone else.

The verse’s practical cure is to seek honour through obedience, service, and truth. Status gained by flattering oppression is fragile; honour granted by God does not depend on the approval of those one betrayed.

**Honour cannot be borrowed from oppression**

A person who gains access by flattering an unjust power may receive a title while losing moral independence. The verse asks where honour actually comes from. Seeking Allah’s approval allows believers to cooperate wisely without selling the vulnerable person or principle that the alliance was supposed to protect.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:140

> He has already revealed to you in the Book that when you hear Allah’s revelations being denied or ridiculed, then do not sit in that company unless they engage in a different topic, or else you will be like them. Surely Allah will gather the hypocrites and disbelievers all together in Hell.

### Expanded Commentary

**Do not normalize ridicule of revelation**

The verse commands believers not to sit with people who deny or ridicule Allah’s revelations unless they change the subject. Remaining in such a gathering without protest can communicate approval and gradually dull the heart. Association becomes morally relevant when it helps falsehood continue.

**Exit and conversation**

The instruction does not ban every encounter with a critic or prevent respectful discussion. It distinguishes dialogue aimed at understanding from a setting in which sacred things are mocked for entertainment and believers are expected to laugh along. If the topic changes, the situation changes; boundaries need not become permanent hostility.

The warning that one could become like them concerns participation and acceptance, not accidental proximity. The believer protects the tongue and heart while remembering that people may still repent. The final gathering of hypocrites and disbelievers in Hell shows the seriousness of choosing companionship that normalizes contempt.

**Boundaries around ridicule**

A believer may leave a conversation without hating everyone present. The boundary protects the heart from normalizing contempt while leaving open the possibility of a serious, respectful discussion later. The verse thus combines moral clarity with social composure rather than demanding constant confrontation.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:141

### Expanded Commentary

**The two-sided speech of opportunism**

The hypocrites wait for the outcome, then claim loyalty to whoever appears victorious. To the believers they say they were on their side; to the disbelievers they claim they protected them. Their language has no centre beyond personal survival. They treat relationships as wagers and suffering as a price others must pay.

**Judgment belongs to Allah**

The final judgment will expose both claims. A person may persuade two audiences in the moment, but cannot make contradictory loyalties true before God. The verse therefore comforts those who were betrayed by people who later claimed credit for their work and warns the opportunist that history’s temporary ambiguity is not divine ambiguity.

The statement that Allah will never grant disbelievers a way over believers is read by scholars in light of the Qur’an’s command to justice and the realities of worldly defeat. It affirms that falsehood has no legitimate final authority over faith, not that believers are guaranteed permanent political dominance regardless of their conduct. When believers betray justice, they can suffer worldly defeat; Allah’s final authority remains.

**The politics of taking credit**

Opportunists often rewrite their role after an outcome is known. Good governance keeps records, honours actual service, and does not reward contradictory claims simply because the claimant is loud. Allah’s judgment offers the final correction, but earthly institutions should still resist rewarding duplicity.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:142

> Surely the hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but He outwits them. When they stand up for prayer, they do it half-heartedly only to be seen by people—hardly remembering Allah at all.

### Expanded Commentary

**The illusion of deceiving God**

The hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but He turns their deception against them. They may deceive people because people see the outward act; God knows the inward refusal. The “outwitting” is divine justice: the space they use for performance becomes evidence of their insincerity.

**Prayer as performance**

They stand for prayer lazily, seeking to be seen and remembering Allah little. The problem is not physical tiredness or a moment of distraction; it is making public appearance the purpose of worship. A person may pray with an imperfect heart and still strive sincerely. Hypocrisy turns the imperfect effort into deliberate theatre.

The verse invites an audit of intention. Would the act continue if no one praised it? Is the delay caused by fatigue or by resentment of worship? The cure is private prayer, honest repentance, and remembrance that returns the heart to God rather than to the audience.

**Private worship repairs public performance**

A person who fears that prayer has become performance can protect some acts from public view, slow down the recitation, and remember the meaning of standing before Allah. Imperfect concentration is not the same as hypocrisy. The danger is making visibility the purpose and remembrance an inconvenience.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:143

> Torn between belief and disbelief—belonging neither to these ˹believers˺ nor those ˹disbelievers˺. And whoever Allah leaves to stray, you will never find for them a way.

### Expanded Commentary

**Suspended between two loyalties**

The hypocrites are torn between belief and disbelief and belong fully to neither. The image is not praise for moderation; it is a portrait of indecision used as a strategy. They want the protection of believers and the influence of disbelievers without accepting the obligations of either commitment.

A person can need time to learn, and a sincere seeker’s uncertainty is not the same as calculated hypocrisy. The verse addresses those who cultivate ambiguity so that every side can be exploited. Eventually the lack of a true direction becomes a direction of its own.

When Allah leaves such a person to stray, no path is found. This is abandonment after repeated choice, not arbitrary prevention of guidance. The remedy while choice remains is decisive truthfulness: acknowledge doubt honestly, seek knowledge, and stop promising allegiance one does not intend to honour.

**Choose a direction**

A person who is unsure should seek knowledge rather than cultivate ambiguity for advantage. Communities can help by making honest conversion, repentance, and questioning possible. The verse warns against the calculated middle position that refuses every obligation while demanding every protection.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:144

### Expanded Commentary

**Do not exchange the community for power**

Believers are told not to take disbelievers as allies instead of believers and are asked whether they wish to give Allah a clear case against themselves. The prohibition concerns political and military loyalty that harms believers, not ordinary relations, commerce, justice, or cooperation in good. The Qur’an’s treaty verses prove that ethical dealings with non-Muslims remain possible.

**“Proof against yourselves”**

A person who uses his authority to strengthen those committed to persecuting believers supplies evidence of betrayal through his own choice. The phrase does not mean Allah needs proof; it means the deed makes the case manifest. Hidden intention becomes visible in the alliance’s consequences.

The verse asks believers to seek honour through principled relationships. Cooperation should be judged by justice and harm, not by tribal hostility or naïve trust. Loyalty to the believing community includes protecting its vulnerable members, while faith also requires fairness toward everyone.

**Alliance and ordinary neighbourliness**

The command does not prevent Muslims from cooperating with non-Muslims in justice, medicine, trade, or public welfare. It prevents a loyalty that strengthens persecution of believers or makes falsehood the community’s protector. Distinguishing these categories is necessary to obey the verse without contradicting the chapter’s treaty ethic.

**The lowest depth and the logic of hypocrisy**

The hypocrites are warned of the lowest depths of the Fire, with no helper. Their position is especially severe because hypocrisy corrupts the inside of the community while borrowing the outward protection of faith. It combines denial with betrayal and makes sacred language an instrument of harm.

Yet the verse should not license public accusations based on mood; its final diagnosis belongs to Allah, while communities deal with observable deception through fair procedures.

**The depth of hidden betrayal**

Hypocrisy is placed in the lowest depth because it injures the community from within and makes sincere trust harder for everyone. ## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:146

### Expanded Commentary

**The door out of hypocrisy**

The exception lists repentance, reform, holding fast to Allah, and sincere devotion. Repentance alone is not presented as a sentence; conduct must be mended. A hypocrite who harmed a community must repair trust, return rights, and stop the double dealing.

Holding fast to Allah means replacing dependence on shifting patrons with steadfast worship and obedience. Sincerity makes the external act and internal purpose converge. Such people are with the believers, not merely tolerated at the edge. The verse therefore refuses fatalism: even a grave pattern can be abandoned while life remains.

The great reward promised to believers belongs to those whose faith becomes reliable. Community protection and mercy work together—do not be naïve about betrayal, but do not tell a repentant person that return is impossible.

**Repentance must alter relationships**

A hypocrite who returns should not only feel regret; he should stop manipulating, return property, correct false reports, and accept reasonable limits while trust is rebuilt. The verse’s welcome to the believers is therefore neither naïve nor vindictive. It joins accountability to the possibility of belonging again.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:147

### Expanded Commentary

**Why would God punish gratitude?**

Gratitude is more than saying praise. It acknowledges the Giver, uses blessings responsibly, and does not convert provision into a weapon against others. Faith is more than affiliation; it becomes trust, obedience, and return after error. When these are present, the believer has entered the path of mercy.

The verse consoles people who fear that their small efforts are unseen. Allah appreciates them fully, while His knowledge also sees the hidden obstacle or the quiet act that humans overlook.

**Gratitude is a reason for hope**

Allah’s appreciation means that a servant need not compete with others to be accepted. Notice the blessing, use it responsibly, and remain faithful in difficulty. Punishment is not the divine default toward the grateful person; the verse invites a life in which gratitude becomes the atmosphere of obedience.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:148

> Allah does not like negative thoughts to be voiced—except by those who have been wronged. Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

### Expanded Commentary

**The exception for the wronged**

Allah does not like negative speech to be voiced, except by one who has been wronged. The rule restrains slander, gossip, public shaming, and the pleasure of narrating another’s faults. The exception protects the victim’s right to complain, seek justice, warn others from harm, and describe the wrong accurately.

The exception is not unlimited permission to insult or exaggerate. A complaint should remain proportionate and directed toward remedy rather than revenge. A victim may tell a judge, counselor, trusted adviser, or relevant community authority what happened; broadcasting details to entertain an audience is a different act.

Allah hears the speech and knows the truth. This protects the silenced victim and restrains the false accuser. It also teaches listeners to distinguish a person seeking safety from a person seeking the pleasure of exposure.

**Complaint and gossip are different**

A victim may name a wrong to obtain safety or justice, while a listener who spreads the story for entertainment commits another harm. The exception should be applied with care: tell enough to protect and remedy, not more than necessary to punish a person socially after the legitimate purpose has ended.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:149

> Whether you reveal or conceal a good or pardon an evil—surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, Most Capable.

### Expanded Commentary

**Goodness may be public or hidden**

Whether a person reveals or conceals a good deed, Allah knows it. Public good can encourage others, support accountability, and mobilize resources; hidden good protects sincerity and the beneficiary’s dignity. The verse does not impose one form on every act.

**Pardoning an evil**

Pardoning wrong can mean refusing retaliation, accepting an apology, or leaving a matter to God after pursuing a legitimate remedy. It does not require a victim to remain in danger or to restore trust without evidence of change. Forgiveness is a virtue, not a command to enable abuse.

Allah is Ever-Pardoning and Capable. The believer imitates pardon while remembering that God alone has complete power to judge and restore. A person who cannot yet forgive may still refuse injustice and seek lawful protection; the verse invites generous strength, not emotional dishonesty.

**Pardon without denial**

A person can forgive inwardly while still requiring a boundary, repayment, or legal consequence. Concealing a good deed can protect sincerity, while revealing it can mobilize support. The verse gives room for both, provided the heart seeks Allah rather than applause or revenge.

**Rejecting some messengers rejects the message**

Receive revelation as a chain from one God, and let the final messenger complete rather than compete with the earlier messengers.

**Respect across the prophetic family**

Belief in all messengers gives Muslims a basis for religious respect without pretending that all theological claims are identical. The Mount raised over them, the covenant, and the compelling proof given to Moses show that revelation comes with authority and mercy. **Evidence and the will to obey**

Not every request for evidence is bad faith, but a person should notice when new demands appear immediately after an answer to the old one. Signs do not help a heart determined to keep an idol. The wise response is to study the evidence already provided and ask what obedience it requires.

**The weight of a covenant**

**Hardening begins with repetition**

The listed violations show why believers should repent at the first sign of resistance. A person who repeatedly rejects correction may eventually interpret a closed heart as an unchangeable fact. The Qur’anic mention of righteous exceptions also prevents collective condemnation and keeps justice specific.

**Slander as a weapon against signs**

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:157

> and for boasting, “We killed the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, the messenger of Allah.” But they neither killed nor crucified him—it was only made to appear so. **What the Qur’an denies**

The wording has generated extensive theological discussion. Mainstream Islamic interpretation holds that Jesus was not killed or crucified and that Allah raised him, while details of how the appearance occurred are not specified by the Qur’an. Responsible commentary should distinguish the certainty of the Qur’anic denial from later theories about the precise mechanism, which should not be presented as revelation.

The verse also criticizes confident historical claims built on assumption. The repeated emphasis on doubt and lack of knowledge teaches epistemic restraint. The issue is not settled by repeating a slogan; it is settled by what Allah has revealed, while human reconstructions remain limited.

**Certainty and restraint**

The Qur’an is certain in denying the boast, while human beings are warned not to build elaborate certainty from conjecture. This is a model for scholarship: affirm what revelation says, identify what is debated, and do not make a later theory equal to the text itself. Respectful disagreement is better than invented precision.

**Raising Jesus and divine power**

It also corrects two opposite errors—denying Jesus’s prophetic status and elevating him beyond servanthood. The Qur’anic Jesus is a sign, messenger, and word from God, not a partner in Godhead.

**Honouring Jesus through servanthood**

The Qur’anic correction is not a diminishing of Jesus but a protection of his prophetic dignity. A messenger raised by God is honoured without being worshipped. Muslims should explain this distinction with gratitude for the shared prophetic figure and with language that does not turn doctrinal disagreement into contempt.

**The future recognition of Jesus**

Every person from the People of the Book will believe in Jesus before his death, and Jesus will witness against them on the Day of Judgment. The verse should produce reverence for Jesus and caution against certainty about details not settled by explicit revelation. It also reminds believers that prophets are witnesses to conduct, not ornaments for identity politics.

**Jesus as witness**

A prophet’s testimony is not a token for communal pride. It is evidence against those who changed his message or used his name for power. Believers should ask whether their speech about Jesus, Muhammad, and the other messengers resembles the truth they claim to defend.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:160

> We forbade the Jews certain foods that had been lawful to them for their wrongdoing, and for hindering many from the Way of Allah,

### Expanded Commentary

**Restrictions as consequences of wrongdoing**

The verse says certain foods were forbidden to the Jews because of their wrongdoing and their hindering people from Allah’s way. The Qur’an elsewhere describes some restrictions as punishment for disobedience, not as a statement that the foods were intrinsically evil from creation. Divine law may impose a burden in response to a community’s history while later revelation changes the dispensation.

**Law and moral conduct**

The mention of hindering others from guidance shows that ritual restrictions cannot be separated from public ethics. A community that claims strict observance while blocking truth and exploiting the vulnerable has misunderstood the purpose of law. Conversely, a later easing of a rule is not permission to despise the earlier command; it reflects divine wisdom in different communities.

Muslims should be cautious about using this verse to condemn Jews as a whole. It speaks of wrongdoing among those addressed and is followed by recognition of knowledgeable, faithful people. The proper lesson is self-examination: do our practices draw people toward God or make us obstacles through hypocrisy?

**Punishment and legal history**

The verse encourages readers to distinguish a temporary restriction imposed for wrongdoing from a universal judgment about a people or a food. Legal history contains discipline, mercy, and changed dispensations. Muslims should therefore avoid contempt for earlier communities while taking the warning about obstructing guidance seriously in their own institutions.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:161

> taking interest despite its prohibition, and consuming people’s wealth unjustly. We have prepared for the disbelievers among them a painful punishment.

### Expanded Commentary

**Two economic violations**

The verse continues the list of wrongdoing by naming interest despite prohibition and consuming people’s wealth unjustly. These are not merely technical financial errors. Interest in the Qur’anic sense can turn need into an instrument of compounding extraction, while unjust consumption removes property through fraud, bribery, seizure, and abuse of trust.

The prohibition of *ribā* belongs beside the chapter’s inheritance and orphan laws: wealth must not flow upward simply because one party has more leverage. Islamic jurists distinguish prohibited increase from legitimate trade, investment, rent, and profit-bearing risk, and the exact application of modern instruments requires careful expertise. The moral principle is clear even when a product’s legal classification is complex: do not guarantee gain by transferring all risk to the vulnerable.

The painful punishment is for those who persist in disbelief and wrongdoing. It should motivate reform of markets, not merely fear of a private penalty. A just economy records debt honestly, provides relief to the distressed, and refuses to make poverty a source of endless profit.

**Finance and moral asymmetry**

A profitable contract can still be unjust when one party has no realistic ability to refuse or recover. The prohibition of interest and wrongful consumption asks financial institutions to examine who bears risk, who receives relief, and whether the debt grows while the person’s capacity shrinks. Ethical finance is measured by human consequences, not elegant paperwork.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:162

> But those of them well-grounded in knowledge, the faithful ˹who˺ believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you—˹especially˺ those who establish prayer— and those who pay alms-tax and believe in Allah and the Last Day, to these ˹people˺ We will grant a great reward.

### Expanded Commentary

**Knowledge that produces faith**

The verse distinguishes those well-grounded in knowledge who believe in the Qur’an and earlier revelation, establish prayer, give zakat, and believe in the Last Day. Knowledge is praised when it leads to humility, worship, and justice rather than when it becomes a credential for argument.

The description includes faithful people from the People of the Book. It preserves the Qur’anic distinction between those who reject and those who sincerely receive guidance. Their reward is great because God judges their response to truth, not the stereotype attached to their community.

Prayer, zakat, and belief in the Last Day form a pattern: inward conviction becomes regular worship, social responsibility, and accountability. A scholar who does not care for the poor has not embodied the verse’s knowledge; a generous person who denies God has not completed its faith. The rewards belong to an integrated life.

**Knowledge should feed the poor**

The learned believer in this verse prays, gives zakat, and believes in the Last Day. Learning that never reaches worship or social responsibility remains incomplete. Communities should honour scholarship that clarifies rights, supports the vulnerable, and makes faith more truthful rather than merely more argumentative.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:163

> Indeed, We have sent revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ as We sent revelation to Noah and the prophets after him. We also sent revelation to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants, ˹as well as˺ Jesus, Job, Jonah, Aaron, and Solomon. And to David We gave the Psalms.

### Expanded Commentary

**One stream of revelation**

The Prophet receives revelation as Noah and the prophets after him did. The long list—Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, their descendants, Jesus, Job, Jonah, Aaron, Solomon, and David—places Islam within the history of prophetic guidance. Each prophet is distinct, yet the source is one.

The list includes figures associated with different aspects of faith: Abraham’s surrender, Job’s patience, Jonah’s repentance, Moses’s leadership, David’s scripture, and Jesus’s sign. The Qur’an does not present these as competing gods or unrelated religions. It calls people to recognize the shared call to worship Allah and live justly.

David’s Psalms are specifically mentioned, affirming that revelation took diverse forms. Muslims believe in their divine origin while receiving the Qur’an as the final criterion. Respect for earlier scripture therefore combines reverence with reliance on the final revelation.

**A common prophetic inheritance**

Remembering the long list of prophets can weaken ethnic pride and strengthen reverence for revelation across history. Each messenger entered a particular people and time, yet the call to God was one. The Qur’an’s final message asks Muslims to inherit that universal concern rather than turn prophecy into tribal property.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:164

> There are messengers whose stories We have told you already and others We have not. And to Moses Allah spoke directly.

### Expanded Commentary

**Known and unknown messengers**

Some messengers’ stories have been told and others have not. The Qur’an’s silence about a prophet’s name does not imply that no message reached a people. Divine guidance is wider than the narrative selected for this Book. This should make believers cautious about assuming that their historical map contains every person God sent.

Moses is singled out as one to whom Allah spoke directly, an honour of intimate communication that does not make him divine. Prophetic rank remains servanthood before God. The verse honours particular forms of revelation without turning them into rival sources of worship.

The existence of untold messengers also supports humility in interfaith conversation. Muslims can affirm God’s universal justice without inventing names or stories that revelation has not established.

**Humility about what is not recorded**

The existence of unnamed messengers teaches that absence from our scripture’s narrative is not evidence of absence from divine care. Muslims should avoid inventing details merely to fill gaps. It is enough to affirm God’s justice and honour the messengers He has named.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:165

> ˹All were˺ messengers delivering good news and warnings so humanity should have no excuse before Allah after ˹the coming of˺ the messengers. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**Messengers remove the excuse**

Messengers bring good news and warnings so humanity has no excuse before Allah after their coming. The verse establishes the moral purpose of revelation: it makes the path intelligible, offers hope for obedience, and warns about the consequence of rejection.

The statement does not erase questions of access, distortion, or capacity. Allah’s justice accounts for what a person actually received and understood. It does mean that once a message is clearly conveyed, indifference is not neutrality. People are responsible for their response.

Allah is Almighty and Wise. His power ensures that messengers can fulfil their task; His wisdom ensures that judgment considers circumstances. Believers should imitate this balance when teaching: convey clearly, warn honestly, and leave final judgment to God.

**The fairness of warning**

The messenger’s arrival makes the moral path visible, but divine judgment still considers access, understanding, and capacity. This guards against both excuses after clear truth and harsh verdicts made by people who know little about another’s circumstances. Convey the message and trust the Wise Judge.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:166

> Yet ˹if you are denied, O Prophet,˺ Allah bears witness to what He has sent down to you—He has sent it with His knowledge. The angels too bear witness. And Allah ˹alone˺ is sufficient as a Witness.

### Expanded Commentary

**Divine testimony to revelation**

When people deny the revelation, Allah bears witness that it was sent with His knowledge, and the angels also bear witness. The Prophet’s truth does not depend on the acceptance of his opponents. Divine testimony stands even when human testimony is hostile.

“To send with His knowledge” indicates that the Qur’an is neither accidental inspiration nor a text produced without purpose. It comes within divine wisdom, carrying knowledge of the audience, the future, and the law’s consequences. The angels’ witness adds the unseen dimension to the public signs of revelation.

Allah’s sufficiency as Witness consoles the Prophet and every believer whose truthful claim is rejected. It also restrains believers from thinking they must force recognition. Convey the truth and embody it; God’s witness is not improved by coercion.

**Revelation does not need applause**

When a truthful message is denied, the believer may feel that silence has defeated it. The verse restores perspective: God and the angels bear witness even when public opinion turns away. The messenger’s task is faithful conveyance, not manufacturing universal agreement by force.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:167

> Those who disbelieve and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah have certainly strayed far away.

### Expanded Commentary

**Straying and making others stray**

Those who disbelieve and hinder others from Allah’s way have strayed far away. The verse gives special seriousness to obstruction because it adds social harm to personal rejection. A person can disbelieve privately, but blocking others through persecution, propaganda, intimidation, or corruption spreads the loss.

“Far away” describes both direction and consequence. The person moves away from truth while attempting to move others with him. Yet the warning also encourages believers to remove barriers to guidance through clear teaching, good character, and justice rather than through coercion. A community that behaves unjustly can become an obstacle even while speaking religiously.

The verse therefore asks whether our conduct opens or closes the path. Knowledge, welcome, and truthful representation are forms of calling; hypocrisy and oppression are forms of hindering.

**Do not be an obstacle**

A person can hinder guidance by mocking, persecuting, misrepresenting, or behaving so unjustly that people associate the faith with harm. Calling others to God therefore includes removing barriers created by one’s own character. Truthful speech and good conduct are part of the evidence a community offers.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:168

> Those who disbelieve and wrong themselves—surely Allah will neither forgive them nor guide them to any path

### Expanded Commentary

**Wrongdoing that becomes settled rejection**

Those who disbelieve and wrong themselves will not be forgiven or guided to a path, except the path to Hell. The wording joins doctrinal rejection with self-injury. Denial harms the person who chooses it, while the next verse explains the final destination.

The verse should be read with the many Qur’anic calls to repentance. Its threat describes persistence in disbelief until death, not a living person who turns back. It also must not become a human verdict on individual hearts; Allah knows when a person’s life ended and what was possible before then.

The severe statement emphasizes that guidance is not a game. A person who repeatedly treats truth and the rights of others as disposable can arrive at a state in which the only road left is the consequence of his choices.

**The seriousness of a chosen direction**

The verse’s warning concerns those who persist in disbelief and self-wronging. It should not be used to deny a living person’s capacity for change. Its urgency is precisely that the direction can still be changed now, before persistence turns into the final description of a life.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:169

> except that of Hell, to stay there for ever and ever. And that is easy for Allah.

### Expanded Commentary

**The path of Hell and divine capability**

The path mentioned is Hell, where the persistent deniers remain, and the verse says that this is easy for Allah. The ease does not imply triviality or caprice. It means that no power can prevent the execution of a judgment grounded in perfect knowledge and justice.

The repeated warning of permanence should awaken urgency. It is not intended to make people despair before death or to encourage believers to play judge. It makes clear that the Hereafter is not a temporary inconvenience that can be negotiated through worldly status.

Allah’s capability also supports the oppressed. If earthly institutions fail to punish a persecutor or restore a stolen right, divine justice is not limited by those failures. The believer seeks justice now while trusting the final court.

**Final justice beyond institutions**

The ease with which Allah can execute judgment comforts those who see injustice go unpunished and restrains those who exploit a weak court. It does not excuse believers from building just institutions. The final account is a guarantee that earthly effort matters even when earthly results are incomplete.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:170

> O humanity! The Messenger has certainly come to you with the truth from your Lord, so believe for your own good. But if you disbelieve, then ˹know that˺ to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**A universal invitation to believe**

The Messenger has come to all humanity with truth from the Lord. The address widens beyond the People of the Book and beyond the first community. Faith is offered for the hearer’s own good; God does not need human belief to complete His kingdom.

If people disbelieve, everything still belongs to Allah. The consequence returns to them, not to a deficiency in God. This is why the invitation combines mercy and independence: believe because truth will benefit you, not because God is dependent on your assent.

Allah is All-Knowing and Wise. He knows who receives the message, how it is understood, and how each person responds. The believer carries the invitation with confidence but without coercion, pride, or despair.

**Faith is for the hearer’s benefit**

God’s independence means the invitation is free from divine need. The believer should therefore call others with sincerity, not manipulate them by pretending that God’s honour depends on winning an argument. Truth benefits the person who accepts it by ordering life toward knowledge, mercy, and accountability.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:171

> O People of the Book! Do not go to extremes regarding your faith; say nothing about Allah except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger of Allah and the fulfilment of His Word through Mary and a spirit ˹created by a command˺ from Him. So believe in Allah and His messengers and do not say, “Trinity.” Stop!—for your own good. Allah is only One God. Glory be to Him! He is far above having a son! To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is sufficient as a Trustee of Affairs.

### Expanded Commentary

**Avoiding excess about Jesus**

The People of the Book are told not to go to extremes and to speak only truth about Allah. Jesus is described as the Messiah, son of Mary, messenger of Allah, His Word cast to Mary, and a spirit from Him. Each title honours his unique birth and mission while retaining his created servanthood.

The command not to say “Trinity” is a rejection of a doctrine the Qur’an sees as compromising divine oneness. Muslim theology should state the disagreement accurately and respectfully rather than caricaturing Christian belief. The verse’s central claim is that Allah is one, without son or partner, and that Jesus’s greatness does not require divinity.

Everything belongs to Allah, and He is sufficient as Trustee. The theological correction is therefore connected to reliance: once God is truly one, no created mediator can share His sovereignty. Believers should avoid both exaggerating Jesus and insulting him; the Qur’an does neither.

**Avoiding two opposite extremes**

Jesus should neither be reduced to an ordinary teacher without miracle or revelation nor elevated into God. The Qur’an’s balanced titles preserve both truths. Muslims can state the doctrine firmly while learning the discipline of speaking about another community’s sacred figure without mockery or careless caricature.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:172

> The Messiah would never be too proud to be a servant of Allah, nor would the angels nearest to Allah. Those who are too proud and arrogant to worship Him will be brought before Him all together.

### Expanded Commentary

**Servanthood is the honour of Jesus and the angels**

The Messiah would not be too proud to be a servant of Allah, nor would the angels nearest to Him. Servanthood is not degradation. It is the highest truth about a created being because it places worship, dependence, and dignity in their proper relation to the Creator.

The verse answers the idea that honour requires sharing in divine status. Jesus’s rank as messenger is protected by affirming his worship. The angels, despite their nearness, also remain servants; proximity does not become partnership.

Those who are too proud and arrogant will be gathered before God. The contrast is moral: greatness is not measured by refusing service but by accepting it. In family and political life, people who demand domination imitate the arrogance condemned here. Worship teaches the powerful that they too are accountable servants.

**The dignity of being a servant**

If the Messiah and the nearest angels are not too proud to serve God, no human office can make service beneath a person. A parent, ruler, scholar, or wealthy donor remains a servant. Worship dismantles the fantasy that authority grants exemption from accountability.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:173

> As for those who believe and do good, He will reward them in full and increase them out of His grace. But those who are too proud and arrogant, He will subject them to a painful punishment. And besides Allah they will find no protector or helper.

### Expanded Commentary

**Full reward and additional grace**

Believers who do good will receive their reward in full, with an increase from Allah’s grace. The promise honours effort without reducing reward to a strict wage. God completes what human beings leave unfinished and adds what they could never earn by calculation.

Those who are proud and arrogant receive painful punishment and find no protector. Their arrogance has rejected the very relationship that could save them. The absence of a helper answers the false promise of self-sufficiency: the person who refused dependence on God discovers that created allies cannot replace Him.

The verse joins doctrine and character. Correct belief about Jesus and Allah must produce humility, worship, and good action. Theology that leaves the ego enthroned has not reached the servant’s heart.

**Grace exceeds calculation**

A good act receives its full reward and then more. This should produce generosity rather than a ledger mentality in which a person demands a precise return from God. The proud person, by contrast, loses protection because he has made self-sufficiency his religion. Humility is therefore both a theological truth and a practical safeguard.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:174

### Expanded Commentary

**Evidence and light**

A conclusive proof has come from the Lord, along with a brilliant light. The proof is the Messenger and the revealed evidence he brings; the light is commonly understood as the Qur’an, which illuminates belief, law, and the hidden causes of human corruption.

Light does not remove the need to walk. A person can possess a lamp and refuse to follow its direction. The verse therefore invites engagement: read, understand, and let the revelation expose both public injustice and private self-deception.

The address to humanity keeps the invitation universal. The light is not the property of a tribe or clerical class. Its benefits are shared by whoever believes and follows it.

**Light must be followed**

A brilliant light is useful only when a person allows it to expose the path ahead. Read the Qur’an not simply to collect quotations but to identify the next duty, repair, or belief it illuminates. The evidence is offered to humanity, so no class may monopolize access to its guidance.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:175

### Expanded Commentary

**Holding fast to Allah**

Those who believe in Allah and hold fast to Him are admitted into mercy and grace and guided to the Straight Path. Holding fast means reliance, obedience, and attachment to revealed guidance when pressures pull in other directions. It is active steadfastness, not a feeling of spiritual security.

The order moves from belief to divine mercy, then to guidance. The believer does not guide himself by pride; he clings to God and is led. This creates humility in religious learning and courage in practice. A person may stumble without being lost if he continues to return and hold fast.

The Straight Path is the road that unites pure worship, justice, repentance, and good action. The final promise answers the uncertainty of the chapter’s warnings: the same Lord who exposes betrayal offers a clear route to mercy.

**Holding fast through pressure**

Clinging to Allah includes returning after a fall, seeking knowledgeable counsel, and refusing to let a hostile environment define the truth. Guidance is described as a gift received by those who hold fast, so perseverance and humility belong together. The straight path is walked one faithful decision at a time.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:176

> They ask you ˹for a ruling, O Prophet˺. Say, “Allah gives you a ruling regarding those who die without children or parents.” If a man dies childless and leaves behind a sister, she will inherit one-half of his estate, whereas her brother will inherit all of her estate if she dies childless. If this person leaves behind two sisters, they together will inherit two-thirds of the estate. But if the deceased leaves male and female siblings, a male’s share will be equal to that of two females. Allah makes ˹this˺ clear to you so you do not go astray. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

### Expanded Commentary

**The closing inheritance question**

The sūrah ends by returning to inheritance. The question concerns a person who dies without children or parents, and the answer gives the sister half of the estate; if a childless sister dies, her brother inherits her estate under the stated condition; two sisters share two-thirds; brothers and sisters together follow the ratio of two shares for a male to one for a female. The passage completes the chapter’s movement from the common origin of humanity to the lawful distribution of what remains when a life ends.

**Why the final verse matters**

The closing ruling is not an appendix detached from theology. It demonstrates that divine guidance reaches the moment when family grief, property, and competing claims could easily generate injustice. The chapter began with taqwā, kinship, and the Watcher; it ends by saying that God makes the matter clear so people do not go astray. Clarity is mercy when inheritance can divide a family.

The term describing the childless deceased was debated by early authorities, and the relationship of siblings to parents, children, and residuary heirs must be calculated with the rest of the Qur’anic law and Prophetic explanation. The verse cannot be applied responsibly by looking only at one sentence. A complete family tree, debts, valid bequests, and the rules of exclusion must be examined by qualified experts.

**The dignity of the sister**

The explicit share for a sister is a final rejection of the custom that treated women as absent from succession. The ratio in a particular case is tied to the financial structure of Islamic law, not to a measure of spiritual worth. The sister owns what she inherits; no brother may take it back through pressure or family shame.

Allah’s perfect knowledge closes the sūrah. Human beings see only the immediate grief and the visible asset; God knows every relationship, obligation, and future need. The faithful response is to obey the ordinance, document the division, protect the vulnerable heir, and let the final chapter’s lesson govern the first: kinship is a trust watched by Allah.

**The final protection of a sister’s share**

The last verse sends the reader back to the chapter’s opening concern: kinship must be governed by clear justice. A sister’s share is not a favour from her brother, and a brother’s share does not authorize him to manage hers. The closing command to seek clarity is a final refusal to let family power become a substitute for divine law.

## Closing Synthesis

**Kinship becomes a covenant of protection**

The chapter begins with a single soul and ends with the estate of a person who dies without children or parents. Between those points it follows the routes by which kinship can become either mercy or oppression. A womb can connect people who support one another, but the language of kinship can also be used to demand obedience, conceal a theft, or keep a widow from choosing her future. The Qur’an does not romanticize family merely because family is family. It commands honour, then tests honour by what happens to the dependent person.

That test explains the prominence of orphans. An orphan’s weakness is visible: the child may not understand a contract, challenge a guardian, or protect an inheritance. Yet every adult may become weak through illness, age, displacement, widowhood, poverty, or political persecution. The rules for orphans therefore teach a general law of power. Whoever manages another person’s property, decides another person’s future, or speaks on another person’s behalf must treat that authority as a trust. The greater the asymmetry, the greater the duty of restraint.

Marriage is treated in the same way. The chapter permits affection and lawful desire, but it refuses to let desire define the whole institution. A dowry belongs to the woman; a widow cannot be inherited; a husband cannot use dislike to confiscate property; a wife who fears neglect can seek a fair settlement; and a household in serious conflict can be referred to arbiters. These provisions show that affection is valuable but not sufficient. A marriage is protected by enforceable rights precisely because human feelings change.

The chapter’s repeated appeals to *maʿrūf*, decent and honourable conduct, prevent the law from becoming a collection of loopholes. A person may ask what the minimum rule technically allows, but the Qur’an asks what a decent person would do with the power in hand. The husband who pays the dowry but humiliates his wife has not exhausted his duty. The heir who calculates accurately but pressures a sister to renounce her share has not acted honourably. The mediator who ends an argument by silencing the victim has not achieved reconciliation. Law and character must meet.

**Property is a moral language**

Al-Nisāʾ speaks about wealth because property records relationships. The way a family handles an estate says who is considered real, whose future matters, and whose voice can be ignored. The way an employer pays, a lender structures debt, or an institution manages donations says the same. For this reason the Qur’an places orphan property, dowry, inheritance, trade, usury, and testimony within a single moral field.

The prohibition of unjust consumption is broader than a ban on obvious theft. It includes the sophisticated theft of a person who signs a document he cannot understand, the quiet theft of an heir who is told that custom gives her nothing, the institutional theft of a charity that spends the beneficiary’s funds on prestige, and the moral theft of a false accusation that makes an innocent person lose employment or trust. In each case, the wrongdoer attempts to convert power into ownership. The chapter interrupts that conversion by insisting that rights remain rights even when the rightful person is too weak to enforce them.

The inheritance ordinances are especially resistant to sentimental manipulation. A parent may love one child more, a relative may be more persuasive, or a family may fear that property will leave the clan. None of these emotions changes the revealed entitlement. At the same time, the chapter encourages gifts, kindness, and provision for non-heirs. Fixed shares establish justice; voluntary generosity expresses excellence. Confusing the two creates either coercion or coldness. A person may give from his own share after the legal rights are known, but he may not make generosity a condition for recognizing another person’s right.

The ban on interest belongs to this same structure. A debt may provide help, but a system that guarantees gain to the stronger party while the weaker party absorbs every hardship can turn need into a permanent source of extraction. The Qur’anic economic vision does not deny trade, profit, ownership, or planning. It asks who bears risk, whether consent is informed, and whether a transaction preserves human life rather than consuming it. A precise contract can still be unjust; a profitable exchange can still be a betrayal.

**Authority is answerable**

The chapter’s political teaching begins with returning trusts and judging fairly. Before discussing obedience to authority, it defines authority as a trust. The ruler, judge, commander, guardian, employer, teacher, and family elder are not owners of the people affected by their decisions. Their office exists for a purpose and must return what it holds to those entitled to it.

Obedience to Allah and the Messenger is absolute in principle because it is obedience to the source of truth. Obedience to those in authority is ordered within that higher framework. This protects society from chaos without sanctifying rulers. A responsible community obeys lawful administration, respects expertise, and resolves disagreements through revelation and sound judgment; it does not obey a command to commit injustice merely because it was spoken by a person with a title.

The chapter also disciplines the witness. Justice must be upheld against the self, parents, relatives, rich, and poor. This is harder than condemning obvious enemies because family loyalty and fear of wealth are intimate forces. The witness who distorts facts to protect a parent may feel loving, but has made affection an instrument of injustice. The witness who assumes that poverty proves innocence may feel compassionate, but has replaced evidence with sentiment. Justice is not the absence of feeling; it is the refusal to let feeling falsify the right.

This standard reaches journalism, research, public administration, and social media. A person who circulates an unverified report of danger may expose a community to panic. A person who selects evidence only to protect a faction becomes an advocate for deceit. A person who turns religious language into a tool for humiliating an opponent confuses victory over a person with service to truth. The chapter’s answer is verification, referral to qualified judgment, clear speech, and accountability before Allah.

**Worship does not withdraw from danger**

The fear prayer is one of the chapter’s clearest demonstrations that worship and practical intelligence belong together. The believers pray in shifts, guard one another, retain precautions, and return to regular prayer when security returns. This arrangement refuses two opposite errors. One error treats worship as irrelevant to public danger; the other treats trust in God as a reason to ignore danger. The Qur’an does neither. It makes room for the body, the weapon, the weather, the traveller, and the appointed time of prayer.

The same balance appears in migration and fighting. Believers are asked to help oppressed men, women, and children, but fighting is described as a response to oppression and hostility, not as a religious appetite. Treaties protect those who do not fight. A person who offers peace is not to be harmed. A believer must verify whom he confronts and may not kill another believer intentionally. These limits are not inconveniences added after the noble purpose; they define the noble purpose.

Migration then reveals that preservation of faith may require movement. The person with a real means to leave oppression cannot always call permanent submission patience. But those without a route—children, helpless adults, captives, and the impoverished—are not condemned for remaining. This distinction prevents the strong from preaching courage to people whose options they have never shared. The secure community is asked to make escape possible, not merely to criticize those who cannot escape.

**The final horizon is clear truth**

The chapter’s discussion of the People of the Book carries the same double movement: continuity and correction. The Qur’an names a large family of prophets and affirms that revelation came to communities before Muhammad. It also criticizes the use of scripture to defend power, the denial of clear signs, the slander of Mary, the boast concerning Jesus, and the division of messengers according to preference. The tone is firm because the stakes are theological and social, but the chapter still distinguishes among people and praises those grounded in knowledge and faith.

Jesus is honoured as Messiah, son of Mary, messenger, Word from Allah, and spirit created by His command. The chapter’s insistence that he is a servant protects the central truth of divine unity and also protects Jesus from being absorbed into a competition of political ownership. The angels nearest to Allah are servants as well. Nearness to God is not shared divinity; it is deeper servanthood. This principle returns the powerful human being to humility and prevents the religious leader from receiving the unquestioned obedience owed only to God.

The chapter closes with light and guidance. The proof has come, the light has been sent down, and the person who holds fast to Allah is guided to the Straight Path. This is not a conclusion of despair after all the warnings. It is the chapter’s final invitation: take the clarity seriously, return after sin, protect the vulnerable, submit disputes to truth, and let worship become visible in the way property and power are handled.

**Questions for the reader**

A reader can carry the chapter into daily life through several questions. What do I manage that actually belongs to someone weaker than me? Have I made a family custom more authoritative than a clear right? When I disagree, do I seek the standard that may correct me or the judge who will flatter me? Do my public religious acts continue when no one is watching? Have I turned a blessing into a reason to envy another person? When I speak about a community, a woman, an opponent, or a prophet, am I repeating evidence or using words to injure?

The chapter also asks what kind of authority we are. Do people near us receive a more secure life because of our presence, or do they merely learn to avoid our anger? Do our decisions return trusts to their owners? Do we make repentance possible without denying harm? Can a person who differs from us still find justice in our hands? The answers are not measured only by intention. They appear in accounts, contracts, testimonies, meals, inheritances, borders, and the quiet choices made after a public promise.

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ therefore begins with one soul and ends with one clear duty: recognize the claims of others before Allah. The chapter’s law, warning, history, and promise all converge on that recognition. Whoever remembers the Watchful Lord will not treat a dependent person as property, a spouse as a possession, an heir as an obstacle, a neighbour as a stranger, a treaty as disposable, or revelation as a costume. The chapter’s justice is demanding because its mercy is real, and its final hope remains open to everyone who turns, repairs, and holds fast.

**A note on interpretation and responsibility**

The verses of this chapter have generated a large body of tafsīr and law because they speak to questions where language, circumstance, and human consequence intersect. A responsible reader therefore distinguishes between what the Qur’an states directly, what the Prophet explains, what early authorities report, and what later jurists infer from combining several texts. This distinction is not a way to weaken revelation. It is a way to honour revelation by refusing to put an uncertain human conclusion on the same level as a certain divine statement.

That discipline is particularly important in the passages on marriage, war, slavery, testimony, and the People of the Book. Some terms describe institutions that existed in the first Muslim community; their regulation cannot be understood by pretending the surrounding society did not exist, nor can a historical category be revived without examining the legal and moral conditions that made the original discussion possible. Other terms have enduring principles that must be applied in changed circumstances: consent, the protection of life, fidelity to treaties, the return of trusts, the rejection of slander, and the obligation to judge fairly.

The chapter also teaches a method for reading difficult passages. Begin with the verse’s immediate context. Read the neighbouring verses that limit or explain it. Compare the Qur’an’s other uses of the key term. Consult the Prophetic practice and the legal tradition, noting genuine disagreement rather than hiding it. Ask who is vulnerable in the situation and what harm the rule prevents. Finally, remember that a conclusion which destroys the chapter’s explicit commitments to justice, mercy, truth, and the protection of the weak cannot be accepted merely because it uses a familiar word.

This method guards against two equal and opposite errors. One error makes every inherited interpretation untouchable, even when the interpretation contradicts clear evidence or turns a protection into an abuse. The other error treats personal intuition as sufficient and ignores language, history, and the accumulated work of scholarship. The reader needs humility in both directions: humility before the text and humility about the limits of one’s own first impression.

In this spirit, the commentary has tried to keep theology connected to conduct. Tawḥīd is not only a proposition about God; it limits every human claim to absolute power. Belief in the Last Day is not only a doctrine about time; it requires truthful accounts and makes hidden injustice serious. Prophetic obedience is not only a slogan; it appears in mercy, verification, restraint, and the refusal to harm those who offer peace. Repentance is not only a feeling; it returns property, corrects speech, and changes patterns. The chapter becomes most intelligible when these connections are kept visible.

The final measure remains with Allah, the Watchful, All-Knowing, All-Wise. Human beings can audit a trust, calculate an estate, hear testimony, and establish a court, but they cannot see every motive or know every obstacle. The law asks them to build the best process they can; the theology asks them to leave ultimate judgment to God. That combination makes the believer neither careless nor tyrannical. He acts seriously because rights are real, and he remains humble because knowledge is incomplete.

**A practice of attentive obedience**

Attentive obedience begins before a dispute arrives. Families can write down ownership, dowries, debts, and promises while relationships are calm. Guardians can arrange independent review before an orphan reaches maturity. Spouses can learn the rights of marriage and separation before anger makes learning feel like accusation. Communities can train mediators who understand both the law and the danger of forcing a vulnerable person into silence. Preparation is one of the chapter’s forms of mercy.

Attentive obedience continues in speech. Before forwarding a report, verify it. Before repeating an accusation, ask what proof exists and what harm the repetition will cause. Before invoking Allah in an argument, ask whether the claim is established or whether the divine name is being used to strengthen a preference. Before praising a public act, remember that hidden sincerity matters more than spectacle. The chapter’s warnings about hypocrisy and slander are therefore relevant to ordinary conversation as much as to courts and battlefields.

It also appears in how a community welcomes correction. A person who points out a missing inheritance share, an unsafe household, a false report, or an unjust alliance may be protecting the command rather than attacking the community. Leaders who respond by investigating and repairing show the humility of stewardship. Leaders who respond by silencing the witness may preserve an appearance while deepening the very hypocrisy the chapter condemns.

Finally, attentive obedience leaves room for hope. The chapter contains severe warnings because people’s lives and rights are serious, but it repeatedly names forgiveness, mercy, and return. The sinner who stops, restores, and holds fast is not defined forever by the moment of failure. The community that has inherited a harmful custom can change. The spouse who has lived in neglect can seek a fair settlement. The refugee who leaves oppression can find provision. The reader who recognizes himself in a warning can still answer it before the final gathering.

This is why the chapter’s many rules do not reduce to control. They are a way of making mercy durable. A dowry makes respect tangible; an inheritance share makes kinship fair; a witness makes truth public; a treaty makes peace dependable; a shortened prayer makes worship possible in danger; a path of repentance makes reform imaginable. The form changes according to the problem, but the purpose remains: human beings must not use power to erase the rights of those whom Allah has placed near them.

**The chapter’s enduring measure**

The most demanding reader of al-Nisāʾ is not the person who can quote the greatest number of legal terms but the person who lets the chapter question his arrangements. Are the people who depend on him safer because of his authority? Does his knowledge make him more truthful, or merely more capable of winning? Does his worship make him more attentive to neighbours and relatives? Does his confidence in a religious identity make him more just toward the person outside it? These questions keep the text from being turned into an object that can be admired without being obeyed.

The chapter also teaches that justice has a memory. The orphan remembers through the account that was preserved; the widow through the dowry that was not seized; the daughter through the share that was not denied; the treaty community through the peace that was honoured; the accused through the charge that was not repeated; and the repentant person through the community that allowed repair. Even when people forget, Allah does not. His memory is the ground of both warning and hope.

To read the sūrah well is therefore to move repeatedly between the household and the horizon of resurrection. A hidden financial decision is connected to the final witness. A greeting is connected to the divine reckoning. A dispute in a family is connected to the command to judge fairly. A community’s response to war is connected to the sanctity of life. A statement about Jesus is connected to the prohibition of exaggerating about any created being. The chapter’s unity lies in this movement: worship shapes law, law protects persons, and protected persons become evidence that worship is sincere.

The reader should leave the chapter with fear of injustice, but not with fear that makes action impossible. The Lord who warns about fire also grants concessions, accepts repentance, knows incapacity, and promises provision to the one who leaves oppression. Reverence is not paralysis. It is the alertness that makes a person return a trust, correct a statement, share a right, restrain a hand, and choose the truthful path when no earthly reward is visible.
