# Sūrah al-Jāthiyah (Chapter 45) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary


## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:1

> Ḥâ-Mĩm.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter That Opens With a Sound, Not a Statement**

The first thing a reciter pronounces in this sūrah is not a sentence to be argued over but a pair of letters: ḥāʾ and mīm. Twenty-nine sūrahs of the Qur'an begin in this way, and the community had a name for the group early — al-muqaṭṭaʿāt, the cut-off letters. A book that works hard everywhere to be understood opens seven of its chapters with a sound that no dictionary defines. That belongs to the revelation, not to a reciter's taste. The Book itself sorts such verses into a class of their own, standing beside the precise verses that carry the rulings: “He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive.” (3:7). A scripture holding only what a reader can master at first hearing would be a manual. This one is a sign, and signs include what stops the hand. The reader's first act here is not interpretation but reception.

**The Sixth of the Ḥawāmīm**

Sūrahs forty to forty-six stand together in the order of the Book: Ghāfir, Fuṣṣilat, al-Shūrā, al-Zukhruf, al-Dukhān, al-Jāthiyah, al-Aḥqāf. Six of the seven open with ḥāʾ mīm; al-Shūrā adds three more letters before its formula closes. The tradition calls the group the ḥawāmīm, and the closeness of the family is not only a matter of spelling. These chapters return to the same arguments — the oneness of God against the many gods of the Quraysh, the ruin of nations that dismissed their warners, the defended authority of the Prophet, and a Resurrection that the comfortable found easy to deny. Al-Jāthiyah sits in the middle of the block in tone as well as in position. Its sentences are short, its cases are put quickly, and it does not linger over an image or a story. What a longer sūrah unfolds across twenty verses, this one states once and leaves standing. The letters shared with its neighbours say only that the same Voice is speaking; the argument that follows is this chapter's own.

**Why the Commentators Left the Letters Alone**

Early explanations of these letters fall into three lines, each with a reason behind it. One line reads them as abbreviations of words or names: a contracted reference to God, to the pen, or to something the text does not spell out. Another takes them as oaths, since the Qur'an swears elsewhere by created things and by the Book itself. A third, and the one carried by most of the classical commentaries, holds the meaning to be known to God alone and stops there. The abbreviations require a key that no sound report supplies; the oath reading assumes a grammatical pattern that is absent when the letters stand by themselves. The third line claims nothing that cannot be checked, and it matches the Book's own division of its verses into the precise and the elusive. Of the elusive, the verse continues, the well-grounded in knowledge say only, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” (3:7). Declining to guess is here a position, not a failure of nerve.

**An Alphabet as the Ground of the Challenge**

The letters are the raw material of Arabic speech, which is why they can carry an argument. The Book that uses them issues a standing demand: “And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.” (2:23). Nothing in that challenge withholds a tool. The opponents of the Prophet had the same alphabet, the same metres, the same market of eloquence in which reputations were made and destroyed, and the same language — none of it in short supply. Muslim scholars later built the doctrine of iʿjāz, the inimitability of the Qur'an, on this ground: the demand is total, and the means to meet it are common property. A chapter opening with two letters is therefore not hiding a message; it is laying the materials on the table. What is claimed is not secret knowledge but an effect that no combination of the available parts has produced. The smallest unit of the language stands at the head of the largest claim made for it.

**From Letters to Names to Signs**

The opening line sets a movement that the verses after it complete. Two bare letters appear first; then those letters give way to names — the Almighty, the All-Wise; then the sky and the earth are called signs. Speech grows from its smallest part to its greatest subject in three steps, and it never announces the ladder it is climbing. The same two names return at the chapter's close, where “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” (45:37) seals a chapter that began with ḥāʾ and mīm, so that one pair of words frames everything between them. The subject is placed first on purpose. Before the chapter tells anyone what to believe about the Resurrection, the Reckoning, or the Law, it establishes who is speaking. A reader who takes the letters as noise loses the first rung of that ascent. A reader who treats them as an unexplained sound stands exactly where the chapter wants him: listening to a Speaker whose authority is accepted before a single proposition has been proved.

**Reading a Sign Requires More Than Decoding**

A sign works by pointing, and a reader can hold a pointer without following it. The letters are the purest case of a sign whose whole function is to refer: they are undeniably present in the Book, undeniably recited, and they carry no claim that a translator can move into another language. That makes them a test of the reader's posture rather than his skill. The Qur'an draws attention elsewhere to people who read a text and miss it, who hear recitation and remain untouched, and it never treats this as a shortage of intelligence; it treats it as a decision about what to do with what lies in front of you. Two letters at the head of a chapter teach what the sun in the sky and the body of the reader himself teach equally well. None of these facts forces belief. Each of them, taken seriously, leaves a person facing a decision he can no longer pretend he has postponed.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:2

> The revelation of this Book is from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Names the Sender Before the Content**

This verse makes one claim, without ornament: what the reader holds was sent down, and God sent it. The form is a nominal sentence, a bare declaration of origin. Other chapters open in almost the same words — “The revelation of this Book is from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise.” (39:1) stands at the head of al-Zumar, al-Aḥqāf repeats the sentence word for word (46:2), and Ghāfir opens, “The revelation of this Book is from Allah—the Almighty, All-Knowing,” (40:2). The Qur'an does not always begin a chapter with a claim about itself. When it does, the order carries weight. Nothing is said here about the Resurrection, the angels, the messengers, or the Law; all of that comes later. First the reader is told who is speaking, because a speech is judged by its speaker. The rest of the chapter presses exactly this point. What the deniers reject, in the end, is not a set of propositions but a sender.

**The Almighty: Power Behind What Was Sent Down**

Almighty renders al-ʿAzīz, a name built on the root ʿ-z-z, which carries the sense of strength that cannot be overcome. The Unconquerable is a fair translation in plain words. Its work in this sentence is to answer a question about the sending itself, because a message is only as safe as the guarantee behind it. The man who received this Book in Makkah had no army, no treasury, and no standing among the powerful families that could have protected a document they valued. The claim that its author cannot be defeated tells the reader that the message will not be lost the way a human document is lost: no force can cancel what this speaker wills. The name also closes an obvious escape route for the sceptic — that the speaker is weak, or that other gods have overpowered him. The same name returns at the end of the sūrah, and it appears in other chapters in the same pairing: “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” (59:24).

**The All-Wise: Order in What Was Sent**

Al-Ḥakīm, the All-Wise, comes from a root that covers wisdom and also judgment, the settling of a matter with authority. If power answers whether the message can survive, wisdom answers whether it deserves to. The threats scattered through the sūrah are not gusts of anger; they describe a moral order that its author knows from the inside, down to its ends. That is what ḥikmah means when the Qur'an applies it to God: not cleverness, but complete knowledge of what everything is for. A ruler who had power without wisdom would be a danger, and a counsellor with wisdom but no power would be a bystander. The verse joins the two names in one breath so that neither can be read alone. The Book that teaches the Resurrection is guaranteed by strength, and the strength that guarantees it is directed by a purpose the reader is invited to trust before he can see it.

**Why the Two Names Come in This Order**

Power is named first. The sequence is not accidental, since the Qur'an's own habit is to put the attribute that answers the immediate objection at the front, and the immediate objection in this sūrah concerns authority — who has the right to command. Once that ground is cleared, wisdom explains the content. Truth by itself, spoken by a being of unlimited force, would be coercion rather than guidance; the wisdom of the One who sent the Book is the reason its warnings arrive as speech, addressed to minds that can weigh them, spread across a lifetime of chances to change. Other chapters close with the identical pair, as in al-Ḥashr: “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” (59:24), and in al-Taghābun: “˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and unseen—the Almighty, All-Wise.” (64:18). The Qur'an returns to the combination whenever it wants its readers to hold authority and purpose in the same hand.

**A Book That Speaks About Its Own Descent**

The claim is that the Book came down. Arabic expresses this with the word tanzīl, a form that pictures the process as gradual rather than instantaneous, and the Qur'an uses it for revelation again and again. The sūrah will keep both sides of that process in view: the Book is from Allah, and it is We who recite it to the Prophet — “These are Allah's revelations which We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth.” (45:6). Downward movement and spoken recitation are the two ends of one act. That is why the claim about origin is put where it is, at the start, before the reader hears any of the recitation's demands. He is told first that what follows is not the private wording of a poet or the report of a narrator, and then he is handed the words themselves to judge. A text that asks to be weighed does well to state its provenance in the plainest available sentence.

**What the Verse Does Not Argue**

There is no syllogism here, no appeal to the reader's experience, and no sign pointed at as proof. The verse asserts and stops. That austerity is the point, because a claim of this kind cannot be supported by the same means it supports everything else; if it could be proved by an argument that needed no revelation, the Book would be redundant. The chapter's confidence rests on stating the matter once, plainly, and then living with the consequences for thirty-five more verses. The reader who rejects the sentence is not left with a smaller claim but with a harder problem, since every description of the created order that follows in vv. 3–5 presumes a speaker with authority over what he describes. The argument of the sūrah begins exactly where this verse ends: with a sentence that can be denied in a moment and cannot be overturned by anything the chapter subsequently says or does not say.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:3

> Surely in ˹the creation of˺ the heavens and the earth are signs for the believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The World Is Already Speaking**

The verse hands the reader two vast objects and calls them signs. The word is āyāt, and a sign is anything that points beyond itself: a marker on a road is not interesting for its own sake, only for the destination it names. Applied to the heavens and the earth, the term claims that the created order has no neutral surface. It is addressed to someone. The claim is not that the sky argues, but that it indicates — and that the indication is strong enough to be treated as evidence. The same word, āyāt, is also the standard term for the verses of the Qur'an, and it returns in this short chapter twelve times, moving between the created world and the recited Book as though the two were one argument in two media. A reader who takes the verse seriously finds that he is not looking at an empty sky and then, separately, hearing a message. He is looking at the older of the two books.

**Why Believers Are Named First**

The verse does not say that the sky is a sign for everyone. It names the believers, and the naming is deliberate rather than a compliment. Every such sentence in the Qur'an attaches a description of the reader, and the descriptions vary with the demand each sign makes: the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of day and night are called signs for people of reason (3:190); for people of understanding (2:164); for people who reflect (13:3; 30:21); for those of sound knowledge (30:22); for people who listen (10:67); for those who are mindful (10:6); for people who believe (27:86); for people of sure faith (51:20). The list is not a ranking of IQ. The signs stand there whether or not anyone reads them, so the qualifier tells the reader what kind of attention the reading requires. Belief in this verse is the posture that lets a person admit that the world is talking.

**One Word for the Sky and the Scripture**

Because āyāt covers both the created world and the revealed text, the two are never far apart in this sūrah, and the first three verses already show the pattern: the letters, then the names of God, then the heavens and the earth, and later the recitation itself. The connection is not a poet's flourish. A sign in either domain is defined by the same relation — a created or spoken thing that points to its maker. The Qur'an states the programme in a sentence of its own: “We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth.” (41:53). Two books with one author, is how the Muslim scholarly tradition has often put it. The visible order and the recited word are read together or not at all.

**Observation Is Not Reading**

Nothing in the verse asks the reader to distrust his eyes. Observing the sky closely, counting its seasons, measuring its distances — none of that is denied or discouraged. What the verse denies is that observation finishes the job on its own. A man may know the movement of the stars exactly and still conclude nothing about the one who set them moving, not because the evidence failed him but because he never asked the question the evidence was waiting to answer. The verse therefore works as a demand rather than a report: the creation of the heavens and the earth is not offered as raw material for theorising but as an address that expects a response. The next lines make the expectation concrete: “There are ˹countless˺ signs on earth for those with sure faith, as there are within yourselves. Can you not see?” (51:20–21). The question is put to the eye, and it is answered by the will.

**Faith as the Faculty That Reads**

Believers are named because belief is the capacity in question, the way hearing is the capacity for music. A person without it is not blind to facts; he is deaf to the sense they make together. That is why the verse's qualifier is not a boast about those who hold it. The believers of this verse have seen what anyone standing under the same sky can see, and they have let the sight travel to its conclusion, which is not a feeling but a judgment about how the world came to exist and who is entitled to speak about it. The chapter will later move from the sky to the human being, then to time, rain and wind, then to the recited verses, then to the history of a whole people. The first step is taken with the largest object available, because the argument begins where the reader stands and only afterwards narrows to what is closest to him.

**A Claim That Doubt Cannot Disprove**

The verse does not promise that the evidence will force agreement. It says that the signs are there and that they belong to the believers. This is a stronger claim than it first appears, because the Qur'an argues elsewhere that the signs were not merely placed in the world but made clear for the readers who use them: “This is how We make the signs clear for people who reflect.” (10:24). Clarity, in the Book's usage, is not the same thing as compulsion. A demonstration that left no room for refusal would end the trial that this sūrah keeps in view, the one in which every person is paid back for what he has done. So the signs are decisive for anyone who reads them honestly and avoidable for anyone determined to look past them, and the verse names the second group's absence instead of pretending it does not exist. Another chapter puts the matter without comfort: “Know that Allah revives the earth after its death. We have certainly made the signs clear for you so perhaps you will understand.” (57:17). Clearing and understanding are two acts, not one, and the verse honours both.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:4

> And in your own creation, and whatever living beings He dispersed, are signs for people of sure faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sign You Cannot Step Outside Of**

After the sky and the earth, the verse turns to the reader's own body. This is the one sign that cannot be avoided, packed away, or viewed from a distance. The Qur'an presses it repeatedly: a human being begins as a drop, becomes a clot, then a lump of flesh, is born helpless, reaches his prime, and then declines into the weakness of age. Sūrah Ghāfir states the sequence in one sentence: “He is the One Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He brings you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime, and become old— though some of you ˹may˺ die sooner—reaching an appointed time, so perhaps you may understand ˹Allah's power˺.” (40:67). Every stage is witnessed by the person living through it, and the last stage is witnessed by every person who meets an elderly man. A being that grew by ordered stages is a document of intention.

**Made to Know, Not Merely to Live**

The creation mentioned here is not only flesh. The Qur'an treats the human being as a creature built for understanding, and it links that capacity to a rank: “He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you.” (6:165). The verse before the creation of Adam in al-Baqarah states the same appointment in plainer form, when God announces, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” (2:30). The point is not that the species owns the planet; the point is that a creature able to name things, weigh consequences, and ask where it came from is itself evidence of a designer with intentions for him. The body supports the argument of the previous verse; the mind extends it. Between them, the reader is supplied with a sign inside and outside his skin.

**Whatever Living Beings He Dispersed**

The second half of the verse widens the view to every creature that moves. The Arabic describes beings scattered and spread through the earth, and the Qur'an uses the image of dispersal elsewhere in its account of nature: “the scattering of all kinds of creatures throughout” appears in a list of things that are signs for people of understanding (2:164). What is claimed is not a catalogue but a pattern. Thousands of species share a single planet, fill its niches, and each is fitted to the life it leads; the fit is precise enough that a change in one small feature would end the creature. The verse names them as a crowd rather than a collection of specimens. If the sky shows one kind of order — vast, regular, indifferent to the observer — the animal world shows a second kind, intimate and adjusted, where every structure answers a need. Both are counted as signs, and both are credited to the same sender.

**Certainty Is a Destination, Not a Mood**

The audience here is described as people of sure faith — those who reach firm conviction rather than those who hold an opinion. Certainty in the Qur'an is the end of a process, not a temperament, and the Book treats it as the proper result of attending to the signs it names. The contrast is drawn sharply in this sūrah, which will describe deniers who possess no knowledge and follow nothing but conjecture, and whose descendants will confess on the Day of Judgment that they were never convinced. The difference between the two groups is not intelligence but where they stopped. One group looked at its own hands and at the animals on the road and concluded that such order did not assemble itself; the other looked at the same things and declined to finish the thought. The verse supplies the evidence; the qualifier tells the reader what finishing the thought is called.

**Evidence in Two Directions at Once**

The creation of the human being proves two things at the same time, and the sūrah needs both. It proves a Maker, because the stages of human growth display intention and design. It also proves the possibility of resurrection, because a being who was nothing and then became a person cannot claim that returning him to life is beyond the power that made him the first time. The Qur'an regularly argues the second point from the first, asking its sceptics to consider their own beginnings when they doubt their endings. The verse's phrase about the scattered creatures does the same work: life was handed out across the earth once already, so the objection that the dead cannot be raised has already been answered by the animals in the field. What the reader is asked to do with all of this is not to admire it but to trust the one who arranged it.

**The Scattered Creatures as a Refutation of Accident**

The animals the verse mentions are called a community rather than a set of mechanisms: “All living beings roaming the earth and winged birds soaring in the sky are communities like yourselves. We have left nothing out of the Record.” (6:38). The Book treats that shared standing as a fact with consequences, and it draws attention to the variety contained in it — “And Allah has created from water every living creature. Some of them crawl on their bellies, some walk on two legs, and some walk on four. Allah creates whatever He wills.” (24:45) — as material the reader is expected to handle. The argument is not that complexity proves God, since a person can always say that complexity assembled itself; the argument is that the Book refuses to let the reader leave the observation unfinished. It sends him out to look at the same phenomena on purpose: “Travel throughout the land and see how He originated the creation, then Allah will bring it into being one more time.” (29:20). The verse counts as signs the creatures a man passes on any road, which means no expedition is required to reach the evidence it names.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:5

> And ˹in˺ the alternation of the day and the night, the provision sent down from the skies by Allah —reviving the earth after its death—and the shifting of the winds, are signs for people of understanding.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Time Is a Sign Because It Is Ordered**

Day and night take turns, and they have done so without a missed turn for as long as anyone has counted. The verse names that alternation among the signs, and the other chapters that list it are careful to keep the same company of witnesses: “Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night there are signs for people of reason.” (3:190); “Surely in the alternation of the day and the night, and in all that Allah has created in the heavens and the earth, there are truly signs for those mindful ˹of Him˺.” (10:6). Regularity is the point. A rhythm can be predicted, relied on, planted by, and sailed by, which is exactly what a world intended for accountable creatures requires. Time is also the one sign a person consumes rather than observes. It measures out his own life while he watches it measure the sky, and the sūrah's fourth and fifth verses keep the two facts side by side without comment.

**Rain Called Provision Before It Is Called Rain**

The verse calls the water that descends from the sky provision, and that is the claim, not a metaphor. Nothing in the sūrah's list keeps a human being alive more directly: crops, livestock, drinking water, and the whole chain of food that depends on rain rest on a transfer of water from the sky to the ground that no one on earth can arrange. The Book points out that the same descent revives dead ground, and it draws the comparison openly: “He is the One Who sends the winds ushering in His mercy. When they bear heavy clouds, We drive them to a lifeless land and then cause rain to fall, producing every type of fruit. Similarly, We will bring the dead to life, so perhaps you will be mindful.” (7:57). A second passage makes the resurrection the explicit conclusion: “And it is Allah Who sends the winds, which then stir up ˹vapour, forming˺ clouds, and then We drive them to a lifeless land, giving life to the earth after its death. Similar is the Resurrection.” (35:9). Rain is the annual rehearsal of a promise the sūrah will later require its readers to take seriously.

**Winds: One Word, Two Offices**

The winds shift, and the Arabic word for their shifting also covers the changes in direction that carry moisture from one place to another. The Qur'an normally speaks of winds in the plural when it means mercy: “And He is the One Who sends the winds ushering in His mercy, and We send down pure rain from the sky” (25:48), and “And one of His signs is that He sends the winds, ushering in good news ˹of rain˺ so that He may give you a taste of His mercy, and that ships may sail by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and perhaps you will be grateful.” (30:46). When the Book turns to punishment, it usually speaks of a wind in the singular — a single blast aimed at a single people. That grammatical difference is not a grammatical accident; it matches the difference between a system that serves everyone and a sentence served on one nation.

**Four Signs in One Sentence**

The sentence piles up its objects before it delivers its verdict: alternation, provision, revival, wind — then a single predicate, “are signs.” The structure enacts the argument. Four separate mechanisms, each with its own laws and its own domain, are gathered under one noun and credited to one speaker. The reader who studies them separately will find four subjects; the verse asks him to find one author. This is why the list gets longer as it goes: the alternation of day and night is a matter of astronomy, rain is meteorology, the revival of the earth is botany and soil, the winds are fluid motion. Each addition makes it harder to hold the parts apart. The verse's closing phrase marks the reader who can do it: people of understanding, those who take what they know and let it argue for something beyond itself.

**For People Who Understand**

Understanding here renders the verb ʿaqala and its noun, a word whose older sense is to restrain or bind — a person of ʿaql is one who holds himself back rather than one who has scored well on a test. The final qualifier in the list is therefore the most demanding of the three in this passage: the believers of v. 3 accept the witness, those of sure faith in v. 4 reach firm conviction, and those of understanding in v. 5 act on what they have grasped. The Qur'an never treats reason as the enemy of faith or faith as a substitute for reason; it treats both as faculties that answer the same evidence, and it blames the refusal to use either on the will rather than on the mind. Rain falls, winds turn, day follows night. What the reader does with the conclusion those facts support is the only part of the transaction that was ever his to decide.

**A World Kept Running Is a World Managed**

The list mixes two directions. Day and night turn in a cycle, winds shift and travel, and the rain comes down from above, so the reader lives inside a system that has both a source and a rhythm. The verse names the source in the middle of its own catalogue, attaching God's name to the provision after three impersonal descriptions of weather: the rain comes down “by Allah”. That placement keeps the sentence from reading like a report on nature and turns it into something closer to a statement of ownership. Other passages make the upkeep explicit: “It is Allah Who sends the winds, which then stir up ˹vapour, forming˺ clouds, which He then spreads out in the sky or piles up into masses as He wills, from which you see rain come forth.” (30:48). The Qur'an returns to this theme constantly because the temptation it addresses is constant — the assumption that a settled pattern of rain and wind and daylight is simply how the world is, and therefore needs no one to keep it running. A system that never fails is not on that account an ownerless one.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:6

> These are Allah’s revelations which We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth. So what message will they believe in after ˹denying˺ Allah and His revelations?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Crosses From the World to the Word**

Three verses have pointed at the sky, the human body, the animals, the rain, and the wind. This verse changes the object of attention without changing the vocabulary: the signs that were visible in creation are now recited, and the same word, āyāt, carries both. The verb is tilāwah, the ordinary word for reading aloud a text, and it makes the recitation an act in which God is the speaker and the Prophet is the one through whom the words arrive. The created world points silently; the recitation speaks. That is the difference between the two kinds of sign in this chapter, and it is why the argument can move from one to the other without a break. Anyone who has just concluded that the sky and the body and the rain have an author is now handed the author's own sentences about them.

**Recited in Truth**

The phrase translates the Arabic bi'l-ḥaqq, and it does three jobs at once. It tells the reader that the content of the recitation matches reality — no promise in it will fail and no threat in it is bluff. It tells him that the manner of the sending is honest, with no concealment of what the message costs its hearer. And it tells him that the recitation has a purpose rather than a decorative one, since truth in the Qur'an is never a merely factual property; it is what a thing is for. Other chapters use the same formula to make the same point: “These are Allah's revelations which We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth. And you are truly one of the messengers.” (2:252), and again, “That is because Allah has revealed the Book in truth. And surely those who differ regarding it are totally engrossed in opposition.” (2:176). The Book's truthfulness is stated in the same breath as its purpose.

**The Question That Leaves No Substitute**

The verse closes with a question, and the question is built to have one answer. If the recitation is rejected, what else is there to believe? The structure of the sentence forces a comparison: message against message, discourse against discourse. The Qur'an uses the same tactic in the closing verse of al-Mursalāt, asking what report after this one could possibly be accepted. The logic is not that no other literature exists — the Arabs had plenty, and the chapter's own seventh verse will describe one who trades in foreign tales. The logic is that nothing else on offer claims to be an address from the creator of the heavens and the earth. A reader who sets aside the revelations has not chosen a different account of the same subject; he has decided to live without an account, and that decision is what the question exposes.

**Two Readings, One Conclusion**

The transmitted readings differ on a single letter at the end of the verse. In the reading followed by most reciters, the question asks whether they — the deniers just described — will believe; in the other well-attested reading it becomes a direct address, asking whether you will believe. The difference shifts the audience but not the force. One reading looks past the immediate opponents to everyone who will hear the verse afterwards; the other makes each hearer the subject of the question before he can place himself outside it. Both readings agree that the alternatives are exhausted. The verse is not inviting its reader to shop among religions or philosophies and choose the most attractive. It is asking a narrower question about the same evidence the chapter has assembled: after all of that, what would it take?

**A Question the Reader Must Answer in Person**

This is the first place in the sūrah where the argument stops being about the world and starts being about the person receiving the description of the world. The verses that follow will show what denial looks like when it hardens into a character — a man who hears and walks away as though nothing happened. The question here is addressed to anyone who hears the recitation before that hardening occurs, and it has no neutral reply. Silence is already an answer. The chapter's title comes from a scene later in the sūrah where every community appears on its knees before its record, and that scene is where the question is finally answered for each of them. Until then, the reader is left holding the only two options the verse allows: accept the message, or explain what he will accept instead.

**Denial Described as Something That Happens After Hearing**

The wording of the question deserves attention. It describes people who deny “after ˹denying˺ Allah and His revelations” (45:6), and every word of that phrase marks sequence. The people in view have heard the recitation before they rejected it, so their rejection is described not as ignorance but as a verdict given after the evidence arrived. That is the pattern the sūrah returns to at its close, where the disbelievers are reminded of what was said to them about God's promise and the Hour, and where they finally admit that they were never convinced of anything. The Qur'an's authority rests on that order of events. A person can be excused for not knowing what he never encountered; the verse removes the excuse from the people it names, and in doing so it sets the measure by which the chapter's warnings are to be read. The message has been recited aloud, in truth, by a man his hearers could see and question. What remains is a decision about words that have already reached them.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:7

> Woe to every sinful liar.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words, Both Built for Intensity**

The verse is four words long in Arabic and it convicts with two nouns. The first, athīm, comes from a root meaning to commit sin, and its form is intensive: not a man who has sinned once, but one whose life has settled into the practice. The second, affāk, is built on afaka, to turn a thing from its proper face, and it names the person who does that to the truth habitually — the fabricator, the one who reverses what he has heard and hands it back in a new shape. The pairing is deliberate. A man may be a liar by trade without being wicked in private, and a man may be a sinner without inventing anything about God. This verse fuses the two, describing a character in which the fabrication has become the sin. The Qur'an uses the same second word for the sorcerer and for those who made the messengers out to be deceivers; it always carries the sense of turning something right-side up into its opposite.

**"Woe" Is a Verdict, Not an Emotion**

Wayl, rendered here as woe, is not a sigh. In the Book's usage it is a noun of ruin, a sentence passed on a class of people, and it stands at the head of some of the shortest and heaviest verses in the Qur'an. Other chapters attach it to those who write the Book with their own hands, to those who hoard and count their wealth, and to those who use false measures. In each case the word announces a verdict rather than describing a feeling, which is why the sūrah can move from it directly into an account of how the condemned man behaves. The brevity of the verse is part of its force: the reader is given no narrative, no name, no circumstance, only the sentence and the two qualities that earned it. A longer verse would invite discussion of the case. This one closes the file. Anyone who recognises his own habits in the two nouns has been told what the verdict is before he has had a chance to argue.

**The Man Who Has Persuaded Himself**

The root behind "liar" describes a turning, and the turning can begin at home. A person who tells a lie often enough may end by believing it, not because the lie becomes true but because his judgment has been bent into the shape of the interest it serves. That is how the Qur'an usually presents the hardened denier: not as a man tormented by evidence he cannot answer, but as one who has arranged his ideas so that the evidence never has to be faced. The verse's condemnation therefore falls on a condition rather than an isolated act. Habitual lying about the things that matter most, revelation and its messenger, is not a small vice among others; it is the mechanism by which a person keeps his life the way he prefers it. The two words describe a man at peace, which is what makes the verse frightening rather than merely harsh.

**Who the Verse Reached, and Who It Keeps Reaching**

Reports connect the verse to Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, a well-born Qurayshite who had travelled in the lands of the Persians and brought back their stories. He is said to have set his tales against the Qur'an, offering crowds an alternative entertainment when they gathered to hear the Prophet recite, and to have argued that the old stories were the better of the two. The report is not the verse's scope. Commentators treat the address as a standing description that applies to everyone who fabricates in the same way, at any time, and this is why the verse names no one. The Qur'an's habit in such cases is to leave the historical occasion where it stood and to widen the terms until they cover the reader. A specific man may have prompted the words; the class they describe will outlast him, and the judgment they announce cannot be avoided by the accident of being born after he died.

**Fabrication Is a Heavier Charge Than Doubt**

The Book treats doubting differently from manufacturing. A person who has not yet worked out what he believes can be answered, and the Qur'an answers that person at length, in arguments that fill pages and address a reader who is still deciding. The fabricator is a different case, because he has already supplied his own replacement for the message being offered to him, and the replacement needs an audience to survive. Doubt asks for evidence; fabrication builds a market. This is the evil the verse names, and it explains why the punishment described in the following verses falls so heavily on those who mock rather than on those who merely hesitate. Mockery is what fabrication does once it has an audience, and the two verses that follow describe that audience being gathered. A trade in false stories is not a neutral alternative to faith, since it claims for itself the authority it denies the revelation.

**A Class the Reader Is Asked to Examine**

"Every" is the widest word available, and the verse uses it. The Qur'an often names its opponents, records their arguments, and replies to each one in place, but here it does something blunter: it defines a type and warns it. That definition stands as a mirror set at the door of the sūrah. Every person who has repeated a claim about God without checking it, or shaped the truth to fit an audience, finds the two adjectives waiting for him, and there is no name attached by which he can deflect them onto someone else. The rest of the chapter will describe what becomes of those who hear the recitation and walk away. This verse marks the point before that walk, in a man whose mouth has already replaced the words he heard.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:8

> They hear Allah’s revelations recited to them, then persist ˹in denial˺ arrogantly as if they did not hear them. So give them good news of a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hearing That Leaves No Trace**

The verse describes a physical act with no inner result. The man hears the revelations being recited — the words reach him, in his language, from a mouth he can see — and then carries on as though nothing had been said. The Qur'an returns to this picture often, and it gives it in almost the same language in another chapter: “Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they turn away in arrogance as if they did not hear them, as if there is deafness in their ears. So give them good news ˹O Prophet˺ of a painful punishment.” (31:7). The comparison in that verse is to a closed ear, and the earlier reference to those covered and deafened states the same condition (6:25). The Book is not describing a defect in the ear but a decision about the message: the sound was admitted, the claim was understood, and the will refused it. What the verse calls arrogance is that refusal after comprehension.

**Arrogance Comes Before Disbelief**

The Arabic adds a verbal form that means to swell or to hold oneself above, and the Qur'an uses it wherever status is the real subject. What stops the men of the Quraysh is not a missing argument; it is the demand that they stand level with the man reciting to them, worship as he worships, and admit that their ancestors were wrong. Pride resists that cost, and the resistance expresses itself as a claim about the text. This order — arrogance first, argument second — is the Qur'an's constant analysis of rejection, and it is why the Book so often addresses the will behind the reasoning rather than the reasoning itself. A person who refuses to be a listener has protected himself from having to answer. The verse's detail that he behaves "as if he did not hear" is exact psychology: the pose is assumed, and the pose is the point.

**"Good News" as a Deliberate Irony**

"Give them good news" is a command built on a word that normally belongs to the messengers of mercy and the announcement of paradise. Here it is attached to a painful punishment, and the effect is sarcastic without being playful: the sentence hands the man the opposite of what he thought he had earned. The Qur'an uses the same inversion elsewhere, as when those who kill the prophets are told they have glad tidings of a painful punishment (3:21), and when the mockers are given the identical sentence with the identical wording (84:24). The device does more than sting. It exposes the denier's own logic: he treats warnings as entertainment, treats reciters as performers, and treats the Hereafter as a tale. So the announcement is delivered in his own register — news, provided with a certainty he had denied, from a source he cannot sue.

**The Report Behind the Verse**

The verse is reported to have been revealed concerning Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, the same opponent whose imported tales appear in the previous verse's background. The report says he would sit within hearing of the recitation and then amuse himself by treating it with contempt, unable or unwilling to respond to what it actually said. Exegetes take the verse's address as general, whatever the occasion, and the wording supports them: nothing in the sentence is tied to a single man, no incident is named, and the behaviour it describes is available to any reader. A revelation that listed one offender and moved on would be a chronicle. This verse gives the type and its outcome, and then leaves the reader to notice how easily the type is reproduced in a different century, by people whose reasons for refusing have changed and whose behaviour has not.

**The Punishment Fitted to the Offence**

The phrase translated "painful punishment" joins a word for torment to a word for pain, and Arabic uses that pair for the kind of suffering that is meant to be felt by the one who is punished. The sūrah will return to the subject of mockery three more times, and its final statement on the matter will say that the evil consequences of what people did will unfold before them and that what they used to ridicule will close in on them. That is the shape of the sentence announced here: the very thing the man treated as a joke will be handed to him as a fact. The recitation was a serious claim and he answered it with amusement; the answer he will receive is the claim, still standing, addressed to him alone. Nothing about the punishment is arbitrary, since its form is set by the offence itself.

**The Command Is Given to the Messenger**

The verse opens with description and closes with an imperative, and the imperative is addressed to the Prophet, not to the denier. He is told to deliver the announcement and to leave the response where it belongs. That division of labour runs through the sūrah: the messenger's task is the recitation, the warning, and the refusal to soften the words for the sake of an audience that has already decided. The denier's task, if he can be said to have one, is the reckoning. Any reader tempted to soften the message in order to keep his hearers comfortable is being told here that the comfort of the hearer was never part of the assignment, and that the argument does not have to be won by the reciter in his own lifetime. The words are delivered; the judgment is not his.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:9

> And whenever they learn anything of Our revelations, they make a mockery of it. It is they who will suffer a humiliating punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Whenever" Describes a Practice**

The verse opens with a form that means each time, on every occasion, with no limit set. It is not reporting an incident but a habit: the man hears something from the revelations, and the effect is predictable. That construction matters for the reader's understanding of the people in view, because it rules out the excuse of a single bad day or a momentary lapse into cruelty. A practice has roots, and the practice described here is the second half of the portrait the sūrah has been building — the liar of v. 7, the arrogant listener of v. 8, and now the mocker who has made derision his ordinary way of handling what he hears. The Book's habit is to show the interior condition and then the behaviour it produces. Mockery is the behaviour.

**Any Fragment Will Do**

The verse says they learn anything of the revelations — a part, not the whole. That detail is accurate about how contempt usually works. A person need not master a text to jeer at it; a clause heard in passing, a verse repeated in the market, a rule that seems inconvenient taken on its own, will do. What the Qur'an criticises here is not the study of parts but the use of parts as a substitute for the whole. Someone who takes one sentence away from its context and laughs at it has not refuted anything; he has announced the size of the effort he is willing to make. The same defect shows up in the person who knows the Book only as performance, as a sound he has heard and never as a claim he must answer. Surface knowledge is not a lesser form of knowledge here. It is the raw material of the mockery.

**Two Kinds of Sign, One Kind of Contempt**

This sūrah has spent six verses teaching that the word for signs covers two domains: the created order and the recited text. Contempt can be aimed at either. A person mocks the signs in creation when he treats the sky, his own body, and the animals as accidents and refuses to grant that they point anywhere; a person mocks the revealed signs when he laughs at the sentences of the Book itself. The two insults have different flavours — one runs through ingratitude, the other through denial — but they come from the same refusal, which is the refusal to be addressed by anything. This is why the verse bundles them under one verb. The man who jokes about rain and the man who jokes about revelation have both decided that nothing out there has a claim on them, and the sūrah treats the decision, not the joke, as the offence.

**Humiliation Trails the Proud**

The punishment is called humiliating, and the choice of word answers the crime in the language of honour. The men in view wanted standing; status was the thing their mockery protected. The Book has already named God "the Almighty" in the second verse of the chapter, and the word used here for the punishment they receive comes from a root meaning low, base, and brought down. The Qur'an sets up this contrast repeatedly: what the proud protect is their rank, and what they are given instead is public dishonour. The reckoning is not a private disappointment but a humiliation, seen and acknowledged. In the world of the first hearers, where a family's standing was the currency of daily life, no sentence could have cut deeper, and the Book states it as a certainty rather than a threat.

**Mockery Needs an Audience, and Finds One**

Laughter is social. A man mocking the Qur'an in private would barely be a mocker of anything; the act exists in front of others, and it recruits. The verse uses a plural, "they", and its punishment is collective too, which fits the way derision works: one man's joke lowers the cost of the next man's doubt. This is why the Qur'an treats public mockery of revelation as a category of its own, distinct from private disbelief. The private doubter can still be argued with, and the Book argues with him at length. The mocker has already turned the question into a performance, and a performance has no interest in being corrected; what it needs is a crowd, and the crowd it gathers is the court in which it will later stand. The sūrah keeps the two cases apart because their remedies are different, and the second has none offered.

**The Verdict Keeps Its Shape Across the Sūrah**

This is the first time the sūrah states the penalty for mockery, and it will state it three more times before the chapter ends: what they ridiculed closes in on them, the Fire becomes their home, and their own deeds unfold before them. The repetition is not redundancy. It builds a pattern in which the punishment is never arbitrary and never invented at the end of the story — it is the thing the man himself chose to treat as nothing, returned to him as everything. The "humiliating punishment" of this verse is the first turn of that screw, and the word for it stays fixed through the verses that follow. What the mocker cannot keep is his pose, because the audience he gathered will see him in the end as the one position he never expected to occupy.

**The Two Audiences of Every Recitation**

A recitation always has two audiences, and the sūrah keeps them apart with care. One consists of the people standing in front of the reciter, who may mock, argue, or walk away; the other consists of the one whose words are being recited, who is present in the transaction whether or not anyone acknowledges him. The mocker believes he is performing for the first audience and that the second is a fiction, which is why the verse's sentence comes from the side he had written off. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same mistake in plainer terms, saying that a record is kept by honoured scribes who know what is done (82:11–12), and that no soul is left without a vigilant recorder (86:4). If the account is being taken by the very party being mocked, then contempt is not a cheap gesture at all. It is a statement made in front of the court, entered into evidence, and answered in the verses that follow.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:10

> Awaiting them is Hell. Their ˹worldly˺ gains will not be of any benefit to them whatsoever, nor will those protectors they have taken besides Allah. And they will suffer a tremendous punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Behind Them or Ahead of Them**

The phrase rendered "awaiting them" is literally "beyond them" — min warāʾihim — and Arabic allows the expression to point in either direction, since a thing that is behind you is out of your sight and a thing ahead of you is not yet in your hand. Commentators take both readings. If Hell lies ahead, the image is of men walking toward a place they have not noticed, which fits a sūrah whose deniers are cheerful and unhurried. If Hell lies behind, the image is of men who have turned their backs on it and pretend it is not there, which fits the arrogance already described. The two readings are not rivals for the text so much as two views of one situation. The stronger emphasis runs in the direction of what comes next, because the sentence goes on to describe a fate that arrives rather than a fate that has been left. Either way, the phrase denies the denier any sense of safety in his present position.

**Wealth and Children Do Not Testify**

The verse names what they have gained, and the Qur'an's standard list of such gains is property and offspring — the visible marks of success in the society the Prophet addressed. A person may trust that what he has accumulated will speak for him, buy him protection, or at least earn him respect in the court that is coming. The Book will not have it: “Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire.” (3:10). The same sentence appears in another chapter with the emphasis sharpened: “Neither their wealth nor children will be of any help to them against Allah whatsoever. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.” (58:17). Two things cannot be bought with goods: the verdict of a judge who owns everything, and time that has already been spent.

**The Protectors Who Cannot Protect**

The second thing the deniers trust is a set of guardians taken besides God — idols in the first instance, and behind the idols the patrons, tribes, and powers a person leans on for safety. The Qur'an addresses the idols directly in one place, treating them as defendants rather than assets: “Certainly you ˹disbelievers˺ and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell. You are ˹all˺ bound to enter it.” (21:98). Another verse stretches the same warning to every form of reliance when it asks whether the disbelievers thought they could take God's own servants as patrons instead of him (18:102). The point is not that patronage is wrong in itself; the sūrah will tell believers plainly to be patrons of one another. The point is that at the one moment when a protector would be needed absolutely, the ones chosen cannot appear in the room.

**Why the Punishment Is Called Tremendous**

The word for the punishment is ʿaẓīm, which names size and weight, and the Qur'an uses it for what is beyond ordinary measure. Its placement here is a comparison the verse makes without stating it: against a great punishment stands a small profit. Everything the deniers collected, the property and the alliances and the standing, had a limited value in a limited world; what they receive instead has no limit attached to it. The sūrah has already called God the Almighty in its second verse, and the size of the penalty is a function of the one who issues it. If the judge is greater than anything the defendant ever gained, then the sentence he pronounces is greater than anything the defendant can offset against it. The word is doing arithmetic, not decoration.

**A Ledger With Only Two Columns**

The structure of the verse is a small balance sheet. On one side sit gains and protectors, both things a person gathers and keeps. On the other side sit Hell and a punishment without measure, both things a person receives. The Qur'an returns constantly to the language of profit and loss when it speaks about the Hereafter, and this chapter will use it again near its end, where those who made false claims are said to have lost. The commercial vocabulary is aimed at a trading people who understood a bad bargain when they saw one. What the verse shows them is a transaction they never agreed to enter and cannot refuse: the goods are handed over, and the price is exacted. No column of the ledger can be moved to the other side after the account is closed, and the sūrah's next verses show the account being read.

**What the Idols Were Made Of**

It helps to know what the "protectors" actually were, because the verse's confidence depends on a fact the first hearers knew about their own shrines. The gods of the Quraysh were stone, wood, and metal, worked by human hands, housed in buildings that men had raised, fed with offerings that men had brought. The Qur'an never treats them as rival powers; it treats them as manufactured objects, and it says so in verse after verse, including the passage that consigns them and their worshippers together to the Fire (21:98). A man who trusts an object he could lift has placed his safety in the least reliable thing in the room. The verse's list is therefore not a catalogue of temptations but an inventory of failures, and the failure is not that the protectors are weak in comparison with God — it is that they were never protectors at all, and their clients knew it whenever they were honest with themselves.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:11

> This ˹Quran˺ is ˹true˺ guidance. And those who deny their Lord’s revelations will suffer the ˹worst˺ torment of agonizing pain.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guidance Said Without Qualification**

The verse opens with a demonstrative and a noun: this is guidance. The demonstrative points at what has just been recited, and the noun is stated without a limiting clause, so the claim is not that the Book contains guidance among other things but that it is guidance in its own identity. Other chapters describe the Book as a guide for the mindful or as a mercy for believers, and those descriptions add an audience. This verse does not. It states what the Book is before it says what happens to those who reject it, and the order of the two halves is the sūrah's own logic: the value of a thing is established before the loss of it is described. A reader who has just met the mockers of the previous verses is now told what their mockery was aimed at.

**The Word Behind the Claim**

Guidance translates hudā, from a root that means to lead someone gently toward a destination, and it carries more than information. A signpost gives directions; a guide takes you there. The Book is presented in this chapter as the second, which is why its demands are practical rather than speculative and why the sūrah's arguments keep moving toward action — recall, submit, be grateful, forgive. The prophets are described in the Qur'an as callers who guide by God's command, and the Book is described with the same root, so the claim in this verse is that the direction it gives is the direction of God, not a proposal among the proposals of the world. In a chapter that has argued from the sky, the body, the rain, and the wind, the final sign is the one that tells a person what to do with the conclusions he has reached.

**Denial Addressed to a Lord, Not a Proposition**

The verse does not say that they deny the revelations; it says they deny the revelations of their Lord, and the possessive is doing work. The signs are not anonymous data; they arrive already belonging to the one who owns the people they address. The same construction appears throughout the chapter, where the signs are God's and the sea is subjected by God and the Book descends from God, so that every object a person could argue about has already been claimed. Denial, in this light, is not the philosophical position of a person weighing evidence; it is the rejection of a master by someone who is owned. That is why the punishment attached to the offence is so heavy, and why the Qur'an treats the hardening of the heart rather than a shortage of proofs as the real subject of the whole discussion.

**The Same Sentence Used Elsewhere**

The penalty here is described with a phrase that the Book uses more than once: the worst torment of agonizing pain. Another chapter pronounces it on those who work against the signs in order to discredit them: “As for those who strive to discredit Our revelations, it is they who will suffer the ˹worst˺ torment of agonizing pain.” (34:5). The wording fixes a correspondence between the crime and the sentence. What the deniers did was aim at the revelations, exerting effort to weaken them in other people's eyes, and what they receive is a torment aimed with the same precision at them. The Qur'an's habit of reusing a phrase for a repeated offence is one way the reader learns to hear the Book as a single coherent speech rather than a collection of separate warnings, each one arrived at independently.

**Guidance and Warning in One Breath**

The sentence joins the offer and the threat without a break, and the two are not alternatives competing for the reader's attention; they are the two faces of the same recitation. What guides one person condemns another, not because the text changes but because the response to it differs, and the sūrah has already shown both responses in operation. A reader who takes the first half seriously cannot treat the second as a distant threat aimed at other people, since the qualification for the threat has been described in detail and its symptoms are ordinary. Denying revelation begins as a habit of hearing without answering, which is available to anyone. The verse leaves the reader standing between the two halves with nothing in the text to keep them apart, and no position from which to choose neither.

**Guidance That Has to Be Accepted as a Whole**

The claim that the Book is guidance carries an implication that a modern reader may miss: guidance is only useful if it is followed where it is inconvenient. A person who accepts the parts of the scripture he already agrees with has not been guided by it; he has used it to confirm himself. The Qur'an makes the point through its own descriptions of the Book as a guide for those who are mindful, and it warns in several places against the habit of taking one part and leaving another. The verse's brevity leaves no room for a selective reading: the same Book is guidance in full, and the same denial attaches to the person who rejects what it says about the Resurrection, the Reckoning, or the moral demands it places on his wealth and his household. The final warning of the sentence is not aimed at ignorance. It is aimed at a hearing that has already been given and a decision that has already been made.

**A Superlative Chosen on Purpose**

The Qur'an ranks its warnings, and this verse uses the strongest form available for the penalty it announces. Other passages speak of a great punishment or a humiliating one; the phrase here names the worst torment of its kind, which tells the reader that the Book is measuring accountability rather than frightening everyone equally. The measure belongs to the offence described in the previous verse: a person who has had the Book recited to him, understood what he heard, and then denied his Lord's revelations has been given more than the man who never encountered them at all. The Qur'an makes that distinction in several places, warning that a heavier account awaits those who were given knowledge and turned from it. The superlative is therefore a statement about responsibility. It is not that God is especially angry with this group; it is that this group was given the most and returned the least.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:12

> Allah is the One Who has subjected the sea for you so that ships may sail upon it by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and that perhaps you will be grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sea Handed Over With Orders**

Subjection is a strong word to use of an ocean. The sea is the least governable environment a person can enter, and the Qur'an names it as a service rendered: the water carries what is placed on it, the wind does the rest, and a hull of joined planks crosses in weeks what a caravan could not cross at all. Other chapters list the same favour among the signs, including the ships that travel the sea for the benefit of humanity (2:164) and the statement that God has subjected the sea so that ships sail through it by his command (22:65). The verse does not present the seafarers' skill as the cause of the arrangement; it presents the arrangement as something given, in which skill is permitted to work. Anyone who has stood on a shore and watched a loaded ship come in has seen the favour in operation without having to interpret anything.

**Three Purposes in a Single Sentence**

The verse chains its purpose clauses. First the ships sail, so that trade and travel are possible; then the passengers seek bounty, so that the crossing has an economic point; then the aim closes on gratitude, which is the only one of the three that belongs to the human being rather than to the ocean. That ascent is the sentence's design. A fact about water leads to a use of the fact, and the use of the fact leads to a question about the one who arranged both. The grammar is patient, moving the reader a step at a time from the physical to the personal, and it never announces the final step as a demand. The order matters for the sūrah's argument, because a reader who has followed the chain from wave to cargo to thanksgiving is already standing on the ground the following verses occupy.

**Bounty That Must Be Sought**

The word for bounty, faḍl, means excess, the overflow beyond what is strictly owed, and the verse attaches it to a human action: seeking. The Qur'an does not treat commerce as an unfortunate necessity whose profits have to be excused. It places honest gain within the purposes for which the sea was made, and elsewhere it lists the same benefit among the signs: “And among His signs are the ships like mountains ˹sailing˺ in the sea.” (42:32). The merchants of Makkah lived by these routes, and their caravans and ships gave the sūrah's first hearers an immediate example of a good that everyone in the room depended on. The verse uses their world without flattering it. A man who sails in order to earn is doing something the Book has already designated, provided he notices whose hand steadied the water under him.

**Why the Sea Comes Before the Sky**

The sūrah has already walked through the heavens and the earth, the body, and the weather. It now returns to the created world through a more intimate door: the water a merchant crosses. The heavens can be admired from a distance, and the alternation of night and day happens whether a person looks or not, but a voyage is entered, paid for, and survived, and the sailors who return know exactly what carried them. This is the Qur'an's teaching method throughout the passage: the same truth is presented in larger and smaller versions until the reader finds one that sits against his own life. A merchant in the sūrah's first audience had the sea in his ledger already. The verse simply writes in the margin the name of the one who keeps it under his command.

**Gratitude as the Point of the Whole Arrangement**

The verse closes with hope rather than command: that perhaps you will be grateful. The Qur'an prefers this construction for human responses, because it leaves the outcome with the person while showing what the outcome should be. Shukr in Arabic is the acknowledgment of a benefit along with the use of it, and the verse puts it at the end of a chain that began with the sea. The arrangement — the ocean's behaviour, the ship's buoyancy, the wind's direction, the appetite that sends men to market, and the safe return — was never organised for the purpose of making anyone rich. It was organised so that a person might notice, and the notice is called gratitude. The next verses will widen the list of things subjected once more, but the progression from provision to thanksgiving is the sūrah's own stated goal.

**A Favour That No One Can Claim to Have Earned**

The sea's obedience is older than any sailor, and this is what sets the verse's favour beyond dispute. A benefit that a person earns can be treated as wages and forgotten once paid; a benefit he received before he existed cannot be accounted for that way, and the Qur'an draws attention to that gap whenever it lists the created order as a favour. Water covered the planet before anyone built a boat, and salt water was separated from fresh water before anyone needed to drink. Men did not arrange these things, could not have arranged them, and would not have survived the attempt to live without them. The verse's verb for subjection keeps the arrangement in the past tense, where it belongs, and the only response left to the beneficiary is the one the sentence names at its end. Gratitude, in that setting, is not a compliment paid to a patron. It is an accurate description of the state of affairs.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:13

> He ˹also˺ subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth—all by His grace. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Everything in Both Realms, Named Without a List**

The previous verse named one thing, the sea. This verse names everything, and it does so without a catalogue: whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. The claim is enormous and it is made in a single breath, with the same verb of subjection attached. Other chapters state it in the same sweeping form, as when the reader is asked whether he has seen that God has subjected what is in the heavens and what is on the earth and lavished on humanity favours both seen and unseen (31:20). Nothing is excepted — not the sun, the rain, the minerals in the ground, the animals that can be tamed, the plants that can be eaten, or the fire that can be lit. The verse does not say that a portion of the world has been made useful and the rest is neutral. It says the arrangement is total, and the phrase "all by His grace" marks it as a gift rather than a wage.

**Being Given Use of What You Do Not Own**

Subjection is a legal-sounding word in English, and the Arabic behind it is careful. It describes something placed under command and made serviceable; it does not describe a transfer of ownership. The Qur'an never tells its readers that the world is theirs to do with as they please. It tells them, in other places, that they have been appointed as successors on the earth and given rank over other creatures as a test (6:165), and it recalls the announcement in which God tells the angels that he is placing an authority on the earth (2:30). Both passages keep the ownership where it belongs. The practical consequence is that waste, cruelty, and destruction are not merely imprudent; they are the misuse of property that was never handed over. What a person has is a grant of use, and a grant of use can be reviewed by the one who granted it.

**The Grace That Arrived Before Anyone Could Earn It**

The verse ends its clause with the word for grace, the same word used for the bounty a sailor seeks. Here it is expanded to cover the whole of the created order, and the word's meaning matters: it names what is given beyond obligation, not what is owed. Nobody earned the sun's heat, and no generation paid for the atmosphere it breathed. The Qur'an piles up this kind of statement because gratitude is easier to argue for than to feel, and it lists the gift before it asks for the response. Two nearby verses from other chapters press the point further, speaking of the sun and moon made to follow their courses and the ships driven across the water for human benefit (14:33; 22:65). Repetition of this sort is not decoration. It is the slow construction of an accurate picture of the human position, which the sūrah needs before it can ask anything of its reader.

**Serving What Was Made to Serve You**

There is an inversion the verse is quietly preventing. If the world has been placed at a person's service, then a life spent in service of the world has the order backwards, and the chapter states the remedy in a sentence of praise used by travellers: “Glory be to the One Who has subjected these for us, for we could have never done so ˹on our own˺. And surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return.” (43:13–14). The words that acknowledge the gift end by naming the return, and the two belong together. Wealth, comfort, and the produce of the earth are instruments, and instruments have a purpose beyond themselves. A man who spends his life arranging instruments and never uses them for the work they were built for has not been prudent. He has been defeated by his own equipment, and the verse's closing phrase names the faculty that would have noticed.

**Reflection as Slow Work**

The audience here is described as people who reflect, using a verb that pictures thought turning a matter over and over. It is the least dramatic of the descriptions attached to the signs in this passage — the others call for reason, certainty, understanding, gratitude — and it may be the most demanding, because reflection takes time and leaves nothing visible behind. A person can measure a coastline or count a harvest without ever turning a matter over in his mind, and the Qur'an's account of the created order is aimed at the second activity. The verse supplies the raw material; the reader does the turning. Between the two there is no shortcut, and no amount of information about the world will produce the thought the verse is asking for, unless the person who holds the information decides to think it through to the end.

**A World Fitted to a Creature That Thinks**

The arrangement described in this verse has a curious feature. The world is not merely usable; it is intelligible. Water has a chemistry a mind can follow, seasons have a pattern a farmer can predict, and the heavens have regularities that permitted calendars to be drawn before telescopes existed. A universe that could be understood by the creatures living in it is not the bare minimum needed for survival, and the Qur'an treats that fit as part of the gift. The verse's word for reflection, then, is not a demand to perform an intellectual exercise for its own sake. It is an invitation to use the one faculty that matches the world it inhabits, on the evidence that everywhere surrounds the person who possesses it.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:14

> ˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the believers to forgive those who do not fear Allah’s days ˹of torment˺, so that He will reward each group for what they used to commit.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Forgive People Who Have Not Apologised**

The instruction is strange at first hearing. The believers are told to forgive people who have shown no regret, sought no pardon, and in several cases were actively working to harm them. Nothing in the verse asks the offenders to change first. If anything, the description attached to them is unflattering — those who do not fear the days of God — so the command is not based on the worthiness of its object. This is the Qur'an's characteristic way of handling the desire for revenge. It does not deny the injury, does not call the injury small, and does not pretend the offenders are decent. It moves the whole matter to a higher jurisdiction, where the case will be heard by someone who saw every detail, and it tells the injured party to leave the file on that desk. Forgiving, in this verse, is not a judgment about the offender's character. It is an act of trust in a court that has not yet sat.

**What the Days of God Are**

The phrase translated "Allah's days of torment" has a longer history than the verse's occasion. The days of God are the days in which he acted in the world: the drowning of Pharaoh's army, the rescue of Noah, the destruction of nations that mocked their messengers, and the deliverances granted to the weak. A person who does not fear those days has concluded, in effect, that history contains no verdicts — that the powerful have always won and that no power above them intervenes. The Qur'an treats that conclusion as a form of blindness with consequences, and it also treats compassion as compatible with it. The believers are not asked to believe that nothing happened to those who oppressed them. They are asked to live as people whose lawsuit has been filed, whose case is strong, and who do not need to enforce the verdict themselves.

**The Incident Reported Behind the Command**

Reports tie the verse to an expedition against the tribe of Banū al-Muṣṭaliq and to a well where the Muslims had stopped. A servant of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, the leader of the hypocrites of Madinah, is said to have returned without water because the servant of ʿUmar had taken the well for the Prophet's household, and ibn Ubayy is said to have responded with a bitter proverb comparing the newcomers to a dog that is fed until it turns on its feeder. The report continues that ʿUmar heard it, took up his sword, and was on his way to kill the man when the verse arrived. Exegetes note that the verse may already have been revealed earlier and been recited on this occasion to calm him. Whether the words were new or repeated, the effect asked for is the same: a community with real grievances is told to hold its hand.

**The Other Report: A Loan to God and a Saying in the Market**

A second account attaches the verse to an insult of a different kind. When the words of the Qur'an that ask who will lend God a good loan were recited (57:11), a Jewish resident of Madinah named Finhas is said to have remarked that Muhammad's Lord must be poor, since he borrows. ʿUmar is said to have set out after him with his sword, and the report continues that the angel Gabriel came to the Prophet with the instruction that the believers forgive those who do not fear the days of God. Both accounts are reported rather than established, and both show the same situation: a hot word, a drawn sword, and a revelation telling the injured community to stand down. Whatever the exact occasion, the command is where the sūrah places it, in the middle of a chapter about signs and their rejection.

**Why Restraint Was the Stronger Option in Madinah**

The believers in Madinah were a minority with powerful enemies and a treaty to manage, and the verses of this period repeatedly restrain them for reasons that are not merely moral. Killing a leader of the hypocrites would have plunged the city into a war the Muslims could not afford, and it would have handed their opponents an argument. The Qur'an's instruction therefore has a prudential edge as well as a generous one, though it never presents restraint as cunning. Other passages set out the standard in plain terms, asking the believers to respond to evil with what is best (41:34), to pardon and forgive as they hope to be forgiven (24:22), and to forgive the relatives and spouses who lagged behind (64:14). None of these verses promises that the offender will change. They promise that the one who forgives loses nothing in the end.

**The Clause That Explains Why Forgiving Is Safe**

The verse ends with the reason, and the reason is not a lesson in ethics: so that he will reward each group for what it used to commit. Justice is being deferred, not cancelled. The offender's account is still open, and the injured party's restraint is being recorded as something in his own favour. That structure explains why the Qur'an can ask so much of people who have been wronged. It is not telling them to pretend the injury did not matter; it is telling them that a book is being kept in which nothing is lost. Both parties will be paid in full, in the same court, according to what they actually did, and the believers are being asked to wait for a payment that God has already guaranteed. The command is heavy, and the guarantee behind it is the reason it can be carried.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:15

> Whoever does good, it is to their own benefit. And whoever does evil, it is to their own loss. Then to your Lord you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Account Belongs to the One Who Acts**

The verse states the most basic rule of the Qur'anic moral order: a person's deeds are credited to that person, in both directions. Good done by one person cannot be charged to another, and harm done by one person cannot be collected from another. The same sentence opens another chapter almost word for word, where it is completed by a statement that God is never unjust to his creation (41:46). The Book repeats the rule because human beings resist it in both directions, sometimes claiming credit for what others did and sometimes hoping that someone else's merit will cover their own deficit. Two short verses near the end of the Qur'an state the accounting with unmatched brevity: whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (99:7–8). Nothing is too small for the ledger, and nothing is filed under the wrong name.

**Why This Sentence Follows the Command to Forgive**

The placement of the verse is part of its meaning. The previous command asked the believers to forgive without demanding justice first, and a reader might reasonably ask what an unpunished wrongdoer will face. The answer is given before the question can be put: the damage an evildoer does lands on himself. That reorders the whole dispute. The Muslim who restrains himself is not permitting a wrong to go unanswered; he is declining to compete for a debt that the debtor already owes to himself, and the loss is stated plainly as the evildoer's own. A community acting on this rule can afford patience, because it does not need the offender's punishment in order to be satisfied. The verse supplies the accounting that the previous verse had referred to, and it does so with a symmetry that leaves no room for the injured party to feel cheated.

**Loss That Is Not a Metaphor**

The Book's word for the second half of the equation is loss, and it uses it the way a merchant would. The Qur'an describes the people who trade their guidance for error as those who have ruined themselves, and it will say later in this very sūrah that on the day the Hour arrives, those who made false claims will have lost. The clearest statement of what this involves comes earlier: the true losers are those who will lose themselves and their families on Judgment Day, and that is called the clearest loss (39:15). The phrasing is precise. The loss is not a penalty added to an otherwise intact life; it is the life itself, and in some cases the household along with it. The verse therefore does not tell the reader that evil has unpleasant consequences. It tells him that evil spends the capital, and the account is his own.

**No One Carries Another's Burden**

Underneath the sentence lies a doctrine the Qur'an states many times: no soul bears the burden of another. The doctrine cuts against almost every instinct people bring to religion, because the alternative — that merit can be pooled, inherited, or traded — is convenient and socially comfortable. It was the claim of those who said they would be saved by the merit of their ancestors, and it is the assumption of anyone who counts on family, nation, or community standing in place of his own record. The verse's possessive language leaves no gap for that assumption: the benefit and the loss are each attached to the person who acted. What a believer can do for another, the Qur'an says elsewhere, is pray for him, advise him, forgive him, and ask mercy for him. What he cannot do is finish another person's account after that person has closed it.

**Return as the Reason for the Accounting**

The verse's final clause explains why the ledger exists: everyone is going back to his Lord. The return gives the sentence its seriousness and also its comfort, because it means that the judge at the end of the process knows the case from the inside. The Qur'an closes ethical instructions with a reference to the meeting in this way so often that the pattern becomes a signature: act, then answer for the act, then meet the one who kept the record. Almost every chapter establishes the same movement, and one of them states the reckoning itself in a single sentence: on that Day no soul will be wronged in the least, nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do (36:54). The verse before us is that rule stated in advance, for a reader who is still able to change what is written next.

**Good Done in the Dark Is Still Recorded**

There is one more consequence that the wording leaves open. The verse does not say who will notice, only that the benefit returns to the doer. A generous act performed where nobody saw it can feel like a thing lost, and a corrupt act performed where nobody noticed can feel like a gain, because in this world the account often seems closed by the absence of witnesses. The verse says the second half of the transaction has not happened yet, and that it will happen under different conditions. The person who did good in obscurity will receive what he could not see when he did it, and the person who did harm in obscurity will be handed the same bill. Between now and then, the only thing being tested is whether a person believes that the account exists at all.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:16

> Indeed, We gave the Children of Israel the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood; granted them good, lawful provisions; and favoured them above the others.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Gifts That Rank Above Everything Else**

The list is short and deliberately ordered. The Scripture comes first, because a community's understanding of what is required of it depends on the revelation it holds. Judgment comes second, the capacity to read that revelation and settle cases by it. Prophethood comes last and completes the set, since it means the revelation is not a dead document but a living instruction delivered by men who speak with authority. The sequence traces an ascent from the text to its application to the persons who carried it. Elsewhere the Book gives the same inventory when it speaks of what was handed to the house of Israel, and the exegetes take the Scripture here to be the Torah, with judgment naming the understanding of it granted to its scholars. A community given all three was not merely well equipped. It had received the complete apparatus of guidance.

**Judgment as a Gift Rather Than a Claim**

The middle item deserves a closer look. The word used for judgment also carries the sense of wisdom and of the settled authority to decide, and the same root supplied the divine name used at the head of this sūrah, the All-Wise. When the Qur'an applies that root to human beings, it means something specific: the ability to see a case clearly, hold to the principle, and not be swayed by interest. That ability is listed as a gift from God, not as an achievement of the gifted. The point matters for the chapter's later argument, because a people who received judgment as a grant had no basis for treating their standing as a personal possession. What they were given could be withdrawn, and the verse records it in the past tense — "we gave" — leaving open what the present may hold.

**Good and Lawful Provision After the Spiritual Gifts**

Material blessing appears fourth, after revelation, judgment, and prophethood, and the order is the argument. The Qur'an tells its readers that the Children of Israel inherited the wealth of the Egyptians and were fed in the wilderness with manna and quails (7:137; 2:57; 20:80), and it counts those things as genuine favours rather than as compromises. They take the fourth place, though, because a community with a scripture and no bread is tested differently from a community with bread and no scripture. The ranking is a rebuke to anyone who measures standing by the second list alone. Spiritually valuable gifts are placed before the material ones so plainly that a reader who skims the verse and remembers only the wealth will have misread the sentence in front of him.

**"Favoured Above the Others" — Whom, and When**

The phrase is often quoted as a permanent claim, and the Qur'an itself limits it. The same favour is mentioned in the chapters delivered to the Israelites in rebuke, and the commentators explain it as honour above the peoples of their own time — the contemporaries of Moses and the generations that followed, not the whole of humanity for all ages. The Book addresses them again and again as a people who received what the surrounding nations did not have, and it also criticises them in terms that would be pointless if the favour were a racial deposit. The position was historical and instrumental. It lasted as long as the trust it described, and it was never a licence to treat other peoples as material to be used rather than as addressees of the same message.

**Being Given Much Is Not the Same as Succeeding**

A reader who has followed this sūrah from its opening letters to this verse will notice what has been assembled here: the Book, the wisdom, the prophets, the provisions, the honour. Then the chapter continues, and the gifts become the setting for a failure. The Qur'an's regular pattern is to describe a community's blessings in full before it reports what the recipients did with them, so that no one can claim afterwards that the disaster came from a lack of resources. The gifts were sufficient. Nothing was missing from the provision of guidance that reached them, and this is exactly why the account that follows is presented without excuses. The verse reads as a list of mercies, and in the chapter's architecture it also reads as a record of what the trial was made of.

**What the Gift of Prophethood Says About Prophethood**

The verse lists prophethood among what was given, and the grammar is important: prophets are given, not achieved, and they are given to a people rather than taken by them. The Children of Israel received a line of prophetic messengers, and the fact that they received it explains why the next verses of this chapter treat their divisions as a grave matter. A community entrusted with the institution of prophethood is in a different position from one that never encountered it, because its members have been shown the tool by which disputes are supposed to be settled. The favour, in other words, is also a description of responsibility. What a people does with a prophet in its midst is a more serious matter than what it does with a book on a shelf.

**A List That Honours a People Without Flattering Them**

The Qur'an is even-handed in a way that surprises readers who expect scripture to divide the world into heroes and villains. This verse credits the Children of Israel with the highest gifts a community can receive, and the same Book elsewhere criticises them in detail, recording the hardening of their hearts, their distortion of the words of the Scripture, and their neglect of a portion of what they were commanded to keep (5:13). Neither judgment cancels the other. A gift that is acknowledged to be real can also be described as misused, and a people who are criticised for their conduct are not thereby stripped of what they were given. This is a discipline for the reader as well. A community that discusses its predecessors with contempt learns nothing from them; a community that praises them without correction learns nothing either. The verse asks for the whole account, gifts and outcome together, and it is the honesty of the second half that makes the first half credible.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:17

> We ˹also˺ gave them clear commandments regarding ˹their˺ faith. But it was not until knowledge came to them that they differed out of mutual envy. Surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Commands Clear Enough to Remove the Excuse**

The clarity of what the Israelites received is stated before their division is reported, and the order is the charge. The Qur'an says the commands were clear proofs, the kind of indication that leaves a reasonable person in no doubt about what is being asked. A community in that position cannot plead that the instructions were obscure or that the messengers were vague, and the verse does not allow the reader to construct that defence on their behalf. The same standard will later be applied to the readers of this sūrah, who have been given signs in the sky and in their own bodies. The verse is therefore not only a report about a people in the past. It establishes what the Book means by a clear revelation, so that every case in which that revelation is disputed can be judged against the standard set here.

**Division That Arrives After the Knowledge**

The sequence in the verse is precise: the difference between them did not arise until knowledge had come. That ordering is repeated across the Qur'an whenever the subject of schism appears, and the repetitions carry the same diagnosis — humanity was one community, then they disputed after clear proofs had reached them (2:213); those who were given the Scripture did not dispute out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them (3:19); a people should not behave like those who split into sects and differed after clear proofs had come (3:105); and the people of the Book did not become divided until the clear proof came to them (98:4). The pattern is consistent enough to be treated as the Qur'an's considered explanation of religious conflict: disagreement is not a symptom of too little information but of something else operating after the information has arrived.

**Envy as the Engine of the Split**

That something else is named in this verse. The Arabic describes a mutual transgression or jealousy among the disputants, and the exegetes understand it as rivalry over position and leadership. The logic is uncomfortable because it is familiar: a body of knowledge creates status for those who hold it, and the holders can begin to compete for that status as though the knowledge existed to serve them. When that happens, the shared text no longer determines the outcome; the contest does. Al-Rāzī, quoted in the classical commentaries, observes that knowledge itself does not lead people to differ, but the manner in which some seek advancement through it produces the envy, and the envy produces the split. The verse does not say the disputants were ignorant. It says they were envious, and the two conditions are compatible.

**Two Ways of Reading the Clear Commandments**

Commentators differ about whose proofs are in view. Read in the light of the preceding verse, the plain clear indications belong to Moses and the Israelites, since the whole passage concerns the gifts given to them. Read in the light of the following verse, some take the phrase as a reference to the signs given to the Prophet Muhammad, including the indications that he would migrate to Madinah and find help there, with the disputes then belonging to the Jews of Madinah who rejected his prophethood out of jealousy. The first reading holds the passage together as a single account of one people's history; the second explains why the sūrah turns to the Prophet immediately afterwards. The first is the stronger fit for the verse as written, since its pronouns stay with the Israelites throughout, and the second is a legitimate application of the pattern rather than a rendering of the words.

**The Judge Who Does Not Need the Disputants' Consent**

The verse ends by stating that God will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences. That is the answer to a division which no human court could settle, since the parties each claimed the same authority and each rejected the other's evidence. The Book offers the same comfort and the same warning elsewhere, saying that God will decide between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute (2:113; 32:25). The parties to a religious dispute are not told that their disagreement will be tidied away in this world, or that one side will be vindicated in a manner visible to all. They are told that the case is already docketed, that the judge saw everything, and that no argument made from envy will survive the hearing.

**The Pattern That Watches the Reader**

This verse is not only an indictment of a people who lived long ago. It describes a mechanism that operates wherever a community inherits a revelation: knowledge arrives, authority attaches to those who hold it, and the holders begin to compete in ways the knowledge does not authorise. The sūrah's first readers had just been reminded of what was given to Israel, and the next verse turns to the Prophet with a command about the road he is to follow. The warning is placed between the two so that no reader can dismiss it as ancient history. A community that splits over the meaning of a clear text, in a dispute driven by standing rather than evidence, has reproduced the very pattern the verse condemns, and can expect the same hearing.

**Why the Book Repeats This Diagnosis**

The same finding about division after knowledge is stated in half a dozen chapters, which means the Book is teaching a recognisable mechanism rather than recording one dispute. The repetition also crosses the boundaries between communities: the Israelites who quarrelled over the Torah, the Christians who quarrelled over the Gospel, and the Muslims warned not to become like those who split into sects after clear proofs had come to them (3:105). Each community receives a clear text, sets up teachers of it, and then begins to fight over who will speak for it. The pattern is so reliable that the Qur'an can describe a group who split apart centuries after their own scripture arrived and expect the reader to recognise the sequence instantly. The verse holds that continuity up to the sūrah's audience as a mirror rather than an accusation directed only at people long dead.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:18

> Now We have set you ˹O Prophet˺ on the ˹clear˺ Way of faith. So follow it, and do not follow the desires of those who do not know ˹the truth˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Means a Road Before It Means a Law**

The word rendered here as the Way of faith is sharīʿah, and this is the only place the noun occurs in the Qur'an. Its earlier sense is concrete: a track leading to water, the kind of path that a traveller in the Arabian desert needed in order to survive. That image is not lost in the verse. The Prophet is described as being placed on a road, and the road leads to something that keeps a person alive. Later Muslim usage made the word the standard term for the revealed law as a whole, which is a natural development of the image but which can obscure what the verse is doing. Here the emphasis falls on the path itself, on its clarity, and on the water at the end of it rather than on the body of rulings that later generations would derive.

**Placed on the Road, Not Asked to Build It**

The verb is decisive: We have set you on it. The road is not the Prophet's invention, and the verse does not present him as a thinker who worked out a religious system and then recommended it. God placed him on an existing path, the same path travelled by the messengers before him, and the word for placement suggests deliberate appointment rather than accidental arrival. The same construction appears elsewhere when the Book speaks of God placing a people in a land or placing an authority on the earth. A person placed somewhere has been given a position rather than a project, and the work that follows is the work of remaining there. That is the picture the verse paints of prophethood: not authorship of a way but firm footing on one.

**Command That Follows the Gift**

The imperative comes second: so follow it. A road is no use to someone who stands still at its head, and the verse is explicit that the gift of being guided does not remove the obligation to walk. The Book treats this combination consistently. Guidance is granted, and the guided person still has to act on what he was shown; the two are never presented as alternatives. The command also sets the direction of travel for everything else in the passage. If the road is given, then the disputes of the previous verse look different, because a community with a clear path in front of it has no need to argue about which way to face. The verse addresses the Prophet directly and, through him, everyone who accepts that the path is there.

**The Desires of Those Who Do Not Know**

The prohibition is stated in the plural: do not follow the desires of those who do not know. The Arabic word for desires carries the sense of inclinations that pull a person downward, and the Qur'an uses it for the appetites that shape a mind before evidence can reach it. The people in view are not described as enemies or criminals. They are described as people without knowledge, which makes the warning sharper rather than softer, because an argument can be answered while an appetite cannot. The Book returns to this command repeatedly, telling the Prophet that he will never satisfy the People of the Book until he follows their religion (2:120), that each community has been given its own code and way (5:48), that he should not follow the desires of the ignorant (42:15), and that he should bow to no one's demand for compromise (68:8–9). The danger is not only intellectual. It is social, and it arrives as an invitation to be reasonable.

**A Reported Offer and a Standing Warning**

Commentators report that the verse was revealed after leaders of the Quraysh approached the Prophet with an offer: they would follow him if he returned to the religion of his forefathers, whom they maintained had been better men than he was. The report is reported rather than established, but it matches the shape of the command, which answers an offer rather than a philosophical position. The pressure described is not the pressure to accept an argument; it is the pressure to stop making trouble, to trim the message for the sake of agreement, to grant the old ways the respect they had always been given. Every generation of believers faces some version of that offer, and the verse's answer is a flat refusal, expressed as a command about following rather than as an argument about who is right.

**"Those Who Do Not Know" — a Description, Not an Insult**

The verse does not say that its opponents are stupid or that their position has been refuted. It says that they do not know, which in the Qur'an's vocabulary is a factual description of a person whose judgments are being formed by something other than the evidence. The same Book says elsewhere that assumption cannot replace the truth, and it distinguishes carefully between those who are unaware and those who have been shown and have refused. The distinction preserves the possibility of argument in both directions: a person who does not know can come to know, and the verse's command to follow the path does not forbid explaining it. What it forbids is letting the desires of the uninformed set the agenda for the one who has been placed on the road.

**One Road, Many Codes, One Destination**

The Prophet is not the first traveller on the path. The Qur'an says that God placed him on a clear way just as he had placed the messengers before him, and it also says that each community was given a code of law and a way of life (5:48), which means the different codes were never meant to be melted into a single legal system by human negotiation. What they share is the destination, and what distinguishes them is the guidance granted to each people in its own time. This is why the verse can command following without commanding isolation. The Prophet is told to walk the road assigned to him and to refuse the desires of those who do not know, at a time when the roads of the People of the Book were still in use and their scholars were still respected. A road is followed, not negotiated, and no traveller improves his position by turning back.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:19

> They certainly can be of no benefit to you against Allah whatsoever. Surely the wrongdoers are patrons of each other, whereas Allah is the Patron of the righteous.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict on the Alliance**

The verse follows a prohibition with a fact. The Prophet has just been told not to follow the desires of those who do not know, and the reassurance that follows is not an argument but a statement about what those people can actually do. They can be of no benefit against God at all. In the society of Makkah, this was the practical question. A man who broke with the powerful families of the city lost what protected him, and no amount of doctrinal correctness replaced a patron with men and money. The verse answers that anxiety directly. Whatever the alliance was supposed to purchase, it cannot purchase anything at the moment when purchases matter, and the person who holds it is holding a document that will not be honoured.

**Patronage as a System, Named Plainly**

The Book seizes the language of tribal protection and turns it into a description of the two camps. Patrons, in this kind of Arabic, are the people who stand for you: relatives, allies, sponsors, whoever will answer when your name is called. The verse says the wrongdoers are patrons of one another, which is a statement of fact about how the world arranges itself, and then it says that God is the patron of the righteous, which is a statement of allegiance. There is no third position, because everyone is protected by someone, and the only real question is by whom. The Book makes the alternatives concrete elsewhere when it describes the believers as guardians of one another (9:71) and when it warns the believers that if they do not act as allies to each other there will be oppression in the land (8:73).

**What the Muslims Risked by Refusing**

The stakes should not be softened. A tribe's protection was a person's insurance, and the Muslims who broke with their clans were not choosing a metaphor. They were choosing exposure: no guarantee of safety on the road, no one obliged to avenge them, no place to stand when a powerful man took offence. The Qur'an meets that risk with the claim that they had acquired a patron who cannot be outmatched. This is not a promise of safety in this world — the sūrah already knows the fate of people who were destroyed for their message — but it is a promise that their protection is not a local arrangement dependent on the health of a clan or the mood of a chief. The verse honours the sacrifice by taking the security question seriously and answering it.

**Two Networks Set Side by Side**

The sentence is constructed as a comparison, and the comparison is not symmetrical. On one side stands a network of the wrongdoers, people bound to each other by interest. On the other stands a single patron, who is named with the definite article: the patron of the righteous. The first network is horizontal and needs constant maintenance; its members protect each other because each one may need protection later. The second relationship is vertical and does not depend on reciprocity, since the one who gives the protection needs nothing from those he shields. The Book draws the contrast in other places as well, reminding its readers that the disbelievers are guardians of one another (8:73), and warning them not to take guardians from among those who would not hesitate to ruin them (5:51).

**"Against Allah" — the Phrase That Settles the Debate**

The preposition carries the whole argument. The allies in question might well be useful against a rival tribe, a lawsuit, or an accusation in the market, and the verse does not deny it. What it denies is usefulness against God. This is the only sort of help that finally matters, because the fate of every soul is in a single hand, and no network extends far enough to reach it. The same point is made to the Prophet when he is told that the People of the Book would not be satisfied with him unless he followed their religion, and that if he did, no protector or helper would be found for him against God (2:120). The logic is the same in both verses: any alliance that involves disobeying God purchases a benefit that God can withdraw in an instant.

**The Quality That Qualifies for Protection**

The righteous, in this verse, are those characterised by taqwā: a carefulness that keeps a person within limits out of reverence, and the Book uses the term for conduct rather than for lineage. This is the answer to the tribal premise. In the older system, protection was a matter of birth; in the verse, patronage is a matter of devotion, and a person can enter it by choice. The point recurs through the sūrah, which keeps opposing the claims people make by descent to the deeds they actually perform. When God is described as the patron of the righteous, the sentence quietly announces that the leading men of the Quraysh have no claim they can present by name, and that a new community is being built on the only title that survives the grave.

**Loyalty Is Never Neutral**

A reader might treat patronage as a private matter of preference, and the verse will not allow it. Every person is aligned with some network, and the alignment shows itself in whose interest he defends when the two conflict. What the chapter has been building toward is the recognition that this alignment is the real content of a person's religion: whoever is followed when it costs something is the god that person serves. The righteous in this verse are not simply the people who hold a correct opinion. They are the people whose patron is God, and whose conduct shows it, and the sūrah's next verse restates what such people have been given.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:20

> This ˹Quran˺ is an insight for humanity—a guide and mercy for people of sure faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Insight: a Book That Lets a Person See**

The word rendered here as insight is the plural of an Arabic term for inner sight, the faculty by which a person perceives what is actually there rather than merely registering what is in front of him. The Qur'an applies the same word to itself in two other places, calling itself an insight from the Lord that is a guide and a mercy for those who believe (7:203), and saying of itself that it is an insight to humanity, a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing (3:138). The claim is not modest. A text that supplies insights is claiming to repair the reader's perception, and the chapter has been arguing since its third verse that the created world is full of indications. What was available to the eye in the sky is available to the understanding in the recitation, and this verse names the second kind of seeing.

**"For Humanity" — the Widening and the Narrowing**

The sentence opens wide and then narrows, and both halves matter. Insight is described as being for people in general, without qualification, so no nation or class can claim the Book as private property. Then the guidance and mercy that flow from it are attached to people of sure faith, which describes a state of conviction rather than a category of birth. The Book makes the same universal claim about the Prophet's mission in other places, saying that he was sent to all of humanity as a warner and a bringer of good news (34:28) and that he was sent as a mercy to the whole world (21:107). The offer is public; the benefit is received by those who take it seriously. That combination is the ordinary pattern of the Qur'an's invitations, and it appears here without defence or explanation.

**Guide and Mercy in the Same Breath**

The two nouns are not synonyms and the pairing is deliberate. Guidance supplies direction, and mercy supplies what a person needs after he has been told where to go, including forgiveness for the failures that follow. The Book describes itself in similar pairs elsewhere: it has come as a warning, a cure for what is in the hearts, a guide, and a mercy for the believers (10:57); those who receive it discover a guide and a healing (41:44); and it restates the claim in the direction of patience and forgiveness when the Prophet is told that God's promise is true (40:55). The order in this verse is the order of experience. A person receives direction first, and what he finds along the road afterwards is the mercy that keeps him travelling.

**What Certainty Adds to the Offer**

The qualifier is not a formality. The verse says the mercy belongs to people of sure faith, and the Book explains in other passages what happens when the same recitation reaches a person who has decided in advance. The words heal and raise the believers and increase the wrongdoers in loss (17:82). Nothing in the text changes between the two cases, since the same recitation is heard by both. What changes is the receiver, and the Qur'an's habit of attaching an audience to its promises is its way of saying that the offer is not a mechanism that operates without consent. Certainty, in the Book's usage, is not a feeling of confidence; it is the settled state of a person who has finished weighing the evidence and has decided where he stands.

**Insight Is Not the Same as Information**

A reader can accumulate a great deal of information about the Qur'an and still miss the insight the verse describes, because insight is what happens when facts about the world and facts about the text are brought together in a single mind. The chapter has been building exactly that combination: signs in the creation, then signs in the recitation, and now a claim about what the two produce when they meet. Information about the sky does not by itself produce reverence, and information about the Qur'an does not by itself produce obedience. The insight of this verse is the perceived connection between them, and the Book presents itself as the agent that makes the connection available to anyone willing to look at both.

**The Book Named at the Chapter's Centre**

This verse sits at the middle of the sūrah, and it restates in summary form what the chapter has been arguing since its opening letters. After the letters come the names of God, then the world as evidence, then the recitation that reports the evidence, then the deniers who refuse it, and now one sentence that gathers the whole offer: this is insight, guidance, and mercy. The placement gives the reader a resting point before the sūrah turns to the arithmetic of good and evil and to the arguments of those who deny the resurrection. Everything before this verse explained what the Book is; everything after it will show what happens when it is rejected. A summary in the middle of an argument is a rare thing in scripture, and its presence here tells the reader that the essentials have now been stated.

**Mercy, Not a Burden**

The Book calls itself mercy, and the choice of word answers a suspicion that its commands invite. Guidance, heard alone, can sound like an imposition of rules by a power that has no interest in the people being ruled; mercy names the purpose behind the rules. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the Prophet himself as a mercy sent to the world, and it treats the revelation as a benefit rather than a test imposed for its own sake. The verse does not deny that the guidance will be demanding. It asserts that the demands are directed at the good of the person on whom they are made, which is why they can be described with the same word used for rain reviving dead ground.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:21

> Or do those who commit evil deeds ˹simply˺ think that We will make them equal—in their life and after their death—to those who believe and do good? How wrong is their judgment!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Equivalence Nobody States Out Loud**

The verse opens with a question that proposes something absurd in order to expose it. It asks whether those who commit evil deeds imagine they will be placed on the same footing as those who believe and do good. Very few people would put the claim into words, which is why the Book puts it for them and then condemns it. What the verse is describing is not a doctrine but a hope: that the moral difference between two lives makes no difference in the end, that the world treats everyone alike and will go on doing so. The question is built to make that hope visible to the person holding it. Once stated, the proposition looks as unreasonable as it is, and the reader who recognises his own unspoken expectation has been answered before he can defend it.

**Two Readings of the Word for Equal**

The transmission of the verse includes a small grammatical difference that changes the shape of the question. In one well-attested reading the word functions as an adverb attached to the verb, so that the sentence asks whether God will treat the two groups equally in their life and their death. In the other reading, the word is a predicate, so the question becomes whether the life and death of the evildoers are equivalent to the life and death of the believers. The commentators report both and note that the second reading shifts the focus onto the two states themselves, meaning that the believer and the denier will not be on the same footing in the Hereafter, whatever appearance this world gives. Both readings reach the same conclusion, and the second adds the point that the difference will become visible where it now is not.

**Equality in This World or the Next**

The phrase "in their life and after their death" is the verse's decisive clause, and it covers the whole span a person can argue about. If the evildoer's life is indistinguishable from the good man's, and if their deaths are indistinguishable too, then the moral order the prophets announce has left no trace anywhere. The Qur'an answers by asserting the difference that the present world conceals. It says that God created the heavens and the earth for a purpose, precisely so that every soul may be paid back for what it has done, and that no one will be wronged. It also gives the settlement of the dispute in a single sentence elsewhere: on the Day of Judgment the two groups will be ranked according to what they have done, so that the full reward may be given, and no one will be wronged (46:19).

**Judging by the Only World That Can Be Seen**

The mistake the verse identifies is a method: reading the value of a life off its visible circumstances. The prosperous denier and the suffering believer have always existed side by side, and the observation that they often end the same way is accurate on the evidence available to an observer who ignores everything else. What the verse denies is that this is the whole evidence, or that the visible world is the standard by which the invisible is settled. The Book's answer to the problem in other passages is a warning about the length of time involved, and a reminder that the day of settlement has not arrived. Until then, the appearances remain appearances, and the difference the verse asserts is being accumulated out of sight.

**Why the Book Refuses to Flatten the Difference**

The Qur'an will not grant the premise that good and evil are variations on a single theme. It keeps its two categories distinct and ranks them in the plainest words: no soul will be wronged on that Day, and each person will be repaid exactly for what he did (36:54); whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (99:7–8). This is not severity for its own sake. If the two ends were the same, the moral effort of a lifetime would be meaningless, and the sacrifices the sūrah has been asking of its readers, including forgiveness when they have been wronged, would be pointless courtesy. The distinction between the two groups is what makes the guidance of the previous verse worth accepting.

**A Judgment Called Wicked, Not Merely Mistaken**

The verse closes with a verdict on the verdict. It does not say that those who think this way are confused or under-informed; the word used pronounces their judgment to be a corruption of the faculty itself. The Qur'an uses the same root when it speaks of the standards by which people weigh things, and the criticism lands on the process rather than on a single conclusion. A person whose weights are wrong will get every subsequent measurement wrong, and this is how the Book treats a mind that has decided the moral difference between lives does not matter. The error is not a gap in information that a new fact could close. It is a settled disposition that inverts the scale, and the verse names it before moving to the argument that corrects it.

**What the Deniers Have Correctly Observed**

The verse does not accuse its opponents of being unable to see what is in front of them. The world really does treat the good and the wicked alike in many respects, and the same sun rises on both; the rain falls on the just and the unjust, as the sūrah's own passage on provision insisted. What the deniers do not see is what follows from that fact. The Qur'an grants the observation and denies the conclusion, and the argument it makes is that a world arranged this way tests people rather than settling their value. The settlement is a separate event, announced and delayed, and the sūrah turns next to the purpose for which the whole creation was made.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:22

> For Allah created the heavens and the earth for a purpose, so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed. And none will be wronged.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation With an Aim**

The verse gives a reason for the difference the previous verse asserted, and it grounds the reason in cosmology. The heavens and the earth were not made for sport; they were made for a purpose, and the phrase translated "for a purpose" is the one the Book uses when it insists that the created order is not a game. The same claim is stated across many chapters: the heavens and the earth and everything between them were not created in play (44:38–39); they were created with a purpose and a fixed term (46:3); and the alternation of sun and moon exists so that people may count years and reckon time (10:5). The handful of passages that deny a purposeless creation are the Book's answer to any philosophy that treats the world as an accident of matter. The order a person sees in the sky is the visible edge of an intention.

**The Purpose Named: Repayment**

The verse does not leave the aim undefined; it names it. The creation exists so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed. This is a startling order of explanation, because it makes the moral accounting of human beings part of the reason for the physical universe. The exegetes take the verse in close connection with the dispute it answers: since things are made in truth and not at random, the lives of the believers and the deniers cannot be interchangeable. The pairing of a vast creation with an individual's deeds runs through the Book, and it rests on the premise that the two are connected. If the world has a purpose, then what people do inside it matters to that purpose, and the accounting follows.

**"What It Has Committed" — the Language of Earning**

The verb used for the accounting describes earning, and it carries the sense of work that attaches to the worker. The Book uses it whenever it wants to make the connection between a deed and its owner immediate, as when it says that no soul will be wronged in the least on that Day and that people will be rewarded only for what they used to do (36:54). The two groups of the previous verse are ranked according to what they have done, and the ranking is described as full payment (46:19). The metaphor throughout is commercial and it is not accidental. The first audience understood wages, debts, and balances, and the Qur'an presents the Hereafter as an account that is settled exactly, neither inflated nor discounted.

**"None Will Be Wronged" — the Guarantee**

The verse ends with an assurance that the Book repeats more often than almost any other: no one will be treated unjustly. The phrase appears in the same form in the passage on the two groups and, in a fuller scene, on the day when the record of deeds is laid open and the wicked cry out that it has left nothing out, small or great, and they find everything they did present before them, and the verse states that the Lord will never wrong anyone (18:49). The repetition has a function. The Book is describing a court that will rule on every case at once, and the natural human fear is that such a court cannot be fair to everyone. The assurance is that the judge has full knowledge and perfect justice, so the fear is misplaced.

**Equality Denied by the Purpose Itself**

Read against the previous verse, this sentence is the whole answer to the deniers' assumption. If the creation exists in order to repay each soul for what it has earned, then a final equality between the good and the wicked would defeat the purpose for which the universe was made. The two claims cannot both stand, and the verse gives the one that can. What the deniers were hoping for was not merely a reprieve for themselves; it was the abolition of the distinction that gives their lives weight. The Book's reply is that the distinction is built into the fabric of things and will be honoured at the end, whether or not the parties to the dispute accept it now.

**A World That Is Not Indifferent**

Most people live as though the question of the world's purpose were theoretical, and the verse makes it practical. A universe with a purpose is a universe in which nothing done inside it is lost; a universe without one is a place where the difference between cruelty and kindness is a matter of preference. The sūrah's readers had already been told that the sea was subjected for them and that the created order was given by grace. This verse completes the picture by saying what the gift was for. Everything was made so that a person's choices could be weighed in a setting that gives them weight. It is the last claim a denier wants to hear and the only claim that makes the rest of the message worth the cost of accepting it.

**The Purpose Hidden Until It Is Enforced**

The verse states the aim in the present tense as a fact, and the enforcement of it belongs to a day the sūrah has already mentioned and will describe in detail later. Between the statement and its execution lies the period in which every human being acts, and the Book treats that interval as the whole point of the delay. If the purpose were enforced immediately, the choices would lose their character as choices. The verse does not present the delay as divine indifference or as forgetfulness, since it has just said that the repayment is certain. It presents the world as an arranged place with a fixed schedule, in which the only unknown is what each person will do before the schedule moves forward.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:23

> Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and hearts, and placed a cover on their sight. Who then can guide them after Allah? Will you ˹all˺ not then be mindful?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A God Made of Appetite**

The image is exact and shocking: a person takes his own desire as a god, and the Book treats this as an accurate description rather than an insult. A god is what a person orients his life around, what he will not cross, and what he sacrifices for. On that definition, a man whose decisions are set by appetite has a deity, and it lives inside him: it commands, he obeys, and it is never satisfied. Idols of stone at least stay where they are put. The Arabic word used here for desires is the same word used in the previous verse's command not to follow the inclinations of those who do not know, and the sūrah has been closing in on this condition for several verses. What the Book has been describing as a refusal of evidence is named in this verse as a form of worship.

**The Word That Means Blowing and Falling**

The Arabic term for desire comes from a root whose verbs mean to blow, as wind does, and to fall, as a body does from a height. The lexicographers of the classical tradition used the connection to describe a soul blown in one direction and then another, with no fixed point, and they linked the noun for the abyss, the lowest depth a person can reach, to the same root. The Qur'an speaks of hearts that are empty on the Day of Judgment, using the same letters in the sense of a void, and it describes the abyss as the final resting place of those whose scales are light. The associations are not fanciful readings imposed on a word. They spell out the difference between a life anchored somewhere and a life that is moved by whatever wind arrives.

**Led Astray After the Choice**

The verse's sequence is the crux of a long theological argument, and the text states it in order: they took their desires as gods, and then God left them to stray, with knowledge. The Book's own reading of such statements is given elsewhere when it says that God misleads none but the rebellious. The Ashʿarī and Muʿtazilī schools of theology drew opposite conclusions from verses of this kind — one holding that God's guidance and misguidance are his direct and unconstrained acts, the other arguing that his leaving a person to stray always follows a prior human decision. The order of this verse supports the second reading, since the taking of desire as a god is placed first, and the leaving to stray is stated as a consequence with the word "so" attached to it. What God knows is not the same as what God forces.

**Three Faculties Closed**

The verse names hearing, the heart, and sight, which are the organs by which a person receives almost everything. The order is not random: the ear takes in the recitation, the heart processes it, and the eye sees the signs in the world. A cover placed over all three describes a person who still possesses the equipment and cannot use it for the purpose it was made for. The Book returns to the image repeatedly for those who have refused to use their faculties on the evidence in front of them, and it states the rule of the arrangement in another chapter: this is how God seals the hearts of those unwilling to know the truth (30:59). The sealing in this verse is described as arriving after the refusal, and the condition is therefore a consequence, not a cause.

**The Question No One Can Answer**

"Who then can guide them after God?" The question is not an invitation to look for another teacher; it is a closing of the door. If the source of guidance has left a person to wander, no human effort recovers the ground that was lost, and the Book makes the same point elsewhere when it says that whoever God allows to stray has no guide and is left wandering blindly in his defiance (7:186). The verse gives the readers of the chapter something to consider, since the risk it describes is not reserved for the Quraysh. A person who repeatedly sets appetite above evidence is being described in the process of becoming the man in this verse, and no appointment with a scholar will reverse what has already been settled in the heart.

**The Call to Remember**

The verse ends by asking whether anyone will take the lesson to heart, and the verb behind the question means to recall and be reminded of something already known. The Book does not address its readers as people who have never heard anything true. It addresses them as people who know, and who are being asked to bring what they know back to mind. That is the pattern of the whole passage: signs in the sky, signs in the body, signs in the weather, and then a portrait of a man who has turned all of it into a servant of his appetite. The question at the end of the verse assumes the reader is not yet that man, and the assumption is the last invitation the verse offers.

**A Portrait With a Purpose**

The command "have you seen" is addressed to the Prophet, and through him to whoever reads the chapter. The purpose of the portrait is not curiosity about a character type. It is recognition. The sūrah has already shown what the signs are for and what the Book claims about itself, and this verse shows the condition of the person who has turned from all of it by slow degrees, one preference at a time. A reader who finds the portrait uncomfortable is exactly where the verse intends him to be, and the discomfort is the point of the description: the man has no excuse in his circumstances, since he was given the same hearing, the same heart, and the same sight as everyone else.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:24

> And they argue, “There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time.” Yet they have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this ˹claim˺. They only speculate.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opponents Are Allowed to Speak**

The verse hands the argument to the deniers and lets them state their case without interruption. This is the Book's regular method, and the position is stated here with unusual clarity: nothing exists beyond this life, generations replace one another, and the only thing that ends a person is time. The logic is coherent on its own terms, and no caricature of it appears in the sentence. The Qur'an records its opponents' arguments in their own words before answering them, which is one reason its replies have force. A reader who expects scripture to defeat a straw man will find instead that the case for disbelief has been given at full strength, in a form its holders would have recognised as fair.

**"We Die, and Others Are Born"**

The first half of the claim describes the world accurately as far as it goes. Individuals die, and the species continues; a generation leaves the stage and another arrives to fill it. The same words appear in another chapter's report of the same denial, where the claim ends with the assertion that none will be resurrected (23:37). What the statement leaves out is the person. A cycle that replaces human beings as a field replaces its crops is a description of continuity, not of destiny, and it answers nothing about the fate of the particular man who is making the argument. The verse does not dispute the biology. It lets the claim stand and moves to its second half, where the real error lies.

**"Nothing Destroys Us but Time"**

The Arabic word here for time is dahr, and the phrase names a habit well known among the Arabs of the Prophet's lifetime: when hardship came, people cursed time itself, treating it as the agent that had struck them down. The practice was serious enough that the Prophet was instructed to forbid it, and the report is established in the two most authoritative collections. Abu Hurayrah narrates that the Messenger of God said: "The son of Adam hurts Me for he abuses Time though I am Time: in My Hands are all things, and I cause the revolution of day and night." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4826; the same meaning is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2246). The point of the prohibition is precise. Time is a measure of what happens, not a cause, and abuse aimed at it travels past it to the one who actually does the deeds.

**What the Deniers Have Described Correctly**

Time does swing between people, and the years do end the lives of the strong as well as the weak. The Book itself presents the alternation of day and night as one of the signs that reveal the creator, and the sūrah's own fifth verse used it that way. The deniers have taken an accurate observation and attached an agent to it that is not there. Every human system does something similar: effects are credited to the nearest visible cause, and the cause behind the cause is set aside, because acknowledging it would carry obligations. The verse's criticism is therefore not that the deniers described a world of change. It is that they stopped at the description and named the wrong author.

**"They Have No Knowledge" — an Epistemic Verdict**

The sentence that follows the quotation is a judgment about evidence rather than a denial of competence. The deniers have stated what happens after death without access to the information; they have asserted a negative about the unseen with no more support than the person who asserts a positive. The Book delivers the same verdict on the same kind of claim twice more, saying that people follow nothing but inherited assumptions and that assumption cannot replace the truth (53:28), and that those who argue this way have no knowledge to support the claim, and produce nothing but lies (43:20). The criticism is not that they are unintelligent. It is that they have pronounced on a matter on which no one can pronounce without being told, and have treated their guesswork as a settled report.

**Conjecture Is Not Humility**

A person might answer that his position is at least modest, since he asserts only what he can see. The verse denies the modesty. A negative claim about what follows death — nothing, forever — is as large as a positive one, and it carries the burden of proof that the positive claim carries. The refusal to believe is not the suspension of judgment; it is a judgment, made in the same courtroom as the position it rejects and on the same evidence. The sūrah has already shown what was available to these people: a sky, a body, weather, and a recitation, all pointing in a single direction. Their speculation is described as the product of a will that had already selected its conclusion.

**The Answer the Sūrah Will Give**

The Book's counterargument to this verse is not a philosophical demonstration but a statement about who holds life and death, which the following verses supply. Until that answer arrives, the chapter has set the debate up as clearly as possible: a group asserting that nothing survives the grave, without evidence; a revelation asserting that everything does, on the basis of the testimony of the one who made both the grave and what follows it; and a reader who is asked to weigh the two. The sūrah leaves him in no doubt about which side it considers honest, because it applies the word conjecture to one of them and reserves the word knowledge for the other.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:25

> And whenever Our clear revelations are recited to them, their only argument is to say: “Bring our forefathers back, if what you say is true!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Answer They Have Left**

The verse says their argument amounts to nothing else, and the phrase is a verdict as much as a description. The recitation comes as clear proofs, in the ordinary language of the audience, from a man they can question; the Book leaves the reader in no doubt that the case has been made and that nothing has been presented against it. What remains is a rebuttal that is not a rebuttal: a demand for a demonstration. In an argument, a person who cannot answer the claim can always test the claimant, and this verse shows the move being made. The Qur'an's response is to let the demand stand in the record without flinching. It does not treat the challenge as a serious rival to the proofs, and it does not pretend that the proofs needed the challenge to be answered first.

**"Bring Our Forefathers Back"**

The demand is precise: produce the ancestors, and then we will believe. The same challenge is reported in another chapter in almost the same words, where the deniers say to the Prophet, "Bring ˹back˺ our forefathers, if what you say is true" (44:36). The demand has the appearance of empirical rigour, and that appearance is what makes it attractive to its makers. It asks for a public event with witnesses, something no one could dispute, and it treats the absence of such an event as decisive. But the request has already assumed what it is supposed to test. If the dead are raised only when God decides, and if the raising of a generation of ancestors is not on the schedule of the world, then the absence of such a spectacle proves nothing about the claim being made. The demand is not an experiment. It is a refusal dressed as one.

**What the Demand Reveals About Their Picture**

A deeper problem sits inside the challenge, and the Book's answer exposes it. The deniers were asking for the dead to be returned to this life as it is, walking among them, taking up their old positions. The resurrection the Qur'an announces is not a return to this world but a transition to another order, in which the record is read and the account settled. To ask for the fathers to be produced in the marketplace is to treat the Hereafter as a spectacle within history, and it cannot be granted on those terms because those are not its terms. The confusion is not a small one. It is the difference between adding more years to a life that has already been lived and answering for the life that was lived, and the verse's silence about the demand is a way of showing that the request was addressed to the wrong question.

**No Evidence Would Have Settled the Matter**

The Book is consistent about what happens when proof is granted to a heart that has already decided. The sūrah has already described people who hear the recitation, understand it, and walk away with their posture intact, and it has promised them a punishment for exactly that. In the chapter's account, the problem was never a lack of evidence. The heavens, the rains, the winds, and the reader's own body were already in place as signs, the recitation was in place as a clear proof, and the demand was still being made. What the deniers wanted was not information but a spectacle that would leave them free of the obligation the information carried. A person in that position can always ask for one more demonstration, and the requests can continue indefinitely, which is why the Book treats the demand as a symptom rather than a question.

**The Answer Already Present in the World**

While the challenge waits for a resurrection it will not see, the chapter has already pointed at the annual one. The rain descends on ground that was dead and the ground comes back to life, and the sūrah named that revival among its signs in its fifth verse. The Book uses the comparison deliberately, and other chapters draw the conclusion out loud, saying that the one who gives life to the earth after its death is capable of raising the dead. The deniers had seen the argument every year of their lives and had not treated it as one, because it did not arrive in the shape they had specified. The verse's report of the demand is therefore also a report about a kind of attention that had already stopped looking.

**A Demand That Outlives Its First Speakers**

The verse opens with the word that means whenever, and the choice is important. The demand it records was not a single event in the Prophet's career; it is the standing posture of a mind that has decided in advance what kind of evidence it will accept. Every generation reproduces it in its own idiom, asking for a sign on its own terms, in a form it has selected, at a time of its choosing. The Book treats the request as an old argument with new speakers, and the sūrah's silence about the result of the challenge is part of its answer. The question was never whether the demand could be met. It was whether the people making it would still be standing in the same posture when the answer came, and the next verse tells them who holds the dead.

**The Poverty of a Demand Without a Case**

There is a final observation in the shape of the sentence. The verse does not say the deniers made a weak argument; it says they made no argument at all, and the word for argument here carries the sense of a thing said merely in order to be saying something. What they offer is not a counter-claim about the nature of the world, not an alternative account of death, and not an objection to the moral content of the message. It is a condition: prove it my way and I will consider it. The Book records the condition and moves on, and the reader who has followed the sūrah from the letters at its opening to this point knows that the case for the resurrection has already been laid out and that the demand has not touched it.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:26

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ gives you life, then causes you to die, then will gather you ˹all˺ on the Day of Judgment, about which there is no doubt. But most people do not know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply Put in the Prophet's Mouth**

The verse is issued as a command: say. The Prophet is not told to argue, to rebut the challenge of the previous verse point by point, or to plead for a hearing. He is given a sentence to deliver, and the sentence contains the whole answer. This is the Qur'an's regular way of handling a hostile question. The messenger states what is true about God and lets the statement do its work, and the response of the audience is not his responsibility. A reader who has watched the demand for the ancestors to be produced might expect a defence of the possibility of resurrection. What arrives instead is an assertion about who gives life in the first place, and the shift is the answer.

**Life and Death as the Work of One Hand**

The two verbs are placed together as a single act of a single agent. This is the direct reply to the claim that nothing destroys people except the passage of time. If the same power that gives life also takes it, then time is not the destroyer; it is the interval, and the one who set the interval is also the one who ends the life inside it. The Book states the pairing in verse after verse, as when it says that God is the one who gives life and causes death (53:44), when it describes him as the one who gives life and causes death and who only has to say "Be!" for a matter to exist (40:68), and when it says that he created death and life as a test of who would act best (67:2). The denial of the resurrection begins with a misreading of the most ordinary fact available: that a person did not give himself life and cannot keep it.

**The Gathering After the Dying**

The third clause of the sentence moves past the point at which the deniers stopped. After life and death comes the gathering on the Day of Judgment, and the Book attaches the same certainty to it in other places: God has taken it upon himself to be merciful, and he will certainly gather all of humanity together on that Day about which there is no doubt (6:12). The order of the three clauses is the order of the argument. The first two are uncontested facts that the deniers themselves had admitted in different words; the third follows from the nature of the one who performs the first two. If a person's life is given and taken by a power outside himself, the question of what happens to him afterwards is not settled by the observation that his body lies in the ground.

**"About Which There Is No Doubt"**

The phrase is not a rhetorical flourish; it describes a property of the Day itself rather than the state of mind of the people hearing about it. The Hour is certain, and the uncertainty belongs entirely to those who doubt it. The Book holds this line consistently, describing the Hour as coming without warning and asking why people question it, and it treats the confidence of the believer as a share in a fact rather than a feeling of optimism. The sentence in this verse therefore answers the sceptic's posture rather than his objection. He had asked for proof that the dead can return. The reply informs him that the event has an appointed time, that its occurrence is not in question, and that his inability to schedule it does not affect the case.

**"But Most People Do Not Know"**

The clause appears throughout the Book in nearly the same wording, and it serves as a frame for a certain kind of fact: people do not know about the Hour (7:187), most people do not know that the Prophet was sent to all of humanity (34:28), and most do not know that God gives life and death. The repetition is not contempt for the human race; it is an accurate description of a majority condition on matters of the unseen, and the Book uses it to prepare the believer for being in the minority. The sentence is delivered to an audience that had just made a demand, and the clause tells them, in plain language, what their position amounts to. They are not the holders of a difficult truth. They are the people who do not know.

**The Answer to the Demand for a Sign**

The challenge was to bring the ancestors back so that belief could follow. The answer names the one who has them, and who gives and takes according to a schedule none of the challengers controls. No miracle is staged, and none is promised. What the verse offers instead is an ownership claim: the dead are not in the hands of the men who demand them, and the Day on which they will all appear has already been set. The Book's restraint here is the point. A revelation that produced a demonstration to order in the market would have made belief a function of spectacle, and the sūrah has spent its first half arguing that the evidence has already been provided in the created order.

**A Message the Majority Would Not Accept**

The three facts of the verse are simple, and the verse itself says most people will not know them. The sūrah never pretends otherwise, and the next few verses turn to the scene in which the matter is settled: a kingdom that already belongs to God, an Hour that will be established, and the loss of those who worked to make the truth look false. The Prophet is instructed to deliver the sentence and to leave the outcome alone. The chapter has no interest in winning an argument in the marketplace. It is interested in stating the situation clearly, and the situation is that life, death, and judgment are all in the hands of the one who made them.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:27

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. On the Day the Hour will be established, the people of falsehood will then be in ˹total˺ loss.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Authority Stated as Ownership**

The verse opens with a claim of ownership, and the Book states it again and again in almost the same words: to God belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, and he is capable of everything (3:189); to God belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and all that is between them (5:17); to him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, and to him is the return (24:42); and to him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, forgiving whom he wills and punishing whom he wills (48:14). The repetition is not padding. It is the foundation on which every other claim in the chapter rests, because a court that will judge all things must first own all things. No part of the sky or the ground lies outside the jurisdiction that the verse announces, and the denial of that kingdom is the mistake that all the sūrah's opponents have in common.

**The Day the Hour Is Established**

The Hour, in the Book's usage, is the moment the present order ends and the accounting begins. The word for its establishment carries the sense of standing up and being set in place, as a thing that has been prepared is finally brought forward. The sūrah mentioned this Day earlier when it reassured the Prophet that the gathering would happen, and it now describes the effect of the Day on a particular class of people. The Book often presents the Hour as sudden, and just as often insists that it is fixed and known to God. Those two descriptions do not conflict: the date is set, and the people living before it will not be told when. The verse's scene therefore opens at a moment nobody can schedule and everyone will attend.

**Who the People of Falsehood Are**

The Arabic word describes those who work at making things false — the ones who try to reduce a truth to nothing, to prove that a claim has no foundation, or to show that a matter is hollow. The lexicographers of the classical dictionary tradition give all these senses, and each fits the men the sūrah has described: they mocked the signs, they demanded the ancestors be produced, and they asserted that time alone destroys. The punishment is a mirror of the offence. People who spent their lives trying to make the truth look null find themselves nullified, and the Book's wordplay is deliberate. Falsehood is not merely a mistaken belief that happens to be punished; it is an activity, and the chapter has been showing the activity in operation since its seventh verse.

**Loss Described in the Book's Own Vocabulary**

The Book has a whole vocabulary for losing, and it uses it in the commercial register the sūrah's readers knew well. Elsewhere it says that those who have ruined themselves will never believe (6:12); that those who have lost their souls will never believe (6:20); that on the Day the warning is fulfilled the wrongdoers will say that the messengers brought the truth, and will ask for intercessors and for a return, only to find that they have lost themselves and that what they fabricated has failed them (7:53; 11:21); that those whose scales are light have doomed themselves (23:103); and that the true losers are those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Judgment, which the Book calls the clearest loss (39:15; 42:45). The vocabulary is consistent because the concept is: a person has one capital, and losing it cannot be reversed.

**The Force of "Then"**

The small word rendered "then" marks a change of state rather than a delay in time. What the people of falsehood possess before the Hour may look like profit: standing, wealth, the success of their arguments in the marketplace, followers who repeat what they said. The verse says that on the day the matter is established, the appearance reverses and the loss becomes visible. It was always there, since a debt incurred is a loss recorded, but the account had not been called in. The meaning of the Hour, in this sentence, is disclosure. The same structure appears throughout the Qur'an's descriptions of the Last Day, where the purpose of the scene is not to create an outcome that was in doubt but to reveal one that was settled in advance.

**The Kingdom and the Loss in One Sentence**

The two halves of the verse belong together. Because the kingdom belongs to God, the verdict belongs to God, and because the verdict belongs to God, the loss of those who disputed it is total. A lesser court could be appealed, or its jurisdiction avoided, or its records lost. This court owns everything it judges and keeps an account of everything that was done, and the sūrah has already established both facts earlier in the chapter. The people who worked to make the truth appear hollow have been dealing with the property of a king while denying the king, and the verse closes off the possibility that the arrangement could hold: no business conducted on the king's land can be settled by denying the owner.

**A Loss Measured by What Was Fabricated**

Another chapter states the outcome in a way that completes this verse: they have ruined themselves, and whatever gods they fabricated will fail them (11:21). What a person builds against the truth is exactly what he loses, and the sūrah has already named a man who took his own desires as a god. That project has an end, and the verse before us announces it. The loss is not an arbitrary penalty imposed from outside; it is the collapse of the structure that was raised in place of the truth, and its collapse takes its builder with it. The Book's language of trade returns here at full strength: what the people of falsehood invested in is worthless, and the Day will be the moment the market finds out.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:28

> And you will see every faith-community on its knees. Every community will be summoned to its record ˹of deeds˺. ˹They all will be told,˺ “This Day you will be rewarded for what you used to do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Scene Nobody Can Stand Above**

The verse puts the whole of humanity in one posture. Every community appears on its knees, and the word translated community covers religious bodies and nations, the groups into which people have divided themselves across history. The sūrah takes its name from this scene, and the image has the effect of cancelling every distinction the earlier verses have been examining — the allies who protected one another, the leaders who held their ground, the scholars who disputed, the men who followed their appetites. All of them are kneeling. The Book elsewhere pictures a different moment in which people arrive with heads raised and eyes fixed, unable to blink, their hearts empty (14:43), and the two images belong to the same day. What the verse does with a single word is to show that the audience of the judgment is comprehensive.

**Why Kneeling**

Kneeling in the presence of a sovereign is the posture of a person who has no standing to claim. The exegetes give two reasons for the picture. Some say the kneeling expresses dread at what is being read out, since the record is about to be opened in front of everyone. Others say it expresses the humiliation of being judged after a lifetime of denial, and this fits the sūrah's earlier promise that the mockers would receive a humiliating punishment. In either reading, the posture is imposed rather than chosen, and the picture has the additional effect of making every difference of rank among the assembled irrelevant. A man may have spent his life standing over others. On that day he kneels with the rest, and his record is summoned like everyone else's.

**Summoned to Its Record**

The verse moves from the posture to the procedure. Each community is called to its book, and the Book elsewhere describes the same scene with the record laid open, the guilty afraid of what is written in it, crying out that nothing, small or great, has been left out, and finding everything they did present before them (18:49). The records are mentioned in several places: an angel is said to record every soul (86:4); the books are kept by honoured scribes who know what people do (82:10–12); a person is bound to his destiny and will be handed a record that he finds spread open (17:13); and the command is given to him to read his own book, since he is sufficient that day to reckon with himself (17:14). The image is forensic, and it is built on documents rather than on testimony.

**One Ledger, or One Book for All?**

Commentators differ about whether the singular "book" names a single register or the collection of each person's individual ledger. Some take it as the record kept for each individual by the angels at God's command, gathered for the occasion so that the community appears with all its files at once. Others take it as the Preserved Tablet, the register in which everything has been inscribed and which is described in another chapter as a well-guarded writing (85:22). The first reading explains why a community can be told to appear with its book, since the books of its members would be brought together; the second explains the singular and the thoroughness of the record. The difference does not affect the doctrine, which is that nothing has been lost, and it does not affect the verse's drama, which is that the file being read is your own.

**"This Day You Will Be Rewarded for What You Used to Do"**

The address is direct and it names the standard of payment: what you used to do, in the habitual sense the imperfect tense carries. People will not be judged for their final moments or for a single gesture made under pressure; the criterion is the pattern of a life. The Book states the same rule elsewhere, saying that no soul will be wronged in the least and that people will be rewarded only for what they used to do (36:54). The wording also closes an exit. A person cannot plead the difficulty of his circumstances, since the circumstances were part of what he used to do, nor can he plead that his intentions were good while the pattern was something else. The sentence read out on that day is drawn from the ordinary texture of a lifetime.

**Every Community, Including This One**

The verse addresses all communities, and that universality is a warning to whoever reads the chapter. The Muslim community is not exempted from the scene, and the sūrah offers no assurance that a community's book will be read leniently because of its name. The same Book criticises earlier communities which confused their standing with a guarantee, including the earlier passage in this sūrah in which the Children of Israel were given every gift and then divided out of envy. The scene of kneeling is the reply to every privilege claimed by inheritance, and the sūrah places it directly after the arguments about patronage and lineage that fill the middle of the chapter. On that day the file, not the family, determines where a person stands.

**A Hearing Rather Than a Battle**

The imagery is legal because the reality is a hearing. Records are produced, communities are summoned, and the verdict is announced in the words of a judge. Nothing in the scene suggests that anyone argues his way out of the outcome, or that the assembled crowds can influence the reading of the file. The Book's descriptions of that day rarely feature a defence, and when they do, the answer given to the accused is the record itself, which speaks with an accuracy no cross-examination can shake. The verse therefore presents the judgment as the least dramatic event imaginable: a call, a file, a sentence, and an assembly of communities on their knees while the paperwork of their lives is opened and read.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:29

> This record of Ours speaks the truth about you. Indeed, We always had your deeds recorded ˹by the angels˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Book That Speaks**

The record is not described as evidence waiting to be interpreted but as a speaker. It gives testimony, and what it says about the people standing in front of it is true in a way that cannot be corrected or softened. The Book's phrase for accuracy is repeated in several places where the outcome of a matter is announced, and the point of the metaphor is the absence of any party through whom the truth could be bent. Other passages picture the same document being handed over and read: the record is laid open and the wicked see what is in it and cry out at its completeness (18:49). What the sūrah adds here is the voice. At this hearing the file is not a silent exhibit that requires an advocate; it states the case.

**No Accuser Is Needed**

Human courts depend on witnesses, and witnesses can be frightened, bought, or destroyed. This verse removes the entire apparatus. The sentence does not say that angels will testify or that prophets will prosecute; it says the book itself speaks, and its account of the accused is true. Elsewhere the command given to a person on that day is to read his own record, with the remark that he alone is sufficient that day to take account of himself (17:14). The arrangement leaves no role for an accuser whose credibility might be challenged and no gap in which a defence could be constructed from the absence of witnesses. A person who has ever hoped that his deeds would go unrecorded is shown the document, and the document does not need anyone's help to be believed.

**What "Our Book" Claims**

The possessive is doing real work. The record belongs to God, and the scribes who filled it were acting under his command rather than in pursuit of their own interests. The Book describes them elsewhere: every soul has a vigilant angel recording what it does (86:4); the scribes are honoured and they know whatever people do (82:11–12); and two recording angels sit to the right and the left of each person, so that no word is uttered without an observer ready to write it down (50:17–18). The picture removes another possible objection, which is that the record might reflect the bias of its authors. Nothing in the account suggests that the angels interpret or evaluate what they write. They note, and the noting belongs to a system whose owner is the judge.

**A Record of Facts, Not a Verdict**

The verse says that the deeds were recorded, not that the record has determined the outcome. That distinction matters for the theology of the chapter. The book is accurate; the verdict is God's, and the Book says in many places that he forgives whom he wills and punishes whom he wills. A person whose record is full may still be shown mercy, and the Qur'an mentions believers whose evil deeds God will replace with good ones. The record is therefore indispensable but not interchangeable with judgment. It establishes that the hearing will be about facts rather than impressions, and it leaves the disposition of the case where the Book always leaves it: with the Lord of the assembled communities.

**The Book as a Proof For or Against Its Reader**

The Prophet's teaching supplies the frame for this scene in a single sentence preserved in the most authoritative collections. Abu Malik al-Ashʿarī narrates that the Messenger of God said, among a series of images for the parts of faith, that the Qur'an is a proof for you or against you (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 223). The report captures exactly what this verse is describing. The recitation that was delivered in this world will function at the judgment as evidence, and whether it is evidence for the reader or against him depends on what he did when it reached him. The sūrah has shown both cases: those who mocked the verses and those who received them with certainty, and the same words belong to the file of both.

**Truth That Cannot Be Edited**

The phrase rendered "with truth" marks the record as incapable of error in either direction. It does not exaggerate a good deed, and it does not omit a sin. The Book makes the same guarantee about the scenes of judgment in several places, and it states elsewhere that not the smallest atom's weight of good or evil will be missing from the accounting (99:7–8). The certainty that this removes is a comfort to the wronged: nothing they suffered has been forgotten, and nothing their oppressors did has been mislaid. It is a warning to the wrongdoer for the same reason: no favourable circumstance of the trial has been overlooked. The record is complete, and the chapter's argument throughout has been that completeness is what the deniers were really hoping to avoid.

**Living in Front of a Recorder**

The practical consequence of believing this verse is a kind of alertness that the Book praises under the name of God-consciousness: the awareness that one's words and acts are being noted by a writer who does not forget. The Qur'an's descriptions of this are not meant to produce paralysis. They are meant to place a person's ordinary life inside a story with consequences, so that a decision made in secret has a public dimension and a moment of cruelty is not merely a moment. The sūrah reaches this point after thirty verses of argument about signs, signs in creation, signs in the recitation, and demands for proof, and it ends the debate by reminding everyone that the proof has been accumulating all along, in writing, in a book that will be brought out and read.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:30

> As for those who believed and did good, their Lord will admit them into His mercy. That is ˹truly˺ the absolute triumph.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Conditions, Never Separated**

The Qur'an almost never mentions faith without attaching action to it, and this verse belongs to that pattern. The phrase describes people who believed and did good, and the two verbs are joined so closely that they read as a single description of one life. The Book does the same thing in dozens of places, and the practice is consistent enough to be treated as a rule: belief is the root, and good deeds are the fruit that shows the root is alive. What the verse does not do is split the pair, so that a reader who claims faith while his conduct ignores it has no verse to lean on. Nor does it accept a life of good works with no belief behind them, since a deed is given its value by the one to whom the doer is answering.

**"Their Lord Will Admit Them"**

The verb describes an entry that has been granted rather than an ascent that has been achieved. The object of the entering is God's mercy, and most exegetes take mercy here as naming the Garden itself, since the Book elsewhere uses the same word for the destination of the righteous. The possessive attached to the Lord is intimate: not the Lord of the worlds in this sentence but their Lord, the one they dealt with in private and obeyed in difficulty. The language matches the pattern set earlier in the sūrah, where the signs belonged to the believers, the certainty belonged to those of sure faith, and the guidance and mercy belonged to the convinced. The whole chapter has been assigning its promises to a specific group, and this verse names the endpoint of the road.

**Mercy That Is Given, Not Earned**

The Book is careful to keep salvation out of the ledger of strict accounts, and this verse states the reason in its grammar. Believers who did good receive an entrance into mercy, which is the category for what is not owed. If the reward were a wage, the sentence would speak of payment; because it speaks of mercy, the reward is described as a gift from a Lord to servants who never acquired a claim on him. The Qur'an puts the same point in other chapters, instructing the Prophet to tell the believers that they enter by mercy rather than by merit, and it warns those who trust their own records against the accounting they are inviting. Faith and good deeds determine who receives the gift and what it consists of. They do not oblige the giver.

**The Absolute Triumph**

The word translated here names the winning of a contest or the achieving of a goal, and the Book uses it for the final success of those whose business in this world ended in profit. It is not a modest reward; it is the great achievement, and the Book reserves the phrase for the states that no loss can reach, since everything earned in this world can be taken away. The same expression appears in the scene where the faithful are told that God is pleased with them and they with him, and that this is the greatest triumph (5:119). A farmer who brings in a harvest, a merchant who closes a profitable voyage, and a traveller who arrives at his destination are all pictures the Book draws on. The verse's chosen phrase is the last of them: arrival after a journey, in a place where no further journey begins.

**The Two Destinies Side by Side**

The verse sits directly after the announcement that the people of falsehood are in total loss, and the placing is the chapter's design. Two groups, two verdicts, one hearing. Nothing in the sentence requires the reader to guess where he belongs, since the qualifications have been stated in the plainest terms across the whole sūrah: believing, doing good, receiving mercy, entering, triumphing. The Book regularly structures its accounts of the Hereafter in this paired fashion, and the pairing is part of the persuasion. A person who reads the loss of the previous verse and the triumph of this one is being asked to choose, and the sūrah assumes that the choice is a rational one once the alternatives are clear.

**Why the Reward Is Named Rather Than Described**

The verse gives no courtyard, no river, no fruit, and no company; it names the verdict and stops. Other chapters supply the descriptions in abundance, and this one is not short of imagery elsewhere. The restraint fits the argument it is making. What the deniers of the chapter had denied was not the quality of the Hereafter but its existence, and a sūrah that has been arguing about proof and evidence does not need to win an audience with pictures of gardens. What the believers receive is described in the terms that matter at this point in the argument: they are admitted to mercy, and they have won. Everything else, the Book has already established, is beyond what anyone has seen or heard.

**Belief and Action as a Single Life**

The two clauses of the qualification are worth holding together to the end, because the whole sūrah's moral teaching depends on their union. The command to forgive people who have not apologised, the refusal to follow the desires of the uninformed, the insistence that each soul bears its own burden, and the warning about time and conjecture all assume a person who is neither merely correct in his opinions nor merely decent in his habits, but both at once. The verse does not describe a state achieved in an hour of decision. It describes a life that held together, in which what a person believed shaped what he did, and what he did confirmed what he believed, until the record was closed and the meeting arrived.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:31

> And as for those who disbelieved, ˹they will be told,˺ “Were My revelations not recited to you, yet you acted arrogantly and were a wicked people?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Put to the Condemned**

The address at the judgment is a question, and the form is important. The disbelievers are not lectured about their crimes; they are asked whether something happened, and the answer is already known to everyone present. The Book uses this construction at the reckoning in several places, and it has a double edge. It forces the accused to acknowledge the fact under discussion, and it removes any possibility of complaint about a verdict delivered without notice. The sentence also shows who is speaking. The voice says "My revelations" — the first person singular, the voice of God at the hearing — so the question is not put by an angel reading a charge sheet. The owner of the message is asking the men who rejected it whether it reached them.

**"Were Not My Revelations Recited to You?"**

The question names the central fact of the case. The message was delivered, in their language, in their city, by a man they knew, and the Book records elsewhere that the same admission is drawn from the people of the Fire when the keepers of Hell ask them whether messengers did not come to them reciting the revelations of their Lord and warning them about the meeting with that Day (39:71). The answer in that scene is yes, and the keepers reply to their plea for prayer by asking whether messengers did not come with clear proofs, to which they again answer yes (40:50). The pattern in the Book is consistent: the condemned never claim that they were left uninformed. What they lose at the judgment is not an argument but a hope, and the question in this verse is the first movement of that loss.

**Arrogance Named as the Refusal Itself**

The charge has two parts, and the first is the behaviour the sūrah has already diagnosed three times. The proud listener of the eighth verse behaved as though he had not heard; the mockers of the ninth verse made derision their habit; and here the accusation is stated as a settled character: you acted arrogantly. The Arabic verb pictures a person inflating himself above the position he has been offered, and the Book treats that inflation as the underlying motive of disbelief rather than one vice among many. The disbeliever does not generally reject the argument on technical grounds. He rejects a message that requires him to stand alongside people he regards as beneath him and to admit that his inherited way was wrong. The Book records the same motive in other chapters, where the refusal is traced to a reluctance to be levelled with the poor and the weak, and it treats that reluctance as the point at which the question stopped being academic for the men of Makkah.

**"A Wicked People"**

The second half of the charge uses a word built on a root that means to cut off or to sever. A crime, in this vocabulary, is an act that cuts a person away from what he was joined to: from his Lord, from the truth, and from the community of those who answer. The Book applies the term to the hardened deniers in particular, and the plural here describes a group rather than an individual — a body of people whose shared refusal has become a corporate act. That is the final shape of the condition the sūrah has been tracing. The sin began as a preference, became a habit of mockery, hardened into a policy, and ended as a description of a whole people. The word is not aimed at occasional failures; it names what the group has become.

**Two Halves of One Charge**

The two clauses are joined by the "yet" that makes them a single accusation. The first states the opportunity, the second the response. Arrogance explains the refusal, and the word rendered wicked describes its outcome in the community's life. Read together, they rule out the two standard defences. The accused cannot say that no one told them, because the revelations were recited. They cannot say that they meant well, because arrogance was the settled posture of a whole people. The sentence is therefore complete before an answer is given, and the place where the answer should have appeared is exactly where the silence falls.

**A Rebuke That Closes the Options**

The question is unanswerable, and its unanswerability is the verdict. A person who has been asked whether he was spoken to cannot raise a philosophical objection to the content of what was said, because the objection is a different matter and the question was not about it. The Book's procedure at the judgment is consistently to begin with the fact of delivery and only then with what the recipients did. That order is a mercy in its way, since it gives the condemned every chance to produce a genuine defence about delivery before the discussion moves on. What the question establishes is that there was no barrier between these people and the message: no distance, no language problem, no shortage of time.

**The Question Every Reader Will Face**

The verse is addressed to those who disbelieved, and the reply it expects is available to anyone who reads the sūrah. The revelations have been recited, in this case to an audience that includes everyone who encounters the recitation at any time, and the two behaviours named in the charge — arrogance and the cutting-off that follows it — are not limited to seventh-century Arabia. The chapter has already described the man who takes his desire as a god, and it has promised that he is not beyond the reach of this question. What the verse leaves in the reader's hands is a simple matter of fact to be established later, in front of witnesses, and the Book has never suggested that the answer will be a surprise.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:32

> And whenever it was said ˹to you˺, ‘Surely Allah’s promise ˹of judgment˺ is true and there is no doubt about the Hour,’ you said ˹mockingly˺, ‘We do not know what the Hour is! We think it is no more than speculation, and we are not convinced ˹that it will ever come˺.’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply Recorded Word for Word**

The verse continues the judgment address and quotes the defendants directly. This is the Book's characteristic method of reporting an argument: the opponents' case is given in their own terms, without caricature, before it is answered. Here the words are spoken at the reckoning, so their content has a second function, since it now serves as testimony against the people who said it. The sentence is short and its components are ordinary: they were told that God's promise is true and that the Hour admits no doubt, and they replied that they had no idea what the Hour was. The Book simply places the two statements in sequence and lets the reader see the disproportion between them.

**"We Do Not Know What the Hour Is"**

The exegetes give two readings of the clause. One takes it as a profession of uncertainty about whether the Hour is real at all; the other takes it as uncertainty about when it will arrive. Both readings are recorded in the major commentaries, and the second has a certain plausibility, since the deniers of Makkah repeatedly asked when the promise would be fulfilled. Yet the sentence does not stop at uncertainty. It continues into a statement of what the speakers do believe and what they refuse to accept, and that continuation gives away the fact that the profession of ignorance was not a plea for information. It was a way of dismissing a warning without having to answer it.

**Speculation and the Missing Conviction**

The two closing clauses are precise. They describe their view as nothing more than conjecture, and they state that they are not convinced. The vocabulary matches the sūrah's earlier verdict on the same people, where they were described as having no knowledge of the matters they asserted and as following nothing but speculation. At the reckoning they repeat the diagnosis that the Qur'an had already delivered about them: their relationship to the truth about the Hereafter was guesswork. The conformity of the two statements is part of the scene's power. The men are not defending a considered position in front of the judge; they are restating the state of mind that produced their denial, and they are doing it in the past tense, as a fact that no longer helps them.

**The Weight of the Word "Only"**

The clause rendered "no more than speculation" turns on a single word of limitation. The deniers are not saying that they gave the matter some thought and arrived at a probability; they are saying that the promise amounts to nothing beyond a guess, which is a claim about the quality of the evidence as they judged it. The Book's response to that judgement was given earlier in the chapter, where the signs of the created order were set out as evidence and the reader was asked whether he would take the lesson. The word "only" is therefore the hinge of the whole dispute, and the sūrah has already established that what the deniers called speculation was supported by more evidence than their own denial.

**Mockery as the Manner of the Answer**

The verse attaches a description to the way they spoke: they said it mockingly. Content and tone are both placed in the record, and the tone matters because it fixes the character of the reply. A person who genuinely wants to know what the Hour is will ask questions and wait for answers. A person who jeers has already settled the matter and is managing his audience. The sūrah has documented mockery as the practice of these men three times already, and the Book elsewhere states what happens to those who make a habit of it, saying that those who ridiculed the messengers were overtaken by the very thing they mocked (6:10; 21:41). The manner of the reply in this verse is the reason the answer at the judgment takes the form of a sentence rather than a discussion.

**A Confession Spoken Too Late**

The final irony is that the words themselves are accurate. The deniers say that they are not convinced, and the Book agrees with them; it has already said that most people do not know and that the doubters possess no knowledge. What they get wrong is the implication they draw from their own state of mind, since a person's failure to be convinced says nothing about whether the thing he doubts is real. The statement that they are unconvinced will be repeated by them in front of the one who knows their thoughts, which turns a private admission into public evidence. Said in the marketplace, the same words would have been an invitation to be taught. Spoken at the reckoning, they seal the case.

**Words That Would Have Been Faith in Another Place**

The sentence is the tragedy of the sūrah in miniature. Nothing in the deniers' reply is absurd; it is merely misplaced and unexamined. They admit ignorance, and the Qur'an praises those who admit what they do not know. They describe their state as conjecture, and the Book asks people not to build on conjecture. They confess that they are not convinced, and the honest confession of doubt is the beginning of every search for certainty. What makes their statement damning is that it was made after a warning, in a tone of contempt, and never acted on. The verse leaves the reader to notice that the same words, said at the right time and with the right intention, would have been the first step of the journey rather than the last word of the defence.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:33

> And the evil ˹consequences˺ of their deeds will unfold before them, and they will be overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Deeds That Come Out to Meet Their Owners**

The verse speaks of the evil consequences of what people did, and the wording treats a deed as something that produces a visible result rather than merely a fact recorded in a file. The Book's moral universe works this way throughout: an act is not exhausted when it is performed, and the sūrah has already told its readers that each soul will be paid back for what it has earned, with no one wronged. What the verse adds is a scene. The consequences appear in front of the people who caused them, and the appearance is not a private matter of conscience but a public unfolding in which the person sees what his own hands manufactured. The Book's word for this includes the sense of becoming clear after being hidden, which fits a lifetime in which the connection between an act and its meaning was deliberately blurred.

**What Was Hidden Becomes Visible**

The verb in the sentence describes something emerging into view. The sūrah's opponents had spent their lives treating the warnings as empty, and a person cannot sustain that treatment while the consequences of his conduct are visible to him. So the consequences were hidden, which is how the deniers were able to speak of promises and threats as poetry. The day described here ends that arrangement. The same phrasing appears in another chapter almost word for word, where the evil consequences of what people did unfold before them and they are overwhelmed by what they used to mock (39:48). The uniformity of the wording across the Book indicates that this is a fixed scene of judgment rather than a figure of speech, and the sūrah presents it as the ordinary result of a life being examined by its owner.

**Overwhelmed by What They Mocked**

The second half of the verse states the most exact justice in the chapter. What overwhelms the deniers is not an unrelated punishment imposed from outside; it is the very thing they laughed at, now arriving with full weight. The Book repeats this sentence in passage after passage, attaching it to the messengers who were ridiculed (6:10; 21:41), to nations that were destroyed (16:34), to a people whose respite ran out (11:8), and to whole communities who persisted in denying the signs (46:26). The repetition is deliberate. It establishes that the connection between mockery and ruin is not a coincidence of the judgment but a law that the Book has been announcing from the beginning, and that the derision of the previous verse was not a harmless mood but the thing that would one day be handed back to its owner.

**The Justice of a Punishment That Was Announced**

There is nothing arbitrary in a penalty that was described in advance. The men of Makkah were warned that the mockery they enjoyed would return to them, and the warning was repeated in their hearing as part of the recitation they were mocking, which gives the scene an uncomfortable symmetry. The punishment they receive is the fulfilment of the warnings they treated as empty, and its arrival establishes that the message they dismissed was accurate in every detail. The Book draws attention to this pattern rather than hiding it, because the point of the judgment is not the suffering of the condemned but the vindication of the truth they denied. What overwhelms them is the accuracy of the text they laughed at.

**No Injustice in the Accounting**

The Book never allows the punishment to be separated from the deeds, and it states the principle in a sentence that could serve as a heading for this whole scene: God does not wrong people in the least, but it is people who wrong themselves (10:44). The verse before us follows the rule. The evil consequences that unfold belong to the people who produced them, the thing that overwhelms them is the object of their own derision, and the account is exact. Nothing has been added to the ledger by a judge who needed to be angry, and nothing has been forgotten by a court that wanted to be harsh. The suffering of the condemned is the working out of what they did, which is why the Book can describe a punishment this severe without describing it as cruelty.

**The Moment the Joke Ends**

Contempt depends on distance. A man can mock a threat while it remains a threat, because the mocking places him above the thing he is describing and gives him a position from which to look down. The verse describes the moment the distance is removed. The threat arrives, and the pose that made the mockery possible cannot be maintained for a second longer. This is why the Book can call the punishment humiliating and mean it: the men had built a public identity on their superiority to the message, and the sentence in this verse dismantles the identity by showing them overwhelmed by the very thing they had used to establish their standing. There is no audience they can still impress, and there is no position from which the joke can be retold.

**The Mechanism Rather Than the Penalty**

The sentence is worth reading closely one more time, because it does not describe an external sentence being handed down. It describes a consequence unfolding and a thing arriving. The earlier verse in which the two groups were promised that no one would be wronged supports the same reading. The Book's account of damnation, at least in this chapter, is structural rather than punitive: a life spent refusing the truth ends by receiving exactly the truth it refused, and the only surprise the condemned experience is the accuracy of a message they thought was a fiction. What the verse finally demonstrates is that the mockery was the most expensive form of attention a person could have paid to the message.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:34

> It will be said, “This Day We will neglect you as you neglected the meeting of this Day of yours! Your home will be the Fire, and you will have no helpers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict Delivered as Speech**

The sentence begins with the passive report that it will be said, so that the emphasis falls on the words rather than on the speaker, though the following clause makes the speaker plain: the voice says we, and it is God who is not named. The judgment in the Book is often conducted in this manner, as a spoken address that the condemned hear and understand, rather than as a mechanical process operating on them from a distance. The condemned are told the meaning of what is happening to them while it happens. That detail preserves the chapter's picture of the judgment as a hearing with a verdict rather than a natural disaster. It also gives the sentence its force, since a punishment that arrives with an explanation cannot be dismissed as bad luck.

**Neglect Answered With Neglect**

The word translated against neglect is the ordinary verb for forgetting, and the Book's use of it for God is a deliberate mirroring: their treatment of the appointed day is returned to them in the same coin. The exegetes explain by noting that the Book elsewhere makes God's forgetting mean abandonment rather than a lapse of knowledge, since nothing is outside his knowledge. The construction recurs in several chapters: those who took religion as amusement were told that God would ignore them as they ignored the meeting of that day (7:51); the hypocrites forgot God and were forgotten in turn (9:67); a man who neglected the revelations is told that he is neglected that day (20:126); and the warned are told to taste the punishment for neglecting the meeting of their day, with the promise that God will neglect them too (32:14). The measure is exact and the Book states it without softening.

**The Meeting They Never Scheduled**

The phrase describes the day as theirs — this Day of yours — and the possessive is one of the sharpest ironies in the sūrah. The men denied that any such day existed, and the verdict hands it back to them as an appointment they kept. The Book's term for it is the meeting, which carries the sense of a face-to-face encounter rather than merely an event, and it uses the word for the encounter with God throughout the Qur'an. The deniers had been told to prepare for a meeting they refused to believe would occur, and the verse shows them attending it anyway, unable to decline. Nothing is more completely out of a person's control than an appointment he does not believe in, and the sentence closes that gap in a single phrase.

**"Your Home Will Be the Fire"**

The word rendered home names a place where someone settles, and the Book uses it for both the Garden and the Fire in the same sense of final residence. The homes of the men in question had been fine houses in a well-watered valley, and the sūrah had earlier described a man who trusts in wealth and children and protectors to shield him. The sentence replaces all of it with a dwelling that has no climate, no furniture, and no exit, and it states the replacement as a fact rather than a threat. That is the register of the judgment scenes in the Book: the verdict is announced in the present tense, and the condemned learn their permanent address at the moment they hear it.

**The Protection Network Collapses**

The final clause states that they will have no helpers, and the phrase closes a theme that has run through the whole chapter. Earlier verses considered the protectors taken besides God and asked whether they could be of any use against him; the chapter described the wrongdoers as patrons of one another and the righteous as having a single patron. In the marketplaces of Makkah a man survived by belonging to a group that would answer for him, and the sūrah gave that system its due weight by repeatedly telling the believers they were risking it. The sentence in this verse is the moment the system's limits are shown: the allies and clients and patrons are all present at the same hearing, standing in the same posture, and none of them has anything to say.

**A Scene in Which the Accused Understand**

The Book's judgment scenes repeatedly give the condemned a voice. They argue, they admit, they ask for a second chance, and in one chapter they confess that the messengers brought the truth. This verse continues the pattern by giving them a sentence they can follow. Whatever the metaphysics of that day, the sūrah presents an event in which every participant understands what is being said and why. That presentation matters for the reader, since a punishment described as a mechanism would leave no room for the moral weight the chapter has been building. The condemned are addressed as the people they were, with their history and their choices intact, and they are told the reason for the sentence in words that nobody in the room can misunderstand.

**Measure for Measure**

The comparison that runs through the verse — as you neglected, so we will neglect — sets out the Book's principle of reciprocal justice. Elsewhere the sūrah has promised that no one will be wronged, and this sentence shows what that promise looks like in practice for a particular class of offenders. They had received years of warning, prosperity, and opportunity, and they had used the time to construct arguments they did not believe. What they are handed is the same treatment they meted out to the day that structured their lives: indifference for indifference, absence for absence. The verse does not need to describe the flames. The exactness of the exchange is the punishment, and the Book presents it as the fitting end of a life that had already decided what to ignore.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:35

> This is because you made a mockery of Allah’s revelations, and were deluded by ˹your˺ worldly life.” So ˹from˺ that Day ˹on˺ they will not be taken out of the Fire, nor will they be allowed to appease ˹their Lord˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason Stated to Their Faces**

The Book never leaves a punishment unexplained. The sentence begins with a word of causation, and what follows is a short account of the two things that led to the verdict: mockery of the revelations, and the deception of worldly life. That pattern holds in the sūrah's other judgment scenes, where the accused are told precisely what they are being punished for. There is a purpose in the clarity. A verdict that cannot be understood could be resented as arbitrary, and the Book removes that possibility by naming the offence in ordinary words at the moment the sentence is passed. The condemned are not left to guess what they did wrong. They are told, in the second person, with the whole assembly listening.

**Mockery Named First**

The list puts the mockery of the revelations ahead of the delusion of the world, and the order reflects the sūrah's own account. The seventh verse pronounced woe on the sinful liar, the eighth described the man who hears and walks away, the ninth made derision a habit, the twenty-fourth recorded the mockers' arguments, and the thirty-second quoted their scoffing words at the judgment itself. The charge now gathers all of it into a single clause. What the men did with the message was not a private opinion but an activity: they took the revelations and turned them into material for jokes, in public, before audiences that they were trying to keep from believing. The Book treats that labour as the substance of their crime, and it names it first because everything else followed from it.

**Deluded by the Worldly Life**

The second cause is deception, and the Book uses it constantly for the relationship between a person and the present world. The world is said to have deluded the deniers (3:185), to be nothing more than play, amusement, luxury, and competition (57:20), to be a pleasure that distracts from what matters (9:38), to be a fleeting enjoyment compared with the Hereafter (13:26), and to be the home of settlement only for those who have understood what it is (40:39). None of these passages says that the created world is evil. The sūrah itself has just spent a dozen verses praising the sea, the rain, and the winds as gifts. The deception is not the world's existence but its promise: it presents itself as permanent and sufficient, and it cannot deliver on either.

**The Door That Stays Closed**

The sentence then closes the case with a firm statement: they will not be taken out of the Fire. The Book's accounts of the Hereafter do not include a second probation for those who reach this point, and it explains the reason through the plea of the condemned themselves. In one passage the wrongdoers beg to be sent back to do good rather than what they used to do, and the answer informs them that they were given lives long enough for anyone who wanted to take heed, and that the warner had already come (35:37). Another verse reports that if they were sent back they would only return to what they had been forbidden, and calls them liars (6:28). The refusal is not a refusal of mercy in the abstract. It is a refusal to run an experiment that has already been tried.

**Appeasing the Lord**

The verse's final clause removes even the smallest avenue. The Arabic verb covers asking for a favour, seeking to please, and offering something to restore a relationship, and the exegetes note its use in the context of a plea or compensation. The Book says the condemned will not be permitted to do it, and the wording implies that the impulse will be there. The men who spent their lives denying the meeting will want, when they see the outcome, to make amends. Their request will not be granted, and the verse gives no reason beyond the sentence itself, since the reasons have already been stated: they mocked, and they were deceived. A person who has been shown the truth and has built his life on deriding it has no remaining currency to offer.

**The Justice of a Final Verdict**

The finality of the sentence sits alongside the chapter's earlier guarantee that no one will be wronged, and the two statements are not in tension. The Book's account of the judgment is that every case is heard with complete knowledge and perfect fairness, and that the outcomes differ according to what people actually did. A verdict that cannot be reopened is not necessarily unjust; it is a completed judgment, and the whole point of the sūrah is that the moment for weighing evidence has an end. The condemned in this verse spent their lives treating that end as a fiction, and the sentence they hear is the last correction of their estimate. Nothing in the account leaves the reader with a complaint about the fairness of the exchange.

**The Two Things the Believer Refuses**

Read as a description of two dangers, the verse names what a believer has to hold off for a lifetime. On one side is contempt for what God has handed down: the habit of treating the message with a joke, the move that allows a man to keep his standing and his opinions intact. On the other side is the slow absorption in a world that never stops offering something worth having. The sūrah has spent thirty-four verses arguing that the signs are real, that the Book is insight and mercy, and that the meeting is certain. This verse states what happens when both are refused at once, and it places the two refusals side by side so that the reader can recognise the shape of the life it describes.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:36

> So all praise is for Allah—Lord of the heavens and Lord of the earth, Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Praise Placed After the Judgment**

The chapter has just finished a scene of condemnation, and the verse that follows it is a declaration of praise. The shift is not a change of subject. The Book's habit is to state its praise at the moments where the truth of its claims has become undeniable, and the reckoning is the greatest of those moments, since it settles every dispute the sūrah has been tracing and answers every mocker it has described. Praise at that point is not a religious formula inserted to fill a gap. It is the response that the completed account of God's rule calls for, and the Book gives it in advance so that the reader can join in before the scene arrives. Other chapters close in the same way, with praise pronounced at the end of a long argument rather than at its beginning.

**Three Titles and a Widening Horizon**

The verse names God as Lord of the heavens, Lord of the earth, and Lord of all worlds, and the three titles build outward in a deliberate order. The heavens come first, then the earth, and then everything that exists. The structure is not a list of separate domains but a single ownership stated in increasing scope, and the final title leaves nothing outside it. The Book uses the same expression for the same purpose elsewhere, saying that praise belongs to God in the heavens and the earth (30:18). The effect is to make the praise universal by its content rather than only by the sentiment of the speaker. A reader who declares that God is Lord of all worlds has made a claim about reality, not expressed a feeling.

**Praise as Instruction, Not Only Devotion**

The sentence is given to the reader to say, and the Book uses this construction frequently: a declaration of praise that doubles as a command to praise. The most famous occurrence is the opening of the Fātiḥah, where the words all praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds (1:2) stand at the head of the recited book and are repeated in every prayer, and the same sentence recurs throughout the Qur'an, in the speech of the angels around the throne (39:75), in the greeting of the blessed in the Garden (10:10), among the saints who praise God for removing their sorrow (35:34), and in the invitation to call on God with sincere devotion (40:65). A formula repeated this often is not decoration. It is the sentence the Book teaches its readers to say about the world.

**What "Lord of All Worlds" Contains**

The Arabic term rendered worlds covers everything other than God: peoples, nations, and the orders of creation, including those no human being has seen. Commentators note that the plural has no single referent in view and is used to gather every category of existence under one relationship with its creator. The phrase therefore does not say that God rules the world human beings know. It says that every world there is belongs to him, including the domains this sūrah has been pointing at since its third verse: the heavens, the earth, the creatures scattered through it, and everything the reader has never observed. The praise in this verse is the fitting response to a lordship whose scope cannot be surveyed.

**The Praise of Everything That Exists**

Other passages make the point that praise is not confined to human beings or to believers. Whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies God (59:24), a statement that places the reader's own act of praise inside a chorus far larger than the assembly of his fellow believers. The sūrah has treated the created order as a book of signs since its opening verses, and this verse supplies the response that the signs were pointing toward. A person who has understood the argument of the chapter is not being asked to invent a sentiment; he is being placed alongside the creation in a declaration that everything belongs to the one who made it. Praise in that setting is closer to an acknowledgment of the facts than to a mood.

**Praise as the Correct End of the Argument**

The placement of this verse after the scene of judgment deserves one more look. A reader who accepts the sūrah's case has been told that the world is a gift, that the Book is insight and mercy, that the meeting with God is certain, that the records are complete, and that the verdicts are exact. The natural last word in such an account is not a warning but a praise, because the account has described a system that works. The deniers are punished, the wronged are vindicated, the truth is established, and the kingdom that belonged to God all along is acknowledged at last by everyone present. Praise is the sound the truth makes when it has been settled, and the sūrah is nearly finished with its argument.

**A Sentence the Reader Carries Out of the Chapter**

The verse offers the reader something to take away from the whole sūrah. The chapter opened with two letters whose meaning is left with God, and after thirty-four verses of signs, warnings, mockery, disputes, and judgments it closes with a sentence that a person can say and mean: praise belongs to God, the Lord of the heavens, the Lord of the earth, the Lord of all worlds. Whatever else the reader understands or fails to understand in the chapter, this declaration is placed at its end as the summary of the position the argument has been establishing. The next verse completes the doxology with the attributes of majesty, and the two together form the last word the sūrah leaves in the mouth of the reader.

## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:37

> To Him belongs ˹all˺ Majesty in the heavens and the earth. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Majesty as a Property of Ownership**

The word rendered Majesty names greatness and the grandeur of a sovereign, and the verse places it in the same two realms it has been discussing since its third verse. The heavens and the earth hold no grandeur that does not belong to their owner; the phrase does not mean that his greatness is distributed across creation, but that everything in both realms acknowledges a greatness that is his alone. The Book uses the same construction at the close of other chapters, where it places the attributes of God after a survey of his rule, and the effect is always to leave the reader with a claim about God rather than a task to perform. The chapter has just pronounced praise; this verse states the ground on which praise rests.

**The Two Names Return From the Opening**

The final words of the sūrah are the two names that appeared in its second verse, where the revelation of the Book was attributed to God the Almighty, the All-Wise. The repetition is a bracket around the entire chapter. At the beginning the pair guaranteed the message and its sender; at the end the pair seals the account of the judgment and the praise that follows it. The sūrah's first movement concerned what God has said; its final movement concerns what God has done and will do. The same two attributes cover both, and the reader who reaches this verse has been told that the power and the wisdom announced at the start have been in operation the whole way through.

**How the Two Occurrences Differ**

There is a difference in the way the names function in the two verses, and the commentators draw it out. At the opening, the attributes speak to the act of revelation: a book sent down requires power to protect it and wisdom to compose it. At the close, they speak to creation and judgment: the order of the heavens and the earth, and the exactness of the reckoning described in the preceding verses, display a might that is never arbitrary and a wisdom that misses nothing. The two kinds of signs that the chapter has been discussing since its third verse — the signs in the created order and the signs in the recitation — are therefore brought together under the same pair of names, which is the sūrah's way of saying that both came from the same hand.

**The Frame of a Chapter That Began With Letters**

The opening of the sūrah was an unexplained pair of letters, and its closing is a pair of names with their meanings spelled out. Between the two, the chapter has argued from the heavens and the earth, the human body, the animals, the alternation of night and day, the rain, and the winds, and it has reported a recitation and a reckoning. A reader who takes the whole journey knows more at the end than the muqaṭṭaʿāt disclosed at the beginning, and the sūrah provides that knowledge in the form of the two names that define the sender. The letters still have their secret, but the character of the one who pronounced them is now as clear as the argument can make it.

**Why the Chapter Ends Without a Command**

Almost every sūrah closes by turning the reader toward an action: obey, glorify, repent, be patient. This one closes with a statement about God. The Book does that when the argument itself has been the instruction, and this chapter has been an argument from beginning to end: signs, then claims, then objections, then a verdict. The last thing the reader is given is not a task but a description of the one who will judge the task, which returns the reader to where the sūrah started. The chapter's final word is therefore not about human effort at all. It is a name and an attribute, stated in the present tense, holding good whether anyone acknowledges it or not.

**Where Else the Book Uses This Pair**

The pairing of Almighty and All-Wise recurs at the close of other sūrahs and always in the context of God's ownership and rule. One chapter lists it in a passage that ends with the glorification of everything in the heavens and the earth (59:24), and another uses it in a description of the knower of the seen and the unseen (64:18). The formula also appears at the head of the chapters that open with ḥāʾ and mīm, as this sūrah has shown. The sūrah's final sentence is therefore not a private ending. It places the chapter inside a wider pattern of praise that the Book establishes elsewhere, and it lets the reader hear the last words of al-Jāthiyah as a line in a chorus rather than an isolated conclusion.

**The Last Word and the First**

Set the sūrah's last verse beside its first. It began with two letters whose meaning is known to God alone, and it ends with two names whose meaning is the entire subject of the chapter. That movement from an unexplained sign to an acknowledged sovereign is the shape of the argument the reader has been following, and it is also the shape of the response the chapter was asking for. A person who began by accepting what he could not understand has been led to the point where he can say what the verse says: majesty belongs to God in the heavens and the earth, and he is the Almighty, the All-Wise. The sūrah offers nothing further, and it does not need to.
