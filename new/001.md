# Sūrah al-Fātiḥah (Chapter 1) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**Seven Names for Seven Verses**

No sūrah in the Quran carries as many names, and each name records a use. *Al-Fātiḥah* is "the Opening": it opens the Book, and it opens every cycle (*rakʿah*) of the canonical prayer. *Umm al-Kitāb* and *Umm al-Qurʾān* — "Mother of the Book," "Mother of the Quran" — record the claim that the whole of the revelation is contained in it in principle. The Quran itself calls it *the seven often-repeated verses* (15:87), from which comes *al-Sabʿ al-mathānī*. Tradition adds *al-Shifāʾ*, "the Cure," because it is recited over the sick; *al-Asās*, "the Foundation"; *Sūrat al-Ḥamd*, "the Chapter of Praise," from its second verse; and *Sūrat al-Ṣalāh*, "the Chapter of the Prayer," because no prayer stands without it.

A sūrah acquires a name from what people do with it. Seven names for seven verses means this is the most used passage in the Book, recited several times a day by every praying Muslim and spoken at the thresholds of ordinary life — when someone dies, when a couple marries, when a child is born, when a contract is signed, when a traveller leaves.

**A Makkan Revelation, With One Dissent**

The overwhelming majority of scholars place al-Fātiḥah among the earliest Makkan revelations. Mujāhid (d. 104/722–23), a leading figure of the second generation, is reported to have held that it was revealed in Madinah, and most authorities regard that as impossible, on the ground that the Muslims could not have performed the prescribed prayers without it.

The objection is weaker than it looks, since it assumes the prayer was always performed exactly as it was later legislated. But the majority position has the better evidence regardless: the sūrah's vocabulary, its brevity, and its subject — the nature of God, worship, and the request for guidance rather than legal rulings — match the earliest Makkan stratum. The dissent matters for a different reason. It shows how early the sūrah was tied to the ritual prayer in the community's own memory, to the point that a scholar could argue backwards from the prayer's existence to the sūrah's date.

**Six Verses or Seven: The Count That Divides the Schools**

The sūrah counts as seven verses or as six, and the difference is the opening formula. Those who count *In the Name of Allah* as a verse of the sūrah reach seven, and those who treat it as a separator between sūrahs reach six. The Quran's description of the sūrah as *the seven often-repeated verses* (15:87) is the standard textual argument for the first count.

The disagreement is not academic, because it determines what is recited aloud. The Shāfiʿī school and the Jaʿfarī school recite the basmalah aloud at the head of the sūrah in audible prayers; the rest do not. Both practices rest on reports from the Companions, and the question is argued out at 1:1 below. No school, meanwhile, doubts the sūrah's presence in every cycle. The report of the man who prayed badly, and the many declarations that a prayer without it is deficient, make its recitation the one universally agreed element of the ritual.

**Three Movements and a Hinge**

The sūrah falls into three movements, and the division is not imposed on it from outside. The first three or four verses speak *about* God — His Name, His praise, His mercy, His ownership of the Day of Recompense. The fifth verse speaks *to* Him, and turns from description to address. The last two verses ask Him for something.

A well-known ḥadīth qudsī confirms the structure by narrating it as a conversation. God declares that He has divided the recitation between Himself and His servant into two halves. When the servant praises, God answers that His servant has praised Him, lauded Him, glorified Him. At the verse of worship, the answer changes to *This is between Me and My servant*. At the closing petition, it becomes *This is for My servant, and My servant will receive what he asks for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). The half belonging to God is praise; the half belonging to the creature is request; the verse between them belongs to both.

**Recited in Every Cycle, and Outside the Prayer**

Because the sūrah is recited in every cycle of every prayer, it is the most frequently repeated text in Muslim practice — several times a day for anyone who prays five times. The reports about its status are correspondingly numerous. One describes the Prophet telling a man he would teach him the greatest sūrah, and then naming *the seven often-repeated verses* (15:87). Another states that nothing like it was sent down in the Torah, in the Psalms, in the Gospel, or even in the Quran.

Its use outside the prayer is verified in the same collections. A group of the Companions came upon a tribe whose chief had been stung; one of them recited al-Fātiḥah over him, and the man recovered. When the Prophet heard of it he approved the recitation and asked, *How do you know that it is a ruqyah?* — then told them to keep the flock they had been paid and to give him a share (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5736; also 2276). The title *al-Shifāʾ*, "the Cure," comes from reports of this kind.

**Why the Shortest Complete Sūrah Is Called the Mother of the Book**

The strongest claim made for al-Fātiḥah is that it contains the whole Quran in principle. A saying attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib (d. 40/661) compresses it further: the whole Quran is in the Fātiḥah, the whole Fātiḥah in the basmalah, the whole basmalah in its opening letter, and that letter in the point beneath it. [UNVERIFIED: this saying is widely reported in the commentaries but I could not verify a chain or a source text for it.]

The claim should be read carefully rather than literally. The sūrah does not summarise the Quran's laws, narratives, or rulings; what it states is the framework every one of those presupposes — who God is, what a human being is before Him, what is asked for, and how a person goes wrong. On that reading the title is exact. A reader who understands these seven verses understands what the rest of the Book is doing, and a reader who does not will misunderstand every ruling in it, however well he memorises them.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence With No Verb**

The sūrah opens with a phrase that is not a full sentence. *Bi-smi Allāh* — "in the Name of Allah" (1:1) — rests on a preposition, and Arabic does not leave a preposition unattached; a word must be supplied. Al-Rāzī gathered what the grammarians proposed. One reading puts the missing verb first, so the sense is *I begin* in the Name of Allah, or *I seek help*. A second puts it last: in the Name of Allah I begin. A third supplies a noun rather than a verb — the beginning of what follows stands in the Name of Allah — and so the phrase is complete without any act being named at all.

The options are not interchangeable. When the verb comes first, the act is the subject of the sentence and the Name qualifies it: a person acts, and acts with God in view. When the verb comes last, the Name is established before anything is done and the act is then carried out inside it. That difference is small in grammar and large in practice, because only the second reading lets the phrase stand at the head of any lawful act — a letter, a journey, a slaughter, a marriage — rather than at the head of a recitation alone. Later jurists built the etiquette of beginning lawful acts with the Name on exactly that elasticity.

**Is This Line Part of the Sūrah?**

Every sūrah but one opens with this formula, and it also occurs once inside the text (27:30). Whether it counts as a numbered verse of the sūrah it opens has been argued since the first generations, and each side has evidence rather than mere preference. Anas ibn Mālik reports that he prayed behind the Prophet, Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, and ʿUthmān, and that each of them began the recitation with *All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds* (1:2), without mentioning the basmalah (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 399c; the shorter form in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 743). Against that, Abū Hurayrah is reported to have recited the basmalah aloud when he led the prayer and then said that among those present he was performing the prayer closest to the prayer of the Messenger of God. Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq's description of the basmalah as the greatest verse in the Book of God is the ground on which Shiʿi scholarship counts it as a verse of every sūrah that begins with it.

Two further considerations settle the weighing. The first is textual: the Quran calls this sūrah *the seven often-repeated verses* (15:87), and it reaches seven only if the basmalah is counted. The second is functional: Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that the Prophet did not know where one sūrah ended and the next began until this line was revealed — which makes the formula a boundary marker. Both claims can be true at once, and that is the strongest resolution available. The line separates sūrahs and is a verse of the one it opens; the schools differ on whether it is recited aloud in the prayer, and the disagreement is about ritual practice, not about the text's standing.

**A Word the Pagans Already Used**

*Allāh* did not enter Arabic with the Quran. Ask the idolaters of Makkah who created them and they answer with this very word (43:87; 29:61; 31:25; 39:38). What they would not grant was its consequence: that the One who creates also commands, and that no lesser being shares His rule. The Quran's argument against them therefore never has to establish that God exists; it presses the admission they already make to its conclusion.

Whether the word has a root was disputed. One view treats it as a proper name with no derivation, unique and unanalysable. The other derives it from *al-ilāh*, "the Divine," contracted by dropping the hamza with the article fused. The derived reading explains something the other does not: why the word was intelligible to pagan hearers at all, since *ilāh* was already in their vocabulary for a deity. The non-derived reading protects what the derived one risks — that a shared root might imply a shared kind. Al-Ghazzālī's observation holds the two together: Allāh is at once the widest of the divine names, gathering every attribute, and the narrowest, because no creature can be called by it, whereas a human being may be called knowing or merciful in a qualified sense. *He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺* (112:1) puts the point beyond argument.

**Three Names in a Fixed Order**

The formula names God three times, and the sequence is never varied. Allāh, then the Most Compassionate, then the Most Merciful. One line of commentary, associated with Kāshānī, reads the descent as a movement through levels: Allāh points to the divine Essence as such, al-Raḥmān to the unity of the attributes, and al-Raḥīm to the unity of the acts. Read that way, the basmalah is a compressed theology — Being, then what Being is like, then what Being does.

The order also carries a rhetorical decision. The name that cannot be shared with any creature is followed at once by mercy, and not once but twice. Nothing in the opening line threatens; the first things the Quran says about God are the things about Him that attract. That the two mercy names are not synonyms is argued in the commentary on their repetition at 1:3; what belongs here is that they arrive in a fixed slot, always after the unshared Name and always before any mention of judgment. The two names are also interchangeable in address: *Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names* (17:110). The formula is therefore not three rival designations but one Name stated at three levels.

**What the Missing Verb Allows**

Because the phrase is incomplete, it can be completed by whatever follows it. Anything a person does may be taken up into it, which is why the formula governs the whole range of permitted acts in Muslim practice and not only the recitation of scripture. The Quran supplies its own precedent at the moment the ark is boarded: *Board it! In the Name of Allah it will sail and cast anchor* (11:41). A vessel's voyage is placed in the Name before it moves.

A report is often quoted for the same rule in the form, "Every important matter not begun with the Name of God is cut off." That exact wording does not appear in the nine canonical collections; the wording that does appear is Sunan Ibn Mājah 1894 — *Every matter of importance that is not begun with the praise of God will be left deficient* — which Ibn Ḥajar graded ḥasan. The legal ruling that one should open acts with the Name does not depend on that single report; the jurists ground it in the general principle that acts belong to God and are to be performed for Him. [UNVERIFIED: the "In the Name of God" wording attributed here to Ṭabarsī could not be located in a canonical collection; only the "praise of God" wording (Ibn Mājah 1894) was verified.]

**Solomon Opens His Letter the Same Way**

The one place the basmalah occurs inside a sūrah rather than at its head is a letter: *It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful’* (27:30). The formula sits at the opening of a written message sent to a queen who does not yet believe, and it is the first thing she is given to read. That placement tells the hearer what the phrase is for. It belongs at the beginning of things — a book, a voyage, a letter — and it says, before any content is stated, whose the undertaking is.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Praise Stands Before Any Request**

The first sentence the worshipper speaks in the prayer, after the opening formula, is not a request. It is an assessment: *All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds* (1:2). The report that explains why the sūrah is built this way is the well-known ḥadīth qudsī in which God describes the recitation as a conversation. When the servant says, *Praise be to Allah, the Lord of the universe*, the response is *My servant has praised Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). The whole sūrah is then divided: the opening lines belong to God as praise, and the later lines belong to the servant as petition.

That order is the etiquette the Quran teaches for asking. A request that begins with a claim about the one asked is different in kind from a request that begins with a need. Praise establishes that the giver owes nothing and the asker has no claim, which is precisely the recognition a petition is supposed to contain. The sūrah can be read as a single act of address in which the worshipper spends three verses saying who God is before saying, in the fourth and fifth, what he wants. The request for guidance at 1:6 is therefore not a demand placed on a neutral power but an appeal to a Lord already acknowledged as the source of every good.

**Praise Is Not Gratitude**

*Al-ḥamd* is routinely translated as praise, and routinely confused with thanks. The distinction the commentators draw is precise. Gratitude (*shukr*) is a response to something received; it exists only after a favour has been given, and it ends when the benefit is forgotten. Praise is given for a quality the one praised possesses in himself, whether or not anything has been handed over. God is praised for being wise, powerful, and merciful before any gift reaches a creature, and He would be praised if no gift ever did. On that reading, praise is the wider category, and gratitude is one of its occasions.

The definite article carries the same insistence. The verse does not say that some praise belongs to God, or that praise is due to Him in this instance. It places the whole genus — every act of praise, by every praiser, for every reason — with its object. Whatever anyone has ever admired in anything, the verse claims the admiration as ultimately His, since whatever was admired came from Him. This is why the formula is recited on completion of an act rather than at its beginning: the act is over, the outcome is known, and the speaker locates the good in it outside himself.

**The Praise No Creature Can Finish**

The same logic produces a problem, and the tradition states it without softening. If praise is owed for who God is rather than for what He gives, then no created mind can discharge it, because no created mind comprehends who God is. The Prophet is reported to have said in prostration, *I cannot enumerate the praise due to You; You are as You have praised Yourself* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 486; also Sunan al-Nasāʾī 1100). The sentence concedes the limit and then resolves it: the adequate praise of God is the praise God gives Himself.

Several commentators take this as the reason the sūrah was revealed in God's words rather than left to human composition. Human beings were given the vocabulary they could not have invented, so that their praise might be praise in His speech and not merely in their own. The scale of the gap is stated elsewhere in a way that removes any vanity from human worship: *There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification* (17:44). Creation praises without ceasing; the worshipper is joining a chorus, not founding one.

**Lord: Master, Arranger, Cultivator**

*Rabb* is rendered Lord, and the word is denser than the title suggests. It carries three senses at once: a master who is obeyed, one who sets affairs in right order, and one who owns. Applied to God, the three combine into a single claim — that He possesses every creature, arranges every creature's affairs, and is therefore the one to whom the creature's obedience is owed. Lordship here is not a rank; it is a description of what is actually happening.

A fourth sense sits behind the others, since the root is also tied to *tarbiyah*, cultivation or rearing. God is the One who brings things along stage by stage, taking each to the state proper to it. That reading changes the tone of the verse considerably. A lord who cultivates is not a distant owner collecting dues but a caretaker whose work is visible in what the thing becomes. The Quran tests the claim by asking pagans who acknowledges it: *Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah!”* (13:16). The admission is expected; what is missing is its consequence.

**"Worlds," and How Many They Are**

The word is plural, and the plural is where the argument begins. *ʿĀlamīn* may denote the communities of beings within each order of existence — humans, jinn, angels, and satans — or only humans and jinn, on the reasoning that the Prophet is called *a warner to the whole world* (25:1) and only those two communities need a warner. Others take it as every generation of humankind, or every species in creation, or simply all existent things other than God.

The second reading has the better textual evidence for inclusion and the weaker conclusion. That the Prophet warns humans and jinn proves those two are among the worlds; it does not prove nothing else is, since a warner sent to some does not exhaust a ruler's domain. The universal reading — everything other than God — is therefore the stronger one, and it is the reading that makes the verse fit its position. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī pressed it as far as it will go, arguing that nothing prevents God from actualising worlds and orders of being of which human beings have no knowledge, so that the phrase covers what can be seen, what can be imagined, and what neither. Praise is then owed by every order of being, known and unknown.

**The Phrase That Closes Things**

This doxology is not unique to the opening of the Quran; it recurs wherever the Book closes a matter. The angels around the Throne end with *Praise be to Allah—Lord of all worlds* (39:75). The people of Paradise close their prayer with it (10:10). It concludes the account of destroyed peoples (6:45) and stands at the end of the sūrah of the Ranks (37:182), and God instructs the Prophet to call upon Him *˹saying,˺ “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds”* (40:65). The formula appears at verdicts, at endings, at the settling of accounts.

What the recurrence establishes is the sense in which the phrase is an assessment. Praise is due before the outcome is known and after it, in hardship and in ease, at the beginning of the Quran and at the end of every judgement in it. The worshipper who opens the prayer with these words is speaking the same sentence creation will speak when the matter is decided.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Names Come Round Again**

Both names appeared three words earlier, at the head of the opening formula. The Quran does not usually spend a verse repeating itself, so the question of what the second occurrence adds has a grammatical answer and a ritual one. Grammatically, the phrase attaches either as a description of the Divine Name in the verse before it or as a substitute standing in its place; on either reading it does work rather than echoing. The ritual answer is the more decisive. In the ḥadīth qudsī that maps the recitation as a dialogue, the servant's saying *The Most Compassionate, the Merciful* draws its own reply — *My servant has lauded Me* — separate from the *My servant has praised Me* that answers the verse before it (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). An echo does not receive a second answer.

The verse therefore lifts the two names out of the formula that opens an act and makes them a statement about God in their own right. What the basmalah attached to a beginning, this line asserts outright.

**A Name Makkah Refused to Write**

The choice of word carried a cost, and the evidence for it survives in the treaty of al-Ḥudaybiyyah. When the Prophet dictated the opening of the document, he began *By the Name of Allah, the most Beneficent, the most Merciful*. Suhayl ibn ʿAmr, negotiating for Quraysh, objected to the second name specifically: *As for 'Beneficent,' by Allah, I do not know what it means. So write: By Your Name O Allah, as you used to write previously* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2731–32). The Prophet accepted the substitution and went on to concede the description of himself as Messenger as well.

Two things follow from the exchange. The first is that al-Raḥmān was not a familiar divine title in Makkah; the Quran records the same objection in almost the same words — *“Prostrate to the Most Compassionate,” they ask ˹in disgust˺, “What is ‘the Most Compassionate’?* (25:60). The second is what the concession cost: a name was surrendered to secure a peace, while the sūrah that opens the Book insists on it. The word did not come from the religious vocabulary of the Quraysh. It was installed against their objection, and the Quran later rules the two designations interchangeable in address: *Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names* (17:110).

**Two Forms Built From One Root**

Arabic makes the distinction in the shape of the words. *Raḥmān* follows the pattern that expresses fullness — a quality present in its entirety at once. *Raḥīm* follows the pattern that expresses a settled trait, something its subject does again and again. The first is read as mercy in its widest reach, the second as mercy in its exercise.

The Quran confirms the difference by whom it lets each word describe. *Raḥīm* is said of a human being: the Prophet is *gracious and merciful to the believers* (9:128). *Raḥmān* is said of no creature, literally or figuratively, and the ground the commentators give is that it names the mercy by which existence is given — an act no creature performs. The two are not, then, one idea at two intensities; one of them cannot be shared. That is why al-Raḥmān is placed nearer the unshared Name, Allāh, and why an entire sūrah carries it as its title.

**Mercy That Gives Existence, Mercy That Keeps It**

A second reading divides the pair by function rather than degree. The mercy of al-Raḥmān is the mercy of bringing forth: a thing exists at all because being was given to it. The mercy of al-Raḥīm is the mercy of keeping: what exists is sustained, nourished, and brought along, each according to its kind. On this account every breath instantiates the first and every provision the second, and the pair covers a creature from origin to end.

The distinction is drawn inside the Quran and not imported into it. The universal mercy attaches to the Name that *˹is˺ established on the Throne* (20:5) — the act by which the order of things is governed. The particular mercy is described as directed: *For He is ever Merciful to the believers* (33:43). Both are then summed in a sentence that settles the scope question — *My mercy encompasses everything* (7:156) — and, more strongly still, in the declaration that *Your Lord has taken upon Himself to be Merciful* (6:54). Mercy in God is not a mood that something might interrupt. It is a rule He has written on Himself.

**Where Severity Fits**

That mercy encompasses everything collides with the world as it is, and the commentators took the collision on rather than around it. If every event is mercy, then punishment, loss, and destruction are mercy, and the word risks meaning nothing. The answer offered is that the divine names of rigour — the Avenger, the Abaser — are not outside mercy but mercy acting under the aspect of majesty and decree. Kindness and determining power are inseparable in the single agent, so what is severity on the receiving end is no departure from mercy at its source.

The position can sound like a verbal escape, so its strengths and costs should be named. Its strength is that it refuses to split God's acts between two rival sources. Its cost is that it cannot be checked case by case: why a given suffering is merciful is usually not available to the person suffering it, and the Quran does not pretend it is. What the sūrah does with the tension is order it. Mercy is stated here, before reckoning is stated in the verse that follows, and even the universal statement arrives with a condition attached — mercy is ordained *for those who shun evil, pay alms-tax, and believe in Our revelations* (7:156). Reach and gift are held together, and the two are not the same thing.

**The Sūrah That Takes This Name as Its Title**

Sūrah 55 opens with the single word *The Most Compassionate* (55:1) and builds an entire argument on the favours that follow from it, returning after each to one refrain: *Then which of your Lord’s favours will you ˹humans and jinn˺ both deny?* (55:13). The structure shows what the name is doing here. Mercy is not offered as consolation once the argument is finished; it is the premise the argument runs on.

That is also why the Opening puts these names where it does. The worshipper has just addressed the Owner of every order of being (1:2), and before anything is said about reckoning, is told twice over that this Owner is merciful. The recitation turns next to the Day of Recompense, and it does so from a Name already established.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Readings That Complete Each Other**

The word has been recited in two ways since the first generations, and both go back to the Prophet. Five of the seven canonical readers — Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, Ibn ʿĀmir, and Ḥamzah — recite *malik*, "King"; ʿĀṣim and al-Kisāʾī recite *mālik*, "Owner," and it is ʿĀṣim's reading, through the transmitter Ḥafṣ, that most of the world recites today. Neither reading can be preferred to the exclusion of the other, and the commentaries argued the comparison in both directions.

One side holds that *malik* is the more intensive term, since every king is a master while not every master is a king. The other holds that *mālik* is the wider term, since ownership carries unrestricted power of disposal, which kingship does not necessarily imply. The dispute is not resolvable by the lexicon, and it does not need to be: the two words name two failures that human authority suffers and divine authority does not. Among human beings there are kings who own nothing and owners who command no one. The recitation says both words at once, in the sense that whichever a worshipper recites is confirmed by the other. In the ḥadīth qudsī that tracks the recitation verse by verse, this line draws the reply *My servant has glorified Me*, with one transmitter adding *My servant entrusted his affairs to Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). Glory and entrusting are exactly what a king who owns produces in a subject.

**Owner, Not Merely Judge**

The construction is an annexation: God is Master *of* the Day. Ownership of a day is a stranger idea than presiding over it. The verse does not say that God will judge on that Day, though He will; it says the Day belongs to Him, and everything that occurs in it is the disposal of an owner over His property.

The consequence is that no part of that Day is independent of Him — not its timing, not its procedure, not the standing of anyone in it. The Quran states the same claim as a question asked into a silence: *“Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!* (40:16). Another sūrah makes the exclusivity explicit: *˹It is˺ the Day no soul will be of ˹any˺ benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺* (82:19). Every form of leverage a person relied on in the world — wealth, lineage, influence, friendship — is property that has changed hands.

**What "Dīn" Carries**

The word translated judgment is *dīn*, and its semantic range is wider than any single English equivalent. It is used in the Quran for obedience, for custom or way of life, and most often for religion itself. It is also tied to *dayn*, a debt, the word used in the long instruction on recording loans: *When you contract a loan for a fixed period of time, commit it to writing* (2:282). The connection is not a pun. A debtor owes what was given; a creature owes what it was given. God is called *al-Dayyān*, the One who settles accounts, and the Day of *dīn* is the day the account comes due.

Read that way, the verse supplies the metaphor the rest of the Quran builds on. Existence is a loan, the term is fixed, and the day named here is the day of repayment. The Quran states the settlement in plain commercial terms elsewhere: *Today every soul will be rewarded for what it has done. No injustice Today! Surely Allah is swift in reckoning* (40:17), and *On that Day, Allah will give them their just penalty in full* (24:25). The scales are set up for exactly this transaction: *We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least. And ˹even˺ if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth* (21:47).

**The Day Every Other King Is Absent**

The claim is not abstract, and the tradition pictures it in a scene. The Prophet is reported to have said that God will take the earth in His grip and fold the heaven with His right hand, then declare, *I am the King: where are the kings of the earth?* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7382; also Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The Quran gives the same image: *when on the Day of Judgment the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand* (39:67), followed by the observation that those who heard this had not estimated God at His true worth.

The scene is what the verse is for. Human authority ends at death and is spectacularly useless afterwards, which is why the sūrah pairs the title with a day rather than with a realm. Nothing about the arrangement changes on that Day — God owns everything now, too — but the ownership that was always true becomes the only one left standing. The Quran describes the currency of this world being refused as legal tender there: *a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession* (2:254), and *a Day in which there will be no ransom or friendly connections* (14:31).

**The Last Name in a Descent**

The four opening verses can be read as a sequence, and several commentators read it as a descent. Allāh names the Essence. Al-Raḥmān names the mercy that encompasses everything. Al-Raḥīm names the mercy directed to whom He wills. Al-Mālik — or al-Malik — names the management of the created order, and it is in a sense the lowest of the four, because it requires an "other" to be real. Mercy can be conceived without a recipient; a king cannot be conceived without subjects.

That reading explains the shape of the whole sūrah. The first half descends from the Divine Principle to the created order, and the second half, from 1:5 onward, ascends from the creature back to the Principle — the worshipper speaking, asking, naming the path, and being answered. The verse on which the two halves turn is this one: the point at which God's governance of everything becomes the worshipper's own future, and therefore his own business.

**Reckoning as a Measure of the Mind**

If the account is real and the date is fixed, then the rational response is preparation, and the tradition measures intelligence by exactly that. When the Prophet was asked which of the believers is wisest, he answered: *Those who remember death often and have best prepared for it with good deeds; such are the wisest* (Sunan Ibn Mājah 4259, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī). The first part of his answer to the same question — which of the believers is best — was those with the best character, so wisdom here is not cleverness but foresight applied.

The Quran gives the same instruction in its own terms: *O believers! Be mindful of Allah and let every soul look to what ˹deeds˺ it has sent forth for tomorrow* (59:18). The "tomorrow" is the Day this verse names. A person who recites *Master of the Day of Judgment* several times a day is rehearsing a verdict, and the rehearsal is meant to change what is sent ahead.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Turns and Speaks to Him**

The first four verses talk about God. This one talks to Him. Everything before it was description in the third person — Name, praise, mercy, ownership — and here, without announcement, the worshipper switches to *You*. Arabic rhetoric has a name for the switch (*iltifāt*), and its effect is immediate: a passage that could have been recited as information becomes an encounter.

The report that maps the recitation as a dialogue registers the break. For the three verses of praise, the response is *My servant has praised Me*, *has lauded Me*, *has glorified Me* — God receiving what is His. For this verse the response changes character entirely: *This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asked for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). God does not receive this clause as praise due to Him; He claims it as the one place where the worshipper and He are both parties. The verse is the hinge of the sūrah, the point at which the recitation stops being about God and becomes addressed to Him, and the point from which everything that follows is a request.

**The Object Stands Before the Verb**

Arabic normally puts the verb first. Here the object is pulled to the front: *iyyāka naʿbudu*, literally "You — we worship." The displacement is the standard Arabic device for restriction, and it changes what the sentence asserts. *We worship You* would report an action and leave room for others. *You we worship* excludes them: worship goes to You and to no one else.

The restriction falls on both halves, and that is easy to miss. The verse does not only say that worship belongs to God alone; it says that the request for help does too. The grammar binds the two clauses into one claim, so that asking anyone or anything else for what only God gives is placed in the same category as worshipping someone else. The verse thus legislates the interior life as well as the ritual one: the reflex of reliance — on wealth, on standing, on one's own planning — is what the fronting is there to cut away.

**Why the Worshipper Says "We"**

The verb is plural. A person praying alone in a room still says *we*, and the commentators give several reasons that reinforce rather than compete. The plural is the voice of the congregation, and the prayer is recited as one body even when it is performed in solitude. It is also the voice of the whole community across time, so that no worshipper prays as a self-contained individual.

Two further reasons go deeper. Humility is one: the word *I* belongs properly to God, and to stand before Him saying *we* rather than *I* keeps the creature from pretending to a singularity that is not his. The other is representative. Human beings are described as those God *placed… as successors on earth* (6:165), and on that basis the worshipper is understood to speak on behalf of creation rather than only for himself. The recitation is never private in the sense of being about one person's needs, and the plural makes that a grammatical fact rather than a pious intention.

**Worship Here Means Servitude**

The verb is *naʿbudu*, from the same root as *ʿabd*, a servant or slave. The English word worship carries chiefly the sense of ceremony, but the Arabic carries the sense of belonging to a master, and the two are not interchangeable. Servitude is the larger category: a person can perform ceremonies and remain his own, and a person can be a servant of God in acts that no one would call ritual.

The Quran states the condition as universal and unavoidable. *I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me* (51:56) makes worship the purpose of the creature rather than one activity among others. *There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission* (19:93) makes it the destiny of everything, willing or not. What is left to human choice is therefore not whether to serve but whom, and the Quran defines the commanded form as *to worship Allah ˹alone˺ with sincere devotion to Him in all uprightness* (98:5). Sincere devotion is the variable, and it is precisely what the fronted object in this verse is protecting.

**The Request Is for Help to Do the First Half**

The order of the two clauses is deliberate and the commentaries give the reason: a person does not ask a king for help without first acknowledging the king's standing. Servitude is confessed, then aid is requested. The sequence is also logical rather than merely polite, since asking for help from someone whose power has not been conceded is not really asking.

What is being asked for deserves the closest attention. The natural reading — help me with my affairs, my illness, my debts — is not wrong, but several commentators tighten it: the help requested is help to worship, since the worshipper has just undertaken something he cannot carry out unaided. On that reading the verse is a circle, and a deliberate one. The servant pledges worship, then immediately asks for the strength to do what he has pledged, which means the prayer contains its own answer to the pride that would make the pledge in the first place. The Quran matches the structure elsewhere: *Seek comfort in patience and prayer* (2:153), and *And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is a burden except for the humble* (2:45). The grounding statement is blunt about who needs whom — *Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺* (47:38).

**Three Motives, Ranked but Not Void**

A report attributed to Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, preserved in al-Kulaynī's *al-Kāfī*, divides worship by motive: those who worship from fear worship as slaves, those who worship for reward worship as hirelings, and those who worship out of love worship as free people, which is the most excellent. A parallel division is attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib in *Nahj al-Balāghah*, with gratitude replacing love in the highest rank.

The ranking should not be read as a disqualification, and the Quran prevents that reading. Fear and hope are named repeatedly as legitimate motives for ordinary believers: *They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear* (32:16), and *call upon Him with hope and fear* (7:56). The tradition is therefore ordering excellence, not sorting the saved. What makes the highest rank highest is that it survives the removal of the other two — the person who would worship God were there no Garden and no Fire is not serving for payment, and the verse's fronted object belongs to him without residue.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Thing Asked For**

Four verses establish who God is and one establishes the worshipper's posture toward Him. Only now does a request appear, and it is not the request a person in difficulty would think to make first. No provision is asked for, no health, no relief, no forgiveness yet — only direction. The sūrah treats guidance as the one gift without which every other gift can be misused.

The report that tracks the recitation as a dialogue gives this half of the sūrah a distinctive ending. When the worshipper reaches the request for the path, the response is *This is for My servant, and My servant will receive what he asks for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). The previous clauses were answered with statements about God — praised, lauded, glorified — and this one is answered with a grant. The structure of the sūrah therefore carries its own argument: a creature who does not know which way to walk has no use for anything else he might be given, and a creature who does know can be given everything.

**"Guide Us" Presumes a Road Already Found**

*Ihdinā* is usually rendered "guide us," but the request is being made by people who already have a revelation, a prophet, and a community. Several commentators therefore read the verb here as asking for something other than first-time direction: perseverance on the path one is already on, and firmness in the truth one already holds. The same verb covers both, and the Quran uses both senses — *And Allah increases in guidance those who are ˹rightly˺ guided* (19:76) describes guidance arriving to people who already have it.

The two readings are complementary rather than rival, and the difference between them is the difference between conversion and endurance. A worshipper who recites this line as a new Muslim is asking to be shown the road. The same worshipper reciting it forty years later is asking not to be taken off it. The Quran promises the second as a consequence of faith rather than a reward for it: *Surely those who believe and do good, their Lord will guide them ˹to Paradise˺ through their faith* (10:9) — their faith is named as the instrument of the guidance, not its price.

**Straight, Not Short**

The adjective is *mustaqīm*, from a root meaning to stand upright. A straight path is defined by the absence of crookedness, and the Quran's own definition of it is negative and then positive in the same breath: *Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way* (6:153). The straightness is a property of the road; the danger is in the alternatives, which are plural while the path is singular.

Some commentators took the straightness to imply a middle way in conduct as well as in belief — a life that neither indulges the world nor flees it, and a religion that holds the outward discipline of the law together with the inward work of the heart. The Quran supports the balance from its own vocabulary, describing the community as *an upright community* (2:143) placed between extremes. What the adjective rules out, in any case, is the idea of a path that bends to suit the walker. A road that accommodates every preference is not straight, and the request in this verse is to be kept on one that does not.

**What the Commentators Said the Path Is**

The identifications differ, and the disagreement is worth reporting with its grounds rather than as a list of opinions. Some said the path is the Quran itself. Others said Islam, or the Prophet. Shiʿi commentaries frequently identify it with ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, or with the Prophet and the Imams after him; several Sunni commentaries name the Prophet together with his first two successors, Abū Bakr and ʿUmar.

These are not competing definitions of a word. Each names what a given tradition regards as the surest guarantee of being on the road, and the guarantee is what is at issue, since the verse is a request and not a puzzle. The reading that best fits the Quran's own usage is the one that keeps the path a direction rather than an institution: *As for those who believe in Allah and hold fast to Him… He will… guide them to Himself through the Straight Path* (4:175). The path leads to God, and everything else — scripture, messenger, community, exemplar — is named as the means by which a person is kept on it. That is also why the complaint of the prophets is phrased as it is: *Surely my Lord’s Way is perfect justice* (11:56).

**The Parable of Two Walls**

The Prophet is reported to have described the path in a parable. Al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān relates that he said God has struck a parable of a straight path with walls on both sides, open doors in the walls, and curtains hanging on the doors. A caller at the head of the path cries, *O people! All of you enter the path and do not deviate*, and a caller above the path warns anyone who moves to open a door: *Woe to you! Do not open it, for if you open it, you will enter it.* The Prophet then supplied the keys: the path is Islam, the walls are the limits set by God, the doors are the things He has forbidden, the caller at the head of the path is the Book of God, and the caller above it is God's admonishment in the heart of every Muslim (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2859; Musnad Aḥmad 17634).

The parable answers an objection the request itself raises. If the road is straight and God is asked to guide along it, why do people leave it? The answer the parable gives is that leaving is never a single leap but an ordinary movement toward an opening that looks like part of the journey. Protection comes from two directions at once — a text outside the walker and a warning inside him — and both are described as callers, which means neither forces the outcome.

**The Road That Becomes a Crossing**

Later tradition read *ṣirāṭ* in a second sense as well: the traverse every creature must make at the end, set over the Fire. The Prophet is reported to have said that the bridge will be laid across Hell and described it as slippery, fitted with clamps and hooks like the thorn of as-Saʿdān, which people cross at speeds matching their deeds — some like the blink of an eye, some like lightning or wind or racing horses, some scratched and saved, and some falling, with the last dragged across (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7439).

The same word thus covers the road of this life and the crossing of the next, and the two are held together by the request in this verse. Guidance now is what makes the passage then. [UNVERIFIED: the widely repeated description of the traverse as "finer than a hair and sharper than a sword" could not be located in the canonical wording; the verified description is the one in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7439.]

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Who the Blessed Are**

The path requested in the previous verse is specified here by the company travelling it. Guidance is never offered in the Quran as an abstract state; it is a road, and roads have people on them. The verse therefore does two things at once: it names the destination's travellers, and it names two groups whose way must be avoided.

The Quran supplies the roll of the first group elsewhere: *whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company!* (4:69). Blessing is described in the past tense — *those You have blessed* (1:7) — and the tense is doing work. The favour is treated as already fixed and settled, not as something pending review, so that the worshipper is asking to be joined to a company whose standing is not in doubt.

Two further descriptions in the Quran indicate what qualifies people for that company. It belongs to *those who are faithful and do not tarnish their faith with falsehood* (6:82), and to those God *elevate[s]… in rank* by faith and knowledge (58:11). Obedience is the entry; knowledge and unclouded faith are the degrees within it.

**The Best-Known Identification, and How Far It Goes**

Commentators proposed several ways of filling in the two negative categories, and the proposals are not all compatible. Some said those who incur displeasure are the idolaters and those astray are the hypocrites. Others mapped the pair onto kinds of wrongdoing — disbelief drawing wrath, lesser sin producing error. Others again read them as moral types: the heedless and the profligate.

By far the most cited identification traces to a report of ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, who said that the Prophet said, *The Jews are those who Allah is wrath with, and the Christians have strayed* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2954, graded ḥasan). The reading has support in the Quran's own usage, since wrath is repeatedly attached to the Children of Israel in their history (2:61; 2:90; 3:112; 5:60) and straying is warned against in language applied to earlier communities (5:77).

The identification should nonetheless be held as a specification, not a definition. It names the clearest historical instances available to the first hearers; it does not restrict the categories to those instances, since the Quran applies both terms elsewhere to idolaters, hypocrites, and murderers (48:6; 4:93; 16:106). What survives the identification is the moral distinction behind it, and that is the substance of the verse.

**Two Ways of Going Wrong**

The two categories are not two intensities of one failure. The tradition distinguishes them by what the person had. Wrath attaches to those who knew and turned away — the failure of a will that has been informed. Straying attaches to those who lack the knowledge in the first place, and it can therefore be involuntary.

The Quran shows this by using the second term of people who are not condemned. Mūsā, describing the blow he struck before his mission, says of himself that he acted when he was among those astray (26:20) — the Arabic is from the very root used here, though most English renderings soften it to "lacking guidance." The same root is used of the Prophet's own pre-revelatory state: *Did He not find you unguided then guided you?* (93:7). Neither passage is a condemnation, and that is the point. Straying can describe a condition someone is in rather than a choice someone has made, which is why the Quran pairs it with the possibility of being found.

The contrast with wrath is sharp. Those who *trades belief for disbelief* has *truly strayed from the Right Way* (2:108) and those who *denies Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, and the Last Day has clearly gone far astray* (4:136); but the specific gravity of the first category lies in knowledge resisted. Where guidance has arrived and been refused, the Quran speaks of wrath, and it speaks of it as earned: *They have invited the displeasure of Allah* (3:112).

**The Grammar Leaves a Door Open**

A detail of construction has drawn the commentators' attention. The blessing is stated as an act with God as its agent, in the past tense. The displeasure is stated in the passive, with no agent named and no assertion that it has actually fallen. The difference is not stylistic.

The Quran's own principle explains why: *Allah does not wrong people in the least, but it is people who wrong themselves* (10:44). Attributing the outcome to the sufferer rather than to God is consistent with the refusal to say that He has acted against them, and the passive form keeps the sentence from foreclosing what has not yet happened. Someone described as subject to wrath may still not have reached it. *Allah… guides to Himself whoever turns to Him* (13:27), and the verse asks to be kept away from the path of those under displeasure while that turning is still available.

The particle *ghayr* — "not" — can be read as attached to the path or to the people, giving either "a path other than theirs" or "the path of those You have blessed, who are not those under displeasure." Both readings were defended, and both arrive at the same meaning, which is why the disagreement has no practical consequence.

**Three Directions**

Read geometrically, the three groups describe three motions. The blessed ascend toward God. Those under wrath descend away from Him. Those astray move sideways, wandering without arriving anywhere. The three exhaust the possibilities of a creature's direction, and the Quran poses the choice as a question about posture: *Who is ˹rightly˺ guided: the one who crawls facedown or the one who walks upright on the Straight Path?* (67:22).

Every person is moving, and the question is only which way. The sūrah that opened with God as the owner of every world and every day ends by sorting the inhabitants of those worlds into three, then asking to be placed with the first.

**Āmīn, the Worshipper's Seal**

The sūrah ends on a request, and the worshipper answers it with a word that is not part of the Quran. The Prophet is reported to have said: *Say Amen when the Imam says, 'not the path of those who earn Your Anger, nor of those who go astray'; all the past sins of the person whose saying of Amin coincides with that of the angels will be forgiven* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 782). A brief pause separates the recitation from the response, marking the word as the worshipper's and not the text's.

The word is usually understood as a supplication — *O God, answer* — and some took it to be one of the divine names. Its position is the point. Having been taught what to ask for, the worshipper adds nothing new, asks for no change, and simply asks that what was said be accepted. Seven verses begin with God and end with a request; the response of the one reciting is to ratify the whole of it.
