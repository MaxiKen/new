# Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn (Chapter 63) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary


## Introduction to the Sūrah

Al-Munāfiqūn is a Madinan sūrah of eleven verses, and it is the only chapter of the Qur'an named for the hypocrites. Its subject is the community that formed around the Prophet ﷺ after the migration: men who entered the new faith outwardly while keeping their old loyalties intact, and who therefore had to be told, in writing, that the arrangement was known.

The sūrah falls into two clear parts. Verses 1–8 address the hypocrites directly, exposing their false testimony, their oaths used as protection, their sealed hearts, their boycott of the Emigrants, and their boast about expelling the believers from Madinah. Verses 9–11 turn to the believers with a warning that echoes the diagnosis from the inside: wealth and children can pull a sincere person away from the remembrance of God, and the time available to give and to repent is shorter than anyone plans for.

The pairing with the preceding sūrah is deliberate. Al-Jumuʿah addresses the believers and legislates the Friday gathering; al-Munāfiqūn follows it with a rebuke to the men sitting in the same gathering. The Messenger ﷺ recited the two sūrahs together in the Friday prayer, al-Jumuʿah in the first cycle and al-Munāfiqūn in the second (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 877), so that the community heard both the invitation and the warning on the same day, every week.

Hypocrisy in this sūrah is not doubt. It is a method: two exits, one public and one hidden, so that a man can leave the room whenever the cost of staying rises.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:1

> When the hypocrites come to you ˹O Prophet˺, they say, “We bear witness that you are certainly the Messenger of Allah”—and surely Allah knows that you are His Messenger—but Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are truly liars.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A True Sentence That Is Still a Lie**

The sentence they pronounce is correct. Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah, and they say so. The verse offers no correction of the wording, because none is needed; what collapses is the speaker, not the statement. Faith in the Qur'an is an affirmation of the heart, and the tongue is its witness. When the two separate, the testimony becomes perjury. So the verse does not say that they misspoke. It says that Allah bears witness that they are liars, and it delivers that verdict in the same breath as its confession of the Messenger's rank.

The ninth sūrah makes the same move against the same men: *“They swear by Allah that they never said anything ˹blasphemous˺, while they did in fact utter a blasphemy, lost faith after accepting Islam, and plotted what they could not carry out.”* (9:74). An oath repeated is not evidence. Knowledge belongs to God, and His knowledge of what a man holds inside is what this verse appeals to. Speech is judged here by the state that produces it.

**Two Witnesses at One Threshold**

The sentence is built on a deliberate contrast of verbs. Of the Messenger it says that Allah *knows* — a statement of fact, undisputed. Of the hypocrites it says that Allah *bears witness* — the same word the hypocrites had just used of themselves. They arrived with a shahāda, a formal attestation, and they receive one back. The difference is that the second attestation cannot be impeached.

There is also an asymmetry in the naming. The Messenger is identified personally and emphatically: *you are certainly the Messenger of Allah*. The accusers remain a category: *the hypocrites*, a class, not a roster. The verse declines to convict named individuals on its own authority, because the record of their words is enough. What the sūrah will do afterwards is describe them so precisely that the description itself becomes the identification.

The outcome of the whole chapter is thus announced at its opening. This is not an inquiry into who is sincere. It is a disclosure of what God already knows, set down for a community that could not see it.

**The Report Behind the Verse**

The occasion that Muslims have transmitted for this passage is concrete. During an expedition, a quarrel broke out between a man of the Emigrants and a man of the Helpers, each calling on his own group for support. ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl, the leading man of Madinah before Islam arrived there, said of the Emigrants that they should not be supported and that on the return to Madinah the mightier would drive out the weaker. Zayd ibn Arqam, then a young man, heard it and reported it. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2772 collects the narration of Zayd himself, who said that the Prophet sent for Ibn Ubayy and his companions; they swore that they had said nothing, and the sūrah then came down. The Prophet recited it to Zayd and told him, *Allah has confirmed your statement*.

Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4907 preserves the same episode from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh, including ʿUmar's demand that the man's head be struck off and the Prophet's refusal: *Leave him, lest the people say that Muhammad kills his companions*. The revelation of the verse, in other words, did not end an argument; it settled one, publicly and in writing.

**"When They Come to You" — the Habit, Not the Incident**

The opening uses the plural noun with the present-tense verb: whenever the hypocrites come to you, they say. The construction describes a practice rather than an accident. These men return, repeat, and repeat again, and the verse marks the repetition.

Their movement is also directional. They come *to you* — to the Prophet in person, not to a doctrine in the abstract. What they are seeking is proximity, and proximity in Madinah was worth having: it carried standing, protection, and a share in decisions. The declaration of faith is the price of admission, and the verse reports how cheaply they pay it.

That is the difference between this passage and a general denunciation of unbelief. An open denier stays away; the hypocrite arrives, sits in the gathering, and speaks the community's own language. His attendance is the disguise. The rest of the sūrah will strip it away piece by piece, beginning here with the words he uses to enter.

**The Burrow With Two Doors**

The Arabic noun for hypocrisy, nifāq, is built on nafaq, a tunnel or burrow, and the lexicographers connect it to the desert jerboa, which digs a passage with more than one opening. One mouth is plain to see; the other, the nāfiqāʾ, is hidden, and through it the animal escapes when the first is threatened. Al-Qurṭubī records this derivation and draws the moral from it. A hypocrite is a creature of two exits, always able to leave by the door nobody is watching.

The image explains the behaviour in the two clauses that follow: an oath becomes a shield, and the covenant is kept only while it protects. It also explains why this verse can call sincere-looking men liars without alleging that every sentence they utter is false. The lie is structural. What they present in the open and what they keep in reserve are not the same thing, and they know it.

The form of the word is durative, a pattern that suggests habitual practice rather than a single lapse. Hypocrisy, in this reading, is not a moment of weakness but a settled method of moving through a community.

**Faith Is Held in the Heart, Not Sold by the Tongue**

The Prophet ﷺ described the behavioural marks of the disease with precision: Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 33 has three — lying when speaking, breaking promises, betraying trusts — and Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 34 adds a fourth, foul and unrestrained speech in disputes. He called the man who carries these traits a hypocrite *until he gives it up*, which means the condition can be shed by repentance and repair. The scholars of the discipline distinguished this lesser, practical hypocrisy from the greater hypocrisy of belief that this verse confronts.

The Qur'an states the greater case plainly. Those who say one thing in public and another in private are described as mocking even while they testify: *“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.””* (2:14). And their self-deception is measured in another place: *“They seek to deceive Allah and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it.”* (2:9).

Both verses hold the tongue and the heart to a single standard. What 63:1 records is the failure at the point where the two are supposed to meet.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:2

> They have made their ˹false˺ oaths as a shield, hindering ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah. Evil indeed is what they do!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Turned Into Armour**

The noun here is junnah, a shield or a covering used in battle, and the verse says the hypocrites have taken their oaths and made one. The comparison is exact, because an oath in the Muslim polity did two jobs at once: it bound a person to the community, and it placed his life, property, and honour under its protection. The men of 63:1 had sworn that they had said nothing of what Zayd reported. Their denial was not a slip of the tongue. It was a piece of equipment.

The same image is used against the same behaviour in another sūrah, in almost the same words: *“They have made their ˹false˺ oaths as a shield, hindering ˹others˺ from the cause of Allah. So they will suffer a humiliating punishment.”* (58:16). The repetition matters. It shows that this is not a portrait of one man's temper but a recognisable strategy, and the Qur'an names it as such twice.

**What the Shield Is For**

Armour is defensive, but it is carried in order to keep fighting. So it is here. Behind the oath, the hypocrite retains everything: his standing in the tribe, his alliances outside the faith, his freedom to sit in the mosque and speak against what is decided there. Nothing in his circumstances has to change, and no question about his loyalty can be pressed, because pressing it would mean calling a man a liar on his oath.

The verse then supplies the offensive use. They hinder others from the Way of Allah — the road, the manner, the whole pattern of life that the revelation marks out. The exegetes read the participle in two directions, and both readings sit in the text. They kept themselves back, and they held others back. Their presence among the believers was the instrument for both: from inside the ranks, a voice can dissuade with far more credibility than from outside.

**Two Ways of Blocking the Road**

Al-Qurṭubī reports the view that their efforts discouraged some of the Jews and the wavering in Madinah from entering Islam, and they did this while professing it. The half-committed look to such men as evidence: if this is what belief looks like, and these are its notables, then the claim on offer is a small one. A community's worst argument is a member.

The second reading is quieter and harder to answer. They turned away themselves, having once come near, and their turning became visible in the shape of their lives rather than in any formal renunciation. Nobody resigns from a mosque; the prayer simply becomes shorter, the alms narrower, the advice more careful. The Way of Allah is blocked by attrition as effectively as by attack.

Either way, the accusation is not of private doubt. It is of obstruction, which is a public act with public costs.

The Qur'an's fuller portrait of the same group confirms the two-front reading. In Sūrah al-Tawbah they are described as a body that works the community's machinery in reverse: *“The hypocrites, both men and women, are all alike: they encourage what is evil, forbid what is good, and withhold ˹what is in˺ their hands. They neglected Allah, so He neglected them. Surely the hypocrites are the rebellious.”* (9:67). Encouraging and forbidding are public acts; withholding is a private one. The verse names both because the disease operates in both.

**Trading a Covenant for a Price**

Elsewhere the Qur'an gives the transaction its commercial name: *“Indeed, those who trade Allah's covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain will have no share in the Hereafter. Allah will neither speak to them, nor look at them, nor purify them on the Day of Judgment. And they will suffer a painful punishment.”* (3:77). A covenant is capital; the hypocrite liquidates it for whatever the market will pay — a reputation, a season of influence, a share of the spoils.

Two further verses sharpen the motive. Fear, not conviction, produces the oath: *“They swear by Allah that they are part of you, but they are not. They only say so out of fear.”* (9:56). And that fear has a target, since the same men dread the believers more than they dread God. What appears as religious profession is, beneath the surface, risk management.

Even their worship is measured on the same scale: *“And what prevented their donations from being accepted is that they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger, they never come to prayer except half-heartedly, and they never donate except resentfully.”* (9:54). Giving that is calculated, prayer that is performed reluctantly — the accountancy of a man who fears the community more than he fears the One he claims to serve.

**The Prophet's Own Description of the Disease**

The Messenger ﷺ gave the marks by which this condition is detected in ordinary life. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 33 records three: a liar when he speaks, a breaker of promises, and a betrayer of trusts. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 34 records four, adding that when he quarrels he behaves with coarse injustice — and notes that a person with one of these traits carries one trait of hypocrisy *until he gives it up*. The door of return is built into the definition.

These narrations describe what the scholars later called the practical hypocrisy of conduct, which a believer can fall into and must repair. The greater hypocrisy — profession without belief — is what 63:1–3 confronts. The two belong together in this sūrah, because conduct forms the habits that make the deeper betrayal possible, and the oath used as a shield is where the habit begins.

The distinction also protects a community from the wrong kind of suspicion. A man who has broken a promise is not thereby an unbeliever; he is a man with a fault to mend, and the Prophet ﷺ said the trait remains only *until he gives it up*. What this verse describes is different in kind, because the oath was never meant to bind the speaker in the first place.

**"Evil Indeed Is What They Do"**

The sentence closes with a verdict whose grammar is worth pausing over. It does not say, evil is what they did, referring to the single denial of that day. It says, evil is what they were doing, a continuous past which the Arabic imperfect sustains, so the whole settled practice falls under judgement.

Against this, the parallel in Sūrah al-Mujādilah ends with a humiliating punishment, and the difference in the two endings is instructive. Here the emphasis falls on the act and its ugliness before any mention of its penalty: the thing itself is evil, and the sentence stands on that fact before reward or punishment is discussed. A community that keeps this verse in view has a standard for judging speech that does not depend on whether the lie is ever proved.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:3

> This is because they believed and then abandoned faith. Therefore, their hearts have been sealed, so they do not comprehend.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cause Is Named Before the Consequence**

The verse opens with a connective that ties it back to everything just said: this is because. The oath turned into a shield, the road blocked, the practice called evil — all of it is traced to one event, a belief that was taken up and then let go. Nothing about the men of 63:1–2 is treated as inexplicable. They are not described as naturally deceptive or constitutionally blind. They are described as people who had something and released it.

That order matters for how the rest of the sentence is read. The sealing of the heart is not presented as a wall dropped from outside onto an innocent man. It comes after a departure, and it answers it. The Qur'an repeatedly arranges cause and effect this way, and the arrangement is the doctrine: God's closing of a heart is a judgement on a direction already chosen, not the reason the man could not choose.

**Which Belief, Which Abandonment**

The Commentators take the first clause in two ways, and each has a reason behind it. On one reading, the hypocrites genuinely believed at first and then reverted, and the verse narrates a real history of apostasy and return to unbelief. This fits the Qur'an's other statements about a people who believed and later disbelieved, and it explains the severity of the language: a fall from real faith is worse than a failure ever to reach it.

On the second reading, their belief was verbal only — they professed the shahāda while denying it inwardly — and abandonment means the exposure of that condition. The reason given for this reading is the need to explain how men whose hearts were never convinced could be said to have believed at all; the answer is that the Qur'an sometimes reports people as they present themselves.

The first reading is stronger. The verse's force depends on a real movement — up, and then down — and the language of going back to disbelief is used in the Book precisely for that.

**The Same Diagnosis in the Language of the Sūrahs**

A near-identical description appears in Āl ʿImrān, where a party of the same sort is exposed in the middle of a battle: *“They were closer to disbelief than to belief on that day—for saying with their mouths what was not in their hearts. Allah is All-Knowing of what they hide.”* (3:167). The phrase about mouths and hearts is the definition the earlier verses of this sūrah were working towards.

Sūrah al-Ṣaff supplies the mechanism in one sentence: *“So when they ˹persistently˺ deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate.”* (61:5). Deviation is presented as an action of theirs, carried on until it becomes an action upon them. Sūrah al-Rūm states the rule in general terms: *“This is how Allah seals the hearts of those unwilling to know ˹the truth˺.”* (30:59). In each case the human decision comes first, and the divine seal ratifies it.

**The Seal as Ratification, Not Fate**

The verb ṭabaʿa means to stamp or imprint, and the Qur'an uses it for the heart because the heart is the organ of understanding. A stamp fixes what is there. Read across the passages where the image occurs, the pattern holds: the seal is applied to a heart that has already turned, and it confirms the turning so that the same inclination becomes an incapacity.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf states the outcome in a way that has unsettled readers of every generation: *“Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment.”* (2:7). What such verses rule out is the idea that unbelief is a neutral position awaiting information. What they do not do is excuse it. A person who has stopped listening is not being described as one who was never offered a hearing.

The two truths stand without contradiction in the Qur'an's own wording, and this verse is where both are visible at once.

**A Sūrah Announced and Men Who Slip Away**

Sūrah al-Tawbah leaves a scene that shows what the seal looks like on an ordinary day. A passage of revelation arrives; the men in question glance at one another and ask whether anyone is watching, then edge out of the gathering. The verse explains the exit in the same words this one uses: *“Whenever a sûrah is revealed, they look at one another, ˹saying,˺ “Is anyone watching you?” Then they slip away. ˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has turned their hearts away because they are a people who do not comprehend.”* (9:127).

The parallel is exact and worth holding beside 63:3, since the ending is identical: they do not comprehend. Loss of understanding is not, in these verses, an intellectual limitation. It is the last stage of a process that began with men who could hear perfectly well and chose to leave the room. The revelation they walked out on was addressed to them, in their own language, in their own city.

The scene also explains why the sūrah's opening verdict is phrased so sharply. Allah does not need the confession of a sealed heart in order to name the condition of that heart, and the men who slip away do not realise that their exit has been written down.

**"So They Do Not Comprehend"**

The last clause names the faculty that has been lost. The verb is related to fiqh, which in Arabic means to grasp a matter, and the Qur'an places its operation in the heart rather than in the mind alone. Understanding of revelation is not a matter of intelligence; the same men who could not follow a prophet's argument could manage their interests with great skill.

That is the cruelty of the condition. Nothing in their reasoning has stopped working. They calculate, negotiate, and evaluate risks, as 63:2 said they did when they used oaths as shields — an entirely rational strategy for a man under scrutiny. What has gone is the ability to see what the strategy costs, and to grasp the difference between a treaty kept for advantage and a faith held for God.

The verse therefore ties knowledge to character. A heart that has decided against the truth will not be persuaded by evidence, because the obstacle is not the evidence.

The next verse will describe what such people look like from the outside.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

> When you see them, their appearance impresses you. And when they speak, you listen to their ˹impressive˺ speech. But they are ˹just˺ like ˹worthless˺ planks of wood leaned ˹against a wall˺. They think every cry is against them. They are the enemy, so beware of them. May Allah condemn them! How can they be deluded ˹from the truth˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Impression They Make**

Two effects are conceded before either is judged. Their bodies impress the onlooker, and their speech holds attention once they start. The exegetes understood the description to fit ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy and some of his circle, who were reported to be tall, well built, and articulate — the kind of men whose presence carries a room. The verse does not deny the effect. It records it.

The Qur'an returns to this danger elsewhere with the same caution: *“There are some ˹hypocrites˺ who impress you with their views regarding worldly affairs and openly call upon Allah to witness what is in their hearts, yet they are your worst adversaries.”* (2:204). Two faculties are at work in such men, appearance and eloquence, and both are worldly assets. Neither is evidence of faith. A community can be captured by the qualities that make a person pleasant to watch, and the cost is exacted later.

What the verse does is separate the impression from the man. The first clause describes what the eye reports; the next sentences describe what the fact is.

**Timber Propped Against a Wall**

The comparison is the sharpest in the sūrah. The Arabic is khushub musannadah: planks or timbers, propped up. The word khushub is the plural of khashabah, a piece of wood, and musannadah is a passive participle of the verb sanada, applied to something leaned or made to rest against a support.

Three points follow from the grammar before any interpretation is added. The planks are plural, so they lean on one another as much as on the wall. They are propped rather than planted, which means they have no roots of their own. And they are wooden, which means they are dead material carrying the shape of something useful.

Al-Ṭabarī reads the image as dependence: these men have no standing that is theirs; they hold position only because the building behind them holds them. Al-Rāzī draws the consequence that dry wood understands nothing, which is why the verse can say of such people that they do not comprehend. Al-Zamakhsharī adds a second possibility, that the reference is to carved wooden panels fitted into walls for ornament: fine to look at, sound to the eye, contributing nothing that supports the house.

**Every Cry Is Against Them**

Anyone who is propped up knows how unstable the arrangement is. So the next clause is a matter of course: they hear every shout as aimed at themselves. The Arabic refers to any cry, and the commentaries take it as the call of a battle, the alarm of a raid, the rise of voices in the market — anything that might mean discovery.

The Prophet's own community supplied an example. In the quarrel narrated in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4907, a scuffle between a Helper and an Emigrant produced two shouts, O the Helpers, and O the Emigrants, and ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy heard in it the beginning of his own end. He was not being threatened. He was being revealed, and the fear of exposure produces the same physical reaction as fear of attack.

The Qur'an describes that fear in another sūrah with cold precision: *“Indeed, there is more fear in their hearts for you ˹believers˺ than for Allah. That is because they are a people who do not comprehend.”* (59:13). Men who feared God as they feared a congregation would have no need of shields.

**"They Are the Enemy, so Beware of Them"**

The verse removes the ambiguity that the previous lines had built up. It does not say they may be difficult, or that their loyalty is questionable. It says they are the enemy, and it follows with an instruction: be on your guard.

The instruction is worth reading exactly as given. It asks for wariness, not for expulsion, and not for the sword. The same discipline appears later in the sūrah, and it appears in the Prophet's reply to ʿUmar when he offered to strike off Ibn Ubayy's head: *Leave him, lest the people say that Muhammad kills his companions*. A community that has just been told it cannot see the difference between a sincere man and a polished one is told to proceed with care rather than with fury.

Beware of them means, in practice, that no secret of the believers should be trusted to them, no decision should hang on their approval, and their presence should not be mistaken for a commitment. It is a policy of distance within the same city, and it is the most a fair-minded community can do when the offence is inside the will.

**A Curse and a Question**

The verse then breaks into imprecation: *May Allah condemn them!* The Arabic phrase is a formula of rebuke, used in the Book for those whose conduct has exhausted description. It is followed by a question that repeats the sūrah's central complaint in another vocabulary: how can they be deluded from the truth, or, in the root sense of the verb, how are they turned away and reversed?

The question is not a request for information. It is the shape a verdict takes when the matter is beyond explanation. The previous verses have given the answer already: they turned and then were turned about; they left the road and were led from it. What remains is the astonishment that anyone would trade a security they hold for the arrangement they chose instead.

The verse that began with the impression a man makes ends with a question about how a man can be reversed. Everything the eye admired has dropped out of the account, and only the direction of the heart remains.

**Judging the Man and Not the Image**

Nothing in this verse denies the value of good appearance or clear speech. The Qur'an praises neither one as a sign of faith, and it refuses to let either stand as proof of it. What is ruled out is the transfer of confidence from a person's presentation to that person's commitment.

The test supplied by the earlier verses of this sūrah can be applied by anyone: the honest man's words and his private choices run in the same direction, while the man who keeps a second exit will eventually be seen leaving by it. That test does not require intuition, and it does not require spying on anyone. It requires only patience, because the wall each man leans on eventually shows whether it can hold him.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:5

> When it is said to them, “Come! The Messenger of Allah will pray for you to be forgiven,” they turn their heads ˹in disgust˺, and you see them ˹O Prophet˺ turn away in arrogance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Invitation, Not a Summons**

The sentence begins with a kindness. What the hypocrites are told is not that they must answer for what they said, nor that witnesses have been collected against them. They are invited to come forward so that the Messenger of Allah may pray that they be forgiven. The offer is mediation, not cross-examination, and it is made first to the men, not to God.

The Qur'an places the same generous option before them in another sūrah in almost identical terms, and it states the outcome plainly: *“˹It does not matter˺ whether you ˹O Prophet˺ pray for them to be forgiven or not. Even if you pray for their forgiveness seventy times, Allah will never forgive them. That is because they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger. And Allah does not guide the rebellious people.”* (9:80). Prophetic prayer is offered as a door, and the men of this verse refuse to walk through it.

Their refusal is what makes the verse so severe. The other community in the Qur'an at least asked for a sign. These men were given an intercessor and turned him down.

**The Head Twisted Away**

The Arabic describes a physical gesture: they twist their heads. The image is not simply of a man declining to answer; it is of a man who finds the proposal distasteful, and lets his neck say so. The commentaries read the movement as mockery and scorn, the sort of response a proud man gives when something beneath his dignity is proposed.

The gesture is followed by another: you see them turning away in arrogance. Two motions, and the second is the one the Prophet ﷺ is told to watch with his own eyes. The verse is unusually direct with its witness, addressing him and reporting what he will observe. The men who were so impressive when they chose to speak are now described in silence, their argument reduced to a twist of the head.

Qur'anic narration takes gesture seriously as evidence, because the body is quicker than the tongue to state a position. Nobody in that gathering had to be told what the answer meant.

**Arrogance, Not Uncertainty**

The cause named at the end is not doubt. The verse does not say they were unconvinced, or that they needed time, or that their circumstances pressed them. It says they turned away as men showing arrogance, and that word carries the whole explanation.

Arrogance of this kind is treated elsewhere as a condition that closes the door on guidance permanently: *“This is how Allah seals the heart of every arrogant tyrant.”* (40:35). The same verdict appears in the final clause of 9:80, where the reason given for unanswered prayers is a loss of faith and the outcome is described as God's refusal to guide those who break out of bounds. What the arrogant man wants is not information. What he wants is to be the one who grants or withholds recognition, and a prophet offering to pray for him is offering a gift that he alone can give.

That is why no amount of proof could have moved him. The obstacle was never the evidence.

**What the Early Commentators Record**

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this passage, reports from the two early authorities Qatādah and al-Suddī that the verse concerns ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy and that when he was urged to go to the Prophet and let him seek forgiveness on his behalf, he refused. In the report as it came down, he refused in a form that inverts the offer: I do not wish that he ask Allah to forgive me. A man invited to accept mercy treated it as an insult.

The reports also show how the Muslims around him read the moment. They had just watched revelation expose a lying oath; they had seen the man's defense collapse; and they assumed that any sensible person would now take the route of apology. They miscalculated the man, not the situation. Repentance does not look like defeat to a heart that wants forgiveness, because a man who wants forgiveness is not counting the cost of the gesture.

**The Cost of Refusing the Prophet's Prayer**

The verse exposes something deeper than a single refusal, since a Muslim's request for prayer is normally an act of humility, and the Prophet's prayer is a recognized means of mercy. To refuse it as humiliating is to have understood the offer as a claim on his standing rather than as a benefit to his soul.

Compare the adulterer and the thief from another setting, who came forward saying they had wronged themselves and were given the Prophet's prayer and the ruling to follow. Compare also the confession at the founding of the community: Moses acknowledged a wrong as his own before it could be found out. What these men have in common is the ability to accept that the honest word is said about them by someone else.

The men of 63:5 lost precisely that. Their dignity rested on never being the ones described from outside, and the price of keeping it was to walk away from forgiveness itself. The verse records the trade without comment because the absurdity speaks for itself.

**The Body That Speaks Before the Mouth**

Everything in this scene is enacted rather than declared. Nothing is said by the accused; a head is turned. That is how the sūrah keeps working: it reads the signals a man sends without intending to, and reports them with the same authority as an oath.

The first verse of the sūrah treats a spoken testimony as a lie; this one treats a silent motion as a confession. A community that learns to read both will not be deceived by the rhetoric of presence. And the Prophet ﷺ himself is told to see the turning away rather than merely to hear about it, since the duty of dealing with such men is now his, and the observation he is asked to make is the honest basis for it.

The next verse says what the refusal means for the prayers that were offered and never received.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:6

> It is the same whether you pray for their forgiveness or not, Allah will not forgive them. Surely Allah does not guide the rebellious people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Door That Has Closed**

The verse opens with an equality that removes an option: pray for them, or do not pray for them. Both cases end in the refusal of forgiveness. The Prophet ﷺ is not being rebuked for praying, and he is not being forbidden to pray. He is being released from an obligation that he had, in his mercy, taken upon himself.

Sūrah al-Tawbah states the same rule and removes every doubt about its force: *“˹It does not matter˺ whether you ˹O Prophet˺ pray for them to be forgiven or not. Even if you pray for their forgiveness seventy times, Allah will never forgive them. That is because they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger. And Allah does not guide the rebellious people.”* (9:80). The number seventy is not a threshold that a further prayer might pass. It is the end of the discussion.

What the verse teaches is that intercession has a jurisdiction. It operates within the will of God and cannot be used to reverse a verdict that has already been pronounced on a heart that died in its refusal.

**The Choice, and the Choice Taken**

The practical moment behind these words is preserved in several narrations in Bukhārī's book of tafsīr. When ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy died, his son ʿAbdullāh, a sincere Muslim, asked the Prophet ﷺ for his shirt to shroud the body in, and then asked him to lead the funeral prayer. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4670 records the request granted. ʿUmar then took hold of the Prophet's garment and protested, naming the man's offences one after another.

The Prophet's reply, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4671, is the answer to the objection: *I have been given the choice, and I have chosen*; and again, *if I knew that forgiveness would come by asking more than seventy times, I would have asked*. He prayed, and the two verses of Sūrah al-Tawbah followed, ending the practice: *“And do not ever offer ˹funeral˺ prayers for any of their dead, nor stand by their grave ˹at burial˺, for they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger and died rebellious.”* (9:84).

Here is the point for 63:6. Even the Prophet's own prayer, offered in the most generous possible spirit, did not overturn the verdict of this verse. Intercession is not a mechanism for bypassing a person's end.

**Why the Intercession Cannot Work Here**

The reason stands in the sūrah's own third verse: the men believed and then abandoned faith, and their hearts were sealed. A prayer for forgiveness asks God to forgive a person who is asking for it. These men were invited to ask and turned their heads. Their refusal was not hesitation; it was a settled position taken openly.

The same reasoning is at work in the prohibition of praying for those who died in a state that they never abandoned: *“It is not ˹proper˺ for the Prophet and the believers to seek forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they were close relatives, after it has become clear to the believers that they are bound for the Hellfire.”* (9:113). That verse came down after the Prophet ﷺ promised to keep seeking forgiveness for his uncle Abū Ṭālib, who had sheltered him for years without embracing the faith; the report is in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from al-Musayyib ibn Ḥazn. The rule it states is hard: affection does not create a jurisdiction.

63:6 is stated in the same key. What is closed is closed from the other side, by the men themselves.

**The Rebels Whom God Does Not Guide**

The verse closes by naming the category: the rebellious, the fāsiqūn, the ones who have broken out of the boundary that was set for them. The same phrase ends the parallel verse in Sūrah al-Tawbah, and it ends the account of Mūsā's people in Sūrah al-Ṣaff: *“For Allah does not guide the rebellious people.”* (61:5). The wording is stable because the diagnosis is.

Two things are being denied at once. Guidance is not distributed according to the merit a man has accumulated, since no community was given more evidence than the one that walked in the desert with a prophet; and it is not withheld for want of evidence either. The obstacle is departure, and departure is an act.

The word fāsiq is worth keeping in view when the sūrah is read whole, because it moves from belief into behaviour. A man who leaves the boundary in his creed and a man who leaves it in his dealings are described by the same term. The second is a warning to the believer who has no intention of ever denying anything.

**The Grief of a Prophet Who Could Not Guide**

A pain runs through this verse that its grammar does not entirely hide. The Prophet ﷺ wanted the men of Madinah to be forgiven; he had asked; he had been told that his asking changed nothing. The Book comforts him elsewhere in similar situations: *“You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills, and He knows best who are ˹fit to be˺ guided.”* (28:56).

The statement is a release from a burden that no sincere man should carry. A teacher's job is to teach, and a warner's job is to warn. Whether the message lands is not his to decide, and the Qur'an repeats that limit because the regret of a compassionate person is a real weight.

The verse's severity is the mercy of a physician stating that a case is beyond treatment. It stops the effort that would otherwise consume the healer and frees him for the ones who will listen.

**Praying for the Living, and Letting the Dead Rest**

The rule in this verse concerns those who die in the state it describes, and it should not be extended to every difficult person in a congregation. Praying for a living man's guidance remains among the most hopeful acts available to a Muslim, and no verse forbids it.

What 63:6 settles is the boundary. A community may pray for those who wrong it; it may not pretend that its prayers can substitute for a change that the offender never made. And it may not offer its standing to men who have made it a shield, which is where the sūrah began.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:7

> They are the ones who say ˹to one another˺, “Do not spend ˹anything˺ on those ˹emigrants˺ with the Messenger of Allah so that they will break away ˹from him˺.” But to Allah ˹alone˺ belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth, yet the hypocrites do not comprehend.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Boycott Aimed at the Community's Wallet**

The plan reported here is not theological. Nobody proposes to argue against the Prophet ﷺ or to disprove his message. The proposal is to stop paying, and the goal is stated in plain material terms: so that they will break away from him. The Emigrants had left Mecca with nothing and were living on the hospitality and produce of the Helpers, so a withdrawal of support would not merely inconvenience them. It would dissolve the body that had formed around the Prophet ﷺ.

Arabic makes the irony legible. The verb for spending here is yunfiqū, from the same root as nifāq, hypocrisy. The man who gives and the man who burrows out of sight are constructed from one set of letters, and the sūrah places them against each other. Where the hypocrite withholds to break a community apart, the believer spends to hold it together.

The verse records the statement as a message passed among them, which is how such plans usually travel. Nobody signs it.

**The Arithmetic They Were Relying On**

Behind the sentence lies a calculation about scarcity. If the Helpers' reserves are finite, then the guests from Mecca are consuming what belongs to Madinah, and every gift to the Emigrants is a transfer away from the people of the city. Ibn Ubayy's circle had watched the balance of wealth move as spoils arrived and the Emigrants grew more self-sufficient, and their resentment had a ledger behind it.

The Qur'an does not answer the arithmetic with a better arithmetic. It answers it by naming the owner of the reserves: to Allah alone belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth. Provision is not a fixed quantity drawn from a Madinan storehouse but a grant from a treasury that no faction controls. The verse elsewhere makes the same claim in the language of measure: *“There is not any means ˹of sustenance˺ whose reserves We do not hold, only bringing it forth in precise measure.”* (15:21).

A man who thinks he can starve God's messenger into submission has misunderstood which side holds the supply.

**A Chapter Heading in the Book of Tafsīr**

The seriousness with which the earliest community treated this verse is visible in the way al-Bukhārī catalogued it. In the Book of Prophetic Commentary, the chapter heading under which one of the narrations of this episode is placed is the closing clause of 63:7 itself: *And to Allah belong the treasures of the heavens and the earth, but the hypocrites comprehend not*. The hadith beneath that heading, Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4907, narrates the quarrel on the expedition, the sentence about returning to Madinah, ʿUmar's demand for the man's head, and the Prophet's refusal to allow it.

The pairing tells us how the report was understood. The revelation was not merely a rebuttal of one slander. It was the public exposure of a faction's ambition, recorded next to the words that defeat it, so that the sentence and its answer stay together in the memory of the community.

**"So That They Will Break Away"**

The plan's target is expressed with a word that means to scatter, to disperse, to be broken up from a gathering. The same word the translation uses of the caravan dispersing after prayer. It is the language of a crowd melting away, and it is exactly what the hypocrites wanted to see: not converts argued out of their faith but a following that quietly loses its centre and drifts.

What made them think they could achieve it was the visible dependence of the early Muslim community on material support. The Helpers had fed and housed the Emigrants for years, and the arrangement was voluntary. A hypocrite thinks in these terms because he thinks in no others: he imagines the faith of others is purchased by money, since his own profession had been.

The answer to such a plan is not a counter-boycott but visible generosity, and the sūrah will name that remedy later when it turns to the believers.

**The Diagnosis Repeated: They Do Not Comprehend**

The verse closes with the same verdict the sūrah has already used. Their failure is not moral laziness or a shortage of information, but a block in understanding, and the word here is fiqh, comprehension of what a thing actually is. They have made a fair analysis of wealth and have missed the only fact that decides the case.

That fact is simple enough to state: whoever holds the supply of the world cannot be coerced, and whoever does not hold it cannot threaten it. A man who expects the Muslim community to collapse for want of funds has bet against the owner of the treasuries. The verse does not call him wicked in this clause, though it does elsewhere; it calls him incapable of understanding, because the error is in how he reasons.

The next verse will show the same men making an identical mistake about honour.

**The Boycott That Did Not Work**

History is not kind to the plan. The community at Madinah outlived the resentment that inspired it, and it did so while the men who had written the sentence kept their seats in its assemblies. No forced redistribution took place, and no starvation campaign was required, because the flow of support was rebuilt by the same people who had been asked to withhold it, acting on a different understanding of ownership.

The verse's placement in the sūrah is itself part of the answer. A strategy aimed at scattering the believers has been recited aloud in prayer by those same believers for fourteen centuries. It is the only thing about the plan that has lasted.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:8

> They say, “If we return to Medina, the honourable will definitely expel the inferior.” But all honour and power belongs to Allah, His Messenger, and the believers, yet the hypocrites do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence About Who Owns the City**

The claim is an insult dressed as a forecast. Once we are back, the mightier will drive out the weaker: two graded words, the honourable and the abject, applied to two groups that the speaker has already sorted. ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, whose sentence this was, had himself in mind among the honourable and the newcomers from Mecca among the inferior. He had been the leading man of Madinah before the migration and had expected a crown; what he got was a guest in his own city who was obeyed.

The verse records the threat without softening it. Nobody in the sūrah is more aware than the hypocrites of the difference between a city's traditional hierarchy and its actual authority, and the statement is an attempt to force the two back into line.

The answer comes in the same sentence. Honour and power belong to Allah, to His Messenger, and to the believers, in that order. What Ibn Ubayy called his was never his to distribute.

**Where the Words Were Said**

The sentence was spoken on the road, and the road ran back from a campaign that had strained everyone. The narrations in the Book of Prophetic Commentary place the episode on an expedition where tempers were short, and where a scuffle between a man of the Emigrants and a man of the Helpers produced a pair of cries summoning their respective factions, the kind of shouting that ends a community if it is answered. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4907 records the quarrel from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh, the Prophet's remark that they should leave the call alone since it was rotten, and Ibn Ubayy's comment that the Emigrants had grown above themselves.

Zayd ibn Arqam heard the sentence about returning to Madinah and carried it back; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4905 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2772 both preserve the report from him. A young man's memory became the record of a leader's threat.

**The Reply: Honour Is Not a Possession**

The Qur'an's answer does not argue about who is noble. It relocates the entire category. Three parties are named as holding honour and power: God, His Messenger, and the believers. Ibn Ubayy had named himself, and the verse does not give him the dignity of a comparison.

Two other passages make the same correction. To those who look for standing in the wrong quarter the Qur'an says: *“Do they seek honour and power through that company? Surely all honour and power belongs to Allah.”* (4:139). And in a sūrah addressed to a Prophet who had just been slandered by the same kind of talk, the comfort is identical: *“Do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Surely all honour and power belongs to Allah. He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”* (10:65).

The second verse explains the first clause of ours. Grief at a loud man's boast is a real temptation, and it is answered by the ownership of the thing boasted about.

**Why the Sentence Was Uttered at All**

Old men do not usually announce a plan to expel their neighbours; they say such things to hear whether anyone agrees. What Ibn Ubayy was testing was the loyalty of his own people, many of whom had entered Islam with him and would have to choose between a chieftain and a prophet. He miscalculated the answer.

The sūrah's own account of his position suggests how the miscalculation formed. He had been the man whose consent mattered, the one who negotiated the city's arrangements and settled its disputes. Islam offered a standard that measured nothing by seniority, and the Emigrants who outranked him in the Prophet's company had no lineage in Madinah at all. A leader facing that change can either accept a smaller seat or try to reverse the arrangement with one sentence.

Ibn Kathīr, in his commentary on this passage, reports on the authority of ʿIkrimah and Ibn Zayd that when the community returned, ʿAbdullāh, the son of Ibn Ubayy, blocked his own father at the city's gate with a drawn sword, refusing him entry until the Prophet's decision was given. The son had understood what the father had not.

**"Yet the Hypocrites Do Not Know"**

The verse ends with knowledge, or its absence, and the verb has changed from the comprehension of the previous verse. There, they could not grasp how provision works. Here, they do not know where honour lies. Both are said of the same men in the same breath, and the sūrah keeps the two failures side by side because they have one source: a mind that measures worth by what can be seen.

The Prophet ﷺ was told that the words were untrue, but he was not told to punish them. Instead, the community absorbed the insult and moved on, which is itself a demonstration of the verse's claim. Men who possess honour do not need to expel anyone in order to prove it.

The honour that endures in this sūrah is the honour God grants to a community that does not answer a threat with a sword.

**The Gift Nobody Can Confiscate**

ʿIzzah, the word rendered here as honour and power, is the strength that makes a person unassailable rather than the respect that makes him admired. It belongs to God absolutely, and it is granted to the believers because they are attached to Him, not because of lineage, wealth, or numbers.

That is why Ibn Ubayy's threat died with him. He did not live long after this expedition, and the community he promised to reorganise kept its order without him. The verse's verdict was tested in the ordinary terms anyone can check: who had the power on the day the sentence was spoken, and whose word stood a year later.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:9

> O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Turns to the Believers**

Eight verses have dealt with men whose profession was a strategy. Now the sūrah speaks to those whose faith is real, and it does not leave them outside the danger it has been describing. The warning is not an accusation; it is a caution, and the caution arrives before the danger has done its work.

The turning point matters for how the sūrah holds together. A community that has just watched a faction exposed for the sake of money and standing might conclude that the disease belongs only to others. The verse refuses that conclusion. Wealth and children can pull a believer off course without any oath being taken, any lie told, or any position betrayed, and the pull is strongest precisely because the goods involved are legitimate.

Being addressed as believers changes the medicine but not the diagnosis. The same two attractions that ruined the hypocrites are named to the believers, and no story of how they ruined them is added, because one is not needed.

**The Two Goods the Qur'an Will Not Call Evil**

Wealth and children are named together because they are the two possessions human beings love without reserve, and the Qur'an grants them their value in plain language: *“Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope.”* (18:46). Nothing in that verse condemns either one. A man's property is a provision from God, and his children are the continuation of his name and his care.

The danger in the present verse is diversion, and the Arabic verb describes being made busy or drawn away. The Qur'an is not asking for withdrawal from either responsibility. It is warning that a good thing can take the room that belongs to something better, and that this happens gradually, without any decision against God being made deliberately.

Which is the harder test to notice. An open sin announces itself; a life filled with legitimate demands can occupy every hour of a believer's attention and be reported honestly as devotion to family.

**What the Remembrance of Allah Covers**

The term is broad enough to hold the ritual and the ordinary at once. It includes the five prayers, the reading of the Qur'an, and the Friday gathering that the previous sūrah had legislated. It also includes keeping God present in decisions about money and household, which is where the two named goods live.

A verse from Sūrah al-Raʿd gives the term its interior face: *“those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort.”* (13:28). Remembrance is not an extra task added to the day. It is the thing that makes the day bearable, and the man who lets it go is losing a support, not merely a duty.

The final verses of Sūrah al-Jumuʿah make the point sharply by showing a congregation leaving the Prophet ﷺ standing while they went after a caravan. The Qur'an's comment on that behaviour is measured: *“Say, “What is with Allah is far better than amusement and merchandise. And Allah is the Best Provider.””* (62:11).

**The Losers, Identified in the Plural**

The verse does not say that such a person loses something. It says that these are the losers, and the plural is deliberate: the man who is diverted, and everyone who depended on what he should have been. The Qur'an calls the class by name: *“the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit. Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺.”* (26:88–89). And elsewhere the same two goods are demoted with a comparison: *“It is not your wealth or children that bring you closer to Us. But those who believe and do good—it is they who will have a multiplied reward for what they did, and they will be secure in ˹elevated˺ mansions.”* (34:37).

The loss, then, is not measured in wasted hours. It is the loss of the one thing that will be carried across, and it is called khusrān, the same word used for a merchant who has emptied his capital into a wrecked caravan.

**A Test, and Sometimes an Enemy at Home**

A harder statement follows close to this one in the Qur'an, and it is worth keeping in view: *“Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward.”* (64:15). The same sūrah calls some spouses and children enemies to the believer, and then immediately orders him to pardon, overlook, and forgive, because the hostility is not always intended. A child who pulls a parent away from God often pulls out of love, and a test that intends no harm is no less a test.

The verse before ours in the sūrah's own sequence shows where an unchecked attachment ends, and the letters of the Book are not gentle about the direction: those men made their arrangements around what they owned. What the believers are offered instead is a standing that survives the loss of everything else.

**The Prophet's Own Saying About Wealth**

The Prophet ﷺ described the same mistake in the voice of the man who makes it. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2959 records him saying that the servant declares, My wealth, my wealth — and then observing that of his wealth only three things are truly his: what he consumed, what he wore until it wore out, and what he gave in charity and so sent ahead. Whatever is beyond that departs from him and is left behind for other people.

The saying reverses the ownership in the sentence. The account that feels solid to the man is the account he cannot keep; the things he thinks he has spent are the things he holds. Sūrah al-Nūr adds the mirror image in a description of men at work in the market: *“by men who are not distracted—either by buying or selling—from Allah’s remembrance, or performing prayer, or paying alms-tax. They fear a Day when hearts and eyes will tremble,”* (24:37).

Both passages make the same demand, and it is not poverty. It is a heart that keeps its bearings while holding whatever it holds.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:10

> And donate from what We have provided for you before death comes to one of you, and you cry, “My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Order to Spend, With a Term Attached**

The command is to give, and the phrase attached to it removes any claim of ownership from the giver: donate from what We have provided for you. The property in a man's hand arrived as a grant, and the giving is a return of a portion of the grant rather than a surrender of his own. The preposition is small and the theology behind it is large.

Then the deadline. Before death comes to one of you — not to all of you, and not at a time announced in advance. The Arabic keeps the event individual and unspecified, so that every reader of the verse stands inside the command without knowing where in the sequence he is standing. The rest of the sentence shows what the deadline will feel like when it arrives, by quoting the person as he arrives there.

Nothing in the verse limits the giving to alms-tax. The term used is a general verb of spending, and the situation it describes is the most ordinary one in a believer's life: money in hand, time in hand, and no plan to spend either of them on God.

**Charity as Proof of a Claim**

Arabic derives the word for charity, ṣadaqah, from the same root as ṣidq, truthfulness, because the money given is how a claim of faith is verified. The word appears in this verse in its verb form, and the man pleading for time uses it as the thing he failed to do. He does not say he would pray more or fast longer. He names the transfer of what he was given.

The linguistic background is worth holding beside the sūrah's opening, where a declaration of faith was shown to be a lie because the heart did not hold it. A claim to believe is verified by conduct, and the conduct the Qur'an keeps returning to is the handling of money. Wealth is the point at which invisible conviction becomes visible, because it is the thing people are least willing to part with for an unseen return.

The verse therefore joins the two halves of the sūrah. The hypocrites of its opening used wealth as a shield; the closing verses show a man learning too late that wealth was the test.

**The Plea That Comes One Moment Too Late**

The words put in the dying man's mouth are a confession disguised as a request. He concedes that he had what he needed and that he did not use it; he asks for an extension not to enjoy himself but to repair the omission. He offers a promise about the future that he had his whole past to keep.

The same plea is recorded in another sūrah in nearly the same form, and the response there is an emphatic refusal: *“When death approaches any of them, they cry, “My Lord! Let me go back, so I may do good in what I left behind.” Never! It is only a ˹useless˺ appeal they make. And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected.”* (23:99–100). Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the rule that governs both texts: *“However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, “Now I repent!” nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.”* (4:18).

What these verses refuse is not repentance. It is repentance as the conversion of a debt into a payment due at the last possible minute.

**What He Promises to Give and to Be**

Two things are promised, and their order is instructive. First he says he would give in charity, an act with a measure and a receipt. Then he says he would be one of the righteous, which is a state of the whole person rather than a transaction. The first is the instrument; the second is the goal. The man who spent nothing had also failed to become what he meant to become.

That is the humbling part of the request. He does not claim to have been righteous and misunderstood. He says he would have become righteous, which is an admission that he never did. Nothing about his own record is defended in those words.

The Qur'an uses the same pairing when it speaks of wealth set in motion toward those who will not repay it in kind: the reward promised is not simply the return of the sum but a station among the people of God. Charity in this Book is never only a subtraction from a balance.

**The Prophet's Warning About Withheld Alms**

The Messenger ﷺ described the withheld payment in terms designed to be unforgettable. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1403, from Abū Hurayrah, reports that whoever has been given wealth by Allah and does not pay what is due on it will find that wealth on the Day of Resurrection in the form of a bald-headed poisonous snake with two dark marks above its eyes, which will be wound around his neck and will bite at his cheeks and say, *I am your wealth, I am your treasure*. The Prophet ﷺ then recited the verse in the Qur'an that warns those who hoard.

The image takes the thing that felt like safety and turns it into the thing that attacks. Every man in the sūrah's audience would recognise the shape of the horror: what you refused to let out of your hands will be the creature that fastens onto you.

**The Delay He Asked For Is the Life He Has**

Nothing in this verse is addressed to a dying man. It is addressed to people who can still open their hands, and the plea it quotes exists in the text so that those people hear the argument in advance, from the losing side.

That is the whole strategy of the closing verses of the sūrah. The man speaks, the answer comes, and the reader of the verse notices that his own hands are still open, that the provision is still in his keeping, and that the moment in which the question is decided has not yet passed. Every day lived in that condition is the reprieve he did not receive.

The next verse states the rule that ended his request.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:11

> But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes. And Allah is AllAware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Plea in the Verse Before**

The request quoted in 63:10 asked for a short extension. This sentence is its reply, and the reply is not a refusal delivered to one man. It is a law stated in general terms so that no one else will ask: Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes. The plea for more time is not met with a lesser amount of time. It is met with the announcement that the quantity was never negotiable.

The Arabic construction carries its own emphasis, an emphatic negation fastened to the noun for a living soul, so the sentence excludes every exception. Rich and poor, tyrant and orphan, prophet and hypocrite: the rule covers all of them, and it is spoken in the same Book that commands men to prepare for it.

The verse deals in the same breath with both halves of the human fear. Nobody dreads dying; people dread arriving without having done what they intended. The answer given here is that the arrival is fixed, which leaves the intention as the only variable a person still controls.

**A Term Set for Every Community, and Every Person**

The Qur'an states the doctrine of the appointed term repeatedly, and the repetition is itself part of the teaching. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf puts it in a form no one can miss: *“For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it.”* (7:34). Sūrah Yūnus speaks the same words to a Prophet being asked for a sign: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah.” For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it.”* (10:49).

Sūrah al-Naḥl gives the reason the delay exists at all: *“If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term. And when their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it.”* (16:61). Sūrah Fāṭir repeats it almost word for word.

The term is thus a mercy before it becomes a boundary. It is the time granted for turning, held open by God for as long as He knows it should be held.

**No Soul Dies Except by the Will of God**

One further statement closes any remaining gap. Death does not happen to a person; it arrives at a time set by God, and it cannot be hurried by a sword or postponed by medicine: *“No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time. Those who desire worldly gain, We will let them have it, and those who desire heavenly reward, We will grant it to them. And We will reward those who are grateful.”* (3:145).

That verse makes the practical consequence explicit, and it is not fatalism. If the term cannot be shortened by prudence or extended by wealth, then the only thing left to decide is what a person wants, and the three options it names are desire for the world, desire for the reward, and gratitude. A man who believes the term is fixed is freed from the two fears that rule the rest of humanity: that someone else's decision will end his life, and that he can buy more of it.

The threat uttered by the hypocrites in 63:8 about expelling the weaker is answered by this same doctrine, quietly, at the end of the sūrah.

**Why the Sūrah Ends on This Sentence**

The sūrah opened with a witness that was false: men saying what they did not hold. It closes with an awareness that cannot be escaped: Allah is All-Aware of what you do. Between those two points it has shown what the difference produces in a community — oaths used as armour, provision mistaken for a weapon, honour treated as property, and a man begging for time.

The final word in Arabic is the divine name that denotes complete, informed awareness, one who knows a thing through and through and is never given news. Coming after a sūrah devoted to the gap between what people show and what they conceal, the name is the verdict on the gap itself. The pretence was never successful, because it was never unseen.

The previous sūrah, al-Jumuʿah, had closed on the same note of things being with God, and the two sūrahs are recited together in the Friday prayer, so the community hears both endings weekly.

**Nothing Was Hidden From the Record**

A person's doings are the subject of the final clause, and the word covers the small and the large: the alms given quietly, the prayer shortened when nobody was watching, the sentence spoken in a moment of anger. God's awareness of them is total, and the verse says so without offering a threat. It does not need one.

That is the soberest ending available to the sūrah, and the most useful. Men who feared every cry because they thought someone was closing in on them are contrasted with an awareness that was never a rumour. The believer reads the same clause and finds something else in it: the companion of the unobserved good deed, the one who saw the charity given when no one else could have known it was given.

The verse closes the chapter without promising the hypocrites anything or threatening the believers with anything. It states what is true, and lets each hearer locate himself in it.

**The Door That Is Still Open**

Everything the man in 63:10 asked for is unavailable after his term comes, and the sūrah has been careful to keep the reader on the near side of that boundary while he reads. That is why the same Book that closes the door so firmly holds it open so widely beforehand: *“Say, ˹O Prophet, that Allah says,˺ “O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins. He is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”* (39:53).

Before the term comes, no record is final and no distance is too great. After it comes, no extension is granted and no promise is accepted. The sūrah of the hypocrites ends with that boundary drawn and the ground on the reader's side of it still free.
