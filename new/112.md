# Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ (Chapter 112) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ 112:1

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺;

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Begins With an Order to Speak**

*Qul* — say. The first word of the sūrah is a command addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and it puts the whole chapter in the register of dictation. What follows is not a description the messenger offers of what he has understood; it is a text he is told to utter, word for word, to whoever asked.

The device is common in the Qur'an and it does specific work. It marks the boundary between the man and the message, and it is used most heavily in the short chapters at the end of the codex — *qul* opens Sūrahs 109, 112, 113 and 114, and appears throughout them. Some scholars of the Qur'anic text, Nöldeke and Wansbrough among them, read these commands as traces of a liturgical instruction: formulas given for recitation rather than paragraphs given for study. The sūrah's later history supports that reading, since this is among the most recited texts in Muslim practice, second only to the Fātiḥah.

A minority of the Companions is reported to have recited the chapter without the *qul*, on the ground that the Prophet ﷺ did not need to be instructed in something he already knew. The argument is theological rather than textual, and the transmitted text keeps the command. Keeping it is also better sense: the verse is a public answer to a public question, and "say" is the direction for delivering it.

**Huwa: A Pronoun Before Any Description**

The subject of the sentence is *huwa* — he. No noun precedes it, no antecedent is given inside the chapter, and the pronoun arrives before any description of the one it points at. The effect is that the sentence begins with pure reference: *He*, and then the naming.

Some exegetes took that bareness as significant in itself and identified *huwa* with the greatest name of God, *al-ism al-aʿẓam*, on the reasoning that a pronoun with no qualifier points to the Divine Self before any attribute is considered. From this came a devotional formula much used in the tradition: *yā huwa yā man lā huwa illā huwa* — O He, O the One other than whom there is no He.

Al-Rāzī pushed the point further and treated *huwa* as indicating a degree beyond what the name Allāh indicates, when Allāh is understood as God in relation to the multiplicity He brings forth. Others held that *huwa* names the Essence considered apart from the attributes, while Allāh names the Essence together with the totality of them. That second formulation is the safer of the two, because it makes the difference one of aspect rather than of rank: not a higher God and a lower God, but the same God considered with and without His descriptions.

**Aḥad Is Not Wāḥid**

The predicate is *aḥad*, and the exegetes insisted that it is not the ordinary word for one. *Wāḥid* is a numerical unity: it is the first in a series, and a second can in principle be added to it. *Aḥad* names an oneness that admits no second and no division. One is a count; the other is a kind of being.

Arabic usage supports the distinction. *Aḥad* appears characteristically in negative sentences — *mā raʾaytu aḥadan*, I saw no one — where it means "anyone at all"; its affirmative use is restricted, and the Qur'an uses it as an affirmative predicate of God here. Some early Muslims are reported to have recited *wāḥid* in this verse, which is one of the reasons the difference was argued about rather than assumed. Others treated the two words as synonyms and considered the dispute empty.

A third formulation refines the pair: *aḥad* conveys an internal oneness, something that is one in itself, while *wāḥid* conveys an external oneness, something that is one in relation to other things. On that account *aḥad* is the stronger word for this verse, since what is being asserted here is not that God outnumbers the gods of Mecca but that He is not the sort of thing that could be numbered. The fourth verse closes the same argument from the other side.

**Allah: The Name That Gathers All the Others**

Between the pronoun and the predicate stands the name: *Allāh aḥad*. In the tradition Allāh is called the gathering name, *al-ism al-jāmiʿ*, because every other divine name and attribute is gathered inside it in principle. The Qur'an says as much in several places: *“Allah has the Most Beautiful Names. So call upon Him by them”* (7:180); *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names.’”* (17:110); *“Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He has the Most Beautiful Names.”* (20:8).

Al-Rāzī used that structure to read this verse as a complete statement of theology in two words. Allāh conveys the totality of the positive attributes — Creator, Knower, Powerful; *aḥad* conveys the totality of the negative ones — without body, without substance, without accident. Affirmation and negation together, in a phrase short enough to say in one breath, are what make the chapter a creed rather than a description.

The arrangement also explains why the sūrah could be called equivalent to a third of the Qur'an: it is the part of the Book that is about God alone, stated without narrative and without law.

**The Question This Verse Was Answering**

The occasion of revelation is reported from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: the idolaters said to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, "Trace the lineage of your Lord for us," and this sūrah came down in answer. The report is in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3364, which Dārussalām grades *daʿīf* on its chain, though its content has been accepted as the occasion by the exegetes across the schools. Other accounts put the same question to a group of Jews, and a third to a group of Christians.

The answer fits a demand for genealogy exactly. A *nasab* requires ancestors and descendants: who begot him, whom he begot. The sūrah refuses both directions in its third verse and refuses comparison in its fourth, and the report in al-Tirmidhī supplies the reasoning — nothing that is born will die, and nothing that dies is inherited from, and God neither dies nor is inherited from. A question about lineage gets an answer about category: God is not the kind of being that has one.

Whether the questioners were pagans, Jews, or Christians changes the polemical target but not the answer, which is why the tradition kept all three versions.

**Equivalent to a Third of the Qur'an**

Two reports in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections state the chapter's weight. Abū al-Dardāʾ narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Is any one of you incapable of reciting a third of the Qur'an in a night?" When the Companions asked how that could be done, he answered that *"Say: He is Allah, One"* is equivalent to a third of the Qur'an — Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 811a. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī reported that a man heard another reciting this sūrah over and over, thought the recitation insufficient, and mentioned it in the morning; the Prophet ﷺ said, "By Him in Whose Hand my soul is, it equals one-third of the Qur'an" — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6643.

The explanation given by the commentators is arithmetical in the strict sense. The Qur'an's content divides into three: what it says about God, what it narrates, and what it legislates. This chapter is the first of the three, in isolation and in its purest form, so reciting it covers one of the three divisions. Reward in the tradition is not proportioned to length; a man who repeats four lines can be doing the work of a man who reads a hundred pages of narrative.

A third report shows the chapter inside daily prayer. A commander sent on an expedition used to close every unit of his prayer with this sūrah; asked why, he said it describes the Most Merciful and he loved to recite it. The Prophet ﷺ answered: "Tell him that Allah loves him" — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7375.

**A Title That Describes Content Rather Than Quoting a Word**

Most chapters take their names from something inside them. This one takes its name from what it does. *Ikhlāṣ* comes from a root meaning to be pure, sincere, and undivided, and it also carries the sense of being saved; the chapter is called sincere because it purifies the one who recites it of every association, and because it is recited with nothing but God in view.

The tradition accumulated many other titles for it — at least twenty are listed — among them al-Barāʾah, the Absolution, because whoever recites it sincerely is absolved of idolatry; al-Munaffirah, the One That Casts Away, because it drives Satan off; al-Najāh, Deliverance, from likening God to creatures; al-Maʿrifah and al-Mudhakkirah, Recognition and Reminder; and Nūr al-Qurʾān, the Light of the Qur'an. Al-Rāzī compressed the whole estimate into an image: the entire Qur'an is an oyster, and the pearl is *"Say, ‘He is Allah—One.’"*

## Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ 112:2

> Allah—the Sustainer ˹needed by all˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Occurs Once in the Whole Qur'an**

*Al-Ṣamad* appears in the Qur'an here and nowhere else. The fourth verse contains another such word, *kufuʾ*, so this four-line chapter holds two of the Book's rarest terms. That rarity is the reason the verse has attracted more interpretation per word than almost any other.

A word used once has no internal context. The Qur'an ordinarily explains itself: a term appears in a narrative, then in a legal ruling, then in a description of the Day, and its range of sense becomes visible from the way it is deployed. *Al-Ṣamad* gets none of that. The exegetes had to leave the text and go to the lexicon, to pre-Islamic usage, and to reports from the Companions, which is why the commentaries record a dozen glosses where they usually record two or three.

The situation also puts weight on the transmitted explanations. When a word is unique, the earliest glosses are not competing readings of a common term but the only evidence there is. Al-Ṭabarī's collection of them, and al-Rāzī's analysis after it, are attempts to reconstruct a meaning that the Qur'an itself declines to illustrate.

**Solid, Not Hollow**

The lexical base is physical. *Ṣamad* describes something solid and impenetrable, a rock with no cavity in it — the opposite of hollow. Applied to a body it means without interior, without a gap that could be filled or a space that could be entered.

From that base the word moved into social usage, where it describes a chief: the *ṣamad* of a people is the one to whom they resort in difficulty, the solid point that does not give way under pressure. Both senses were reported by the early commentators, and the theological readings divide along the same two lines.

Some took the physical sense as a way of saying that God is beyond comprehension — no interior to be entered, nothing to be grasped. Others took the social sense as primary: God is the one all creation turns to, and He turns to nothing. The two are not far apart. A rock with no hollow in it needs nothing put into it; a chief to whom everyone resorts needs no one's help. Both images deny the same thing, which is need.

**The Master Everyone Turns To, and Who Turns to No One**

Al-Zamakhsharī's gloss is the most precise of the social readings. He treats *al-Ṣamad* as a verbal form carrying the sense of a passive participle: the One to whom one betakes oneself — *man ṣumida ilayhi* — when one seeks Him, the Master who is sought, *al-sayyid al-maṣmūd*, in every need. He then draws out the relation: all that is created resorts to Him, none of it is independent of Him, and He is independent of all of it.

That last clause is where the theology sits. Dependence in the Qur'an is directional. Creatures need God continuously, not merely at their beginning; He needs nothing at any point. The image of a solid rock captures this better than the abstract vocabulary does, because a rock does not receive — it is what people lean on and it does not lean.

The verse is also the shortest possible statement of lordship. Two words in Arabic, *Allāh al-Ṣamad*, assert that God exists and that everything else is dependent on Him. Neither claim is argued; both are placed next to the assertion of oneness in verse one, as though the two were one claim.

**The One Who Remains When Everything Else Stops**

A third reading takes *al-Ṣamad* as the eternal, self-sufficient Being who continues after creation has ceased — the One who has always been and will always be. This is the sense in which the word is often translated "the Everlasting" or "the Eternal Refuge," and it is supported by the Qur'an's own statements elsewhere.

*“He is the First and the Last, the Most High and Most Near, and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.”* (57:3) — first without beginning, last without end. *“Everything is bound to perish except He Himself. All authority belongs to Him. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.”* (28:88). And in the sūrah of the Most Merciful: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺.”* (55:26–27).

Set against those verses, *al-Ṣamad* names the survivor. Everything that has a beginning is hollow in the sense the lexicon gives: it has a space in it where its own non-existence sits. What has no such space is what remains when the rest is gone. The reading is not a competing gloss but the temporal form of the same claim — the one who needs nothing is the one who outlasts everything.

**Al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm: The Qur'an's Own Gloss on the Word**

Because *ṣamad* occurs once, its meaning has to be reconstructed from parallel expressions, and the Qur'an has a formula that does exactly the same work in two words. *Al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* — the Ever-Living, the All-Sustaining — appears in the three great verses of lordship: *“Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining.”* (3:2); the same phrase opening the Verse of the Throne (2:255); and *“And all faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining.”* (20:111).

*Qayyūm* is an intensive form meaning the one who subsists by himself and by whom everything else subsists. That is the social sense of *ṣamad* plus the physical sense: no interior need, no dependence, and the standing that everything else leans against. *Ḥayy* adds what *ṣamad* implies: only a living thing can sustain.

Reading 112:2 beside 2:255 is the most reliable way to fix the meaning of the rare word. Where the Throne Verse explains at length — drowsiness does not overtake Him, His seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, their preservation does not tire Him — this verse says it in one word and moves on.

**God Recites This Chapter Back**

A ḥadīth qudsī in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī puts verses two to four into the mouth of God. Abū Hurayrah reported the Messenger of Allah ﷺ as saying: "Allah said: The son of Adam tells a lie against Me, and he has no right to do so; and he abuses Me, and he has no right to do so. His lying against Me is his saying that I will not recreate him as I created him the first time; and his abusing Me is his saying that Allah has begotten children, while I am the self-sufficient Master, Whom all creatures need, Who begets not nor was He begotten, and there is none like unto Me." That is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4975.

Two things in the report matter here. The first is that the sūrah's own verses are quoted as the content of divine speech, which places the chapter in the highest class of revelation for the tradition. The second is the gloss attached to *al-Ṣamad* in the standard translation of the report — "the self-sufficient Master, Whom all creatures need" — which is the social reading, and it is carried by a text in the Ṣaḥīḥ rather than by a lexicon.

The abuse named in the report is the claim of offspring. That is the assertion the next verse of this sūrah denies, and the ḥadīth explains why the denial is not a technicality: to say God has children is to insult Him, because it says He is not *ṣamad*.

**Two Words That Carry the Whole Claim**

*Allāh al-Ṣamad* is the shortest complete theological sentence in the Qur'an. It names God and states His relation to everything that is not God, and it does both without a verb. Arabic allows that economy; the verse uses it.

The density explains the chapter's place in practice. It is recited in the obligatory prayers and in the supererogatory ones, it is the sūrah most often memorized after the Fātiḥah, and it is part of the nightly practice reported from ʿĀʾishah: the Prophet ﷺ would join his hands, breathe into them, recite this sūrah and the two that follow it, and wipe over his body as far as he could, three times — Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī. A text that short can be said in the dark, by anyone, in any state, and it says everything the longer chapters say about who God is.

## Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ 112:3

> He has never had offspring, nor was He born.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Negations Pointing in Opposite Directions**

*Lam yalid wa lam yūlad* — He has not begotten, and He was not begotten. The particle *lam* negates a past action with continuing force, which is why the translation renders it as "has never": the statement is not about a period in God's history but about the whole of it. Nothing that could be called His child has ever existed, and nothing that could be called His father ever did.

The two clauses cover the two directions a genealogy can run. Lineage is a chain, and every link has something above it and something below it. Denying both ends does not shorten the chain; it removes the possibility of a chain altogether. That is why the report from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb connects the verse directly to the question it was answering: nothing born will escape death, and nothing that dies leaves an inheritance, and God neither dies nor is inherited from.

The form of the two verbs also does work. *Yalid* is active and *yūlad* passive, and they are built from the same root, so the sentence is a single word turned around. The Qur'an often makes its negations symmetrical in this way — 57:3, *"He is the First and the Last"* — and the symmetry is the argument. A being with an ancestor has a beginning; a being with a descendant has an end. Neither applies.

**The Claim Being Answered Was a Meccan One**

The assertion this verse denies had a specific shape in the Prophet's audience. The Quraysh and the tribes around them ascribed offspring to God, and the offspring were daughters: the angels. The Qur'an argues with that claim repeatedly and with evident irritation. *“Ask them ˹O Prophet˺ if your Lord has daughters, while the pagans ˹prefer to˺ have sons.”* (37:149); *“‘Allah has children.’ They are simply liars.”* (37:152); *“Has He chosen daughters over sons?”* (37:153).

The same charge appears in al-Anʿām: *“Yet they associate the jinn with Allah ˹in worship˺, even though He created them, and they falsely attribute to Him sons and daughters out of ignorance. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they claim!”* (6:100). And the verse that follows states the argument this sūrah compresses: *“˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and earth. How could He have children when He has no mate? He created all things and has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of everything.”* (6:101).

What is at issue in those verses is physical, temporal procreation — a god who mates, begets, and has descendants. That is the meaning the Meccan claim carried, and it is the meaning this verse refuses. Reading it against that background rather than against later doctrinal controversies keeps the polemics where they belong.

**The Word "Son" Does Not Mean the Same Thing in the Creed**

The verse has been read for centuries as a denial of Christian teaching, and the reading is not baseless: the Qur'an elsewhere rejects the claim explicitly, as in *“The Jews say, ‘Ezra is the son of Allah,’ while the Christians say, ‘The Messiah is the son of Allah.’”* (9:30) and *“They say, ‘The Most Compassionate has offspring.’ You have certainly made an outrageous claim”* (19:88–89).

But the two uses of "son" are not the same, and collapsing them produces a false picture of both religions. In Christian confession the Son is the pre-temporal, uncreated Word, begotten of the Father before all ages — not a being produced at a moment in time, and not the result of a union. In the Meccan claim the progeny of God were physical, temporal, and gendered, and they were the angels of local shrine religion. The Qur'an's own polemic makes the difference visible: it objects that God has been given children *when He has no mate* (6:101), an objection that has no purchase on a doctrine which denies any mate and any moment.

So the primary target of this verse is the pagan assertion. Its extension to Christian doctrine was made early and has been maintained, but the extension works by treating "begetting" as a category rather than as a description, and the verse itself does not settle the question. Both readings are honest; the historical one is better supported.

**Begetting Requires a Peer, and a Peer Divides God**

The argument implicit in the verse is stated openly elsewhere. Offspring is produced by a pair, and a pair consists of beings of the same kind. If God had a child, there would be another of His kind, and the oneness asserted in verse one would be broken from inside. *“How could He have children when He has no mate?”* (6:101).

The Qur'an presses the same point cosmologically: *“Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted. So Glorified is Allah, Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim.”* (21:22). Plurality in the divine produces conflict; conflict produces disorder; the world is ordered; therefore the divine is not plural. That chain runs behind this verse's two short clauses.

The jinn make the same confession in their own chapter, and their wording is almost identical to this one: *“˹Now, we believe that˺ our Lord—Exalted is His Majesty—has neither taken a mate nor offspring”* (72:3), immediately after admitting that *“the foolish of us used to utter ˹outrageous˺ falsehoods about Allah”* (72:4). The denial of mate and offspring is, in the Qur'an's own presentation, what a rational being concludes about God once the foolishness is dropped.

**Nor Was He Born: The Negation of a Beginning**

The second clause is the one that does the strictly theological work. Denying that God has children removes a claim made about Him by others; denying that He was born removes something that would otherwise be assumed by anyone who thinks in genealogies. A god without descendants can still have ancestors, and every pantheon the Arabs knew had both.

Birth implies prior non-existence and a cause. Whatever is born begins, and whatever begins is brought about by something else, and whatever is brought about is not self-sufficient — which is what the previous verse denied by calling Him *al-Ṣamad*. So the two verses are one argument: the solid Master whom all things need cannot have come from another, because that other would then be what everything needs.

*“He is the First and the Last”* (57:3) states the same thing positively. This verse states it negatively, and the negative form suits the question that produced the chapter. A demand for lineage asks where God comes from; the answer is that the question has no application.

**Mujāhid Read It as the Denial of a Consort**

An early gloss from Mujāhid takes the verse to mean that God has no spouse. That is not a competing interpretation so much as the identification of what makes both clauses impossible: a consort is the condition of offspring, and its absence collapses the whole genealogical scheme.

The Qur'an supports the gloss in the passage already quoted (6:101) and in the accusation against those who built kinship between God and the jinn: *“They have also established a ˹marital˺ relationship between Him and the jinn. Yet the jinn ˹themselves˺ know well that such people will certainly be brought ˹for punishment˺.”* (37:158). The Meccan religion had ascribed both children and affinity to God, and Mujāhid's reading attacks the affinity directly.

The advantage of this gloss is that it identifies a single error behind the two negations rather than treating them as separate denials. The disadvantage is that it narrows the verse: read as "He has no wife," the statement is about Meccan mythology, whereas read as it stands it excludes every form of divine generation, in any religion, in any century. The wider reading is the one the tradition kept.

**The Messiah in This Chapter's Own Terms**

The Qur'an's most careful statement about Jesus belongs beside this verse, because it makes the same distinction in the same compressed way: *“It is not for Allah to take a son! Glory be to Him. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!”* (19:35). The negation is followed immediately by the alternative mechanism — creation by command, not by generation.

Al-Nisāʾ states it at length: *“The Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger of Allah and the fulfilment of His Word through Mary and a spirit ˹created by a command˺ from Him. So believe in Allah and His messengers and do not say, ‘Trinity.’ Stop!—for your own good. Allah is only One God. Glory be to Him! He is far above having a son!”* (4:171).

Those verses do what 112:3 does: they remove procreation from God and replace it with command. The difference is only length. This chapter performs the removal in four words of Arabic, which is why it could be memorized by a child and used in an argument by a scholar, and why the tradition counted it as a third of the Book.

## Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ 112:4

> And there is none comparable to Him.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kufuʾ Means Equal, Match, and Spouse**

The word that carries the verse is *kufuʾ*, and like *ṣamad* before it, it appears in the Qur'an only here. Its root denotes equality and correspondence, and Arabic uses the word for a peer, a match, an equal in rank. In the vocabulary of marriage law it names suitability between spouses — the *kafāʾah* that makes two people a proper match — which is why early exegetes connected this verse to the denial of a consort.

The philology was settled early. In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4975, the report that quotes verses two to four as divine speech ends with a note on the word itself: *kufuʾan*, *kafīʾan* and *kifāʾan* are one and the same. The variant forms differ in vowels and in a consonant, not in meaning, and the hadith records that equivalence so that no one would build a doctrine on the difference.

What the word excludes is comparison in either sense. Not merely that nothing matches God in power or knowledge, but that nothing belongs to the same order as He does. A match presupposes a common measure; the verse denies the measure. That is a stronger claim than saying God is greater than everything, and it is the claim that separates this chapter from a statement about superiority.

**Another Verse Already Said This, and This One Stops Earlier**

Al-Shūrā contains the Qur'an's other great denial of likeness: *“˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth. He has made for you spouses from among yourselves, and ˹made˺ mates for cattle ˹as well˺—multiplying you ˹both˺. There is nothing like Him, for He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing.”* (42:11). The two verses agree in content and differ in what follows the denial.

42:11 continues into attributes. Having said that nothing is like Him, it immediately says that He hears and sees — the two faculties creatures also possess, named in the same words used of creatures. The verse therefore performs the difficult operation the tradition calls affirmation without likening: the names are shared, the reality is not.

112:4 stops at the denial. No attributes follow, no qualification, no continuation. The chapter ends on the refusal of comparison, which makes it the sharpest statement of transcendence in the Qur'an and also the one most in need of being read beside 42:11. Taken alone, a bare denial could be mistaken for an emptying of God; the sūrah avoids that by putting the affirmation first, in verses one and two.

**The Word Order Is the Argument**

The Arabic runs *wa lam yakun lahu kufuʾan aḥad* — literally, "and there has not been, for Him, an equal, anyone." The predicate *kufuʾan* is advanced before the subject *aḥad*, and Arabic advances what it wants to emphasize. The fronting puts the emphasis on "equal" rather than on "anyone," so that the sentence denies the category before it denies the members of it.

A secondary effect is that *aḥad* appears a second time in the chapter, in a negative sentence, which is its natural habitat in Arabic. Verse one used it affirmatively, in the restricted way that belongs to God alone; verse four uses it negatively, in the ordinary way, to mean "anyone at all." The same word does both jobs, and the two uses together give the chapter its shape: God is *aḥad* in the sense that no second exists; and no *aḥad* exists in the sense that nothing at all is His equal.

The variant readings here are minor — the case ending of *aḥad* — and they do not affect the sense. What matters is the placement, which is why grammarians cite this verse when explaining that fronting a predicate in Arabic restricts the statement to it.

**Shared Names, Unshared Kind**

Creatures are described in the Qur'an as living, hearing, seeing, knowing, merciful. God is described with the same words. If nothing is comparable to Him, the vocabulary needs an account, and the tradition supplied one: the names are common, the realities are not.

Two errors are excluded by that account, and this verse is the one usually cited against both. The first is *tashbīh*, likening God to creatures — treating His hearing as an organ, His knowledge as a store of information, His hand as a limb. The second is *taʿṭīl*, stripping — refusing God every attribute on the grounds that any attribute makes Him comparable to something. The verse is read against the first because it denies likeness, and against the second because it sits inside a chapter whose first two verses are affirmative: He is Allah, He is One, He is the Master whom all things need.

The distinction between a shared name and a shared reality is not a scholastic refinement. It is what allows a Muslim to say that God is merciful without saying that God's mercy is a feeling, and to say that God hears without saying that God has ears. The denial of comparison is what keeps those sentences from collapsing into either idolatry or emptiness.

**Beyond Likeness and Yet Near**

The verse raises an obvious difficulty: if nothing resembles God, in what sense is He present to the world? The Qur'an answers by holding two statements together. *“Everything is bound to perish except He Himself.”* (28:88). And: *“To Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah. Surely Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing.”* (2:115).

The tradition resolved the pair by distinguishing God as He is in Himself from God as He is disclosed. With respect to His essence, nothing is comparable to Him and nothing perceives Him; with respect to His manifestation in the world, He is nearer than the world is to itself — *“and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein”* (50:16). Transcendence and nearness are not competing claims on this account but claims about different relations.

That resolution is not stated in this sūrah. What the sūrah states is the first half of it, without qualification, in four words. The rest is left to the reader who has the whole Book, which is the usual method of the short Meccan chapters: they assert and they do not explain.

**Why the Chapter Ends on Unlikeness**

The order of the four verses is an order of increasing removal. God is one; God is the Master whom everything needs; God neither begets nor is begotten; nothing is comparable to God. Each step takes away a way of imagining Him — a second, a need, a family, a likeness — and the last step takes away imagination itself.

Ending there is what makes the chapter usable in devotion rather than only in argument. A creed that finished on "He is one" would leave the hearer with a number; a creed that finishes on "nothing is comparable to Him" leaves him with a silence that has been earned rather than assumed. The four verses can be recited in a single breath, and the tradition treats them as sufficient: this is the sūrah a man recited in every prayer out of love, and was told that Allah loved him for it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7375); it is one of the three chapters the Prophet ﷺ recited into his palms each night before wiping them over his body, as reported by ʿĀʾishah in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056.

Al-Ikhlāṣ is usually the second chapter a Muslim child memorizes after the Fātiḥah. What he memorizes last in the chapter is this verse, and what it teaches is that everything he has said so far falls short of what he is talking about.
