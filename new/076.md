# Sūrah al-Insān (Chapter 76) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary


## Sūrah al-Insān 76:1

> Is there not a period of time when each human is nothing yet worth mentioning?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Opens With a Question About Nothing**

The first verse asks about a time when the human being was not anything that could be mentioned. The point is not simply that he did not exist; the wording specifies that he was not *a thing remembered*, not a subject of mention. The distinction is deliberate. Non-existence is one claim; being beneath notice is another, and the second is more humbling. A person does not like to think of himself as having been a non-subject, and the Qur'an's opening move in this sūrah is to establish where every human being started. Before anything else is said about what a person is for, the sūrah says what he was: nothing anyone could point to. The phrase also carries a social sense that the Arabic supports. To be mentioned is to have standing: to be named in an assembly, to count for something in the reckoning of others. The Qur'an's claim is that there was a stretch of time in which the human being had no standing at all, not because someone withheld it but because he was not there to be named. Every status a person acquires later is therefore acquired rather than owned, which is the thought the sūrah will develop in its closing verses about who is admitted into mercy.

**Who Is Meant by Man**

The word is *al-insān*, and the commentators took it in three ways that are not mutually exclusive. It can mean Adam, the first man, during the period before the breath was breathed into him. It can mean the human species, during the stretch of time before it existed. And it can be read as covering both at once, since the account of Adam's formation and the account of every human being's formation are the same account in the Qur'an. The last reading is the strongest, because the sūrah's argument moves in the next verse from this original nothingness to the creation of every person from a drop, and the movement only works if both senses are present.

**Two Ways of Taking the Sentence**

The opening particle allows the sentence to be read as a question or as a statement, and the difference is not trivial. Read as a question, it invites the answer *yes* and makes the point about non-existence emphatic: was there not a period when he was nothing at all? Read as a declaration, it states the fact and moves on: there has come upon man a period of time. The majority of commentators preferred the declaration, on the ground that the sūrah's purpose is to move from that period to what followed rather than to linger on it. A third reading, reported from al-Qushayrī, turns the question the other way and denies that there was ever a time when man was unknown to God, which preserves the emphasis on non-existence while placing it inside divine knowledge. The declarative reading is the strongest grammatically, and it is the one the sūrah's argument needs, since the next verse supplies the contrast: then We created him. There is a rhetorical point in favour of the interrogative reading as well. *Hal* in Arabic introduces a question about a fact already established, and the question form serves to press the known fact on the hearer rather than to elicit information. Both readings therefore do the same work, and the difference between them is smaller than the commentarial literature's attention to it suggests.

**Why Begin With Nothingness**

The opening is also the sūrah's argument in miniature. If a human being was once not a thing at all, then bringing him into existence was not a matter of rearranging existing material; it was the greater act, and everything the sūrah will ask its readers to accept follows from it. The Qur'an makes the comparison explicit elsewhere: *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”* (30:27). A person who concedes that he was made from nothing has already conceded the harder half of the case, and the sūrah is constructed to make him concede it in the first verse.

**The Period Before the Person**

The Qur'an fills in what happened next in several places, and the sequence it gives is consistent: *“He created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺”* (35:11), with the fuller version in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn running from an extract of clay through the drop, the clot, the lump, the bones, and the clothing of the bones with flesh (23:12–14). Between the nothing of this verse and the drop of the next stands the whole of that sequence. What the sūrah asks its reader to hold is the distance between the two ends: from not being mentionable to being a creature with hearing and sight. The middle of that sequence is described elsewhere as beginning with the earth itself — *“And indeed, We created humankind from an extract of clay,”* (23:12), and *“He originated the creation of humankind from clay.”* (32:7). Dust, clay, a drop, a clot, a formed body: the Qur'an never presents the human origin as impressive, and the repetition of that origin across the sūrahs is not an accident of style. It is the answer to pride, and the sūrah will return to it in its final movement.

**Time, and the Name of the Sūrah**

This sūrah is also known as *al-Dahr*, Endless Time, after the phrase used in this verse, and the name is a polemic rather than a label. The Makkan materialists attributed existence and destruction to time: *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time.”* (45:24). The Qur'an's reply is built into the verse itself. The span of time in which a person was nothing is a span that God created and then filled, and time is not an agent that does anything. It is the measure of what the Creator does.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:2

> ˹For˺ indeed, We ˹alone˺ created humans from a drop of mixed fluids, ˹in order˺ to test them, so We made them hear and see.

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Nothing to This**

The first verse named a period in which the human being was nothing that could be mentioned. This verse names the material from which he was made: a drop of mingled fluid. The two verses together form the sūrah's opening argument, and the movement between them is the whole point. A person who was not a thing, and who then became a person, has not arrived at his existence by his own effort, and the sūrah draws the conclusion immediately: he was made *in order to be tested*. Purpose is attached to the origin in the same breath, which is why the sūrah's first two verses are among the most frequently cited in the Qur'an on the question of what a human being is for.

**Amshāj: the Mixed Drop**

The word translated as mixed fluids is *amshāj*, a plural whose root carries the sense of mixing and intermingling. The classical commentators took it as the mingling of the male and female emissions, which is the straightforward reading and the one the early authorities reported. Others extended it to the mixing of constituents within the fluid itself, and later writers noted that the word's shape suits a composite of more than two things. What matters for the verse is the property being named rather than the exact inventory: the beginning of a human being is something mingled, small, and unremarkable, and the Qur'an insists on that beginning every time it describes it — a drop when it is emitted (53:46), a spurting fluid (86:6), and here a mingled drop. The word's grammatical shape is that of a broken plural of the kind Arabic uses for composite things, and the early lexicographers glossed it as things mixed together so that they can no longer be told apart. That is the quality being attributed to the origin of a person: something in which distinct components have been merged, and from which a single human being will be formed.

**Created for the Test**

The purpose clause is explicit: *We created him... to test him*. The Qur'an states the same purpose in its most compressed form elsewhere — *“˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life in order to test which of you is best in deeds.”* (67:2) — and it warns that the test is not avoided by professing belief: *“Do people think once they say, ‘We believe,’ that they will be left without being put to the test?”* (29:2). The wording here is worth attending to, because the test is built into the act of creation rather than added to it afterwards. A human being is not first made and then tried; he is made as the kind of creature who is tried, and the trying is what his life consists of.

**Hearing and Sight as the Instruments**

The two faculties named at the end of the verse are not incidental gifts. They are the channels through which the test is administered, and the Qur'an pairs them with the intellect in its account of human formation: *“And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect.”* (32:9). Revelation comes through hearing, the signs in the world come through sight, and judgement between them is the work of the intellect. A person has therefore been given the equipment the test requires, which is what makes the test fair. He is not asked to know what he has no means of knowing; he is asked to respond to what he has been given the means to perceive. This is also why the Qur'an locates its signs where those faculties can reach: *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth.”* (41:53). The signs are outside the person and inside him, which means the evidence is not reserved for specialists or for a particular place. Someone with working hearing and working sight has everything the argument requires, and where there is a failure, it is a failure of attention rather than of provision.

**The Same Faculties Can Be Refused**

The Qur'an's complaint is never that people lacked the equipment. It is that they had it and did not use it: *“They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with.”* (7:179). The sūrah of the City states the same pairing in a form that makes the responsibility explicit — *“Have We not given them two eyes, and shown them the two ways ˹of right and wrong˺?”* (90:8–10). Faculties given, path shown, and the choice left with the person. That is the whole structure of the test in three lines, and it is the structure this verse has just described.

**A Test That Is Not a Trap**

One further implication deserves stating, because it answers an objection that is often raised against the idea of a test. The One who gave the faculties also set the terms, and the Qur'an is emphatic that the terms are within capacity: *“We never require of any soul more than what it can afford.”* (23:62). A test constructed by the same God who constructed the creature being tested is not a mechanism for catching people out. It is worth saying plainly, because the alternative reading is common: a test is only intelligible if the one setting it wants the passing of the one tested. The Qur'an never describes the trial as a trap and constantly describes it as the condition under which a person becomes something. Gratitude is not demanded by a creditor looking for a default. It is asked for by a Giver who has already given the faculties, the path, and the capacity to walk it. It is the condition under which a free creature can become something, and the sūrah's next verse will say what the test consists of: a way has been shown, and the person must decide whether to be grateful for it.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:3

> We already showed them the Way, whether they ˹choose to˺ be grateful or ungrateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Path Has Been Shown**

Having said that human beings were created to be tested, the sūrah states the form the test takes: the way has been shown. The verb is *hadaynā*, and the object is *al-sabīl*, the road or the way. The combination is precise. What is claimed is not that people were caused to arrive but that the road was made clear to them — the signposting rather than the journey. The distinction is one the Qur'an maintains scrupulously, and collapsing it is the source of most of the confusion about predestination in Islamic thought. God's part in this verse is the showing; the travelling is the human being's, and the two are never confused.

**Two Things the Qur'an Calls Guidance**

Theological precision requires the distinction to be stated. The Qur'an uses guidance in two senses that must not be run together. The first is guidance as showing the way, which is given to everyone without exception — this verse, and the explicit statement in Sūrah al-Layl: *“It is certainly upon Us ˹alone˺ to show ˹the way to˺ guidance.”* (92:12). The second is guidance as enabling, granting success, and opening the heart, which is given to whom God wills. The first makes responsibility possible; the second is a gift. Confusing them produces either the claim that people are condemned without being told, which the Qur'an denies, or the claim that a person guides himself, which it also denies. The practical use of the distinction is considerable. A person who believes the showing is real cannot say of himself that he was never told, and a person who believes the enabling belongs to God cannot say of himself that he arrived by his own strength. The two convictions produce the two poles of the religious life: effort without pride, and reliance without passivity. This verse is a proof text for the first sense, and it is decisive on the point.

**Grateful or Ungrateful: the Words Themselves**

The two outcomes are named with words that carry more than their English equivalents suggest. *Shākir* is grateful, and gratitude in the Qur'an is never only a feeling — it is a response made with what one has been given. *Kafūr* is the intensive form of the root that means to cover over, and it is the same root that gives the word for one who rejects the message. The wordplay is the point: disbelief and ingratitude are the same act in this vocabulary. A person who has been shown the way and does not take it is not making an intellectual error about a doctrine. He is receiving a gift without acknowledging the Giver, and the Qur'an treats that as the root fault from which the others grow.

**The Parallel in Sūrah al-Balad**

The same structure appears in a shorter Makkan sūrah, and the two passages read as variants of a single argument. Faculties are given — *“Have We not given them two eyes, a tongue, and two lips, and shown them the two ways ˹of right and wrong˺?”* (90:8–10) — and the response is left to the person. The two ways are named in the plural to make the point that the alternatives are equally visible. Nobody takes the wrong road because the right one was hidden. He takes it because he preferred it, and the visibility of both is what makes the preference blameworthy. The two passages also share the same grammar of responsibility: the giving is attributed to God, the choosing to the person, and the two are never transferred across. The Qur'an holds that line consistently, and the sūrah of al-Insān is built on it from its second verse to its thirtieth.

**Why Both Outcomes Are Stated Flatly**

The verse states the two possibilities and then stops. It does not resolve them, does not say which outcome is more common, and does not explain why one person is grateful and another is not. That silence is deliberate, and the sūrah will return to it near its close, where it will put human willing and divine willing side by side without resolving the tension between them (76:29–30). The Qur'an's habit throughout is to hold both truths and refuse to systematise them into a theory that would let a person off. What this verse needs to establish is the narrower point: the way has been shown, so no one arrives at the reckoning able to say he was not told.

**A Condition on Punishment Follows**

The legal consequence of this verse is stated elsewhere in the plainest possible terms, and it follows directly: *“And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺.”* (17:15). A shown path is a precondition of accountability, not an optional extra. That is why the Qur'an's descriptions of the Hereafter so often include the question put to the doomed about whether messengers came to them: the answer is always yes, and the admission is what closes the case. Gratitude is owed because the showing happened first. A second consequence follows from the same verse, and it is the one the sūrah will press at its close: because the way has been shown, the failure to take it is attributable to the person and not to the absence of the showing. The Qur'an states the other side with equal bluntness wherever it is raised — *“Whoever Allah guides is truly ˹guided˺. And whoever He leaves to stray, they are the ˹true˺ losers.”* (7:178) — but it never uses that to excuse the refusal. Responsibility and dependence are both asserted, and the reader is left holding both.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:4

> Indeed, We have prepared for the disbelievers chains, shackles, and a blazing Fire.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn Is Immediate**

The previous verse ended with two possibilities — grateful or ungrateful — and this one takes the second of them and states its consequence without preamble. The abruptness is the sūrah's method. No transition is supplied, no apology is made, and the reader is moved in a single line from the offer of guidance to the fate of those who refuse it. The sūrah's material is proportioned deliberately: eighteen verses will describe the reward of the virtuous in concrete detail, and four deal with the punishment of the ungrateful. Stating the punishment first, in a single verse, clears the ground for that generosity. Without it, the reward might read as a general promise made to everyone; with it, the promise is addressed to a particular kind of person, and the reader must ask which kind he is. The effect is to make the two things inseparable: the path was shown, and its refusal has a consequence that is already prepared. Nothing in the verse is conditional, because the condition was stated in the verse before.

**The Vocabulary of Restraint**

Three nouns are listed, and the first two are both instruments of binding. *Salāsil* are chains, the long ones used for dragging; *aghlāl* are shackles or collars, the ones fastened at the neck. The Qur'an uses the second of them in a verse that makes the image exact: *“It is they who have shackles around their necks.”* (13:5). The pairing of the two words covers the whole body — the neck and the limbs — and the picture is of a person wholly restrained, unable to walk where he would go or turn his head to see what he wishes. For a creature whose defining property in this sūrah is that he was shown a way, being bound is the precise inverse of the freedom he was given.

**Why the Punishment Fits**

The correspondence is worth spelling out, because the Qur'an's descriptions of punishment are never arbitrary. A person who was shown the road and refused to be bound by it ends up bound anyway, by something far worse. He declined the restraint of guidance, which is a restraint that leads somewhere, and receives the restraint of chains, which leads nowhere. The principle is stated elsewhere as a rule of proportionality: *“As for those who commit evil, the reward of an evil deed is its equivalent.”* (10:27). Nobody is given a penalty that does not match the act, and the match here is unusually exact. The pattern runs through the whole Qur'anic account of that Day. The mouth that refused to speak the truth is sealed (36:65); the person who would not turn toward his Lord is sealed off from him (83:15); the one who declined to be shown a way ends in a place from which there is no exit. What a person refused to use for its purpose is taken from him, and the loss is not imposed from outside but grows out of what he did with what he had.

**Prepared Already**

The verb is past and completed: We have prepared. The punishment is not being improvised in response to the refusal, and it is not a possibility contingent on outcomes. It is ready, and it has been ready. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the reward — *“And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”* (3:133) — which puts the two destinations on the same footing. Both are prepared, both are real now, and the only open question is which one a person is walking toward. The readiness is part of the warning: nothing about this is provisional. It also tells the reader how the Qur'an conceives of the two destinations. Neither is a metaphor for a state of mind; both are described as prepared places with prepared contents, awaiting their inhabitants. A sūrah that says the punishment is already made is not threatening in the abstract. It is reporting an arrangement that exists now, which is why the warning can be delivered with such economy — one verse, three nouns, and no elaboration at all.

**Who These Disbelievers Are**

The word used here is the active participle from the same root as *kafūr* in the previous verse, and the repetition of the root across two verses is the link the reader is meant to make. These are not a separate category of people who committed a different kind of offence. They are the ungrateful of v. 3, seen at the end of the road. That is why the sūrah can move from *grateful or ungrateful* to *chains and fire* without needing to explain who is being discussed. The alternative was named first, and this is where it goes. There is a further point in the repetition. The Qur'an's categories are moral rather than tribal, and a person enters this one by what he has done with what he was shown rather than by birth, community, or name. No one is described here as belonging to a doomed group by descent, and the sūrah's insistence on the root of ingratitude keeps the fault where it can be repented of. A person becomes grateful rather than ungrateful by an act, and that is available while he is alive.

**The Warning and Its Balance**

A verse of this severity sits in the sūrah next to five verses of extraordinary gentleness about the virtuous, and the placement is deliberate. The Qur'an routinely sets fear and hope side by side, and it refuses to let either be read alone: a warning without a promise produces despair, and a promise without a warning produces presumption. The next verse opens with the other half of the pair — *“Indeed, the virtuous will have a drink”* (76:5) — and the two together are what the sūrah intends its reader to hold. The chains are real, and so is the cup, and the difference between them is gratitude.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:5

> Indeed, the virtuous will have a drink ˹of pure wine˺—flavoured with camphor—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Abrār: the Word and Its Range**

The people being described are called *al-abrār*, from a root whose basic sense is breadth and amplitude. A *barr* is a person whose goodness is wide rather than narrow, given freely rather than measured out, and the derived noun *birr* is the Qur'an's standard term for the comprehensive good that includes right belief, worship, and the care of others. The root is also applied to God Himself — *“Truly He is the Righteous ˹al-Birr˺, the Merciful”* (52:28) — which tells the reader what the word means at its upper limit. The virtuous are named here for a quality of spaciousness, and the sūrah will spend the next eighteen verses describing a reward that matches it.

**A Cup Mixed With Camphor**

The reward's first item is a drink, and its flavouring is named: camphor. In the world the Qur'an was revealed into, camphor was a costly aromatic substance, used in medicine for its cooling properties and in food for its fragrance, and the Arabs knew it as a luxury. The choice is characteristic of the Qur'an's method throughout its descriptions of the Garden: it names the finest thing its first hearers could imagine and then points beyond it. Other passages use the same procedure with different materials — a wine *“whose last sip will smell like musk”* (83:26), and a drink *“crystal-white, delicious to drink”* (37:46). The known object is being used as a pointer rather than as a description. There is a subtlety in the particular substance named here. Camphor is cooling, and the classical commentators who noted this took the property as the point: the drink of the Garden is described with the quality that answers heat, because heat is the condition of the place the inhabitants have left. A reward named by what it relieves is a reward that presupposes what came before it, and the sūrah's hearers, living in a climate where water was the difference between life and death, needed no explanation of the image.

**Why a Drink Comes First**

The order of the rewards is not arbitrary, and the desert setting supplies the reason. In the Arab imagination, and in the Qur'an's own idiom, the first duty of a host to a guest is to give him something to drink, and the first need of someone who has come through a hard journey is water. The sūrah of the Overwhelming lists cups set ready among the furnishings of the Garden (88:14), and the sūrah of al-Wāqiʿah describes the attendants circulating *“with cups, pitchers, and a drink ˹of pure wine˺ from a flowing stream”* (56:18). To open the description of the reward with a cup is to say the obvious thing first: the thirst is over, and the host has already answered it.

**The Mixture, and What It Excludes**

The drink is described as mixed or tempered, which raises a question the Qur'an answers elsewhere. The wine of this world in the seventh century was, as now, a cause of intoxication and its consequences, and the Qur'an's descriptions of the wine of the Garden consistently deny those properties: *“that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication”* (56:19), and *“which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness”* (52:23). The mixture is therefore not a dilution of something harmful but a tempering of something good, and the tempering is part of the pleasure rather than a qualification of it. A drink that harms is not a reward, and nothing harmful is being promised here.

**How the Unseen Can Be Described at All**

A difficulty sits at the centre of every Qur'anic description of the Garden, and the text itself names it rather than avoiding it: *“No soul can imagine what delights are kept in store for them as a reward for what they used to do.”* (32:17). If the delight is unimaginable, then no description of it can be literal in the ordinary sense; if it is undescribable, the hearer is left with nothing. The Qur'an's solution is to use the known as a signpost toward the unknown. Camphor is named so that the reader can place the reward at the far end of a scale he understands, not so that he can picture it exactly. The language is analogical, and it is meant to be read as analogy. That reading is not a modern accommodation; it is what the earliest authorities said about these descriptions, and the Qur'an's own warning against speculation about the unseen supports them. The mistake to avoid runs in both directions. Treating the description as a literal inventory turns the Garden into a version of this world with better materials, and treating it as pure symbol empties the promise of content. The text holds the two together: real delight, described in terms the reader can place.

**The Occasion Behind the Passage**

Verses 5 through 22 are said to have been revealed concerning a particular household: ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Fāṭimah, their two sons, and their servant, who vowed to fast three days and gave their food away each evening to a poor man, an orphan, and a captive in turn. The account is related with variation by several classical commentators. [UNVERIFIED: this report was not located this session in a collection with a graded chain; it belongs to the asbāb al-nuzūl material of the tafsīr tradition.] Its value is illustrative rather than probative, and nothing in the interpretation of the passage depends on it, since the description that follows is general in its wording and applies to everyone who matches it. The wording of vv. 7 through 10 is entirely general — those who fulfil vows, who fear a Day, who feed the poor — and it would be a poor reading of a general text to restrict it to one household. The report's usefulness is that it shows what the general description looks like when it is lived.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:6

> ˹from˺ a spring where Allah’s servants will drink, flowing at their will.

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Cup to Its Source**

The previous verse named a cup; this one names the spring it comes from. The movement is a deliberate widening. A cup is a portion given to someone; a spring is a source he can return to as often as he likes. The reward is therefore not a single act of hospitality but a standing provision, and the difference between the two is the difference between being served once and having been brought to live where the provision is. The sūrah is doing in two verses what several other passages do in one: it gives the drink and then its origin, so that the reader does not imagine a supply that could run out.

**They Make It Gush Forth**

The verb at the end of the verse is striking, and its subject is the people of the Garden rather than God or the attendants. They cause it to gush, and the form of the verb suggests doing so freely and repeatedly. The commentators took this in the straightforward sense: the spring flows wherever they wish and they direct it at will. That is a remarkable thing to say about a reward, because it attributes a measure of command to the inhabitants rather than merely a measure of comfort. The point is not that the inhabitants become creators. It is that the relation between wanting and having, which in this life is mediated by scarcity, accident, and other people's wills, has been made direct. A person in the Garden is not handed what someone else chose for him; he drinks from the spring, and the spring goes where he wants it. The same idea is expressed elsewhere as a description of the whole place: *“There you will have whatever your souls desire, and there you will have whatever you ask for”* (41:31), and *“There they will have whatever they desire”* (16:31).

**The Servants of God**

The people drinking are called *ʿibād Allāh*, the servants of God, which is a different designation from the *abrār* of the previous verse and a different one again from the *muqarrabūn* who appear elsewhere. The term ʿibād is the one the Qur'an uses for the relationship itself: a servant is defined by his relation to his master rather than by his own qualities, and the word appears at the highest point of the Qur'an's address — *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages”* is given to *His servant* (17:106 being addressed to the Prophet in the same vocabulary). To be given the spring is to be given it as a servant, which means the gift confirms a relation rather than establishing a claim.

**Cup and Spring: Two Stations**

The spiritual commentators read the difference between the two verses as a difference of station. Those who drink what is mixed, they said, are those whose love is directed at the divine attributes as they are experienced — mercy, power, generosity — while those who drink from the pure spring love the Giver regardless of which attribute they are receiving at the moment. The reading is reported from Maybudī and al-Kāshānī and belongs to the Sufi commentary tradition rather than to exegesis of the plain sense. What it captures is genuine to the text: the movement from cup to spring is a movement from mediation to immediacy, and the sūrah will repeat it at its close when it says their Lord himself will give them a drink (76:21).

**Provision That Answers Desire**

One of the consistent features of the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden is that the gap between what a person wants and what is available has been closed. In this life, desire and provision are mismatched almost by definition: what one wants is somewhere else, and what one has is not quite what one wanted. The spring that flows at will is an image of a state in which the two coincide. The Qur'an states the same thing about the whole place: *“In it they will have whatever they desire”* (16:31), and this is not described as the satisfaction of every whim but as the removal of the chronic frustration that marks life in a world of scarcity. The distinction matters, because the alternative reading — a place where every appetite is instantly gratified — would describe a nursery rather than a fulfilment. What is being promised is the end of a particular kind of disappointment: the experience of wanting something good and not having it, of being given something and finding it is not what one wanted.

**Why Water Carries the Image**

For the first hearers, and for anyone who has been genuinely thirsty, no metaphor for relief is more immediate. The Qur'an builds its descriptions of the Garden on springs and rivers throughout — *“In each ˹Garden˺ will be two flowing springs”* (55:50), and rivers of water, milk, wine, and honey in the sūrah of Muḥammad (47:15). The persistence of the image is not poverty of invention. It is the choice of the one thing that every human being has wanted at some point and can remember wanting, and it is offered as a pledge: the thirst that is structural to life in this world is not permanent. The Qur'an reinforces the pledge from the other direction as well, by pairing every description of the Garden's water with a description of the Fire's — a boiling drink and no coolness (47:15). The two images are designed to be read together, and the reader is being asked which one he is moving toward. The spring in this verse is therefore not only a comfort. It is a verdict on how the person lived, and the sūrah's audience would have recognised the economy of the image at once: water given away in this life, in a climate where water was costly, is the same water that will be given back without measure.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:7

> They ˹are those who˺ fulfil ˹their˺ vows and fear a Day of sweeping horror,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Marks of the Virtuous Begin Here**

Verses 5 and 6 described the reward; verse 7 begins the description of the people who receive it, and it names two marks. They fulfil their vows, and they fear a Day. The first is an act and the second is a state of heart, and the sūrah will keep the two together throughout the passage. Neither is offered as an isolated virtue. Fulfilling vows without fear of the Day would be mere reliability, and fear of the Day without acts would be anxiety. What is being described is a person whose awe has consequences and whose acts come from awe, which is the Qur'an's standard account of what faith does to a life.

**Vows: the Voluntary Obligation**

A vow, *nadhr*, is an obligation a person takes upon himself rather than one imposed on him. That is what makes it significant as a mark. Anyone may perform a duty that is required of him, and the performance proves little about what he wanted. A vow is different: the person bound himself before he knew what keeping it would cost. The Qur'an treats the keeping of commitments as a defining property of the virtuous, listing it among the items of *birr*: those who *“establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make”* (2:177). The emphasis is not on the size of the obligation but on the fact that it was freely assumed and then honoured.

**The Vow and the Fast**

The classical commentators connected this verse to the occasion reported for the passage — a household that vowed to fast for three days if two sick children recovered, and then kept the fast through hunger. The report is preserved in the tafsīr tradition rather than in a collection with a graded chain, and it has been noted as such in this sūrah's commentary on v. 5. Whether or not that is the precise occasion, the connection to fasting is apt, since fasting is the vow-shaped act par excellence: a person abstains from what is lawful for him, for a fixed period, on his own commitment, at a cost nobody else can see. That is the shape of the virtue this verse names. A vow is also, in the logic of the sūrah, the clearest evidence that a person believes in the Day he fears. Nobody binds himself to an unseen obligation on behalf of a God he expects nothing from.

**A Day Whose Evil Spreads**

The second mark is fear of a Day described in a single phrase: its evil is spreading, or prevailing. The adjective carries both senses, and the commentators took them together. The evil of that Day is not confined to one place or one group; it extends, and it prevails. The same Day is described in the next two verses from the standpoint of those who fear it — they call it *“a horribly distressful Day”* (76:10) — and the fear is attributed to the virtuous rather than to the guilty. Those who will be safe from it are precisely those who spent their lives taking it seriously, and their safety is described later in the sūrah as a rescue from it (76:11).

**Fear as a Standing Condition**

The Qur'an's praise of fear is often misread as a recommendation of anxiety, and the two should be kept apart. The fear described here is a settled orientation rather than a mood, and it is paired with hope throughout the Qur'an: *“They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear, and donate from what We have provided for them.”* (32:16). The same pairing appears in the retrospect the blessed give of their own lives: *“Before ˹this reward˺ we used to be in awe ˹of Allah˺ in the midst of our people.”* (52:26). Awe in the midst of other people — while everyone around was unconcerned — is the mark being described, and it is a description of constancy rather than of temperament. The converse is also stated in the Qur'an, and it is what makes fear a reliable mark: the people who did not fear the Day are the ones who meet it unprepared (74:46–47). Fear, in this vocabulary, is not a feeling that some people happen to have. It is the working of a belief, and a belief that does not produce it is not doing any work. The sūrah is describing people who were not unusual in what they knew and unusual in what they did with it. That is why the description has force as an example: the distance between these people and the ones who neglected the Day is not a distance of information but of attention.

**What the Two Marks Have in Common**

There is a single idea underneath both halves of this verse. A vow binds a person in the present to a commitment he made earlier; fear of the Day binds him in the present to an accountability that lies ahead. Both are ways of letting something other than immediate appetite govern the moment, and the sūrah is describing people who live with their obligations and their end in view at the same time. That is the whole of the discipline the sūrah is recommending, and the next verse will give its most concrete instance: giving food away when the person giving it wanted it. There is also a progression worth observing across the sūrah's description of these people. They fulfil vows (v. 7), they feed others (v. 8), they expect nothing back (v. 9), and they explain their fear (v. 10). The sūrah is not listing four separate virtues. It is describing one life from four angles, and the angles are ordered from the outward act to the inward state that produced it.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:8

> and give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Act the Sūrah Chooses to Name**

When the sūrah wants to show what the fear of that Day produces in a life, it does not name prayer or fasting. It names feeding people. That choice recurs throughout the Qur'an, where care for those who cannot repay is treated as the visible test of a faith that is otherwise invisible. The formulation here is precise: they give food, which is to say a basic necessity rather than a surplus, and they give it to three categories of person who have no claim on them. Nothing in the act is rewarded by the recipient, which is the point the next two verses will make explicit when they record what the givers say to themselves.

**Despite Their Desire For It**

The phrase carries two readings, and the commentators preserved both because the text supports both. The first and plainer sense is that they give the food away while wanting it themselves — while hungry, while the meal is in front of them, at the moment when the claim of appetite is at its strongest. The second takes the attachment differently: they give out of love for God, so that the love which moves them is the love of Him rather than the love of the food. The two are not alternatives in practice, since giving at the moment of one's own hunger is precisely how love of God is demonstrated. The verse therefore holds the cost and the motive in a single phrase. The Qur'an states the test in its most demanding form elsewhere, and the wording is close enough to this verse to be read as its commentary: *“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish.”* (3:92). What is given must be the thing wanted, not the surplus, and the measure of the gift is the reluctance that had to be overcome.

**The Three Recipients**

The people named are a poor person, an orphan, and a captive, and the trio is chosen rather than accidental. The poor person is anyone in want. The orphan is the category the Qur'an returns to more than any other, since an orphan cannot repay and has no one to press a claim on his behalf. The captive is the stranger case, and it is the one that gives this verse its reach. A captive in seventh-century Arabia was an enemy, held after a battle, with no rights that anyone was obliged to recognise. Putting him in the same list as the orphan makes the category of who deserves to be fed depend on need rather than on belonging.

**The Captive, and the Law of War**

The historical setting makes the command concrete. After Badr and the engagements that followed, the Prophet distributed captives among the households of Madinah and instructed them to treat them well, and the resources of Madinah were thin enough that feeding another mouth was a real cost. The commentators took the verse as an instruction to charity towards non-Muslims, including those who had fought against the community, and the Qur'an states the principle in general terms elsewhere: *“Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly and fairly with those who have neither fought nor driven you out of your homes.”* (60:8). Enemy status does not remove the claim of hunger. The Prophet instructed the community in the same three duties in a single sentence, reported by Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī: *“Feed the hungry, visit the sick, and set the captives free.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5373). The pairing of feeding with the freeing of captives is not incidental. Both are obligations toward people who cannot discharge them, and both are stated as duties the community owes rather than as favours it confers.

**Feeding in the Rest of the Qur'an**

The same emphasis appears in the law and in the exhortation alike. Freeing captives is named as a category of expenditure (9:60), and the description of *birr* includes *“freeing captives”* alongside prayer and alms (2:177). The sūrah of al-Balad defines the steep path in the same terms: *“It is to free a slave, or to give food in times of famine to an orphaned relative or to a poor person in dire need”* — and the Qur'an's complaint against the inhabitants of the Fire in Sūrah al-Muddaththir is that *“nor did we feed the poor”* (74:44). Feeding is thus not one good work among many in these texts. It is the test.

**What the Giver Gets Back**

The transaction is deliberately one-sided, and the sūrah makes that explicit in the two verses that follow: the givers want neither reward nor thanks from the people they feed. The Qur'an states the principle governing such giving in the most direct terms available: *“Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good—as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah.”* (2:272). A gift that expects nothing from its recipient is not a loss to the giver, and the sūrah's whole passage is built on that claim. What is given to a poor person, an orphan, or a captive is not spent. It is sent ahead. One further consideration belongs with the first of the three recipients. The truly poor, in the Prophet's definition, are not the people who ask: *“The poor person is not the one who goes around begging people for a date or two, or a morsel of food. Rather, the true poor person is the one who is self-restrained, who does not ask people for anything, and whose poverty is not noticed by others so that they may give charity to him”* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1039; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4539). The verse therefore describes a charity that has to be sought out, since the people who most need it are the ones who will not appear.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:9

> ˹saying to themselves,˺ “We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Words Inside the Act**

The sūrah now gives the sentence that accompanied the giving, and it is the most important verse in the passage. Everything described so far — the vow, the fear, the food handed over at the moment of hunger — is interpreted by this line, and without it the act is indistinguishable from ordinary generosity. The words are most naturally read as something the givers said to themselves rather than to the recipients, and that is a point of some weight. A declaration made to the person receiving the food would risk becoming the very thing the sentence disclaims: a claim for gratitude. Said inwardly, it is a correction of the heart at the moment when the heart is most likely to want something back.

**For the Countenance of God**

The phrase translated as for the sake of God uses the word for face, and the idiom needs unpacking. In Arabic, the face of a thing is the thing itself as it presents itself — its reality rather than its surface — so the face of God is His self and not a part of Him. To do something for the face of God is to do it for Him rather than for what He might give, and the distinction is the one the Qur'an draws constantly: *“Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good—as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah.”* (2:272). The same idea is put in the sūrah of the Night, where the giver is described as seeking *“the pleasure of their Lord, the Most High.”* (92:20). The object of the act is the Person, not the payment. This is also why the sūrah's givers are described as fearing a Day: the same orientation that makes them want nothing from the recipient makes them expect everything from God. The two are not separate attitudes but one attitude seen from two ends, and the sūrah's next verse will supply the second end explicitly.

**Neither Recompense Nor Thanks**

Two things are renounced, and they are not the same thing. *Jazāʾ* is repayment — the return of value given. *Shukūr* is thanks — the acknowledgement that one has been treated well. The first is economic and the second is social, and the second is the harder of the two to give up. A person can manage to want no money back and still want the receiver to know who was responsible, to think well of him, to speak of him afterwards. The verse renounces both, which is why the Qur'an's own definition of the matter is so stringent: the deed belongs to God or it belongs to the desire for recognition, and the two do not share.

**A Second Reading: the Renunciation Has an Exception**

Some commentators took the two negatives differently and read a juxtaposition in the verse: they seek no recompense *from you*, but they do seek it from God. On that reading the sentence is not a renunciation of reward in general but a relocation of it, and the support for the reading is the Qur'an's constant language of recompense as something God gives. The reading has force, and the two are closer than they look: a person who wants nothing from the recipient precisely because he wants everything from God is not a different person from the one described by the plainer reading. The verse's work is done either way, and the weight falls on the preposition — not from you.

**Why the Inward Sentence Matters**

The sūrah is making a claim about where a deed's value resides, and the claim is not peripheral to Islamic ethics. An act is not assessed by its outward shape alone, since the same outward act can be done from half a dozen motives, several of them bad. The Prophet stated the principle in the most cited sentence of the tradition: *“Actions are ˹judged˺ by motives, and every person will have what he intended”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1). The givers in this sūrah are described by what they wanted, which is why the sūrah bothers to record a sentence nobody else heard. The same principle explains why the Qur'an so often reports the private speech of its people — the words of the blessed to themselves, the pleas of the guilty in the Fire. What a person says when no one is listening is the most reliable evidence of what he was doing, and the sūrah treats that private sentence as the act's real content.

The distinction is why the sūrah gives a whole verse to a sentence that no one but God heard. The act was already complete in v. 8; what this verse adds is the reason the act counted, and the Qur'an's interest in that reason is constant throughout its descriptions of the virtuous.

**What This Excludes**

A final implication deserves stating, because it is the one most often resisted. The verse does not permit the giver to feel superior to the recipient, since superiority is a form of repayment in the currency of regard. It does not permit reminders afterwards, which the Qur'an treats elsewhere as an act that cancels the charity it advertises. And it does not permit the calculation that giving will produce a return in this life, in health or wealth or reputation. What is left after all of that has been removed is the act itself, done for a Person who was not obliged to ask for it and who has promised to answer it. The difficulty of the standard should not be understated. A generosity that wants nothing — not money, not recognition, not the private satisfaction of having been generous — is not achievable by an effort of will alone, and the Qur'an's descriptions of the virtuous are descriptions of people who have been changed rather than of people who tried harder.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:10

> We fear from our Lord a horribly distressful Day.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Motive Is Stated Last**

The sūrah has moved from the act to the intention and now to the motive, and the order is the reverse of the one a person would use to describe himself. Asked why they gave, most people would start with the reason and work outward to the deed. These givers are described from the outside in: they fed (v. 8), they wanted nothing (v. 9), and here is why — they fear their Lord and a Day. The placement means that the motive is the innermost thing about the act, the last layer rather than the first, and the sūrah is presenting it as the explanation that makes the other two intelligible. Generosity without this is generosity; with it, it is worship.

**ʿAbūs: the Day That Frowns**

The first adjective applied to the Day describes a face contracted in a scowl. The word is used for a person frowning, and here it is transferred to the Day itself, which is a compact way of saying that the Day's arrival is hostile to those who meet it unprepared. The commentators glossed the severity as belonging to the Day and taking effect on the people in it: the frown is what the Day wears, and the disbelievers' faces adopt it, as the sūrah of al-Qiyāmah puts it when it describes faces on that Day as gloomy (75:24). The image is of an approaching event with an expression on it, and the expression is not neutral. The transference is worth noticing as a rhetorical act. A day has no face, and the Qur'an's habit of describing the Day with the features of a person is a way of saying that the event is not a mechanism. It is an appointment with Someone, and the hostility it carries is directed rather than ambient.

**Qamṭarīr: Severity Compounded**

The second adjective is rarer and heavier. The lexicographers glossed it as an intensification of the first — a frowning carried further — and also as a day that is long, and also as evil folded upon evil. The root suggests contraction and drawing together, which is the same physical movement as a scowl. The effect of the pair is cumulative rather than repetitive: the Day is severe, and then it is severe in a way for which one word is not enough. The Qur'an's vocabulary for that Day is full of such compounds, and the doubling is not decoration. It is the vocabulary of a language straining to name something it has no ordinary word for. The commentators' three glosses also agree more than they appear to. A day that frowns severely, a day that is long, and a day whose evil compounds are three ways of describing the same experience: a distress that does not lift, does not shorten, and does not come singly. Severity measured in duration is how a person in distress actually experiences it.

**They Fear Their Lord, Not Only His Day**

The wording is worth pausing on: *we fear from our Lord* a Day. The fear is directed at God, and the Day is what He is feared in respect of. That construction rules out two misreadings at once. It is not fear of an impersonal process, since the Day belongs to someone and is called His. And it is not a cringing terror of a hostile power, since these are the people the sūrah describes as safe. The fear is the awe of a servant toward a Master whose judgement is real, and it is the same fear that the Qur'an names as the beginning of wisdom in its description of those who stand at night in hope and dread (32:16).

**Why Fear Produces Giving**

The connection the sūrah is making should be stated plainly, because it is the link between the sūrah's two halves. A person who expects to be asked how he used what he was given will spend it differently from a person who expects no accounting, and the difference will show up exactly where this sūrah places it: at the point where the gift costs something and nothing comes back. That is why the Qur'an pairs belief in the Day with care for the poor so consistently, and why its complaint against the guilty in Sūrah al-Muddaththir puts the two failures in a single breath — *“We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the poor”* (74:43–44).

**The Day, and the Two Faces It Produces**

What these givers feared is what the sūrah will say in the next verse they were spared, and the correspondence is exact: they feared the Day and were delivered from it; those who did not fear it will meet it as something that frowns. The Qur'an states the contrast in the language of faces throughout — *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, laughing and rejoicing”* (80:38–39) against faces that are dusty and cast in gloom (80:40–41). The sūrah is inviting its reader to decide, while the decision is still open, which of the two faces will be his. It should be said that the fear described in this verse is not the opposite of hope, and the Qur'an never sets the two against each other. These givers feared a Day and expected a reward, and the sūrah holds both without tension: awe at the judgement and confidence in the Judge are the same posture seen from two sides.

The two adjectives of this verse describe what the second group will be looking at, while the first group will be looking at something else entirely, as the sūrah of al-Qiyāmah says of the blessed when it describes them as looking at their Lord (75:23). Fear of this Day and hope in its Lord are not competing attitudes, and the sūrah holds both without tension.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:11

> So Allah will deliver them from the horror of that Day, and grant them radiance and joy,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Fits the Fear Exactly**

The previous verse ended with these people naming what they were afraid of, and this verse opens with God answering that specific fear. They feared a Day; He will shield them from that Day's evil. Nothing else is mentioned first — no garden, no drink, no garment — because the thing that was dreaded is the thing that is removed. The sūrah is constructed on a principle of exact correspondence between what a person lives for and what he receives, and this is the clearest instance of it in the passage. The reward is not arbitrary largesse. It is the fitting answer to a specific fear held for a specific reason.

**The Root That Gives Both Words**

The verb translated as deliver belongs to a root meaning to guard, to protect, to stand between someone and what would harm him. It is the same root from which the Qur'an's word for mindfulness, *taqwā*, is derived, and the connection is not incidental. Taqwā is the servant's act of putting something between himself and what he fears; God's protection in this verse is the same root applied from the other side. The person who guarded himself against that Day by giving and by awe is guarded from it by the One he feared. The two halves of the root name the two halves of the relationship, and the sūrah's choice of verb makes the link. A person who has spent his life putting the fear of God between himself and what would harm him finds God putting His protection between him and what he feared. Nothing in the verse suggests that the protection was earned by the guarding. It suggests that the two belong together, which is why the Qur'an can promise rescue to those who fear without any hint of a transaction.

**Radiance and Joy: Outside and Inside**

Two nouns are granted, and the commentators read them as a pair covering the whole person. *Naḍra* is a freshness and brightness that shows — the word belongs to the same root used for the radiant faces in the sūrah of al-Qiyāmah (75:22) — and *surūr* is joy, which is inward. The first is what others see and the second is what the person feels. The Qur'an describes the same two together elsewhere: *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, laughing and rejoicing”* (80:38–39), and *“You will recognize on their faces the glow of delight.”* (83:24). What is being promised is not a costume of happiness but a state that reaches the surface.

**Deliverance Before Gift**

The order of the two halves matters. Rescue comes first and the gift follows, which is the reverse of the way rewards are usually imagined. A person pictures himself receiving good things; the sūrah promises first that the thing he was afraid of will not reach him. The distinction is theologically significant, because it means the primary content of salvation is negative before it is positive — an averting rather than an acquisition. The Qur'an puts the same order into the words of the blessed when they look back: *“So Allah has graced us and protected us from the torment of ˹Hell’s˺ scorching heat.”* (52:27). Protection first, then grace. The same order is visible in the sūrah's own sequence: deliverance in this verse, then the Garden and silk in the next, then the ten verses of description that follow. The sūrah takes eighteen verses to describe what the rescued receive and a single clause to say what they were rescued from, but the clause comes first, and the arrangement is the argument.

**The Pattern Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The correspondence between fear and deliverance is not unique to this sūrah. Those who stood in awe in the midst of their people are the ones who are told *“Do not fear, nor grieve. Rather, rejoice in the good news of Paradise, which you have been promised”* (41:30). And the greeting of the angels at the gate names the virtue for which the reward is given: *“Peace be upon you for your perseverance. How excellent is the ultimate abode!”* (13:24). In every case the thing given answers the thing done, and the sūrah of al-Insān is simply the most compact presentation of the pattern.

**What Was Feared, and What Is Removed**

The closing observation is the one the sūrah is built to make. These people are not rewarded because they were afraid in a general sense; they are delivered from the specific Day they feared, and the deliverance is what they had asked for in all but words. Those who did not fear it will meet it as a Day that frowns, wearing the expression described in the previous verse. The sūrah has thus set before its reader two possible relationships to a single event: to fear it now and be shielded from it then, or to disregard it now and meet it then. Everything in the passage hangs on that choice. The verse also answers an objection that might be raised against the sūrah's earlier claim that these people gave out of fear. If fear were merely prudence, it would be a calculation rather than a virtue, and the reward would be a payment. What the sūrah describes instead is a relationship: they feared their Lord, and their Lord shielded them. The reward is personal because the fear was personal.

The verse is also the hinge of the sūrah's structure. Everything before it described what the virtuous did and why; everything after it will describe what they are given. Deliverance is the pivot, and the sūrah moves through it from conduct to consequence without a break.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:12

> and reward them for their perseverance with a Garden ˹in Paradise˺ and ˹garments of˺ silk.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Virtue Named Is Patience**

The reward in this verse is given *for their perseverance*, and the naming is significant because patience is not one of the four things the sūrah has described these people doing. They fulfilled vows, feared a Day, gave food, and renounced repayment. What holds all four together is the quality the Qur'an calls *ṣabr*: the capacity to keep doing a costly thing without immediate return. The sūrah has thus described a life and then named the single virtue the life consisted of, which is the way the Qur'an usually proceeds — the acts are named first and the quality they instantiate is named afterwards. It is also worth noting what patience is not. It is not resignation, and it is not the suppression of complaint for its own sake. The people in this sūrah were active throughout: they vowed, they fed, they renounced, and they kept doing it. Patience here is the duration of a chosen course rather than the endurance of an imposed one.

**Why Patience, and Not Something Else**

Patience in this vocabulary is not passive endurance. It is the steady continuation of an act across the whole period in which it is unrewarded, and the four verses preceding this one are a catalogue of exactly that. Keeping a vow after the crisis that prompted it has passed is patience. Giving away food one is hungry for is patience. Wanting no thanks and receiving none is patience, and it is the hardest kind, since there is nothing in the immediate experience to sustain it. The Qur'an's promise is attached to precisely this: *“Only those who endure patiently will be given their reward without limit.”* (39:10).

**A Reward Without Measure**

The phrase *without limit* or *without reckoning* is the distinctive feature of patience's wage in the Qur'an. Other deeds are repaid by a stated proportion — *“Whoever does an evil deed will only be paid back with its equivalent”* (40:40), and good deeds by tenfold and more — but patience is described as repaid without calculation. The reason usually given is that patience is the one virtue whose exercise is coextensive with a whole life rather than confined to an act, and so the reward cannot be proportioned to a discrete deed. A person who held on until the end has done something that does not reduce to a number.

The same disproportion runs through the Qur'an's treatment of reward as a whole: a good deed is repaid ten times over and more, while the patience that sustained a life of good deeds is repaid without arithmetic at all. The point of the phrasing is not only that God is generous beyond measure, though He is, but that some acts are not measurable, and the wage follows the act.

**Garden and Silk: Place and Garment**

The reward has two parts, and they answer each other. The Garden is where the person is; the silk is what he wears. Neither is incidental, and the Qur'an pairs them with the same consistency that it pairs other rewards: *“It is they who will have the Gardens of Eternity, with rivers flowing under their feet. There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade, reclining there on ˹canopied˺ couches.”* (18:31). The same pairing appears in 22:23 and 35:33. The reward covers the person and his surroundings, which is to say nothing about him has been left out.

**What Is Withheld Now Is Given Then**

The silk deserves a note, because it is the item in this list that would have struck the first hearers most. Silk, like gold, was withheld from men in this life by the Prophet's instruction and permitted to them in the next, The Prophet stated the prohibition with a gesture that made it unmistakable, taking silk in one hand and gold in the other and declaring them forbidden to the men of his community and permitted to its women (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4057; Sunan al-Nasāʾī 5144; Sunan Ibn Mājah 3595, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī). The sūrah's promise of silk is therefore not an arbitrary luxury. It is the return, in a place where it is lawful, of what was given up in a place where it was not, and the same logic governs the whole description that follows.

**The Reward Matches the Life**

The sūrah has now completed its account of the transaction. These people kept a vow, feared a Day, handed over food they wanted, asked for nothing, and did it steadily. What they receive is a place, a garment, and — as the following verses will describe — a cup, a couch, and a kingdom. The disproportion is deliberate and it is the point of the passage: the acts were small and temporary, and what is given for them is neither. Every description of the reward in the Qur'an rests on that disproportion, and the sūrah will spend ten more verses making it felt. The disproportion is the point rather than an embarrassment. If the reward were proportionate, the transaction would be a wage and the relationship would be a contract; the Qur'an's insistence on excess is its way of saying that what is given is a gift from a Giver and not a payment from an employer.

The garment deserves one further note. Clothing in the Qur'an carries a moral sense from the beginning of the human story, and to be clothed is to be covered rather than exposed, honoured rather than shamed. Being given a garment in the Garden is not only an adornment. It is the public reversal of the exposure that the Day threatens, and the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf names the same gift in the same terms (7:26).

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:13

> There they will be reclining on ˹canopied˺ couches, never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Posture of Rest**

The inhabitants are described first by how they are sitting. *Muttakiʾīn* means reclining, leaning back, settled in a posture that requires no further effort, and the word appears in the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden whenever the point is that labour is over. The couch they recline on is called an *arīka*, which is not a bench but a canopied bed set in a bridal chamber — a piece of furniture associated in the Arabic of the period with a private, festive room rather than with a hall. The combination of the two words establishes the tone of the whole passage that follows: these people are at rest, in a place arranged for rest, and nothing in the description is going to require them to get up.

The word for reclining also carries the sense of relying or leaning upon something, and the Qur'an uses the same root for trust. The posture is therefore not only comfortable but expressive: these are people who are leaning on what will not give way. A couch, a canopy, and a settled posture are the visible form of a confidence that has been vindicated.

**Neither Sun Nor Bitter Cold**

The second half of the verse says what they will not experience, and it names both ends of the scale. They will not see a scorching sun, and they will not meet *zamharīr*. The word for the second is the difficult one. The lexicographers glossed it as intense, biting cold, and that is the sense the majority of commentators took: the Garden has no extreme at either end. A minority read the same word as the moon, on the basis of a poetic usage, and produced the reading: no sun and no moon — a light that comes from neither. That reading has an attractive consequence, since it makes the Garden's illumination unmediated, and it fits the Qur'an's statements about the light of that Day. It remains the weaker of the two, since the paired negation of heat and cold is the natural construction and the one the context supports.

**A Place Without Extremes**

The detail is easy to pass over, and it repays attention, because the Qur'an's descriptions of both destinations are built on the principle of exposure. Hell is described as a place where the inhabitants are exposed to scorching wind, boiling water, and freezing cold, and the alternation between the extremes is part of the punishment. The Garden is described by their absence. Human life in this world is lived between the same two poles: most of what a body endures day to day is a matter of being too hot, too cold, too tired, or too thirsty. The promise here is not luxury. It is the end of a chronic discomfort so constant that nobody thinks to describe a life without it.

The removal of both extremes is also a statement about the body. The Qur'an's Garden is not an escape from embodiment but the fulfilment of it, and the promise is made to the same body that was hungry, thirsty, hot, and tired in this life. Resurrection is therefore not the discarding of a body that has become inconvenient. It is the restoration of a body to the condition in which it was always meant to be.

**The Same Imagery Elsewhere**

The picture recurs with only slight variation across the sūrahs. *“They and their spouses will be in ˹cool˺ shade, reclining on ˹canopied˺ couches.”* (36:56). *“˹seated˺ on ˹canopied˺ couches, gazing around.”* (83:23). *“˹They will be˺ on jewelled thrones, reclining face to face.”* (56:15–16). Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah lists the furnishings in the same arrangement — *“along with thrones raised high, and cups set at hand, and ˹fine˺ cushions lined up, and ˹splendid˺ carpets spread out”* (88:13–16). The consistency of the furniture is not a poverty of invention. It is a single claim repeated: the person is at home, and the place has been prepared.

One detail in the list deserves a note, since it recurs as a pair. Couches and shade belong together in every description of the Garden, and the reason is that a couch in the open sun would be no reward at all. The Qur'an's descriptions are internally consistent in a way that suggests they were composed as scenes rather than assembled from a catalogue of pleasant nouns.

**How the Reward Answers the Deed**

The correspondence is worth stating, since the sūrah is built on it. These are the people who fasted, who gave away food while hungry, and who stood in the heat of a Day they feared. What they are given is shade, rest, and a place with no heat in it. The Qur'an makes the same exchange explicit in the case of fasting in particular, where the privation of this life and the provision of the next are set against each other, and it is the same logic that governs the whole passage: what was endured for a short time is answered by what lasts.

**Rest Is the Reward of Work**

One last implication should be drawn. The rest described here is not idleness but the cessation of strain, and its value depends entirely on what preceded it. A couch means nothing to someone who has not laboured, and shade means nothing to someone who has not been exposed. The Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden are therefore addressed to people in the condition of effort, and their force is conditional on the reader knowing what it costs to live. That is why these verses are preceded by eighteen on the cost, and why the reward is described in the vocabulary of relief rather than of indulgence.

The negation in this verse also tells the reader what the Fire is like by contrast. The Qur'an describes the inhabitants of the Fire as moving between extremes of heat and cold, given boiling water to drink and scorching wind to breathe, and the alternation is part of the punishment rather than an incidental feature of the place. Setting the two descriptions side by side is what the Qur'an intends: the choice being offered is between a place with no extremes and a place made of them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:14

> The Garden’s shade will be right above them, and its fruit will be made very easy to reach.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shade That Is Close**

The verse continues the picture of the previous one, and the first detail is the position of the shade. It is *dāniya*, near, hanging close over them rather than standing at a distance they must walk to. The word does the work of an entire climate: the shade is above, it is close, and it does not shift as the sun moves, because there is no sun. The Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden return repeatedly to shade, and always in this register — *“extended shade”* (56:30), *“eternal is its fruit as well as its shade”* (13:35), and *“We will place them under a vast shade”* (4:57). Shade in a desert is not a decorative detail. It is the difference between a place being habitable and not.

The nearness of the shade is what distinguishes the image from an ordinary tree. A person in this world finds shade by moving to where it is, and it moves away from him as the day advances. What is promised here is shade that has been brought to him and stays, which is only another way of saying that the place has been arranged rather than found.

**Fruit Hanging Low**

The second detail is the fruit, and the verb used is derived from a root meaning to make tractable, to bring something under control that would otherwise resist. Fruit trees in this world put their best produce at the top and the far ends of branches, and anyone who has picked fruit knows the labour involved. What is described here is the removal of that labour: the clusters are brought down and made manageable. The same idea is stated in the sūrah of al-Ḥāqqah — *“whose fruit will hang within reach”* (69:23) — and in al-Raḥmān — *“And the fruit of both Gardens will hang within reach.”* (55:54). The tree does not withhold, and the person does not strain.

**Effortlessness as the Mark of the Place**

The two halves of the verse belong together as a single description of a place where the ordinary friction of obtaining things has been removed. In this life almost every good thing is separated from the person who wants it by some work: distance, cost, season, effort, or the will of someone else. The Garden is described throughout the Qur'an as the removal of that gap, and the sūrah of Fuṣṣilat states it as a principle: *“There you will have whatever your souls desire, and there you will have whatever you ask for”* (41:31). The low-hanging fruit is that principle rendered as an image a person can picture.

The tractability of the fruit also carries a moral suggestion that the commentators were fond of drawing out. In this world, good things are withheld or hard to reach, and the difficulty is part of what makes a person who he becomes. In the Garden the difficulty is removed because the person has already been formed by it. The fruit is brought low to those who spent their lives reaching for what was not easy.

**Recognition and Difference**

The Qur'an adds a detail elsewhere that prevents the description from collapsing into sameness, and it is worth placing beside this verse. Of the fruit of the Garden it says: *“Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, ‘This is what we were given before,’ for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺.”* (2:25). The recognition is part of the pleasure, since a person enjoys what he can recognise; the difference is what keeps the enjoyment from becoming repetition. A reward that merely repeats the goods of this world would pall, and the Qur'an's descriptions are careful to avoid that while still using the vocabulary of the known.

The same principle governs the whole description of the Garden's food and drink. Nothing in it is strange, and nothing in it is merely familiar. The reader is given pomegranates, palms, grapes, and springs — the contents of an oasis he can picture — and is then told that what he is picturing is not what he will get, because the delight has not been experienced by any soul yet.

**A Report About the Cluster**

The commentators record a report attached to this verse, in which the Prophet said during his final illness that he had seen the Garden and come upon a cluster of grapes, and that had he taken it the community would have eaten from it for as long as the world remained. The report belongs to the tafsīr literature's narration of the verse rather than to a collection this session was able to check. [UNVERIFIED: the report of the cluster of grapes was not located in a numbered collection this session; it is cited as material preserved in the tafsīr tradition.] Its content is consistent with the verse's own claim, and the point survives without it: the fruit is real, near, and offered.

**What the Image Is For**

Descriptions like this one have a specific function, and it is not to satisfy curiosity. The Qur'an states its own purpose in the most quoted verse on the subject: *“No soul can imagine what delights are kept in store for them as a reward for what they used to do.”* (32:17). Since the delight cannot be imagined, it can only be indicated, and the indicators chosen are the ones that carry the greatest charge for the hearer: cool shade after heat, fruit that does not have to be struggled for, water that does not run out. The image is a promise rather than a photograph, and it is addressed to desire in order to direct it.

There is also a discipline implied in taking the images seriously. A person who believes that shade and fruit and water await him is being given a reason to give away what he has here, and the sūrah has already stated the connection in the plainest terms. The descriptions are not escape literature. They are the motive for the conduct the sūrah described in its opening ten verses.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:15

> They will be waited on with silver vessels and cups of crystal—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Service, and What It Signifies**

The verb means that the vessels are circulated around them — carried from one person to another by attendants rather than placed on a table. Service of this kind in the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden is never presented as the humiliation of the one who serves; the attendants are described in the verse that follows this passage as immortal youths, and their condition is part of the inhabitants' honour rather than a cost borne by someone else. What the image conveys is that these people are guests. They are not managing, fetching, or arranging anything. The meal comes to them.

The Hebrew Bible and the Qur'an share the image of a table prepared, and the Qur'an's use of it here is consistent with the sūrah's opening movement: the people being served are the people who served. Nothing in the description suggests that the honour is unearned, and the sūrah has spent ten verses establishing what it cost.

**Silver, and the Other Passages**

The metal named here is silver, and the choice is worth noting because the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden's vessels vary between silver and gold. Sūrah al-Kahf mentions *“bracelets of gold”* (18:31), Sūrah al-Ḥajj the same (22:23), and Sūrah al-Zukhruf speaks of *“Golden trays and cups”* (43:71). This sūrah names silver both here and in v. 21, where the inhabitants are adorned with bracelets of silver. The variation is not a contradiction to be harmonised; different passages select different materials, and the point in each case is simply that the vessel is made of what the first hearers counted as precious. The substance is a pointer to value, not an inventory.

The sūrah will return to silver in v. 21, where the inhabitants are adorned with bracelets of the same metal, and the recurrence makes this sūrah's palette distinctive. There is gold elsewhere in the Qur'an's descriptions and silver here, and the variation is a reminder that the descriptions are not specifications. They are the same promise made in different registers to different audiences.

**Cups of Crystal**

The second noun is the one that has exercised the commentators, because crystal and silver have opposite properties: crystal is transparent and silver is not. The next verse resolves the difficulty, and the resolution is the point of the pairing — the cups are of silver, and of a silver made transparent like crystal. The description therefore combines two qualities that no single material in this world possesses: the worth of the metal and the clarity of glass. A person drinking from such a cup sees what he is drinking, and the vessel itself is the object of enjoyment rather than a container to be ignored.

**The Cup in the Qur'an's Vocabulary**

The word *kaʾs* is used in Arabic both for the vessel and for what is in it, and the Qur'an exploits the ambiguity throughout. *“A drink ˹of pure wine˺ will be passed around to them from a flowing stream: crystal-white, delicious to drink. It will neither harm ˹them˺, nor will they be intoxicated by it.”* (37:45–47). The sūrah of al-Nabaʾ mentions *“and full cups ˹of pure wine˺”* (78:34), and al-Ṭūr describes a cup *“which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness”* (52:23). The denials are as important as the assertions. Every description of the drink removes the one property that makes wine dangerous in this world.

The drank of the Garden is also described as sealed, which the commentators took as a mark of purity — a vessel no one has opened before it reaches its intended recipient. The sūrah of al-Muṭaffifīn makes the same point when it describes a drink *“whose last sip will smell like musk”* (83:26). Every property attributed to it is a property that has no defect in it.

**The Reversal It Represents**

There is a correspondence here that the sūrah has been building since v. 8, and it should be stated explicitly. These are the people who gave food to others — who handed their meal to a poor man, an orphan, and a captive, and went hungry themselves. In the Garden the direction is reversed: food and drink are brought to them, and they are the ones served. The Qur'an's rewards are consistently shaped as answers rather than as payments, and this is one of the clearest instances in the Book: they fed, and now they are fed.

The same reversal is stated elsewhere as a general rule about giving: whatever a person spends is replaced, and the Qur'an uses the vocabulary of a loan made to God for the same transaction. The point is not that generosity is profitable, though the Qur'an says it is, but that the shape of the return matches the shape of the gift. Food given is food received, at a table that does not run short.

**Vessels, and the Dignity of the Person**

A final observation on the register of the description. Nothing in these verses is grandiose; there is no throne, no crown, and no court. What is described is a meal, served well, in a comfortable place, to people who are at rest. The Qur'an's choice of register is deliberate, and it tells the reader something about what the reward is for. These people were not ambitious, and what they wanted was not elevation over others. They wanted relief, honour, and nearness, and what is described being given to them is exactly that.

It is also worth noticing that the service described here reverses the situation of the givers in this life without shaming anyone. No servant is degraded by serving, and no guest is diminished by being served. The Qur'an's Garden is not a place where the hierarchies of this world are inverted so that someone else can be humbled; it is a place where the need for hierarchy has ended, since no one there is short of anything.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:16

> crystalline silver, filled precisely as desired.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Combination the Verse Names**

The previous verse ended with cups of crystal, and this one completes the thought: crystal, but of silver. The phrase resolves the tension the earlier verse created, since the two materials have opposite properties — one is prized for its clarity and the other for its substance. The commentators took the construction in the obvious way: vessels of silver possessing the transparency of glass. What is being described is a material that does not exist in this world, and the description works by taking two known things and joining their best properties. The method is the one the Qur'an uses throughout these passages: it reaches past the hearer's experience by combining elements drawn from it.

**A Small Grammatical Crux**

There is a question about how the two nouns relate, and the commentators recorded two answers. The first takes the second noun as the material and the first as a description: cups that are crystal, made of silver. The second reverses it: crystal-clear things of silver. The difference is negligible in English but visible in Arabic, and the majority preferred the first on the ground that the vessel had already been called a cup in the preceding verse and the new noun completes rather than replaces it. Neither reading changes the claim. The point of the pairing is transparency joined to preciousness, and both readings assert it.

There is a further reading recorded for the first noun, taken from the root for glass in the sense of something that shows what is inside it. On that reading the emphasis falls on visibility rather than material: the drinker sees what he is being given. The reading is compatible with the others, and the accumulation of them suggests that the verse is meant to be read for its effect rather than parsed for its grammar.

**Filled to the Measure They Wanted**

The final clause is the most interesting in the verse, because of who is doing the measuring. The commentators divided over the subject of the verb. Some took it as the attendants: they filled the cups to the exact measure each person wanted, neither more nor less. Others took it as the inhabitants themselves, on the reading that they determine the fill as they wish. The difference matters less than the shared claim, which is that the measure is exact. Nobody is given more than he wants, and nobody is given less. The cup is not a standard issue; it is proportioned to the drinker.

**The Root That Organises the Qur'an**

The verb used belongs to the root *q-d-r*, from which the Qur'an draws its vocabulary of measure, decree, and power. The same root appears in one of the most compact statements of the Creator's relationship to what He has made: *“He has created everything, ordaining it precisely.”* (25:2). The reuse of the root here is not accidental. The precision that governs the created order governs the reward as well, and a person who has spent his life inside a world measured to the last detail arrives in a place measured with the same exactness — except that in this case the measure is his own desire.

The word also carries the sense of ability and power, and the three senses — measure, decree, and ability — are held together in the Qur'an's usage. To say that the cups were measured out is therefore to say something about the One who ordered the scene. Nothing in the Garden is improvised, and nothing in it is approximate, because the same power that set the measure of the created world has set the measure of this one.

**Why Exactness Is a Perfection**

It is worth asking why a cup filled to exact measure is worth promising, since exactness sounds like a limitation rather than a gift. The answer is that in this life the two are never aligned: what is available is rarely what is wanted, and what is wanted is rarely available in the right quantity. Too much of a good thing is a burden, and too little is a frustration. The promise is that the gap has closed. The measure and the desire coincide because the one who set the measure is the one who gave the desire, and that is a statement about the Giver as much as about the gift.

The verse also quietly settles a question about the nature of desire in the Garden. If the cup is filled to the measure the person wants, then desire has not been abolished; it has been satisfied on its own terms. The Qur'an nowhere describes the inhabitants as having ceased to want things. It describes them as never wanting anything they do not receive, which is a claim about the place rather than about the psychology.

**The Host Behind the Vessels**

The sūrah has now spent four verses on the service of a meal, and the detail is deliberate. Every element of the description — the vessel, its material, its clarity, and its fill — is arranged by someone, and the Qur'an's point in accumulating these details is to make the reader notice the Arranger. The reward is not described as a place that happens to be pleasant. It is described as a table that has been set, by a Host who knows what each person at it wants, and who measured the cups before they were brought out.

The accumulation of detail across these four verses is also an argument against a careless reading of the Qur'an's promises. Nothing here is vague — the material, the transparency, the measure, and the manner of service are all specified. A promise with that much specific content in it is not a metaphor for a mood. It is a description of something the Speaker says exists, and the reader is meant to want it.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:17

> And they will be given a drink ˹of pure wine˺ flavoured with ginger

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Drink, Differently Described**

The sūrah has already given the inhabitants a cup flavoured with camphor (76:5), and here it gives them another, flavoured with ginger. The repetition is worth noticing, because a description that named a single drink would have served the sūrah's purpose. What the doubling establishes is variety: the reward is not one pleasure repeated indefinitely but a table with more than one thing on it. The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere by describing the contents of the Garden in pluralities — rivers of different kinds (47:15) and *“two types of every fruit”* (55:52). Sameness is part of what wears a person down in this life, and it is not part of what is promised.

**Zanjabīl: the Word and the Substance**

Ginger was known in the Near East well before the Qur'an, imported along the trade routes from further east, and it was used as a spice and as a medicine. The Arabic word is not of Arabic stock; the lexicographers recorded it as a borrowed term, which is itself a small piece of evidence for what the first hearers were being asked to imagine: something costly because it had come from far away. The substance is warming and pungent, and the classical commentators who noted the property paired it with the camphor of v. 5, which is cooling and fragrant. The two flavourings sit at opposite ends of the scale, as the two extremes of temperature were excluded in v. 13.

The borrowed character of the word is itself worth a moment. Arabic absorbed terms for spices, fabrics, and gemstones from the trading world around it, and the Qur'an uses several such words in these descriptions. The effect on the first hearers would have been to place the Garden's provision among goods that arrived from the ends of the known world — costly precisely because they were rare and distant — and then to declare that in the Garden they are not rare at all.

**Why the Pairing May Matter**

The suggestion that the two drinks answer each other is attractive and it is reported in the commentaries, but it should be held loosely, since the Qur'an does not say that the ginger is given for warmth or the camphor for coolness. What can be said from the text is that both are named as *mizāj*, something mixed into the drink, and that the mixing is what gives each cup its character. The point of the mixture is tempering rather than temperature: the drink is prepared, and the preparing is part of the gift.

**A Second Reading: the Name of a Spring**

Some commentators took the word here not as the spice but as the proper name of a spring in the Garden, on the analogy of the spring named in the following verse. The grounds for the reading are contextual: the next verse attributes the drink to a named spring, and a reading that makes both nouns names would keep the construction parallel. The majority kept the plain sense of the spice, since the word is used for ginger in Arabic and since the verse says the drink is flavoured *with* it, which is how a mixture rather than a source is described. The plain reading is the stronger one.

The dispute over the word is worth keeping in view because it shows how the commentaries worked. Where a term was ambiguous, they recorded the readings rather than selecting one silently, and where the majority settled on a sense they usually gave the reason — here, the preposition of mixture and the ordinary meaning of the word in Arabic. That method is what makes the classical tafsīr literature usable rather than merely venerable.

**Why the Sūrah Returns to the Cup**

The drink recurs in the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden more often than any other single item, and the reason is not difficult to see. A cup is the smallest unit of hospitality, it is given to a guest immediately, and it is consumed at once — which makes it the most economical image available for a state of being provided for. The sūrahs vary the details freely: a cup from a flowing stream (37:45), a sealed wine whose last sip smells of musk (83:25–26), a spring from which those brought near drink (83:28), and here a cup flavoured with ginger from a named spring.

The repetition also answers a question a reader might raise about the Garden's enjoyments. If the inhabitants have been purified of everything base, why is so much of the description taken up with food and drink? The answer the Qur'an supplies by its method is that enjoyment is not the problem with this world. What is wrong with this world is that its enjoyments are short, mixed with harm, and distributed unfairly, and the Garden is described as the removal of all three defects at once.

**What Is Denied, as Well as Promised**

Every one of those descriptions carries a negation alongside the promise, and the negation is as constant as the assertion. *“It will neither harm ˹them˺, nor will they be intoxicated by it.”* (37:47). *“which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness”* (52:23). *“that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication”* (56:19). The pattern is deliberate. The Qur'an is promising an enjoyment that has had everything destructive removed from it, and it refuses to let the reader imagine the reward as the same thing he knows with the bad parts taken out. It is a different thing, described in the only vocabulary available.

The denials also tell the reader what the Qur'an thinks about intoxication. The pleasure of the drink is real and is promised; what is removed is the loss of self that attends drinking in this world. A reward that required a person to be diminished in order to enjoy it would not be a reward, and the Qur'an's descriptions are careful to promise an enjoyment that leaves the enjoyer fully himself.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:18

> from a spring there, called Salsabîl.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Spring With a Name**

The drink of the previous verse is traced to its source, and the source is given a name. Naming is doing considerable work here. A description that said only *from a spring there* would have conveyed plenty; adding a proper name makes the spring a particular thing rather than a feature of the landscape. The Qur'an uses the same device in the sūrah of al-Muṭaffifīn, where the drink of those brought near comes *“from Tasnîm”* (83:27–28). In both cases the reward is not a general condition but a place with identifiable contents, and the reader is invited to know it as one knows a specific thing.

**Three Accounts of the Word**

The commentators offered three explanations, and the differences between them are worth laying out. The first takes the word simply as a proper name, and this is the reading most of them settled on, since the verse introduces it with a verb of naming. The second parses it as two imperatives joined: *sal* and *sabīl*, meaning *ask for a way* — a reading that turns the name into an invitation addressed to whoever wants to reach the Garden. The third derives it from a root meaning to be easy, smooth, and agreeable to swallow, so that the name describes the water's quality rather than being a label. The third has the strongest lexical support, since the derived form exists in Arabic with that sense; the second is the weakest, since it requires dividing a word the text presents as a single name.

The weakest of the three readings has nevertheless been the most popular in devotional literature, because it converts the name of a spring into an instruction: seek the way. That is a homiletic use rather than an exegetical one, and it should be read as such. The text presents the word as a name, and the name is given so that the spring can be known rather than so that it can be parsed into a sentence.

**What the Name Suggests**

Two of the three readings converge on a single idea, and it is the one the verse's context supports. Whether the word is a proper name or a description, it denotes water that goes down easily — sweet, smooth, and without the harshness that water in this world so often has. The commentators developed the point in some detail: a drink that is easy in the throat, that does not choke, and that leaves no aftertaste. What is being described is the removal of everything in ordinary experience that makes drinking a relief from discomfort rather than a pleasure in itself.

**The Sound of the Word**

Arabic readers have always noticed the shape of this name, and the observation is not merely aesthetic. The word is built on a rare pattern, with a repeated liquid consonant and a long final syllable that sits exactly on the sūrah's rhyme. Placed at the end of a verse, it does what the other rare words in this sūrah do: it slows the reader down. A name that cannot be paraphrased has to be carried rather than understood, and the effect is to mark the thing named as belonging to a vocabulary the reader does not yet possess.

The rare patterns in this sūrah are not confined to this verse. The sūrah uses several words that occur nowhere else in the Qur'an, and the effect across the passage is to give the description of the Garden a vocabulary of its own, marked off from the language of ordinary experience. A reader moving through these verses is being made to feel, by the shape of the words, that he is being shown something he has no prior term for.

**The Source Is Inside the Garden**

The phrase *a spring therein* places the source within the Garden rather than outside it, which may seem obvious but carries a claim. The inhabitants are not supplied from somewhere else; the provision is internal to the place they have been given. The same insistence appears wherever the Qur'an describes the Garden's water — springs running beneath it, rivers flowing under its dwellings — and the point is the permanence of the arrangement. A supply that arrives from outside could be interrupted. One that belongs to the place cannot.

The insistence on the spring's location also answers a question the reader may have formed. If the drink comes from a spring and the inhabitants can direct springs where they wish, as v. 6 said, then the arrangement is entirely within their own domain. Nothing in the Garden is supplied from outside it, and nothing in it depends on a mechanism the inhabitants do not control. That is part of what makes it a dominion rather than a residence.

**Why Specificity Matters**

The accumulation of named detail across this passage — camphor, ginger, Salsabîl, silver, crystal, couches, shade — is an argument in itself, though it is rarely read as one. A reward described with this much particularity is not a symbol for an inner state, and the Qur'an did not choose vagueness where it could have taken refuge in it. The descriptions are addressed to people who have been told to give away what they have here, and they are given something concrete to set against what is being asked of them.

The particularity also guards against a reading that the Qur'an actively resists. If the reward were described only as peace, light, or nearness, a reader could conclude that the promises are about a state of mind attainable here and now. By naming springs, metals, fabrics, and flavourings, the text insists on a future that is as concrete as the present, and on a body that will be there to enjoy it.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:19

> They will be waited on by eternal youths. If you saw them, you would think they were scattered pearls.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Who the Attendants Are**

The service described in vv. 15 and 16 needs someone to perform it, and this verse supplies the servants: *wildān mukhalladūn*. The first word means youths or boys, and the second is a passive participle meaning made to last, kept in a single state, or rendered perpetual. The combination describes attendants who have the vigour associated with youth and none of the instability that goes with it: they do not age, tire, or change. The Qur'an uses the same description in the sūrah of al-Wāqiʿah, where *“They will be waited on by eternal youths with cups, pitchers, and a drink ˹of pure wine˺”* (56:17–18), and the repetition across two sūrahs indicates a fixed feature of the description rather than a local detail.

**A Second Reading of the Participle**

Some commentators took the second word differently, deriving it from a sense of adornment and rendering it as those who wear earrings or are decked with ornaments. The grounds for the reading are lexical, since the root has a branch of meanings connected with jewellery and ornament, and it would suit the simile of pearls that follows. The majority kept the sense of perpetuity, on the ground that the same participle is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for the inhabitants' own permanence, and because the point of the attendants in this context is their constancy rather than their decoration. The majority reading is stronger, and the difference does not affect the claim being made about them.

**The Simile of Scattered Pearls**

The comparison is one of the most remarked-on images in the sūrah, and the commentaries explain the adjective as carefully as the noun. Pearls in a necklace are matched, graded, and strung; pearls that have been scattered are loose, abundant, and each one is seen separately. The simile therefore conveys dispersal as well as beauty — attendants moving about the gathering rather than standing in a row — and the motion is what makes the image work. Two other passages use the same comparison for different subjects: *“They will be waited on by their youthful servants like spotless pearls”* (52:24), and the description of the companions of the Garden as *“like pristine pearls”* (56:23).

The comparison also says something about abundance. A single pearl is a treasure; pearls scattered about are a description of a place where what is precious is not being hoarded. The Qur'an's Garden is not described as a treasury but as a room in which costly things are in ordinary use, and the same note is struck by the vessels of silver being circulated rather than displayed.

**Why Youth, and Why Permanence**

The pairing of the two words is doing a specific job. Youth in this world is a state that a person passes through, and its value is inseparable from its brevity; the attendants here are described as having the quality without the transience. That is the same removal of loss that runs through the whole passage: the Garden has shade that does not move, fruit that does not fail, water that does not run out, and servants who do not age. The Qur'an's method is to take an ordinary good and strip from it the property that makes it temporary.

The permanence of the attendants is also a promise about the inhabitants, though the verse does not say so directly. A place in which the servants do not age is a place in which nothing decays, and the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden consistently deny the passage of time as it is known here. The residents are given *“eternal is its fruit as well as its shade”* (13:35), and the permanence of everything in the scene is the permanence of the whole.

**The Servants Are Not Degraded**

Nothing in the description suggests that the attendants are a cost borne by someone else for the benefit of the inhabitants, and the Qur'an's scenes of the Garden never include anyone who is diminished. The attendants are described in the same terms of beauty and permanence as the people they serve, and the simile used for them is one of value. A reader who worries about the morality of the arrangement has misunderstood the genre: the point is not that some people serve and others are served, but that in this place no one is in want and no one is exhausted.

The same point can be made from the simile itself. Pearls are not a currency in the Qur'an's imagery; they are a value in themselves, and the comparison is made to the attendants rather than to their clothing or their surroundings. Whatever the social arrangements of this world have taught a reader to expect from a scene of service, the text gives no purchase for it here.

**The Reader Is Brought Into the Scene**

The verse addresses the reader directly: *if you saw them*. The shift into the second person is a technique the Qur'an uses at moments when it wants the description to land, and it appears again in the next verse. The effect is to make the reader a spectator of a scene he is being told is real, and the conditional form — *if you saw* — concedes that he has not. The sūrah is describing something its reader cannot yet see and asking him to reason about it on the basis of what he has been told, which is the definition of belief in the unseen.

The direct address also serves a rhetorical purpose that runs through the sūrah as a whole. The reader is not being informed about a place; he is being shown it and asked to respond. The conditional form carries an implicit claim about his situation: he has not seen this, and he is going to have to decide what to do about a report he cannot verify. That is the position the Qur'an consistently puts its reader in.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:20

> And if you looked around, you would see ˹indescribable˺ bliss and a vast kingdom.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reader Is Asked to Look**

The second person continues from the previous verse, and the instruction is to look. What follows is not another item in the catalogue but a summary seen from inside the scene: wherever the eye falls, there is bliss and a great kingdom. The verb suggests looking around rather than at one thing, and the effect is to close the list that began in v. 5. Sixteen verses have described the cup, the couch, the shade, the vessels, the attendants; this verse says that what has been described fills the whole field of vision, and that no corner of it has been left out.

**Two Nouns: Bliss and Dominion**

The first noun is the word the Qur'an uses for the condition of the blessed as such — *naʿīm*, a state of ease and wellbeing that has no defect in it. The second is different in kind. *Mulk* is dominion, kingship, the possession of a realm, and its appearance here is unexpected in a sūrah whose inhabitants have been described entirely in terms of service and renunciation. The commentators noted the oddity and explained it in the obvious way: what these people did not seek in this life is given to them in the next, in a form that has none of the corruption that attaches to it here.

The pairing also completes a movement within the sūrah's own vocabulary of recompense. The people described in v. 9 wanted neither reward nor thanks from anyone, and here they are given the two things they refused, in a form that cannot corrupt them — a recompense from God rather than from people, and a greatness that no one else is diminished by.

**The Reward of the Unambitious**

The observation deserves developing, because it is one of the sūrah's sharper reversals. The people described in vv. 7 through 10 wanted nothing from anyone: no recompense, no thanks, no standing. They were not seeking power, and there is no suggestion in the passage that they were frustrated aspirants. What they are given is described in the vocabulary of sovereignty. The Qur'an states the same principle elsewhere in the negative: those who sought greatness in this life are the ones who have nothing in the next, and the inhabitants of the Garden are repeatedly described as having been lowly or unnoticed in their former lives.

**A Kingdom Without Subjects to Harm**

The word *mulk* in this world is inseparable from competition, since a realm is held against rival claimants and administered by force. The kingdom promised here has none of that, and the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden consistently exclude the presence of anything that would require defence: no envy, no rivalry, no fear of loss. The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf states the removal explicitly — *“We will remove whatever bitterness they had in their hearts”* (7:43) — and it is that removal which makes a shared kingdom possible. Dominion without the dominion of anyone over anyone is the odd thing being promised.

The removal of all rivalry is what distinguishes this kingdom from every kingdom the reader has known. Power in this world is exercised over people and is maintained by the exclusion of others; what is promised here is a greatness that requires no one to be beneath it, and that is the only kind of greatness that could be given to people who spent their lives refusing to compete.

**What the Eye Can Take In**

The verse is also the sūrah's clearest statement about the limits of its own descriptions. Everything that has been listed is something the reader can picture; what is promised is a field of vision in which every direction offers something, and no inventory can convey that. The Qur'an says as much directly: *“No soul can imagine what delights are kept in store for them as a reward for what they used to do.”* (32:17). The function of the catalogue was to make the promise credible, and this verse closes it by pointing at what the catalogue cannot contain.

There is a further implication in the instruction to look around. A person who looks at one thing and then another is not in a state of satiety; he is in a state of continuing attention. The blessed are not described as having exhausted the possibilities of their condition, and the sūrah's closing image of a kingdom without end is meant to be read as a promise of inexhaustible exploration rather than of static possession.

**The End of the Reward**

With this verse the sūrah's long description of the reward concludes. It began at v. 5 with a cup and has ended with a kingdom, and the movement between the two is the movement of the whole passage: from the smallest act of hospitality to the largest possession a person can be given. What follows in the sūrah is a change of subject and a change of tone. Having shown what the virtuous receive, the sūrah turns to address the Prophet directly about the revelation that promised it, and about how he is to conduct himself among people who do not want to hear it.

The turn that follows is also a turn in register. Everything from v. 5 to this verse has been descriptive and impersonal; from v. 23 onward the sūrah addresses the Prophet in the second person and gives him instructions about patience, remembrance, and worship. The sūrah therefore moves from what God will give to what God now asks, and the two halves are held together by the logic of the whole: a promise that large is worth a life arranged around it.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:21

> The virtuous will be ˹dressed˺ in garments of fine green silk and rich brocade, and adorned with bracelets of silver, and their Lord will give them a purifying drink.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Fabrics, and the Colour Named**

The garments are described with two words that the classical lexicographers distinguished carefully. *Sundus* is fine, thin silk, the light cloth worn next to the skin; *istabraq* is heavy brocade, the stiff embroidered fabric used for the outer garment. The pairing therefore covers the whole of what a person wears rather than a single item, and it is the same pairing that appears in Sūrah al-Kahf — *“they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade”* (18:31) — and in Sūrah al-Dukhān, where the inhabitants are *“dressed in fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another”* (44:53). Green is named in the first of those passages and implied here, and the colour belongs to the Qur'an's vocabulary for the Garden: *“Both will be dark green”* (55:64).

The two fabrics are also worth distinguishing in function. The fine silk is what is worn beneath and the brocade what is worn over, which means the description covers the whole person rather than the single outer garment a passer-by would see. Nothing here is for display; the inner layer is as costly as the outer one, and the point is the completeness of what has been given rather than its appearance.

**Why Green**

The choice of colour is not incidental, and the commentators connected it to what a person in a dry landscape looks at. Green is the colour of vegetation, of an oasis, of the earth after rain, and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly for the Garden's coverings and cushions — *“All ˹believers˺ will be reclining on green cushions and splendid carpets”* (55:76). A first hearer would have registered the colour without needing it explained: the Garden is described as the place where what is green does not turn brown.

The green is also a fulfilment of something the Qur'an says about this life. Vegetation here appears, flourishes briefly, and withers — the parable of the present world in Sūrah al-Kahf and in Sūrah Yūnus. What is promised in the Garden is green that does not wither, and the same sūrah that names it says *“eternal is its fruit as well as its shade”* (13:35). The colour carries the permanence with it.

**Bracelets of Silver**

The adornment is of silver, which is the metal this sūrah used for the vessels in vv. 15–16 and will keep for the rest of its description. Other sūrahs specify gold — *“There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls, and their clothing will be silk”* (22:23), and the same in 35:33 — and the variation should be read as variation rather than as a discrepancy to be resolved. Both metals are named in different places for the same purpose: to say that what adorns the inhabitants is what their hearers counted as most precious.

The adornment also carries the sūrah's note about reversal. The people being adorned are the ones who cared nothing for how they appeared to others; they wanted no thanks and no recognition. What they are given is the visible honour they never asked for, in a place where being honoured harms no one, since no one there is diminished by someone else's being adorned.

**Their Lord Gives Them to Drink**

The last clause is the summit of the entire passage, and the sūrah has been building toward it since v. 5. Every drink before this one was brought by attendants or drawn from a spring; this one is given by the Lord himself. The contrast is deliberate and the commentators did not miss it: what was served through intermediaries at the start of the description is served directly at its end. The sūrah's reward therefore does not culminate in a thing but in a proximity, and the thing given is the vehicle of it.

**Ṭahūr: a Purifying Drink**

The word describing the drink is an emphatic formation from the root for purity, and its force is that the drink purifies rather than merely being pure. The contrast the Qur'an is drawing is with the wine of this world, which is ritually and physically unclean and whose effect is to cloud the mind. The drink promised here does the opposite: it cleanses. The spiritual commentators developed the point in their own register, one of them saying that it purifies the drinker of everything other than God, and another pairing the wine of love in this life with the pure drink in the next. These are reports preserved in the tafsīr tradition rather than hadith, and they are cited as such. What the text itself asserts is the cleansing, and the cleansing is the final removal of everything that would spoil the enjoyment.

The emphatic form of the word is worth a note on its own. Arabic distinguishes between what is pure and what purifies, and the Qur'an chose the second. A drink that purifies is a drink that continues to act on the person after he has drunk it, and the implication is that the removal of everything spoiled is not completed at the gate but carried on within.

**The Climax, and What It Says About the Reward**

The arrangement of the verse is worth reading closely, because the order is not what a description would naturally produce. Garments come first, then jewellery, then the drink — and the drink, though listed last, is the point of the sentence. The sūrah has dressed the inhabitants and adorned them in order to bring them to this: a cup handed over by their Lord, in a place where nothing needs to be purified except what was left over from the journey.

The verse is also the sūrah's answer to a question raised by its own beginning. The people being rewarded were made from a mingled drop (76:2) and were, before that, nothing that could be mentioned (76:1). What they are given ends with their Lord handing them a cup. The distance between those two states is the sūrah's subject, and every verse between them has been describing the crossing of it.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:22

> ˹And they will be told,˺ “All this is surely a reward for you. Your striving has been appreciated.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Spoken to the Inhabitants**

Eighteen verses of description end with a sentence addressed directly to the people who have been described. Everything before this was reported about them in the third person; now they are spoken to, and the sentence has two halves — a declaration and a commendation. The address matters for the same reason it mattered in vv. 9 and 10, where what a person says and what is said to him carry the weight. The inhabitants are not simply installed in a garden. They are told why, by the One who put them there.

**The Wordplay the Sūrah Has Been Building**

The two nouns in this verse are the two nouns the virtuous renounced in v. 9. There they said, *we seek from you neither recompense nor thanks*; here they are told, *this is your recompense, and your striving has been thanked*. The roots are the same in both verses. The correspondence is too exact to be an accident, and it is the sūrah's final statement of its own argument: what was given up for God is returned by God, and what was refused from people is granted by the One who was never in their debt.

The return is not a repayment in the commercial sense. What the virtuous gave was finite — a meal, a fast, a renunciation — and what they are given is described with a word meaning unending. The disproportion is the same one the sūrah has been stating since v. 12, and it is the Qur'an's standard account of the relation between a deed and its wage.

**God Is the One Who Thanks**

The second half of the sentence attributes to God a quality that is central to the Qur'an's account of Him and easy to overlook. *Shakūr* — appreciative, thankful — is one of the divine names, and it appears wherever the Qur'an wants to say that no good act is too small to be acknowledged: *“so that He will reward them in full and increase them out of His grace. He is truly All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative”* (35:30), and *“If you lend to Allah a good loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you. For Allah is Most Appreciative, Most Forbearing.”* (64:17). The word's use here is the completion of the sūrah's transaction: the givers wanted no thanks from people, and the thanks they receive is God's.

The divine name also reframes the whole of religious practice. If God is the one who thanks, then a deed done without human acknowledgement is not wasted, and a deed done for acknowledgement has already had its return. The sūrah stated the second half of that in v. 9, when its people renounced thanks from the recipients; this verse supplies the first half.

**Effort Is Named, and It Is Real**

The sentence calls the reward a reward and the striving an effort, and both words should be taken at face value. The Qur'an nowhere suggests that works are irrelevant to the outcome, and this verse is among its plainest statements of the connection: *“whoever earns a good deed, We will increase it in goodness for them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative.”* (42:23). The same point is made at length in the response given to the supplication of those who believed and were persecuted: *“I will never deny any of you—male or female—the reward of your deeds.”* (3:195).

The word for striving is the one used for the effort of a journey, and the same root gives the name of the ritual walking between Ṣafā and Marwah. What is commended here is not a single act but the sustained motion of a life, and the commendation is addressed to people whose striving was largely invisible — a fast nobody saw, a meal handed over quietly, a vow kept after the emergency had passed.

**And Yet It Remains Grace**

The balance has to be held, because the sūrah itself holds it. Works are real, and the reward is called a reward; but the sūrah's closing verses will insist that entering the mercy of God is by His will alone, and that no one takes the way unless God wills (76:30–31). The two claims are not reconciled in the text and are not meant to be. The reader is given a reward that is earned and a mercy that is granted, and the only instruction that follows from holding both is the one the Qur'an repeats: strive, and do not presume.

Holding the two together is not a matter of splitting the difference but of taking both texts seriously. The Qur'an's promise is a promise to people who strive, and its warning is that no one arrives except by a will that is not his own. The practical effect of holding both is the one the sūrah itself draws in its closing verses: a person strives as though everything depended on him and relies as though nothing did.

**The Last Word to the Blessed**

This is the final sentence addressed to the inhabitants, and the sūrah closes its account of them with a commendation rather than with a description. Everything they were given — the drink, the shade, the couches, the garments, the kingdom — is gathered into the single word *this*, and the sentence that follows tells them what it was for. Nothing in the passage has been left unacknowledged, and the acknowledgment is the last thing the sūrah says about the reward before it turns to the Prophet and to the revelation that announced it.

The sentence also has the form of a conclusion, and the sūrah gives it that form deliberately. Everything from v. 5 onward has been a description of a place; this verse turns the description into an award, and the award is spoken. A reader who has followed the passage is being told what the whole account amounts to: these people were given this because of what they did, and what they did has been accepted.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:23

> Indeed, it is We Who have revealed the Quran to you ˹O Prophet˺ in stages.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Turns to the Prophet**

Eighteen verses about the reward of the virtuous are followed by a verse addressed to the Messenger, and the transition is as abrupt as the one at v. 4. The sūrah's final movement runs from here to its close, and its subject is the revelation itself: where it comes from, how it arrived, and what the Prophet is to do while he waits for what it promises. The connection to what precedes is direct. The reward just described is known only because this text announced it, and the sūrah now establishes the authority of the announcing.

The transition also mirrors the one that opened the sūrah's middle section. At v. 4 the sūrah moved from the showing of the way to the fate of the ungrateful without pausing; here it moves from the reward of the virtuous to the Prophet's duties without pausing. The sūrah's structure is built on these abrupt turns, and the effect is to keep the reader from settling into one register.

**Revealed in Stages**

The word rendered as in stages is *tanzīl*, which carries both the sense of sending down and the sense of doing so gradually, in instalments. The emphatic construction — *it is We who have sent it down* — identifies the Agent at the same time. The Qur'an states the same fact in several places, and the sūrah of al-Isrāʾ gives the purpose: *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace. And We have sent it down in successive revelations.”* (17:106). Gradualness is not an accident of the delivery. It is the manner chosen, and a reason is given for it.

**Why Not All at Once**

The objection was raised at the time, and the Qur'an records it and answers it in a single verse: *“The disbelievers say, ‘If only the Quran had been sent down to him all at once!’ ˹We have sent it˺ as such ˹in stages˺ so We may reassure your heart with it. And We have revealed it at a deliberate pace.”* (25:32). Two reasons are supplied, and both concern the recipient rather than the text. A single delivery would have overwhelmed the community and left no room for a life to be built under it; the instalments match the pace at which a people can be changed, and they steady the heart of the man receiving them.

A third reason is visible in the text itself and belongs to the community rather than to the Prophet. Revelation that arrives in response to events can be seen to address those events, and a generation that watched it descend had evidence of its relevance that no later generation can duplicate. The Qur'an's own claim to be a reminder sent to particular people in particular circumstances depends on the instalments.

**The Agent Behind the Descent**

The verse's emphatic form also settles a question that the Makkan audience kept raising. The revelation did not originate with the Prophet, and the Qur'an makes the chain of transmission explicit elsewhere: *“which the trustworthy spirit ˹Gabriel˺ brought down into your heart ˹O Prophet˺—so that you may be one of the warners”* (26:193–194). The same emphasis appears in the instruction to follow what has been revealed rather than the desires of the people around him (6:106). The claim of this verse is modest in form and total in scope: the text's origin is divine, its manner of arrival is chosen, and its recipient did not compose it.

The emphatic form is doing polemical work as well. The Makkan objection was not that the text was badly written but that it was composed by a man, and the Qur'an's answer throughout is to name the Agent in the strongest available construction. Whoever sent this down did so in stages, and the choice of stages is not something a forger would think to claim for himself.

**What This Has to Do With the Reward**

The placement of the verse after eighteen on the Garden is the argument. Everything described in the previous passage is unavailable to observation, unverifiable by any method the reader has, and contrary to ordinary experience. The only ground for believing it is that the One who announced it is the One who created the hearer from a drop (76:2), showed him the way (76:3), and sent this text down in stages. The sūrah's warrant for its promises is its source, and it states the source at the point where the promises have been fully given.

The verse is therefore not a digression from the sūrah's subject but the ground of it. A reader who has been asked to believe in a spring called Salsabîl must know who is telling him about it, and the sūrah answers that question at exactly the point where the demand on his credulity is greatest. Everything before this verse was promise; this verse is warrant.

**The Receiving of a Gradual Text**

One further consequence follows, and the sūrah of al-Qiyāmah drew it out in a passage that is almost a commentary on this one: a text that arrives in instalments must be received in instalments, without haste and without conclusions drawn before the whole of it has come (75:16–19). That discipline belongs to the reader as much as to the Prophet. A person who knows that the Book was sent down gradually should not be surprised to find that understanding it is gradual too.

The gradualness also explains a feature of the Qur'an that readers have often noticed: it answers questions as they arise rather than setting out a system. A text delivered over two decades to a living community will have the shape of that community's history in it, and the Qur'an does not apologise for the shape. It points to it as evidence of where the text came from.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:24

> So be patient with your Lord’s decree, and do not yield to any evildoer or ˹staunch˺ disbeliever from among them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Follows the Promise**

Having established the source of the revelation, the sūrah gives the Prophet two instructions: be patient, and do not yield. The order is significant. Patience comes first because it is the standing condition; not yielding is the specific application of it under pressure. The two together describe the posture of a man who has been told something true by a trustworthy source and is living among people who do not want to hear it, and the sūrah's closing movement is addressed to anyone in that position.

The sequence is worth stating plainly, because it is the shape of the whole Qur'anic address. Promise first, then command. The sūrah does not begin by telling the Prophet what to do and offering a reward for compliance; it tells him what is coming, and then tells him how to live in the interval. Obedience in this framework is a response to what has been shown rather than a purchase of it.

**Patience With the Decree**

The phrasing is precise: patience is owed *with the judgement of your Lord* rather than merely with people. The object of the patience is God's decree — what He has decided, permitted, and timed — and not simply the annoyance of opponents. The same construction appears in the sūrah of al-Qalam — *“So be patient with your Lord’s decree, and do not be like ˹Jonah,˺ the Man of the Whale”* (68:48) — and in al-Ṭūr: *“So be patient with your Lord’s decree, for you are truly under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes.”* (52:48). Patience is thus rooted in a belief about who is governing events, which is why the Qur'an pairs it so constantly with the reminder that God sees.

**Do Not Obey a Sinner or a Denier**

The second instruction names two categories of person, and the commentators read them both ways at once: as particular individuals among the Quraysh who approached the Prophet with proposals, and as types. The reports name ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah and al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah as having urged him to abandon the affair. [UNVERIFIED: the identification of these two men as the specific referents was not located this session in a chain-carrying source; it belongs to the asbāb material of the tafsīr tradition.] The general reading is unaffected. The instruction is not to yield to anyone whose position is defined by sin or by rejection of the truth.

The two categories are worth distinguishing. A sinner is someone whose conduct is corrupt, and a denier is someone who has rejected the truth; the first describes behaviour and the second describes a position. The instruction covers both, which means the Prophet was not to follow someone merely because that person was well disposed toward him, nor merely because he was respectable in conduct while rejecting the message.

**What Yielding Would Have Meant**

The Qur'an describes the pressure being applied, and it was not persecution but negotiation. *“They definitely ˹thought they˺ were about to lure you away from what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺, hoping that you would attribute something else to Us falsely—and then they would have certainly taken you as a close friend.”* (17:73). The offer was accommodation in exchange for status, and the sūrah of al-Qalam states the mechanism in a line: *“They wish you would compromise so they would yield ˹to you˺.”* (68:9). The temptation was to adjust the message by a small amount in exchange for being treated well.

The danger in such an offer is precisely that it looks reasonable. Nothing in the proposal required the Prophet to deny anything; it asked only for a small adjustment, in exchange for which he would have been received. The Qur'an's severity here is a measure of how seriously it takes the message's integrity. A truth that can be negotiated by a fraction is not a truth that was revealed.

**Where the Line Is Drawn**

A distinction has to be made, because the Qur'an makes it. Not yielding in the message is not unkindness to people. The same revelation that commands the Prophet not to obey the deniers commands kindness and fairness toward those who have not fought the community (60:8), and it restricts the prohibition to those who have (60:9). The sūrah's own earlier verses commanded feeding a captive taken in war (76:8). The line runs between accommodating people in matters of conduct and yielding to them in matters of truth, and the two are frequently confused.

The two instructions also guard each other. Patience without the prohibition can collapse into endless accommodation, and refusal without patience can become abrasiveness. The Qur'an gives both to the same man in the same verse, and the combination is what it means to hold a position without holding it against anyone.

**Patience as the Thread of the Sūrah**

The command also completes a circle the sūrah has been drawing. Its inhabitants were patient (76:12) — patient in keeping vows, in giving while hungry, in wanting nothing back — and the Prophet is now told to be patient in the same way and for the same reason. The sūrah has one piece of practical advice, and it gives it twice: hold the course, because the One whose decree is being resisted is the One who promised the reward described in the previous eighteen verses.

The command is also the sūrah's last word on the relation between conduct and outcome. Patience is not praised here because it is noble but because it is what the situation requires: the promise has been made, the timetable has not been given, and the interval belongs to God. The instruction to wait is therefore the practical form of believing the promise.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:25

> ˹Always˺ remember the Name of your Lord morning and evening,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhythm Prescribed**

The sūrah has told the Prophet to be patient and not to yield, and it now supplies the means by which that posture is maintained: remembrance at the two ends of the day. Morning and evening are not chosen arbitrarily. They are the thresholds of the day, the points at which a person passes from rest into work and from work back into rest, and putting remembrance at both of them places the whole interval between two acts of attention. The same pairing is prescribed elsewhere in almost identical words: *“and glorify Him morning and evening”* (33:42), and *“So be patient ˹O Prophet˺, ˹for˺ Allah’s promise is certainly true. Seek forgiveness for your shortcomings. And glorify the praises of your Lord morning and evening.”* (40:55).

The two times named also correspond to the two prayers that the Qur'an most often singles out by their position in the day — the prayer at the two ends, which the sūrah of Hūd commands alongside the early part of the night (11:114). The community's practice has always read these verses as the scriptural basis for the times of prayer, and the reading is supported by the way the Qur'an pairs the hours elsewhere (17:78).

**What Remembrance Is**

The word is *dhikr*, and it covers more than repetition. Its function in the Qur'an is to restore a knowledge that has been crowded out: a person knows who his Lord is and what is coming, and he forgets it under the pressure of the hours. Remembrance is the deliberate restoration of that knowledge, and the Qur'an names its effect in the most quoted verse on the subject: *“those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort.”* (13:28). The comfort is not a mood. It is the settling of a person back into the frame he was made for.

The word also carries the sense of mentioning something worth mentioning, which is why the sūrah's first verse is its natural counterpart. The human being was once a thing *not mentioned*; remembrance is the answer to that condition as well. A person who mentions his Lord has, in the most literal sense, been given something to be mentioned for.

**Why the Name, and Not Only the Act**

The verse specifies the Name rather than prescribing a particular formula, and the choice is worth noting. A name in this usage stands for the One named, so that remembering the Name is attending to Him rather than repeating a syllable. The Qur'an uses the same idiom when it describes the places of worship as houses *“where His Name is mentioned. He is glorified there morning and evening”* (24:36). What is prescribed is not a technique but an orientation, and the orientation can be carried in the market as well as in the mosque.

**The Times, and the People Already There**

The two times named are the ones at which the Qur'an describes the believers as already gathered: *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure.”* (6:52). The instruction in this verse therefore places the Prophet alongside the people he was told to keep company with in the sūrah of al-Kahf — *“And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening”* (18:28). The command is not given to a man standing alone. It is given to a man in a community that already keeps the rhythm.

The community reading also has a consequence worth stating. If remembrance belongs at the two ends of the day, then the day itself is not secular time with religious interruptions in it. The whole interval is framed by the two acts, and everything between them is done inside the frame.

**Remembrance as the Equipment for Patience**

The connection between this verse and the two before it should be stated plainly. A person told to be patient and not to yield has been handed a difficult assignment, and the sūrah immediately supplies what makes it possible. Patience is not a matter of gritting one's teeth; it is a matter of what a person is attending to. The Qur'an gives the same prescription to the Prophet in the sūrah of al-Ḥijr: *“We certainly know that your heart is truly distressed by what they say. So glorify the praises of your Lord and be one of those who ˹always˺ pray.”* (15:97–98). The distress is real, and the remedy is prescribed rather than merely sympathised with.

A further point belongs here, and it is the one the sūrah's structure makes. The people it has been praising for eighteen verses did what they did because a Day was real to them. Remembrance is what keeps a Day real. The command in this verse is therefore not an addition to the sūrah's argument but the mechanism by which its argument becomes a life.

**What the Command Presupposes**

Two assumptions sit underneath the verse, and both are worth drawing out. The first is that a person can forget, and that forgetting is a danger rather than a misfortune — which is why the remedy has to be repeated twice a day rather than administered once. The second is that attention is governable: a person can decide what he will be thinking about at the beginning and the end of his day. The Qur'an's anthropology is not pessimistic about this. It assumes that what governs a man's attention governs his life, and that the attention can be placed.

The command is also general in its wording, and the commentators took it as applying to the community and not only to the Prophet. The Qur'an addresses the believers with the same instruction in the plural: *“O believers! Always remember Allah often, and glorify Him morning and evening.”* (33:41–42). What was prescribed to the man who received the revelation is prescribed to the people who received it from him, in the same words and at the same two times.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:26

> and prostrate before Him during part of the night, and glorify Him long at night.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night Is Added to the Day**

The previous verse prescribed remembrance at the two ends of the day; this one adds a third time and a second act. The addition is not a duplication, because what is asked for at night is different in kind from what is asked for in the morning and the evening. Prostration is a bodily posture rather than a state of mind, and glorification through a long night is a duration rather than a moment. The sūrah is therefore prescribing not only that God be remembered but that the body and the hours be given to Him as well.

**Prostration, and Why It Is Named**

The act specified is *sujūd*, the posture in which the forehead is placed on the ground, and the Qur'an treats it as the characteristic act of worship rather than one element among many. It is the posture of the angels at the creation of the first man, the posture into which those recited to fall when the revelation is read to them (32:15), and the posture the Prophet is told to continue in when he is under pressure: *“Never obey him ˹O Prophet˺! Rather, ˹continue to˺ prostrate and draw near ˹to Allah˺.”* (96:19). The act is physical, and part of its point is that it is physical: the body that was made from a drop is the body that bows.

The posture also carries the sūrah's theme of reversal. The people who were promised a kingdom were the ones who wanted no elevation, and the act prescribed here is the one in which a person puts the highest part of himself — his face — at the lowest point. The Qur'an's rewards consistently follow the shape of the acts it prescribes.

**Glorification Through a Long Night**

The second instruction has no specified content — only a duration. *Glorify Him a long night* is an instruction about how much of the night, not about which words, and the measure given is the night itself. The same openness appears in the descriptions of the people the Qur'an praises: *“And glorify Him during part of the night and after the prayers”* (50:40), and *“˹They are˺ those who spend ˹a good portion of˺ the night, prostrating themselves and standing before their Lord.”* (25:64). What is commended is time given, and the time is measured by a clock rather than by a liturgy.

The absence of a prescribed content is itself the point. A person left to fill a long night with glorification has to decide what to say, and the deciding is part of the worship. The Qur'an's willingness to specify the duration and not the formula is a measure of how much it trusts the worshipper.

**Why the Night**

The Qur'an gives its own reason for the emphasis, and it is a practical one: *“Indeed, worship in the night is more impactful and suitable for recitation.”* (73:6). At night there is no audience, no business, and no interruption; what is said is said to God rather than in front of anyone else. The same quality is described from the other side in the accounts of those who *“used to sleep only little in the night, and pray for forgiveness before dawn”* (51:17–18). The night is where a person is least able to perform, and that is what makes it valuable.

There is a further reason the Qur'an gives the night this weight, and it appears in the descriptions of the people it praises. Those who *“abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear”* (32:16) are doing something that costs sleep, and sleep is the clearest of the lawful comforts. The vigil is valuable precisely because what is given up is not sinful in itself.

**The Practice in the Life of the Prophet**

The instruction in this verse belongs to a body of verses addressed to the Prophet about the night vigil, and the sūrah of al-Muzzammil opens with it: *“Stand all night ˹in prayer˺ except a little—or half of it, or a little less, or a little more—and recite the Quran ˹properly˺ in a measured way.”* (73:2–4). The later verses of that sūrah record that the practice became burdensome for the community and was lightened (73:20), which is a reminder that the vigils described in the Makkan sūrahs belong to a particular intensity of circumstance. The sūrah of al-Isrāʾ attaches a promise to the practice: *“And rise at ˹the last˺ part of the night, offering additional prayers, so your Lord may raise you to a station of praise.”* (17:79).

The lightening recorded in the later verses of al-Muzzammil is itself instructive. The Qur'an prescribed an intensity, the community found it unsustainable, and the prescription was adjusted by revelation rather than by quiet abandonment. A law that accommodates human weakness is not a weaker law; it is one given by a Lawgiver who knew what He was asking.

**The Shape of the Sūrah's Closing Advice**

Taken together, vv. 24 through 26 give the Prophet a complete rule of life: patience under pressure, refusal to yield, remembrance at the two ends of the day, and a portion of the night given to prostration and glorification. The sūrah has moved from the reward of the virtuous to the discipline that produced them, and the discipline turns out to be the same one it described in vv. 7 through 10: acts that cost something, repeated, and directed at God rather than at an audience. The last five verses will now say why that discipline is necessary.

The rule is also worth reading as an answer to the sūrah's opening claim about the human being. A person made from a mingled drop, given hearing and sight, and shown the way is a person whose life needs to be structured around the Giver. The commands in these three verses describe what that structure looks like in practice: refusal in public, remembrance in private, and part of the night given over to the One who made the night.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:27

> Surely those ˹pagans˺ love this fleeting world, ˹totally˺ neglecting a weighty Day ahead of them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason for Everything Just Commanded**

The sūrah has prescribed patience, refusal, remembrance, and night worship, and it now gives the reason those things are necessary: the people the Prophet is dealing with love the immediate world and have pushed a heavy Day behind them. The verse explains the situation rather than merely condemning the people in it. A messenger who understands why he is being resisted can be patient about it, and that is what the preceding verses were preparing him for. The diagnosis comes after the prescription because the prescription had to be given first.

**The Same Charge, in the Same Words**

The wording is very close to a verse in the sūrah of al-Qiyāmah: *“Still people want to deny what is yet to come, and neglect the Hereafter.”* (75:20–21). The two sūrahs were revealed in the same period and make the same point in almost the same vocabulary, which suggests a fixed piece of the early preaching rather than a local observation. What this verse adds is the word for weighty or heavy — the Day is not merely future, it is burdensome, and the people who have put it behind them are carrying it without knowing.

The recurrence of the charge across the two sūrahs also tells the reader something about the audience. This was not a rare posture found in a few individuals. It was the default setting of a whole society, and the Qur'an's whole early preaching can be read as a sustained attempt to change what a population considered real.

**Behind the Back**

The verb is the striking element. They have not lost the Day or denied it in so many words; they have *placed it behind them*. The image is of something moved out of the field of vision by a deliberate act and kept there. The same construction appears in the Qur'an's description of the consequence: *“It will be said, ‘This Day We will neglect you as you neglected the meeting of this Day of yours!’”* (45:34), and *“We ˹too˺ will certainly neglect you”* (32:14). What is done to them at the end is the same thing they did to the Day throughout their lives.

The verb is also the one used for leaving a thing behind in a will or a bequest, which gives the image a second edge. What these people have done with the Day is the opposite of what they are doing with their wealth: the one thing that will outlast them has been left behind, and the thing that will not last is what they have taken with them.

**Why the Immediate World Wins**

The contest is unequal, and the Qur'an does not pretend otherwise. What is present, visible, and shared by everyone around exerts a pressure that what is absent cannot match without help. The Qur'an names the imbalance directly: *“They ˹only˺ know the worldly affairs of this life, but are ˹totally˺ oblivious to the Hereafter.”* (30:7). The remedy it prescribes is the one given in the two verses before this one — remembrance at the ends of the day and a portion of the night — because the only thing that can make an absent reality present to a person is repeated attention to it.

The remedy is worth restating because it is the practical heart of the sūrah. A person does not stop loving the world by being told to stop; he stops by attending, twice a day and through part of the night, to what outlasts it. The prescription in vv. 25 and 26 is the cure for the condition described in this verse, and the sūrah has placed the cure before the diagnosis.

**The Choice Is Named as Love**

As in the sūrah of al-Qiyāmah, the charge is love rather than error. These people are not said to have examined the evidence for the Hereafter and found it wanting; they are said to love something else. The Qur'an states the standard by which that love is judged elsewhere, in a verse that lists everything a person might love and sets it against the love of God (9:24). Loving the world is not wrong because the world is evil. It is wrong here because it is preferred, and because what it is preferred to is a Day that is coming.

The diagnosis also explains why argument alone does not move anyone. A person in the grip of an attachment cannot be argued out of it, because the attachment is not a conclusion he reached. What moves him is the slow, repeated restoration of a different reality to his attention, which is precisely what the two verses before this one prescribed.

**The Pathos of the Sentence**

The last thing to notice is the disproportion the verse sets up. The world these people love is described as fleeting — *ʿājila*, the hasty thing, named for its speed — and the Day they have put behind them is described as heavy. The adjectives do the whole work of the argument: what has been chosen is the thing that passes, and what has been postponed is the thing with weight in it. The Qur'an's warning is not that the world is bad but that the exchange rate is absurd, and it says so as plainly as the language allows: *“But you ˹deniers only˺ prefer the life of this world, even though the Hereafter is far better and more lasting.”* (87:16–17).

The verse is also the last of the sūrah's statements about the opponents, and it is noticeably less severe than the threats in the sūrah of al-Qiyāmah. There the denier was warned with a doubled woe; here he is diagnosed. The difference is the difference between a sūrah addressed to a man who had already refused and one addressed to a Prophet who has to keep working with people who have not yet.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:28

> It is We Who created them and perfected their ˹physical˺ form. But if We will, We can easily replace them with others.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Returns to Creation**

The sūrah opened with an argument from origins — a human being was nothing, then a mingled drop, then a person with faculties — and it closes with the same argument, now aimed at the people who have neglected the Day. The structure is deliberate: what established the possibility of the resurrection at the beginning is what establishes the precariousness of the deniers at the end. Nothing in the sūrah's armoury has changed. It has one argument and it uses it twice, at both ends, against two different objections.

**We Made Firm Their Frames**

The verb describing what God did to their constitution means to bind together, to strengthen, to make firm — the same root used for the tightening of a knot or the bracing of a structure. The word *asr* in the object position is taken by the commentators as referring to the joints, the sinews, or the whole binding together of the body. The claim being made is about the integrity of a human frame: bones joined to bones, sinews holding them, the whole thing standing upright. Anyone who has considered how a body holds together will find the word well chosen.

The word also carries a sense of the body's cohesion against its own dissolution, which is what makes it the right word for this argument. A human frame is a collection of parts held in a specific arrangement, and the arrangement is what makes it a person rather than a heap. The One who bound it can bind it again, which is the point the sūrah made at v. 2 and has been building on ever since.

**A Made Thing Can Be Unmade**

The second half of the verse draws the conclusion that the first half was designed to support. The One who bound that frame together can unbind it, and the One who made this people can make another. The Qur'an states the threat in several places with the same bluntness: *“If He wills, He can do away with you and replace you with whoever He wills, just as He produced you from the offspring of other people.”* (6:133), and *“If it is His Will, He can remove you altogether, O humanity, and replace you with others.”* (4:133). The point is not that God is capricious but that no generation is necessary to Him.

The conditional form matters as well. The verse says *if We will*, not *when We will*, and the difference keeps the sentence in the register of warning rather than announcement. Nothing in it has been decided except the fragility, and the fragility is being stated for the sake of whoever is still able to respond to it.

**Replacement in the Qur'an's Warning**

The same warning is addressed to the believers, which shows that it is not a threat aimed only at opponents. *“If you do not march forth, He will afflict you with a painful torment and replace you with other people. You are not harming Him in the least.”* (9:39), and again in the sūrah of Muḥammad: *“If you ˹still˺ turn away, He will replace you with another people. And they will not be like you.”* (47:38). The messengers said the same to their own peoples — *“My Lord will replace you with others”* (11:57). Dependence is total, and no community holds its place by entitlement.

The threat is not arbitrary, and the Qur'an is careful to say what the replacement would be for. The new people are described as not being like the ones replaced — the implication being that a community which neglects what it has been given is not serving the purpose for which it was raised up. The warning is therefore about function rather than about favouritism.

**Why This Answers Love of the World**

The connection to the previous verse is worth stating, because it is easy to miss. The people described there have organised their lives around the immediate world, and the reason they have done so is that the immediate world is the only thing that seems solid. The reply is that the solidest thing they know — their own bodies and their own society — is a made thing, held together by an act, and removable by an act. Nothing in this verse threatens annihilation. It threatens redundancy, which is the precise fear of anyone who has built his life on being someone.

The argument also has a positive side that should not be missed. The same sentence that says God can replace them says that He made them and bound them together. Whoever made a person and held him in one piece has not done so without a purpose, and the sūrah said at v. 2 what that purpose is. Dependence and significance are the two halves of a single claim.

**The Closing Logic of the Sūrah**

The verse also completes the sūrah's answer to the question it asked at v. 1. A human being was once nothing that could be mentioned, and every stage since has been given rather than achieved. A creature in that position does not have the standing to postpone a Day. The last three verses will now state, in the plainest terms available, what is left to a person: a reminder has been sent, the way is open, and the willing that takes it is not finally his own.

The verse is also a warning against a specific piece of reasoning. The deniers did not argue that the resurrection was impossible so much as that it was irrelevant — that nothing would come of it either way. The reply here is that irrelevance is not available to a made thing. A body that was bound together by an act, and a people that can be replaced by an act, do not have the standing to treat the One who made them as though He had nothing further to say.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:29

> Surely this is a reminder. So let whoever wills take the ˹Right˺ Way to their Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What This Refers To**

The demonstrative points at something, and the commentators divided over how much. Taken narrowly, it refers to the sūrah just recited; taken broadly, to the whole Qur'an; taken in between, to the passage that has just ended, in which the creation of the human being and the fate of those who neglect the Day are set side by side. The narrowest reading has the support of a verse in Sūrah al-Muzzammil that is word for word the same: *“Surely this is a reminder. So let whoever wills take the ˹Right˺ Way to their Lord.”* (73:19). In both cases the word lands at the end of a passage and gathers it up.

The narrowest reading is the one that fits the sūrah's practice elsewhere. This sūrah has gathered up its own arguments repeatedly — the reminder of origins in v. 28, the reward summarised in v. 22 — and the demonstrative here works the same way. What is being called a reminder is the whole passage that precedes it, offered to the reader as a single thing he can either take or set aside.

**Reminder, Not News**

The noun is *tadhkira*, and the difference between a reminder and a piece of information is the whole of the Qur'an's claim about itself. A person is not being told something he did not know; he is being asked to remember what he knows and has set aside. The Qur'an states this as its own defence against the charge that it is redundant: *“We have surely varied ˹the signs˺ in this Quran so perhaps they may be mindful”* (20:113), and *“We have certainly set forth every ˹kind of˺ lesson for people in this Quran, so perhaps they may be mindful.”* (39:27). The repetition is the method, because the problem being addressed is forgetfulness.

The word also tells the reader what sort of resistance he should expect. If the message were new information, the appropriate response to unbelief would be more information. Since it is a reminder, the appropriate response to indifference is repetition, which is why the Qur'an says the same things in so many different ways and why this sūrah repeats arguments that appear word for word in the sūrah beside it.

**The Offer Is Open**

The second half of the verse is conditional in form and universal in scope: whoever wills. No group is excluded, no qualification is stated, and no one is told that the way is closed to him. The same openness is stated in the verse that accompanies this one elsewhere: *“So let whoever wills be mindful of it.”* (74:55). The Qur'an's invitations are always issued in this form, and no verse anywhere in the Book tells a living person that the offer has been withdrawn from him.

**A Way, Not a State**

The object of the taking is a path rather than a condition: *take a way to his Lord*. What is offered is a route with a destination, not a feeling to be achieved. The preposition is the same one the sūrah used in v. 9 for the intention behind the giving — *for the sake of* God — and the two belong together. A person sets out on a way toward Someone, and the way is taken by walking rather than by deciding on it once.

The destination named is also worth a note, since the sūrah could have said *a way to Paradise* and did not. The path ends at the Lord rather than at a place, and the same emphasis runs through the sūrah's description of the reward, which culminated in the Lord himself handing the inhabitants a drink (76:21). What is offered here and what was promised there are the same thing.

**The Tension the Next Verse Creates**

The verse stands where the Qur'an deliberately puts it: next to a verse that says a person cannot will at all unless God wills (76:30). The two statements are placed side by side with nothing reconciling them, and the same pairing occurs in the sūrah of al-Takwīr — *“to whoever of you wills to take the Straight Way. But you cannot will ˹to do so˺, except by the Will of Allah, the Lord of all worlds”* (81:28–29). Exegetes have spent centuries on the relation between the two halves, and the text itself supplies no formula. What it supplies is both claims, in the same breath, addressed to the same people.

The pairing has a further consequence that goes beyond theory. If the way is genuinely offered to whoever wills, then no one may say of another person that he is excluded; and if no one wills apart from the divine will, then no one may say of himself that he arrived alone. The two halves of the pair govern a person's judgement of others and of himself respectively.

**Why the Sūrah Ends This Way**

After twenty-eight verses of argument — from the drop to the kingdom, from the vow to the neglect — the sūrah's conclusion is an invitation rather than a deduction. That is worth noticing, because it tells the reader what all the argument was for. Proof is not given in order to win a case; it is given so that the person hearing it can see the road and take it. A sūrah that ended on a demonstration would have produced conviction; a sūrah that ends on an offer produces a decision.

The ending is also the sūrah's answer to the objection that runs underneath it. If the reward is as large as the previous twenty verses have described, someone will ask what is required for it. The answer given here is not a list of conditions but a direction: take the way. Everything the sūrah has described as the conduct of the virtuous follows from having set out, and nothing about the setting out is complicated.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:30

> But you cannot will ˹to do so˺ unless Allah wills. Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Beside It**

The previous verse offered the way to anyone who wills to take it, and this one qualifies the offer in the same breath: no one wills unless God wills. The Qur'an places these two statements together without softening either, and it does so more than once — the identical pairing appears in the sūrah of al-Takwīr: *“to whoever of you wills to take the Straight Way. But you cannot will ˹to do so˺, except by the Will of Allah, the Lord of all worlds.”* (81:28–29). The recurrence tells the reader that the collocation is intentional. The Book is not going to resolve this, and it is not going to let its readers resolve it by dropping one half.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The first thing to establish is what is being denied. The verse does not say that human willing is unreal, that the offer in the previous verse is a formality, or that effort is pointless. The whole Qur'an is addressed to people who are held responsible for what they choose, and its commands, warnings, and promises would be unintelligible if choice were an illusion. What the verse denies is that a person's willing is the last term in the account — that his choosing is uncaused, independent, and the origin of the matter rather than a link in it.

The denial is also aimed at a specific self-flattering inference. A person who takes the way is tempted to attribute the taking to his own better judgement, and the verse removes that comfort in four words. What is left is gratitude rather than pride, and the sūrah's whole description of the virtuous has been a description of people who claimed nothing for themselves.

**What It Does Say**

The positive claim is that every act of willing occurs inside a will that is not the creature's own. The Qur'an states the same thing in the strongest possible terms: *“Had your Lord so willed ˹O Prophet˺, all ˹people˺ on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them!”* (10:99), and *“Had We willed, We could have easily imposed guidance on every soul.”* (32:13). It is also stated of guidance in particular: *“You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills.”* (28:56). The claim is about sovereignty rather than about compulsion.

The claim is stated with equal force in the negative elsewhere, and the two directions belong together. A person is not the origin of his own willing, and he is not exempted from it either. The Qur'an holds both without relief, and the sūrah of al-Insān places them in adjacent verses so that neither can be read in isolation from the other.

**The Recipient of the Address**

There is a variant reading recorded for the opening word, and it changes the address from the singular to the plural: *they do not will* rather than *you do not will*. The difference is small in effect and large in emphasis. On the singular reading the verse speaks to whoever is listening — the Prophet, or any reader — and the claim is personal. On the plural reading it speaks about humanity in general and the claim is descriptive. Both are transmitted, and the majority readings preserve the singular, which keeps the verse in the second person and therefore keeps it as an address.

The variant also has a consequence for how the verse is used. Read as an address it humbles the person hearing it; read as a description it explains the people around him. The tradition has generally kept both in view, using the verse for humility in oneself and for patience with others, and nothing in the text forbids either use.

**Why the Names Follow**

The verse closes with two of the divine names, and they are not decorative. *All-Knowing* rules out the suggestion that God's will is arbitrary or uninformed: what He wills, He wills with complete knowledge of every person, every circumstance, and every outcome. *All-Wise* rules out the suggestion that it is capricious: what is willed is willed for a purpose that is coherent even where it is not visible. The two names are the Qur'an's standing answer to the objection that a sovereign will would be a tyrannical one, and they appear together wherever this subject is raised (2:32; 12:100).

The two names also answer the objection that a sovereign will makes worship meaningless. A will that knows everything and does everything wisely is a will that can be trusted with a person's fate, and the Qur'an's argument throughout is that trust in it is the only rational posture. What is refused is not the sovereignty but the suspicion that sovereignty and wisdom might come apart.

**How the Text Intends It to Be Held**

The practical use of the tension is not difficult to find, even though its theoretical resolution is not given. A person is told to will, and told that his willing depends on a will that is not his. The first statement produces effort and the second produces reliance, and the Qur'an wants both from the same person at the same time. What it refuses to supply is the inference that either the effort or the reliance can be dispensed with. Strive as though the outcome depended entirely on you, and trust as though it depended not at all on you: the two halves of the verse, held together, produce exactly that.

The collocation also settles a question about prayer. A person who believes that his willing depends on God's has every reason to ask for it, and the Qur'an's own prayers are full of requests for guidance, steadiness, and a good end. If the will were entirely the creature's, such prayers would be pointless; if it were entirely God's, they would be theatre. As the verse stands, they are the most rational thing a person can do.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:31

> He admits whoever He wills into His mercy. As for the wrongdoers, He has prepared for them a painful punishment

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Last Sentence**

The sūrah ends with two destinations, stated in a single line and in the order the Qur'an almost always uses: mercy first, punishment second. The arrangement is itself a claim. The two are not symmetrical halves of a balanced account, and the Book's habit of naming the mercy before the punishment, and of describing the punishment as something prepared for a specific group rather than for humanity at large, is consistent enough to be read as deliberate. The sūrah's final words are about who is admitted, and the admission comes first.

**Mercy as a Place**

The word translated as mercy is the one the Qur'an uses for the Garden itself when it wants to name the destination rather than the contents: *“As for those who believed and did good, their Lord will admit them into His mercy.”* (45:30). The commentators therefore divided between reading it as the Garden and reading it as the act of mercy by which a person is brought to repentance, forgiven, and then admitted. The two are not exclusive, and the second reading is the one that fits the sūrah's argument, since the whole passage has been about how a life is conducted and not merely about where it ends.

The reading of mercy as the Garden also ties the sūrah's end to its middle. The reward described from v. 5 to v. 22 is what mercy looks like when it is opened, and the punishments named at v. 4 and here are what withheld mercy leaves. Neither description is complete without the other, and the sūrah has deliberately placed them at its two ends.

**Who the Wrongdoers Are**

The second half of the verse names the other party with a word the sūrah has not used before. *Ẓālimūn* — those who do wrong, who put things where they do not belong — is the Qur'an's general term for the ruined, and in this sūrah it names the people described in v. 27: those who loved the immediate world and placed the weighty Day behind them. They are not identified by their arguments against the resurrection, or by their community, or by any particular sin. They are identified by what they did with what they had.

The word is also the one the Qur'an uses for the gravest wrong in particular — associating others with God — which suggests that the category is wider than a list of offences. Wrongdoing in this vocabulary is fundamentally a misplacement: putting the world where God belongs, putting the immediate ahead of the permanent, and putting oneself where the Giver should be.

**Prepared: the Frame Closes**

The verb is the same one the sūrah used in v. 4, where chains, shackles, and a blazing fire were said to have been prepared for those who were ungrateful. The repetition is the sūrah's closing bracket. What was stated in the fourth verse about the consequence of ingratitude is restated in the thirty-first about the consequence of wrongdoing, and the two words for the people — the ungrateful and the wrongdoers — turn out to describe the same party from two angles. Both destinations were ready before the sūrah began, and the only question the reader has been asked throughout is which one he is walking toward.

The repetition also carries the sūrah's final claim about time. What is prepared is prepared already; nothing in the verse is contingent on a future decision by God, and nothing in it waits to be arranged. The only unresolved element is the reader, and the sūrah ends by leaving him with that.

**Asymmetry, and What It Means**

The two halves of the verse are not parallel in their subjects. Mercy is given to *whom He wills*, and punishment is *prepared for the wrongdoers* — the first names a will and the second names a class. The difference in construction is the Qur'an's way of keeping the two claims in their right proportion: no one enters by entitlement, and no one is punished except by what he has done. The wrongdoers are the people who have wronged; the mercied are those He has chosen to show mercy to. Neither statement is qualified, and the sūrah leaves them exactly as it found them.

The construction also protects against the two errors the Qur'an warns against most often on this subject. Presumption — the belief that one is admitted by one's own merit — is answered by *whom He wills*. Despair — the belief that one is beyond mercy — is answered by the fact that mercy is named first, is offered to whom He wills, and has not been closed to anyone who is still alive.

**The Whole Arc**

The sūrah began with a human being who was nothing that could be mentioned and ends with that being admitted into a mercy or facing a punishment that has already been made. In between it argued from the origin to the resurrection, described the people who feared a Day and what they were given, commanded patience and remembrance to the Prophet, and placed the freedom of human willing next to the sovereignty of the divine will without reconciling them. What remains after all of that is the sentence the sūrah ends on: a door that is open, a warning that has been delivered, and a decision that belongs to whoever is still reading.

The sūrah is also one of the relatively few in the Qur'an that closes by naming both destinations in a single breath after twenty-eight verses of argument in between. The compression is the point. Everything said about origins, vows, feeding, reward, revelation, and worship was said in order to make these last two clauses land on a reader who is still able to choose.
