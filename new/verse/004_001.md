## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:1

> O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Origin That Undercuts All Claims of Rank**

This opening places the entire sūrah on a single foundation before any law is given. Mankind is addressed together — *yā ayyuhā al-nās* — not as tribes, clans, or ranks, but as the offspring of one soul and its mate. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“We created you from a male and a female, and made you into peoples and tribes so that you may know one another”* (49:13), and again *“He created you from a single soul”* (39:6). By beginning legislation about orphans, women, inheritance, and marriage with creation, the verse teaches that law does not invent equality; it remembers it. Every claim that one lineage is higher than another collapses at the first sentence. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the purpose of reminding the audience of a common parentage at this exact point is to soften hearts for the duties that follow, because a person who feels his link to others is more likely to guard their rights. That psychological link matters here: the sūrah will command protection for the weakest — the orphan girl, the widow, the heir with a small share — and it grounds those commands not in pity but in kinship. If all spread from one soul, then wronging another is wronging one’s own extended family.

**From One Soul and Its Mate to Countless Men and Women**

The phrase *min nafs wāḥidah* — from a single soul — is understood by the vast majority of exegetes to mean Adam, with the mate understood as Ḥawwāʾ. The Arabic word *nafs* is grammatically feminine while *zawj* is masculine, yet commentators do not see a problem in the pairing, because the terms describe roles in creation rather than grammatical genders. What the verse stresses is sequence: singularity, then duality, then multiplicity. One became two, and two became countless. That pattern mirrors how the Qur’an elsewhere describes the spreading of humanity as a sign of divine power. Some modern readers have asked whether *minhā* — from it — means from the same substance or from the same kind. The majority read it as indicating that the mate was created from the same nature as the first soul, consonant with the usage of *min anfusikum* in other passages to mean of your own kind (3:164; 9:128; 16:72). Al-Rāzī observes that the Qur’an does not narrate the mechanism of Ḥawwāʾ’s creation in detail; the ḥadīth literature mentions creation from a rib, but the Book itself keeps the language brief, keeping attention on the moral point rather than the biology. That brevity is deliberate: the verse wants the reader to see a shared root before seeing any difference.

**Why Taqwā Comes Twice**

The command *ittaqū* — be mindful, guard yourselves — appears twice in one verse, and both times it points to God. *Ittaqū rabbakum* opens the verse, and *wattaqū Allāh* returns near its middle. In Arabic, *taqwā* carries the sense of placing a shield between oneself and what one fears; the Qur’an uses the same root for protecting oneself from the Fire and for keeping duty to kin. Here the shield is awareness of the Lord who created and who watches. Repetition in such a short space is not decoration. It frames the social duties that sit between the two commands — the appeal to one another in God’s Name and the honouring of womb-ties — as acts of God-consciousness, not merely good manners. The verse also shifts from *rabb* — Lord, Sustainer — to *Allāh*, the divine Name that gathers all attributes. The first is the Lord who creates and spreads; the second is the Name by which people adjure one another. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes that the second *ittaqū* prepares the reader for the specific rights that follow through the sūrah; without that inner guard, the coming laws about property and marriage would be treated as technicalities to be evaded.

**In Whose Name You Appeal to One Another**

The clause *alladhī tasāʾalūna bihi* — in Whose Name you appeal to one another — captures a living practice of the Arabs. People would say, I ask you by God, or I adjure you by God, to settle a debt or to grant a need. The Qur’an does not abolish that speech but sanctifies it, reminding the speaker who the Name belongs to. If you already use God’s Name to ask others for your rights, then be mindful of Him when you are the one being asked. The placement matters: the verse first ties humanity to one origin, then ties daily transactions to God’s Name, making every request and every agreement a place where piety is tested. Another reading vocalises *tasāʾalūna* with a light *sīn* and reads *al-arḥām* in the genitive, yielding and the wombs — do not cut family ties — which many reciters transmitted. The two readings converge in meaning: guard God and guard kinship, because both are invoked in oaths and both are easily violated when advantage beckons. The verse presents God and kinship side by side to show that betrayal of kin is not a private fault; it breaks a tie that was sworn by God’s Name.

**The Womb-Tie and the Name of Mercy**

*Al-arḥām* — family ties, literally wombs — comes from the same root as *raḥmah*, mercy, and the divine Name *al-Raḥmān*, the Compassionate. That shared root was not lost on the first audience. A womb is the first home a human knows, and mercy is the first treatment he receives. The Qur’an elsewhere pairs devotion to God with goodness to kin, as in *“Worship Allah and associate nothing with Him, and be good to parents and relatives”* (4:36). This verse elevates the tie further by placing it immediately after the divine Name people use in their oaths. A well-known ḥadīth, transmitted widely and cited by commentators on this verse, says that *al-raḥim* — the womb-tie — is derived from *al-Raḥmān*, and that God says, whoever maintains it, I will maintain him, and whoever cuts it, I will cut him off. The sūrah will return to relatives repeatedly, assigning them shares, presence at distribution, and kind speech. Here the principle is given before the detail: honour the tie because it shares a name with mercy, and because your own existence passed through it. To be mindful of Allah and then to sever what He linked is a contradiction the verse refuses to allow.

**Watchful Over You Even When No One Else Is**

The verse closes with *inn Allāha kāna ʿalaykum raqībā* — surely Allah is ever Watchful over you. The Name *al-Raqīb* gathers seeing, guarding, and reckoning. It is not only that God knows what people do behind closed doors; it is that He keeps watch like a guardian keeps watch over a ward. That closing fits the sūrah’s character as a book of law for the vulnerable. Guardians of orphans, holders of dowries, distributors of estates — all handle property that the owner cannot yet claim or cannot defend. No court can see every handover. The verse answers that gap with a single assurance and warning: the Watcher is already present. Elsewhere the Qur’an says *“Allah is Witness over all things”* (4:33; 58:6), and the same idea returns at the end of the orphan test in verse 6 with *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6). That thread links creation to accountability. The God who spread countless men and women from one soul is not distant from the countless transactions among them; He observes each one, and what is entrusted to people in this world will be measured with precision in the next.
