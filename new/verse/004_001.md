## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:1

> O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you.

### Expanded Commentary

**The human family before its divisions**

The sūrah opens with an address wider than the community of believers: every human being is summoned to mindfulness of the Lord. That breadth matters because the laws that follow concern marriage, property, inheritance, war, and public trust. They are not rules for a religious elite detached from human nature; they are guidance for creatures who share one origin and therefore owe one another more than convenience or tribal loyalty. The verse puts descent before difference. Men and women, nations and families, all emerge from the same created source.

**“A single soul” and the dignity of persons**

The classical exegetes generally identify the single soul with Adam and the mate with Eve, while also noting that the Arabic expression can emphasize common nature rather than describe a physical process. The Qur’an itself does not require the rib narrative in order to establish its point. Whether “from it” is read as origin from Adam or as likeness in kind, the moral conclusion is the same: woman is not a lesser species and humanity is not divided into superior bloodlines. The passage moves from one soul, to a pair, to innumerable men and women; difference is real, but it is downstream from shared creation.

This is why the verse undermines claims of inherited human superiority. A person may possess a particular language, ancestry, or social office, but none of those can make the other descendants of Adam alien to him. The Prophet’s Farewell Sermon expressed the same principle by returning all people to Adam and Adam to dust. The point is not to erase family identity; it is to prevent identity from becoming a warrant for oppression.

**Taqwā and the rights created by kinship**

The repeated command to be mindful of Allah joins the Creator’s right to the rights of relatives. The Arabic *arḥām* means wombs or kinship ties, and its association with mercy is not accidental: the womb is the first place in which a human being receives care without having earned it. To honour kin is therefore not merely to maintain agreeable relationships. It includes visiting, supporting, reconciling, and refusing to exploit dependence. The following verses apply this principle to orphans and women, whose vulnerability made family both a possible refuge and a possible instrument of abuse.

**The divine Witness over private dealings**

The verse ends with Allah as Watchful. Human law can require witnesses, records, and judges, but none of these sees every concealed bargain. The guardian who quietly diminishes an orphan’s inheritance, the relative who uses a family claim to intimidate another, and the spouse who hides a betrayal may evade human inspection. The Watcher is not an argument for surveillance by people; it is the moral foundation of accountability when no person is looking. Al-Nisāʾ begins by making family ethics a form of worship: reverence for God must become protection of those whom our relationships place within our power.

**A foundation for public ethics**

The opening principle reaches beyond biological relatives. It asks a society to see every person as connected to the same created family, while giving special attention to relationships in which dependence makes abuse easy. Policies for foster care, inheritance, elder care, and family mediation should therefore begin with dignity rather than suspicion. Kinship is not a private sentiment; it creates duties that God will question.
