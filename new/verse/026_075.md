## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:75

> Abraham responded, “Have you ˹really˺ considered what you have been worshipping—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham Demands Reflection Rather Than Repetition**

Abraham answers the appeal to forefathers with a new question: have you truly considered what you worship? The question targets the gap between performing a practice and examining its object. His people have admitted that the idols cannot benefit or harm, yet they continue to worship them because previous generations did so. Abraham asks whether they have ever stopped to think about what that devotion means.

The wording calls for deliberate reflection, not a passing glance. To consider an object of worship is to examine its nature, capacity, origin, and relation to the worshipper. A person cannot justify devotion by saying that he has repeated it many times. Reflection asks whether the object deserves the role assigned to it, even when answering honestly threatens the comfort of custom.

**The Question Includes the Whole History of Their Worship**

The phrase what you have been worshipping gathers present practice and inherited practice into one object of examination. Abraham does not ask only about a single statue or one ceremony. He asks them to look at the entire system by which idols have received their devotion. The community must consider not just what it does today, but what it has been doing across generations.

That wider scope prevents a narrow defense. A person might admit that one image is powerless while preserving the general institution of idols. Abraham asks about the whole direction of worship. If every object shares the same inability to hear, help, or harm, then changing one image will not solve the problem. The community must reconsider the category itself.

**Reflection Can Break the Spell of Familiarity**

Familiar things often escape judgment because they feel natural. A shrine visited since childhood can seem self-evidently worthy, while a new question feels like an attack on reality. Abraham's question makes the familiar strange enough to inspect. What is this object? Why do we bow to it? What has it actually done?

These questions can be unsettling because they separate the object from the emotional atmosphere around it. Music, ritual, family memory, and public honor may have made the idol feel powerful. Reflection strips away those supports and asks what remains. If the object cannot hear, benefit, or harm, familiarity cannot supply the missing divinity.

**The Question Places Responsibility on the Living**

The people cannot answer only by naming their forefathers. Those forefathers are absent from the conversation, while the living are hearing Abraham's challenge. The present generation must decide whether to continue the practice. Ancestry may explain how the idols arrived in the community, but the living remain responsible for what they choose to worship now.

This protects moral agency. People are shaped by family and culture, but they are not entirely excused by them. A person can inherit an error without being condemned for receiving it unknowingly; once the error is exposed, however, continued attachment becomes a new choice. Abraham's question marks that turning point.

**Consideration Is an Act of Intellectual Worship**

True worship involves the whole person, including the mind's honest attention. Abraham does not ask for a blind replacement of one object with another. He asks the people to use the faculty by which they can recognize the mismatch between an idol's appearance and its supposed power. Reason becomes a servant of worship when it removes false objects and directs the heart toward the Creator.

This is why the Qur'anic critique of idolatry is not simply a command to stop. It supplies questions, signs, and contrasts that help the worshipper see why the change is necessary. The heart can be devoted only after the mind has ceased treating a powerless object as a lord. Reflection clears the ground for sincere submission.

**The Question Does Not Depend on Abraham's Status**

Abraham's argument would remain sound even if the people denied his prophetic authority. He asks them to examine their own objects and their own admission. The question stands on observable facts, not on an appeal to his rank alone. This gives the call a fairness that exposes the weakness of the inherited defense.

A reformer should not demand that people accept every conclusion merely because he speaks with confidence. He should show the contradiction and invite honest thought. Abraham's authority is real, but his method also respects the audience's capacity to reason. The people have to look at what they worship and decide whether their devotion can survive that look.

**The Dash Leaves the Examination Open**

The verse ends with a dash because the question continues. The pause creates a space in which the audience is asked to consider before Abraham completes the contrast. That rhetorical space is part of the guidance. It does not let the hearer rush back to ancestral slogans. The object of worship must remain before the mind long enough for its weakness to be seen.

The central issue is already clear. A practice inherited from respected forefathers still requires examination. Abraham's question turns the people from the age of the custom to the nature of the object, where truth can be judged.

Reflection is the opposite of merely repeating a name. It lets the worshipper see whether the object can carry the hopes and fears placed upon it. It also exposes the cost of false worship: the heart is trained to seek security from what cannot give it, while the true Lord is displaced by an image. Abraham's question is mercy because it gives the worshipper a chance to recover clear sight before habit becomes permanent allegiance. The invitation to think is itself a form of release from the authority of custom.
