## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:269

> Allah grants wisdom to whoever He wills. And whoever is granted wisdom is certainly blessed with a great privilege. But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gift and Its Grammar**

The verse's opening clause is a sentence about giving that gives nothing to command: *Allah grants wisdom to whoever He wills*. The grammar is the sūrah's sovereignty-formula — the same structure as the kingship granted to whomever He wills (2:247) and the verses' distribution in the sūrah of ʿImrān — and its application here to ḥikmah makes wisdom a bestowal rather than an achievement. The commentators note what the formula excludes: wisdom is not accumulated by study alone, not purchased, not inherited by lineage, and not distributed by merit visible to men; it is given, and the giving follows the will. The verse's placement inside the spending-section is its first puzzle and its first lesson: between the contest of the two voices (2:268) and the audit of gifts and vows (2:270), the section pauses to name the faculty that reads both — the wisdom without which the threats and promises, the dregs and the good things, the parables of rock and garden, remain information rather than direction. The ḥikmah granted is the section's operating system, and the verse states its origin so that no reader mistakes it for his own.

**What the Wisdom Is**

The tradition's glosses on the verse's ḥikmah are a catalogue, and al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī preserve the range without collapsing it. The exegetical reading: knowledge of the Qur'an's meanings — its abrogating and abrogated, its ambiguous and determinate verses (the ʿImrān distinction at 3:7), its order of revelation, its licit and forbidden. The ethical reading: the fear of God, per the transmitted maxim that the beginning of wisdom is the fear of God. The cognitive readings: intelligence — ʿaql — and comprehension — fahm — the faculties the verse's own closing will name. The theological reading: some see in the verse an allusion to the power of rational demonstration, the wisdom that proves. And the disputed reading: prophethood itself — nubuwwah — which some commentators assign and others contest. The plurality is not indecision but the verse's method: ḥikmah is named as a genus whose species run from jurisprudence to fear to logic to revelation, and the common element the readings share is direction — knowledge that governs conduct. Al-Rāzī states the synthesis: wisdom as knowledge differs from right action, but wisdom proper is the combination — the understanding and the acting founded on it — which is why the verse's gift is called, in its second clause, much good rather than much information.

**Much Good**

*Whoever is granted wisdom is certainly blessed with a great privilege* — khayr kathīr, a good that is much, or much that is good, the Arabic holding both. The commentators weigh the clause against the section's arithmetic deliberately: the grain returns seven hundredfold, the garden doubles, the date becomes a mountain — and the verse assigns to wisdom a multiplication of a different kind, the good whose quantity is its quality. The ḥadīth the exegetes cite here is the tradition's famous permission of envy — there is no envy except in two cases: the man given wealth who spends it down in the way of truth, and the man given wisdom who judges by it and teaches it (in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). The two cases are the section's two currencies — the spent wealth and the exercised wisdom — and the envy permitted for each measures its worth: these are the only possessions whose abundance in another's hands a person may rightfully ache for. The wisdom-case is doubled in the ḥadīth's own verbs: judged by and taught — the ḥikmah that governs its holder and the ḥikmah that governs through him, the private direction and the public teaching. The verse's much-good is thus the section's hidden premise: every ruling about giving — the quality, the silence, the secrecy, the recipients — is wisdom applied to wealth, and the applying is the gift.

**The Nourishment Reading**

Al-Rāzī adds the verse's psychological placement, connecting it to the verse before: the spirit or the intellect takes its nourishment from wisdom, while the ego's desires are fed by the indecencies the previous verse named. The two foods define the two souls of the section — the giver whose hand is opened by promise and the keeper whose hand is closed by threat — and the wisdom-verse states what the contest is actually over: not the dirham but the soul's diet. The reading gives the verse's grant its developmental logic: wisdom nourishes the faculty that recognizes the angel's register, that performs the closed-eyes test honestly, that hears the burnt-garden parable as self-description. Without it the section's commands are heard as restrictions; with it they are recognized as the appetite's proper objects. The commentators connect this nourishment-theme to the wider Qur'anic usage — wisdom as what settles the heart (the 13:28 comfort-language the tradition reads alongside) and as what the prophets were given in childhood (the sūrah of Maryam's Yahyā, granted ḥukm as a boy, 19:12) — a range that fixes the gift as simultaneously cognitive and constitutive: wisdom changes what the soul is hungry for, which is why its grant is called much good and why its absence makes every other gift dangerous.

**People of Reason**

The verse's closing clause restricts its own reception: *none will be mindful except people of reason* — ulū al-albāb, the possessors of the lubb, the intellect at its kernel rather than its surface. The term is the sūrah's own: the law of retaliation was addressed to the same people (2:179) — there is life for you in the retaliation, O people of reason — and in both places the naming does the same work: the legislation or the bestowal is stated openly, and its reception is assigned to the faculty that goes beneath the statement. Al-Rāzī and the tradition gloss lubb as the intellect purified of the desires' interference — the mind operating where the two voices of the previous verse have been discriminated — which makes the clause recursive in the section's exact sense: wisdom is granted to whom God wills; only the people of reason notice the grant; and the reason that notices is itself among wisdom's first fruits. The verse thus closes on the humility its opening commanded: the reader who recognizes wisdom in the section's rulings — who hears the closed-eyes test as protection rather than fastidiousness, the two voices as acoustics rather than mythology — is recognizing the gift he has been given, and the recognition is the mindfulness the clause names.

**The Verse Between Its Neighbors**

Structurally the verse is the section's hinge between the interior and the ledger. Behind it, the spending-legislation has run from the parables to the purse's private contest; ahead, the audit resumes — the gifts and vows known to God (2:270), the secret and the open ranked (2:271), the guidance-decoupling of the giving (2:272), the silent poor described (2:273). The wisdom-verse stands at the pivot and supplies both halves with their faculty: the interior contest requires the wisdom that discriminates voices, and the ledger requires the wisdom that remembers it is watched. The commentators' observation about the verse's sovereignty-grammar completes the placement: the grant being God's will, the section's demands are never addressed to the un-equipped — the giver commanded to select the good, to speak kindly, to give secretly, to recognize the dignified poor, is assumed to have been given, or to be being given, the ḥikmah the commands run on. The verse is thus the section's grace-clause in the literal sense: the statement that the faculty for all this giving is itself a gift — much good, granted by will, noticed by the reasonable, and exercised, once noticed, in exactly the transactions the surrounding verses legislate.
