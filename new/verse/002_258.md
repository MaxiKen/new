## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:258

> Are you ˹O Prophet˺ not aware of the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship? ˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death.” He argued, “I too have the power to give life and cause death.” Abraham challenged ˹him˺, “Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west.” And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck. And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dispute Reported**

The sentence opens with the address to the Prophet ﷺ and records the dispute of a ruler who argued with Abraham about his Lord, and the community's teachers read the account as the model of the encounter between the messenger and the power of the world. The narrative follows the verses about the guardianship of God, so that the argument about life and death is presented as the meeting of two claims to authority.

The wording states the reason for the dispute at its beginning, since the man's kingship is named as the ground of his confidence. The Book places the account before its community as a record of how a servant of God answered a ruler who mistook his power for a share in the divine command.

**The Claim of the King**

The sentence records Abraham's statement that his Lord gives life and causes death, and it records the ruler's answer that he also gives life and causes death. The commentators described what the man did to support the claim, and the report that was transmitted states that he brought out two prisoners, killing one and sparing the other, so that the killing of the first and the release of the second were presented as the exercise of the power the sentence names.

The community's scholars explained the pretence in the account. The ruler disposed of lives that were already in his hand, while the giving of life that Abraham named is the bringing of a creature into existence and the restoration of the dead. The two acts are not of the same kind, and the sentence preserves the exchange without commentary.

**The Sign That Ended the Argument**

Abraham then named a sign that no claim of power could answer, since he invited the ruler to bring the sun from the west after God had brought it from the east. The commentators noted the choice of the argument, because the movement of the sun belongs to the order that no king commands and no subject can dispute.

The sentence closes the account with the statement that the man who disbelieved was confounded, and the teachers read the word as the description of a silence that followed an argument he could not answer. The Book does not record a reply, and the omission supplies the conclusion of the exchange.

The final clause states that God does not guide the wrongdoing people, and the community's scholars read it as the statement about the condition into which the ruler's own conduct had brought him, since the guidance had been offered through the person of the prophet who stood before him.

**The Identity of the Antagonist**

The commentators discussed the identity of the man, and several of them named the ruler whom the later traditions call Nimrod. The teachers noted that the name does not appear in the Book or in the authentic reports of the Prophet ﷺ, and they treated the identification as material received from the accounts of the earlier communities.

The community's scholars also discussed the phrase about the kingship, since the sentence can be read as the statement that the ruler had been given his sovereignty or as the statement that Abraham's line had been given a mighty kingdom elsewhere in the Book. Both readings were set out, and the teachers preferred the first as the one that the account of the dispute supports.

**Sovereignty in the Book**

The sentence belongs to a series of passages in which the Book treats the claims of rulers and the limits of their power. The teachers gathered the verses about the kings who were given authority and those who exceeded it, and they read the account of Abraham's dispute with the light of the whole.

The conclusion that the community drew concerns the source of every authority, since the kingship of the ruler in the sentence came from God and the claim he made within it was false. The passages that praise the rulers who acknowledge the giver of their power were read beside the account as its counterpart.

The community's scholars also noted that the sign Abraham used was drawn from the order of the creation, so that the answer to the ruler's pretence was the ordinary course of the sun. The Book's method of pointing to what is universal appears here as it appears in the sūrah's other arguments.

**The Lesson for the Community**

The account has been read as the model of the confidence with which the believers meet the claims of power. Abraham stood before a ruler who could kill, and his answer placed the matter of life and death in the hand of God, so that the sentence supplied the community with the disposition it required in its own disputes.

The teachers noted that the exchange took place before the arguments of the philosophers and the reasoning of the schools, since the prophet answered the king with a sign from the sky. The simplicity of the answer was preserved in the community's teaching as the pattern of the argument from what is evident.

The closing clause also supplies a warning, since the account describes a man who was confounded and did not return. The community's scholars read the ending as the statement that the proof is not withheld but that the guidance is a gift that accompanies the one who seeks it.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the account of the dispute, the claim that the ruler made, the sign by which the argument was concluded, the confession that followed, and the statement about the guidance that the wrongdoing people do not receive. The teachers used the verse when they spoke about the meeting of the prophets with the powers of their time, and the community's preachers repeated the account when they described the limits of the authority that a ruler may claim over the faith of his people. The passage continues with the account of the man who passed by the ruined city, so that the two narratives of the resurrection stand beside one another.
