## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:258

> Are you ˹O Prophet˺ not aware of the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship? ˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, "My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death." He argued, "I too have the power to give life and cause death." Abraham challenged ˹him˺, "Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west." And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck. And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The King Who Argued**

The verse opens on the Qur'an's only recorded debate between a prophet and a reigning king, and it withholds the king's name. The commentators commonly identify him as Nimrod, but they are candid about the identification's standing: it rests on folkloric material, he is named in no ḥadīth, and his place even in the biblical record is ambiguous. The anonymity is arguably the point — the argument matters, the arguer's biography does not. What the verse supplies instead is the debate's cause: *because Allah had granted him kingship*. The grammar admits two readings, al-Rāzī notes — the sovereignty could belong to Abraham (whose descendants were given a mighty kingdom, 4:54) or to the antagonist — and the majority takes it as the king's: the power that God gave him became the ground from which he disputed God. The verse thus opens with its diagnosis already embedded in its syntax: kingship, a divine grant like every authority, is the instrument of the argument against its Giver.

**My Lord Gives Life and Causes Death**

Abraham's opening statement is a definition of lordship in two verbs: *My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death*. The formula — God gives life and causes death — recurs across the Qur'an as the standing description of the divine monopoly (2:28 among its earliest instances in this sūrah), and Abraham deploys it as a test of the word rabb itself: whoever holds life and death holds lordship, and lordship belongs to whoever holds them. The king's answer is the verse's masterpiece of sophistry: *I too have the power to give life and cause death*. The narrations supply the performance behind the claim — the king brings two men, kills one and spares the other: there, life given and death caused, a ruler's prerogative demonstrated on the spot. The mimicry is exact in vocabulary and empty in substance: the pardoned prisoner was not given life but left alive, and the executed man's death was an inflicted event, not the withdrawal of a sustaining hand. The king has seized the words and released the meaning — which is the verse's picture of how power argues.

**Bring It from the West**

Abraham's rejoinder does not contest the mimicry; it changes the terrain. *Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west.* The commentators note the move's construction: the first claim could be counterfeit because life and death, in human hands, have look-alikes — the pardon, the execution, the reprieve. The sun has none. Its rising is a daily, public, unbuyable fact with no administrative substitute, and the challenge transfers the debate from a domain where sovereignty can perform imitations to a domain where it cannot perform anything. The single imperative — make it rise from the west — is the whole refutation: not an argument to answer but a task to attempt. The verse's next word records the result in one passive verb: the disbeliever was dumbstruck — buhita, confounded, struck silent. He does not concede, does not argue further, does not strike back; the debate simply ends where the pretense can no longer follow. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī read the silence as the verse's epistemology: against the sign that cannot be mimicked, falsehood has no next move.

**The Nature Full of Signs**

The encounter exhibits a theme the commentators name explicitly: Abraham's triumph exposes the ruler's pretense *and* demonstrates that the natural world is full of God's signs. The debate's two rounds are two kinds of evidence — the first the hidden governance of life and death, disputable because invisible; the second the visible governance of the heavens, indisputable because daily — and Abraham's shift between them is the Qur'an's method in miniature: when the invisible sign is contested by a counterfeit, move to the sign whose counterfeit is impossible. The patriarch's career runs on these celestial observations: the sūrah of the Cattle will show him reading guidance in a star, a moon, and a sun that all set (6:76–78), concluding that lordship cannot belong to what does not stay. Here the same sky supplies the refutation of a king. The natural sign, in the Qur'an's usage, is not decoration for the argument — it is the argument's strongest form, because it is addressed to every eye at once and cannot be pardoned, executed, or administratively arranged.

**The Pharaoh Parallel**

The commentators set this king beside his archetype: the debate between Moses and Pharaoh, whose claim escalates to the direct announcement — *I am your lord, the most high* (79:24). The two rulers fail in the same shape and differ in degree. Pharaoh asserts the lordship outright and is answered by signs he can watch and cannot touch — the staff, the hand, the plagues, the sea; Abraham's antagonist asserts only the two verbs and is answered by a single sentence about the sunrise. In both stories the ruler's power is real — the execution happens, the kingdom stands — and in both, the reality of the power is precisely what tempts it into the claim. The verse's causal clause names the temptation's source: *because Allah had granted him kingship*. The grant is not the sin; the argument from the grant is. The Qur'an's kings are not condemned for ruling but for reading their rule as self-derived — the same misreading the handhold-verse diagnosed in the ṭāghūt, the rebel followed in his rebellion, whose guardianship leads out of light and into darkness.

**No Guidance for the Wrongdoers**

The verse closes with the withheld gift: *And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people*. The sentence explains the dumbfoundment's permanence — the king is silenced but not converted, confounded but not guided — and states the verse's final theorem: guidance is not the automatic prize of losing an argument. The wrongdoer's non-guidance is the Qur'an's standing account of the hardened heart: the ẓulm that takes God's grant and argues from it, that mimics the divine verbs on prisoners, closes the road the signs open. Al-Rāzī connects the clause to the debate's structure: the signs did their work — the counterfeit was exposed, the sun was named, the silence fell — and what remained missing was the reception the wrongdoing had disabled. The verse leaves its reader with the encounter's whole geometry: a prophet with no army, a king with one; a claim tested on a prisoner, a claim tested on the sunrise; an argument that ended in a word, and a guidance that did not follow the word. The sūrah's next scene will show the same two forces — death and life, doubt and certainty — at work on a ruined city and a hundred-year sleeper, where the questioning is honest and the guidance, accordingly, arrives.
