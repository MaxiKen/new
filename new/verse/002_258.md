## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:258

> Are you ˹O Prophet˺ not aware of the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship? ˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death.” He argued, “I too have the power to give life and cause death.” Abraham challenged ˹him˺, “Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west.” And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck. And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dispute with the King**

The verse opens with the messenger addressed — a-lam tara ilā alladhī ḥājja Ibrāhīma fī rabbihi — and introduces a ruler who argued with Abraham about his Lord, "because God had given him kingship." The clause is placed at the front, as the Qur'an habitually places the cause of a fault: the man's authority is named before his argument, because the authority is what the argument rested on.

Al-Ṭabarī and the later commentators identify him with Nimrod, but the identification comes from the narrative traditions of the earlier communities (isrāʾīliyyāt) and not from the ḥadīth. The Qur'an leaves him unnamed, and the verse's force does not depend on the name. What is stated is the type: a person whose power over other people's lives persuaded him that he had power over their being.

**"My Lord Gives Life and Causes Death"**

Ibrahim's first move is theological in the plainest terms: the One I call upon disposes of life and death. The answer, "I give life and cause death," is the report of the king's boast, and al-Ṭabarī preserves what was said to have happened next: he brought two prisoners, killed one and spared the other, and called the sparing a granting of life. The early commentators record the anecdote as illustration of the pretence, not as part of the verse.

The exchange exposes a distinction the Arabic preserves cleanly: the king's power was over people already alive. The Qur'anic claim is about iḥyāʾ and imāta as the giving and taking of existence — a claim about creation that no magistrate can imitate. The verse reports his answer without comment, letting the reader see it.

**The Sun from the East**

Ibrahim then changes the terms of the argument: "God brings the sun from the east; bring it from the west." The move is characteristic of the Qur'anic argument from nature, which is the same method 6:78 uses when Abraham watches the rising sun: the sign is not a proposition but the standing order of the world, and no king administers it.

Al-Rāzī observes that the second argument is not a harder version of the first; it is a different kind of argument, chosen because the first could be met with a performance and the second could not. The verse says the man was "confounded" (fa-buhita) — the dictionary sense is a stunned silence impossible to answer, and the Qur'an stops there instead of supplying the reply.

**The Confounded King**

The narrative ends without a conversion. The silence is part of the Qur'an's method: it presents the encounter as a demonstration for the reader's benefit rather than as a story with a satisfying ending. The same ruler-type appears again in 79:24 with Pharaoh's "I am your lord most high," and in both cases the Book's interest is in the internal logic of power claims.

The verse closes with "God does not guide the wrongdoing people," a formula that is often misread as arbitrary. In al-Ṭabarī's reading the clause states a consequence: the one who has bent the truth to his own authority is not in a position to receive the guidance that requires honesty about evidence, and the obstruction precedes the deprivation.

**The Argument from the Order of Nature**

Al-Rāzī stresses that the second argument is a different kind of proof, not a stronger one. The king could stage an execution and call it the granting of life; nobody can stage a sunrise. The Book returns to this order repeatedly: the alternation of night and day is a sign for people who listen (10:67), the sun runs to its appointed course (36:38), and Abraham's own reflection on the rising sun stands in Sūrah al-Anʿām (6:78). The argument is available to every hearer, which is why the Qur'an treats it as the common ground of the discussion with the deniers.

The power claim itself belongs to a theme the sūrah develops elsewhere. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states that God gives sovereignty to whom He wills and takes it from whom He wills (3:26); 2:247 says the same in the matter of Talut; and 4:54 notes that Abraham's own descendants were given a mighty sovereignty. The point of those verses is that authority is a grant rather than a personal property — which is precisely what the ruler of this verse forgot. The verse's closing formula about guidance belongs to the same diagnosis: the person who uses the truth as an instrument of his own standing has put himself where guidance does not reach. The passage then turns to the ruined town, where a different question is asked and answered.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse establishes the pattern by which the sūrah considers power: a claim to kingship, an argument about the divine prerogatives, a sign drawn from the order of creation, and a silence. Al-Rāzī's comment on the whole passage is worth noting: Abraham's argument relied on what everyone could see, and the Qur'an's use of the scene is aimed at the reader — who is invited to choose between the two patrons the previous verse described. The narrative that follows turns from the ruler who could not raise the sun to the man who asked how a ruined city could be raised.
