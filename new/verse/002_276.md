## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:276

> Allah has made interest fruitless and charity fruitful. And Allah does not like any ungrateful evildoer.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Outcomes**

The sentence states that God destroys interest and causes charity to grow, and the community's teachers read the pair as the account of the two paths that the passage has set out. The verse follows the prohibition of the practice and supplies the statement about the outcome that belongs to each of the two dealings.

The wording places the acts together, since the gain that is taken without an exchange and the gift that is offered for the sake of God are presented as two ways of seeking an increase. The Book's answer is that the increase comes from God in the one case and is removed by Him in the other, whatever the appearance of the accounts may be.

The community's scholars noted the connection with the earlier parable of the seed and the harvest, where the same figure of growth is used for the reward of the giver. The passage's treatment of wealth has placed the two subjects beside one another throughout, and the sentence here gathers them into a single statement.

**The Term Used**

The commentators drew attention to the word that the sentence uses for the growth of charity, since it belongs to the same root as the term for the practice that the passage prohibits. The Book sets the two against one another by the vocabulary itself, so that the increase that comes from the law is contrasted with the increase that the practice produces.

The teachers read the choice as one of the passage's deliberate arrangements, and they used it when they explained the subject. The community's scholars described the two forms of increase in their books, so that the difference between the gain of the transaction and the gain of the gift was preserved in the community's teaching.

The sentence in this place therefore explains what the passage has already shown in its parables. The Lord who brings the harvest from the seed is the One who blights the practice, and the two acts of the sentence belong to the same authority.

**The Blighting Stated**

The first clause has been explained by the community's commentators as the statement that the practice removes the blessing from the wealth that is acquired by it. The teachers described the manner in which the loss appears, whether in the property itself, in the affairs of the person, or in the account that is kept for the life to come.

The reports of the community preserve sayings about the practice that describe its outcome, and one of them states that the person who engages in it meets a loss whatever the increase that the accounts of the world show. The teachers used the material when they explained the sentence.

The wording also answers the argument of those who measured the matter by the returns that the practice produced. The Book presents the outcome as the decision of the One who owns the wealth, so that the apparent gain of the practice is not the measure of its result.

**The Growth of Charity**

The second clause states that God causes the charity to grow, and the community's scholars explained the phrase as the description of the reward that is kept for the giver. The earlier passage has compared the gift to the seed that produces seven ears and to the garden that yields its fruit twice over, and the sentence here supplies the summary of those figures.

The teachers drew from the clause the lesson that the increase of the gift belongs to the act that the law approves, so that the community's generosity is not a loss but an investment in the account of the person who gives. The community's preachers used the phrase when they encouraged the believers whose means were modest.

The wording also places the growth in the hand of God, so that the person who gives does not look to the people for the return. The sentence in this place completes the account of the two dealings, and the passage's instruction about the wealth of the community continues with the verses that follow.

**The Attribute at the Close**

The sentence closes with the statement that God does not love any sinful ingrate, and the community's teachers read the pair of descriptions as the account of the disposition that the practice produces. The word rendered as ungrateful carries the sense of a person who denies the favour he has received.

The teachers drew attention to the connection between the practice and the denial of the One who owns the wealth, since the person who takes the gain for the deferment alone has placed his dealings outside the instructions of the law. The community's scholars used the clause when they spoke about the conduct that the passage corrects.

The wording also supplies the answer to the claim that the two practices are alike, since the sentence describes the outcome of each and names the character that belongs to the first. The Book's method of joining its rulings to the description of the persons addressed appears throughout the passage.

**The Place in the Passage**

The sentence stands at the head of the verses that complete the treatment of the practice, and the community's teachers read the arrangement as the passage's summary before its instructions. The Book has set out the judgment in the previous verse, and the sentence here states the two outcomes and the character of the persons who are addressed.

The community's scholars observed that the passage on charity has closed with the description of the givers and that the passage on the practice opens with the account of its result. The two sections were read together when the community taught the subject of wealth.

The verses that follow continue the treatment by commanding the believers to give up what remains of the practice, so that the sentence and the command belong to a single instruction. The community's teachers read them as one body of material about the community's dealings.

**The Teaching Drawn**

The community's teachers drew from the sentence the lesson that the wealth of a person is not measured by the increase it shows in the accounts of the world. The material shows the formula repeated in their exhortations, since the practice was woven into the dealings of the city and the prohibition required a change in the habits of its people.

The scholars of the community also used the sentence when they explained the difference between the increase that the law approves and the increase it forbids. The two words of the verse supplied them with the distinction, and the community's preachers returned to it in their teaching of the passage.

The sentence therefore supplies the principle that the community applied to the transactions of its members, and the passages that follow supply the commands that the principle required. The reader is given the two outcomes and the character of the persons whom the sentence names.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the blighting of the practice and the growth of charity, the contrast that the vocabulary of the two clauses carries, the outcome that belongs to each kind of dealing, and the standing of the sinful ingrate before God. The community's teachers used the verse when they taught the dealings of the believers, and the sūrah's following verses command the community to give up what remains of the practice, so that the sentence stands as the statement of the two outcomes that the commands complete.
