## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:276

> Allah has made interest fruitless and charity fruitful. And Allah does not like any ungrateful evildoer.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Verbs**

The shortest verse of the ribā-block is its thesis-statement, and it runs on two verbs set in deliberate opposition: God *blights* ribā and *causes charity to grow*. The first verb, maḥq, is the wiping-out, the effacement — the word for a decrease that is not subtraction but annulment, the barakah's removal leaving the amount intact and the substance gone. The second verb is where the verse hides its finest device, and the commentators mark it immediately: the form translated *makes fruitful* is ar-rabā — the verbal root of ribā itself. The increase-word the devourers used for their practice is used by the verse for its opposite: God's increasing belongs to the charity, and what the ribā calls increase is, in God's arithmetic, the blight. The single shared root does the whole argument — the two systems both claim to make wealth grow, and the verse concedes the verb to one of them and strips it from the other. Ribā is not increased wealth; it is maḥq wearing increase's vocabulary. Charity is not decreased wealth; it is the true rabā, the growth the word was always describing.

**The Blight's Operation**

The commentators read the blighting across both registers the sūrah has established. In the visible register, the ribā-estate carries the devourer's curse inside its own structure: the compounding that swallowed the debtor's possessions (3:130's doubling and redoubling) builds a wealth whose preservation requires the debtor's ruin, and the tradition's Prophetic saying quoted at the next verse states the visible end — ribā, even when it swells, ends in loss. In the unseen register — the one the section's parables have trained the reader to read — the blight is the dust-on-rock effect at the scale of an estate: amounts that looked like soil over substance, stripped bare by the downpour of the reckoning (2:264). The two registers converge in the parable-language the sūrah already supplied: the grain sown in charity multiplies to seven hundred (2:261), the garden on the height doubles under rain (2:265), and the orchard of a lifetime burns in an hour (2:266) — the ribā-wealth is the orchard whose fire was lit at its planting, and the verse's maḥq names the burning as already underway, invisible to the ledger and total in the fact.

**The Growth's Operation**

The causing-to-grow answers the blight point for point, and the section's own imagery supplies the mechanics. The date-given-in-charity is taken into God's hand and nurtured like a foal until it stands like a mountain — the ḥadīth the garden-verse's commentary carried; the single grain returns seven hundredfold and God multiplies beyond for whom He wills (2:261). The growth is not metaphorical in the tradition's insistence: the charity's increase is a real accretion in the only ledger that survives, deposited with the Lord (2:262, 2:274) where the maḥq cannot reach it. The verse thus restates the two preceding verses' eschatology as economics: the devourers' rising in convulsion (2:275) is the blight made visible at the standing, and the givers' no-fear-no-grief (2:274) is the growth made visible at the same hour. The sūrah of the Byzantines states the same opposition with the same verbs' logic — the interest seeking increase in people's wealth does not increase with God, while the charity seeking God's pleasure is multiplied (30:39) — and the two verses together are the Qur'an's complete rebuttal of the devourers' slogan: they claimed sale and ribā were one thing, and the answer is that ribā and charity are the two things, the false increase and the true.

**The Ungrateful Evildoer**

The verse's second sentence names the person the blight describes: *Allah does not like any ungrateful evildoer* — kaffār, the intensive of ingratitude-unbelief, paired with athīm, the intensive of sin. The double intensity is precise to the ribā-case, and the commentators draw out both halves. The ingratitude: the devourer's wealth arrived as provision — min mā razaqnākum, from what We have provided you, the giving-section's standing premise (2:254, 2:267) — and ribā is the provision turned against its own logic, the gift from God used as an instrument against God's other servants; the kaffār-name says the practice is not merely unlawful but unthankful at the root. The sin: the athīm-name carries the harm's ledger — the debtor's swallowed estate, the household's convulsion, the wrong the Farewell-formula abolished (neither wronging nor being wronged). The love-negation is the verse's relational verdict: the God who blights and grows is described here in His disposition — no love for this combination of names — and the disposition explains the two verbs' direction: the blighting and the growing are not mechanical but personal, the economy of a Lord who loves the grateful giver and does not love the ungrateful devourer.

**Why the Thesis Stands Alone**

The verse's placement between the devourers' portrait (2:275) and the believers' portrait (2:277) gives it the function of a fulcrum, and its brevity is the fulcrum's requirement. What precedes is the case of the ribā: its image, its slogan, its amnesty, its residency. What follows is the case of the community: belief, righteous deeds, the prayer kept, the alms paid, the reward deposited, the fear and grief removed. Between the two cases the sūrah needs one sentence stating the principle both are instances of — that God presides over two opposite arithmetic operations, effacement and increase, and assigns each to one of the two economies — and the sentence is this verse. The commentators' observation that the section's legislation (the commands to the believers in the next three verses) presupposes the thesis is exact: leave what remains of ribā (2:278) is a command that only makes sense to someone who believes the blight is real, and the respite-and-charity upgrade (2:280) is an invitation only attractive to someone who believes the growth is real. The one-line verse is therefore the block's axiom, and the axioms of divine economics, the Qur'an's structure suggests, fit in one line: two verbs, one root, two names of what a person can be.
