## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:24

> And ˹other˺ faces will be gloomy,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Face, and the Word for It**

The sūrah gives the two classes in the briefest possible form: faces radiant, and here, faces *bāsira*. The root of the second adjective is the one used for a contracted, scowling face — the face a person makes when something has gone badly wrong and cannot be undone. The two words are placed two verses apart with nothing in between, and the effect is a single image held up twice: the same humanity, divided by what shows on the face. Nothing is said about which group is larger. The Qur'an's interest here is not in census figures but in the fact that the division is real, visible, and permanent.

**Two Images: Dust and Darkness**

Other passages supply the detail that this verse abbreviates, and they use two different pictures. One is dust: *“while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty, cast in gloom”* (80:40–41). Dust is what covers a person who has been dragged, and in the Qur'an's idiom it is the mark of defeat and disgrace. The other picture is darkness: *“Humiliation will cover them—with no one to protect them from Allah—as if their faces were covered with patches of the night’s deep darkness.”* (10:27). Darkness is the loss of light rather than the addition of filth. Together the two images say that this face has both lost what lit it and acquired what disfigures it, and neither is applied from outside. The defilement and the darkening are what the person has become. A third passage adds the posture of the same people once the sentence is carried out: *“And whoever comes with an evil deed will be hurled face-first into the Fire. Are you rewarded except for what you used to do?”* (27:90). The face leads, and the question attached to it rules out complaint. What is happening to these faces is what they did, returned to them in a form they can no longer edit.

**What the Face Registers**

A face on that Day is registering a verdict, and the Qur'an is consistent about which verdict it is. These are people who had standing — tribe, wealth, followers, reputation — and none of it has been recognised. *“And all faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. And those burdened with wrongdoing will be in loss.”* (20:111). The humiliation is not an additional punishment imposed on top of a sentence; it is the sentence, seen from the inside. A person who built his worth out of things that do not survive discovers, in a single moment and in public, that he has nothing to stand on, and the discovery is legible on his face before a word is spoken.

**Why the Face and Not the Body**

The Qur'an could have divided humanity by destination, by deed, or by the record placed in the right or the left hand — and elsewhere it does all of these. The choice of the face here is deliberate, because in Arabic usage the face is the part of a person that turns toward something. It is the organ of attention: what you face is what you are for. A life spent turned toward the hasty world produces a face that, when the world is gone, has nothing left to turn to. A life spent turned toward God produces a face that is at last looking at what it had been looking toward. The division in these verses is therefore not arbitrary. It is the visible form of the orientation a person lived by. The Qur'an states the same principle in the language of light and sight elsewhere: the blind and the seeing are not equal, nor are darkness and light, nor shade and heat, nor the dead and the living (35:19–22). Those pairings are not poetry about two groups of people. They are a claim about what a life does to the person living it, and the face is where the result becomes visible.

**The Faces of This Life Cannot Be Managed There**

One of the functions of status in this world is to control what other people see. A man can surround himself with people who will not contradict him, buy the appearance of composure, and ensure that his account of himself is the only one in circulation. Every one of those arrangements depends on an audience that can be managed, and the removal of that audience is part of what the Day does. *“On that Day all will follow the caller ˹for assembly˺, ˹and˺ none will dare to deviate. All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate.”* (20:108). No crowd is available to be worked, and no face can be held in an expression it does not have. Pleading is removed as well: *“Then the disbelievers will neither be allowed to plead nor appease ˹their Lord˺.”* (16:84), and the witnesses who could have spoken for them are speaking against them (4:41). The management of appearances was never going to survive an occasion designed to end it, and the Qur'an is careful to say so in advance.

**A Warning Delivered in Advance**

The verse is a description of a future scene, and it is also the sūrah's most compact warning. The faces being described are not yet fixed, which is the entire reason for telling people about them now. The man who is reading this has a face that is still being formed by what he turns toward, and the formation is not complete until the breath stops. That is why the Qur'an pairs every description of these two classes with practical instruction somewhere nearby, and why the sūrah will follow this pair with a scene at a deathbed rather than with more scenery. The division is decided somewhere ordinary, in a life that looks like any other.
