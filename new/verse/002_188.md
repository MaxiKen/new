## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:188

> Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly, nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others’ property, knowing that it is a sin.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wealth Gets Its Food-Law**

The verse arrives between the fast-legislation and the war-legislation, and its position is its argument: after the month that trains the body to refuse lawful food at forbidden hours, the sūrah legislates the permanent refusal — wealth that may never be consumed at any hour, by anyone, however lawfully it is served. The prohibition's first clause is general and mutual: consume not one another's wealth *by the false* — the word *bāṭil* covering every mode of taking that is void, wrongful, or deceptive: theft and fraud, usury and gambling, false contracts and withheld wages, the whole economy of taking what the taking does not entitle. The mutuality matters: *between you* makes the property of every member of the community sacred to every other, and the verse thereby completes the food-legislation it follows — 2:168–169 commanded eating of the lawful and the good and forbade the steps of Satan; this verse extends the same lawfulness from the plate to the purse. The fast trained appetite's obedience for thirty days; this verse legislates appetite's obedience for wealth, where the hunger is less honest and the temptation better dressed.

**Nor Offer It to the Judges**

The second clause narrows to a specific machinery: nor proffer it to the judges — literally, nor throw your wealth to the rulers, so that you may consume a portion of people's wealth in sin, knowingly. The verb's image is of the bribe as something flung toward the bench: wealth deployed not as payment for goods but as ammunition aimed at justice itself. The crime the clause describes is two-staged and both stages are indicted. The first stage is the private injustice — the claim to a portion of another's property that the claimant knows he does not own. The second is the instrumentalization of the court: the bribe exists to convert the unjust claim into a legal victory, to make the machinery of right adjudicate a wrong. The verse's final words — *and you know* — fix the culpability at its maximum: this is not error or disputed entitlement but knowing sin, the briber fully aware that what he seeks from the judge is another's property. Al-Qurṭubī's gloss draws the clause's sharpest edge: even if a judge sincerely decides in the briber's favor, the ruling is not lawful for him, because he knows it to be false in fact — the courtroom's verdict cannot launder a claim its claimant knows to be void.

**The Ruling That Cannot Make Lawful**

From that gloss grows the verse's central legal-moral doctrine: the separation of legal title from moral right. A court decides on evidence and procedure; it can be deceived, out-lawyered, or bought; and when it is, its judgment transfers possession but not entitlement. The tradition's proof-text is the report al-Bukhārī preserves from Umm Salamah (2680): the Prophet said — *I am only a human being, and people bring their disputes to me; some of you may be more eloquent in argument than others, so I may judge in favor of the one whose eloquence prevails. If I give someone what is in truth another's right, I am only giving him a piece of the Fire — let him not take it.* The hadith universalizes the verse's case: the judge is human, the eloquent litigant may win unjustly, and the prophetic verdict converts the ill-gotten award into its substance — fire, the same conversion 2:174 applied to the concealers' gain and 4:10 to the devourers of orphans' property. The briber of 2:188 has purchased exactly this: a favorable ruling that is, before God, a coal. The verse and the report together establish that the law's outer verdict and the soul's inner standing run on separate tracks, and the bribe is the attempt — always futile on the second track — to make the first serve the second's crimes.

**The Corruption of the Instrument**

The clause indicts more than the briber, and the tradition read it that way. A judicial system that can be proffered wealth is a system whose officers are for sale, and the Qur'an's legislation against bribery has its counterpart in its legislation of judges — the sūrah of the Table's command to judge by what God has sent down and its portrait of those who judge by other than it (5:44–45), the sūrah's own instruction that disputes be brought to God and the Messenger rather than managed privately. The bribe's social damage exceeds its private crime: each purchased verdict teaches the community that verdicts are purchasable, and the teaching destroys the instrument that 2:178–179 had just made the alternative to the feud. The retaliation-law replaced blood-vendetta with courts; the courts exist only while their judgments are trusted to track justice rather than money. The briber therefore commits a double betrayal — of the neighbor whose portion he covets and of the system whose integrity his neighbors' lives depend on — and the verse's placement immediately after the qiṣāṣ and bequest legislation is exact: blood regulated, wills regulated, and now the regulator itself, the court, fenced against the wealth that would capture it.

**The Economy of the Fast**

The verse's adjacency to Ramadan is not incidental, and the commentators felt the connection. The fasting month trains the believer in abstention from the lawful — food, drink, intimacy — for a bounded time, for God; this verse commands abstention from the unlawful — other people's wealth — for all time, for the same God. The pedagogy runs from the easier refusal to the harder: if the body can be taught that a morsel permitted at sunset is forbidden at noon because God said so, the hand can be taught that wealth obtainable by bribe is forbidden always because God said so. The *and you know* of the verse addresses the same faculty the fast disciplines — the knowing appetite, the desire that understands perfectly well what it is doing — and the Ramadan just legislated is its annual school. The tradition reinforced the link by requiring the fasting person to fast from false speech and wrongful action as well as food, and by warning that the hunger of the one who does not abandon falsehood gains him nothing with God. The verse stands, then, as the fast's civil code: the month ends, the abstention continues — the plate returned to lawfulness at the ʿīd, the purse never opened to the false.

**Knowing as the Aggravator**

The last words of the verse deserve final weight, because they mark its difference from the errors of property the law also governs. Islamic jurisprudence legislates extensively for honest dispute — contested inheritances, unclear contract terms, mistaken boundaries — and provides courts precisely for cases where knowledge is genuinely divided. This verse is not addressed to those cases. Its target is the taker who *knows*: the claimant aware that the portion is another's, the briber certain his eloquence is purchasing what he has no right to. The knowledge clause strips the crime's usual camouflage — the self-narratives of entitlement, the *it's complicated*, the *technically I'm owed* — and leaves the act naked: a knowing hand, reaching past a known line, using the law's own instruments to do it. The same clause explains the verse's severity of framing: sins of ignorance are the ones repentance most readily reaches, and the sūrah has legislated repentance generously (2:160, 2:182); the knowing consumption of another's wealth is the sin committed with the conscience fully briefed, and its only remedy is the piece of Fire's return — restoring the portion before the verdict of the higher court, where no eloquence prevails and every ruling tracks the truth.
