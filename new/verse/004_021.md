## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:21

> And how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately and she has taken from you a firm commitment?

**Expanded Commentary**

**How Could You Take It Back**

The question *wa kayfa taʾkhudhūnahu wa qad afḍā baʿḍukum ilā baʿḍ* — and how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately — makes retrieval feel unthinkable. The verb *afḍā* — to go through, to open fully — was explained as the complete privacy of marital intimacy, where two lives have intertwined without barrier. That intimacy is not a metaphor; it is the shared vulnerability of a household where bodies, secrets, and daily matters are known. The Qur’an asks how a person who has known that closeness could then turn and demand back a gift that sealed it, as though the shared history could be undone by reclaiming the *qinṭār* mentioned in the verse before (4:20). The question is rhetorical but also practical. Intimacy creates knowledge — the husband knows his wife as no other does — and that knowledge should generate honour, not a strategy for retrieval. To take back the dowry after *ifḍāʾ* is to treat what was most private as if it were a transaction that can be reversed, which the verse presents as morally incoherent.

**A Firm Commitment She Has Taken From You**

The second fact *wa qad akhadhna minkum mīthāqan ghalīẓā* — and she has taken from you a firm commitment — reframes the dowry as part of a covenant, not a payment for a service. *Mīthāq ghalīẓ* — firm, weighty covenant — is language the Qur’an uses for the bond God took from the prophets and from the Children of Israel, a solemn pledge that is not set aside lightly. To say the woman has taken such a commitment from the man places responsibility on him to stand by his word. The gift that accompanied that word — the *ṣaduqah* with *niḥlah* (4:4) — shares its weight. It was given to mark sincerity at entry; to demand it back at exit undermines the sincerity it marked. Commentators noted that the firmness of the commitment comes from several elements together: the proposal and acceptance, the witnesses, the dowry itself, and the life lived thereafter. Once that covenant has been040 entered and lived, the wealth transferred under it acquires a permanence that even a later wish to replace the wife (4:20) cannot dissolve. The question how could you therefore points to the covenant as witness against retrieval.

**Intimacy and Covenant Together Close the Door**

The verse places two facts side by side to close every justification. *Afḍā* answers the thought that the marriage was brief or formal and therefore the gift can be reclaimed; even a short shared intimacy creates a bond whose gift is not retrievable. *Mīthāq ghalīẓ* answers the thought that vows are light and that a heap of gold deserves reconsideration when circumstances change; the pledge was weighty from the start and remains so. Together they show that the dowry is not a deposit held against performance but a settled transfer that belongs to the woman whether the marriage continues or ends. The sūrah has guarded that transfer from the beginning: given graciously (4:4), not to be reclaimed by pressuring the wife to ransom herself (4:19), and not to be taken even when the husband wishes to replace his wife (4:20). This verse completes the guard by giving it a human and a legal reason. The human reason is shared life; the legal reason is a firm covenant. Both say the same thing: what was given freely under a weighty pledge stays with the one to whom it was given.

**Why the Question Form Teaches More Than a Prohibition**

A direct prohibition — do not take it — would have sufficed legally, as the previous verse gave (4:20). The shift to a question — how could you — moves from law to conscience. It asks the husband to imagine the conversation where he asks for the heap back after months or years of *ifḍāʾ* and after a *mīthāq ghalīẓ* was accepted in front of witnesses. The scene itself exposes the request as shameful without needing to add a new penalty. Elsewhere the sūrah pairs law with an invitation to reflect on what is disliked possibly holding abundant good (4:19); here it pairs law with an invitation to feel the incoherence of demanding back a pledge gift after intimacy. That method is consistent with the sūrah’s earlier pedagogy of asking the guardian to imagine his own weak children left behind (4:9) before handling an orphan’s wealth. Law becomes durable when the person sees why it is right, not only that it is commanded. By leaving the question open, the verse lets the husband answer it himself and, in answering no, strengthen his resolve to leave the dowry where it is.

**The Gift That Marked Truthfulness Remains True**

The root of *ṣaduqah* — truthfulness — helps answer the question. A dowry given as *ṣaduqah* was a sign that the husband’s word was sincere at the time. To reclaim it after intimacy and covenant is to act as though that sincerity can be withdrawn retroactively. The Qur’an treats truthfulness as a quality that, once shown in a covenant, should not be contradicted by later advantage. Other passages describe marital life as *“dwelling in tranquility”* with affection and mercy placed between spouses (30:21), and the same tranquility is injured when wealth given to mark truth is seized back. The verse therefore does more than settle a financial rule; it protects the meaning of the sign. A community that learns to leave the *qinṭār* where it was placed learns to mean what it pledged. That habit extends beyond marriage to every firm commitment the sūrah governs — witnesses at handover (4:6), limits in inheritance (4:13–14), and trade by mutual consent (4:29). In each, the pledge and the lived history make return or seizure incoherent. Here the pledge is the firmest named — *mīthāq ghalīẓ* — and the history is the closest known — *afḍā* — so the incoherence of taking back is made most visible.

**Leaving What Was Given Where It Was Given**

The practical outcome is simple: the dowry stays with the woman, whether she was given little or a heap, whether the marriage continues or a new one is desired. The reasoning that stays with that outcome is humane: you have already been close beyond what can be undone, and you have already bound yourself with a weighty word she holds. To ask for the gift back is to ask to keep the closeness while erasing its sign, and to keep the covenant while retrieving its pledge, which cannot be done without *buhtān* — slander — and *ithm mubīn* — clear sin (4:20). The verse leaves the husband with that picture and with no further step to take. It does not add witnesses or a new procedure, because the question itself is sufficient to hold the line. A person who hears *kayfa* — how — and remembers both *ifḍāʾ* and *mīthāq ghalīẓ* will find the answer in the memory of his own home: he cannot, with honour, ask for it back, and so he leaves it where mercy first placed it.
