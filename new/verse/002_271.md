## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:271

> To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Ways Stated**

The sentence permits the charity that is given openly and rates the gift that is given in secret to the poor above it, and the community's teachers read the pair as the statement that the Book judges the act by the intention behind it. The passage has named the display that ruins a gift, and the sentence here sets out the two manners that the community may choose.

The wording places both acts within the good, so that the public gift is not condemned. The scholars noted the point, since the sentence protects the community's visible giving while preferring the manner that is hidden from it.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere commends the concealment of the gift, and the community's teachers gathered the passages when they set out the subject. The sentence in this place supplies the comparison that the community applied.

**The Open Gift**

The commentators explained the first clause as the statement that the community's visible charity serves the life of the city, since the poor learn where help may be found and the wealthy are reminded of their duty. The teachers described the encouragement that such giving provides to those who see it.

The community's scholars also noted the offices that the Book has established among the believers, where the public collection and distribution of alms belongs to the conduct of the community. The sentence's permission covers the appointed charity as well as the free gift.

The reading preserved in the community connects the clause with the report that ranks the open performance of the obligatory alms above the same act done in secret, and the teachers set out the measure when they explained the subject. The sentence in this place states the general principle, and the reports supply the application.

**The Secret Gift**

The second clause rates the gift that is hidden and given to the poor as better for the giver, and the commentators explained the preference in the light of the danger that the public act carries. The teachers described the temptation that accompanies the visible gift, since the giver may come to value the notice of his neighbours above the acceptance of the act.

The community's scholars drew attention to the phrase about the poor, since the sentence describes the gift that reaches the person in need without the embarrassment of the encounter. The Book's concern for the dignity of the recipient runs through the passage, and the hidden gift preserves it.

The teachers also described the practice of the early community, where the giver placed his gift in the hand of a person who did not know its source. The material shows the manner in which the sentence was applied, and the community preserved the accounts of those who gave in that way.

**The Expiations Named**

The sentence states that the secret gift absolves the giver of some of his evil deeds, and the community's scholars explained the clause as the statement that the good acts remove the faults of the person who performs them. The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere states that good deeds take away those that are evil, and the passages were read together by the commentators.

The teachers noted that the sentence places the removal of the faults among the benefits of the hidden gift, so that the act serves the person's own account as well as the need of the recipient. The community's preachers used the clause when they encouraged those whose past conduct troubled them.

The reports of the community describe the saying that a good deed should follow an evil one so that the fault may be erased, and the teachers gathered the material when they explained the sentence. The clause in this place supplies the Book's own statement of the matter.

**The Reports and the Ranking**

The reports preserve the explanation transmitted from one of the early authorities, which distinguishes the obligatory alms given openly from the free gift given in secret and rates each according to its kind. The teachers used the ranking when they answered the community's questions about the two manners, and the material shows the care with which the subject was handled.

The commentators also connected the sentence with the passages about sincerity that appear in the Book, and they drew from the whole the principle that the intention of the giver determines the value of the act. The community's scholars built their teaching of charity upon that ground.

The saying that the left hand should not know what the right hand gives was repeated in the community's instruction, and the teachers used it when they explained the preference of the sentence. The material shows the manner in which the Book's comparisons were received.

**The Attribute at the Close**

The verse closes with the statement that God is aware of what the community does, and the community's scholars read the clause as the ground of the comparison the sentence has drawn. The attribute places the public gift and the hidden one within the same knowledge, so that nothing of the matter escapes the record.

The teachers drew from the closing the lesson that the difference between the two manners lies in the state of the giver rather than in the perception of God. The community's preachers used the clause when they spoke about the display that ruins the act, since the One who sees is not impressed by what the neighbours observe.

The Book's practice of ending its instructions with a description of God appears throughout the passage, and the community's scholars read the arrangement as the statement that the whole of the community's conduct belongs to the record. The sentence therefore leaves the community with the comparison and with the knowledge that accompanies it.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the goodness of the open gift, the preference of the gift that is given in secret to the poor, the removal of the faults that accompanies it, and the awareness of God that closes the instruction. The community's teachers used the verse when they taught the manners of charity and when they ranked the practices of the wealthy, and the sūrah's next verses turn to the guidance that belongs to God, so that the treatment of the gift continues with the statement about the One who accepts it.
