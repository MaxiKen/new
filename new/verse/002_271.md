## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:271

> To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ranking**

The verse legislates the visibility of giving, and its structure is a ranked permission rather than a command: disclosure is good — ḥasan, affirmed, rewarded — and concealment given to the poor is better, and carries an absolution the public gift is not promised. The ranking does not denigrate the open hand; the commentators are explicit that public charity remains a genuine good, and the verse's better operates inside a scale whose lower rung is already approved. The two further conditions of the better case are noted by the exegetes: the hidden giving is specified as *to the poor* — the concealed gift aimed at real need, not at general benefaction — and its advantage is stated twice, once as betterness for the giver and once as the absolution of sins. The verse thus completes the section's movement inward: from what is given (the good things, 2:267), through what follows the giving (nothing injurious, 2:262–264), to where the giving is performed — in the open, lawfully, or in the secret, better. The interior direction of the whole section arrives here at its legislative point: the audience is itself a variable in the deed's worth.

**The Left Hand**

The tradition's commentary on this verse is dominated by one image. Many aḥādīth speak of the virtue of secret giving, Ibn Kathīr notes, often in variations of the saying: let the left hand not know what the right hand has given. The formula's most famous setting is the ḥadīth of the seven whom God shades under His shade on the Day when there is no shade but His — the just ruler, the youth raised in worship, the heart attached to the mosques, two who love each other for God's sake, the man tempted by rank and beauty who says I fear God, the one whose eyes flow in private remembrance, and the seventh: the man who gives charity so concealed that his left hand does not know what his right hand has spent (in the two Ṣaḥīḥs). The image's hyperbole is its instruction: the concealment that satisfies the standard is not merely from the neighbors but from the self's own record-keeping — the giving so unadvertised internally that the left side of the body is uninformed. The verse's better-for-you is measured against this image: every degree of the gift's privacy is a degree of the shade's proximity, because every degree removes an audience the deed could have been performed for.

**The Arithmetic of Ibn ʿAbbās**

The commentators preserve a quantified reading attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās that assigns the ranking its institutional nuance: for obligatory acts — the zakāt, the institutional alms — open performance is twenty-five times better; for voluntary, supererogatory giving, the private is seventy times better. The two ratios legislate two different goods. The open zakāt is public for reasons al-Rāzī lists: it sets the example the community imitates, it shows the poor that recourse exists among their neighbors, and it builds the open bond of provision between a society's members — the assessment's visibility is part of its justice, since a tax unseen is a tax unaudited. The secret nāfilah is private for the opposite reason: it is the sincerity-test, the giving that garners no prestige, where the only possible motive is the motive itself. The verse's ruling and the ratios agree in structure: the obligatory has a public function the voluntary does not, and the voluntary has an interior function the obligatory partly surrenders to administration. The juristic tradition runs on the same distinction — the zakāt paid publicly as norm, the ṣadaqah of the night as excellence — and the verse is the legislation both sides cite.

**The Absolution**

The betterness is then given its specific yield: concealment *will absolve you of your sins*. The commentators connect the verb to the expiation-vocabulary — kaffārah, the covering-and-effacing by which good deeds atone for evil ones — and quote the tradition's summaries: charity puts out sin as water puts out fire (the saying al-Ṭabarī records); and the Prophetic instruction, when you have done an evil deed, follow it with a good deed and it will wipe it away. The Qur'anic parallel is the sūrah of Hūd's axiom — *good deeds wipe out evil deeds* (11:114) — and the verse here assigns the wiping a specific location: the hidden gift. The commentators' logic connects the concealment to the absolution through the deed's purity: the sin-effacing power of charity runs at the charity's sincerity, and sincerity runs at secrecy — the gift with no audience has no alloy, and the unalloyed deed is the one the fire-quenching metaphor describes. The tradition's exchange preserved by al-Ṭabarī extends the absolution's scale: asked whether the remembrance of lā ilāha illa'Llāh counts among the good deeds that wipe, the Prophet answered that it is the best of good deeds — the covering-merit attaching to every unalloyed act of the tongue, the purse, and the heart, with the hidden charity among its purest forms.

**The Reading That Speaks First-Person**

Al-Ṭabarī records a variant that changes the absolution's speaker: in place of the third-person *it will absolve you*, some read the first-person plural — *and We will absolve you of some of your sins* — the promise arriving in God's own grammatical person. The variant is minor in text and major in register: the absolution becomes a speech-act of the Absolver, the deed's effect restated as the Giver's commitment. The commentators preserve both readings without adjudicating, and the section's theology keeps both true: the hidden charity effaces by its nature (the water-fire physics of the tradition) and effaces by promise (the covenant-language of the verse). The first-person reading also sharpens the verse's closing name: the All-Aware who sees the concealed gift is the We who absolves for it — the same divine attention operating as witness and as payer. For the giver the two readings carry one instruction: the concealment is not a loss of credit but a transfer of creditor — the audience foregone is replaced by a Recipient whose awareness the verse's final clause names and whose absolution, on the stronger reading, He pronounces Himself.

**All-Aware of What You Do**

The closing name is khabīr — the All-Aware, the expert-acquainted — the same name that sealed the widow's legislation (2:234) and the intercession-verse's interior (2:235), and its assignment here is exact. The hidden charity is, by construction, outside every human record: no witness, no receipt, no recipient's public gratitude, in the fullest form no left hand informed. The verse's ranking could therefore be read as a loss — the better deed is the one nobody can confirm occurred — and the closing name is the answer: the deed's confirmation exists, and its location is the divine awareness. Khubr carries the acquaintance-with-particulars that the concealment maximizes: the Aware knows the gift's amount, its recipient, the hour of its passing, and the intention's exact weather. The section's whole architecture closes on this name deliberately: the parables ran on what God does with unseen seeds (2:261), the annulments ran on what God records of following speech (2:264), the ledger-line stated that every instrument is known (2:270) — and the ranking-verse completes the system by making the unseen not merely recorded but preferred. The giver is left with the section's central inversion fully legislated: the visibility that makes a gift worth having in this world is the visibility that discounts it in the next, and the awareness that sees the concealment is the only audience whose seeing the verse calls good.
