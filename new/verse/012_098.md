## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:98

> He said, “I will pray to my Lord for your forgiveness. He ˹alone˺ is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise of Intercession**

Jacob agrees, and the agreement is stated in the future tense: I will seek forgiveness for you from my Lord. The verse records a commitment to pray rather than a prayer performed on the spot, and the distinction has been discussed by the commentators at length. What is promised is an act of intercession directed to God, made by a father to sons who deceived him about the death of a child and whose deception cost him his sight. The chapter gives the promise in a single clause and attaches no condition to it — no requirement of restitution, no probation, no demand that the confession be repeated or made public. The absence of conditions distinguishes this from a negotiated peace. Jacob asks his sons to return nothing, to make no statement and to demonstrate no change over time; he says only that he will ask his Lord to forgive them. The chapter reports the promise in a clause and attaches nothing to it, which is how it treats forgiveness everywhere else in the sūrah.

**The Delay Recorded in the Tradition**

The commentators report that Jacob deferred his prayer for a period of time, and they offer a reason: he wished to establish whether the brothers were sincere and truthful in what they had asked. The report is not in the Qur'anic text, and the verse as it stands contains only a future tense. The tradition's interest in the delay reflects a genuine question about the scene — why a father would promise rather than perform — and the answer given is a pastoral one rather than a punitive one: verification, not revenge. The Qur'an's wording is compatible with the report and does not require it, and the chapter's own emphasis falls on the promise and on the two divine names that close it.

**"My Lord"**

The phrase is Jacob's habitual address and it appears at the moments of his greatest strain. He complained of his anguish and sorrow only to God (12:86); he described God as the best of keepers when refusing to trust his sons with Benjamin (12:64); he declared beautiful patience twice after losing a child (12:18, 12:83). The intercession promised here is made to the same Lord, in the same terms, by the same man. The chapter's portrait of Jacob is built from these short addresses, and the consistency is the point: the person who complained only to God is the person who prays for the people who caused the complaint. That continuity is the verse's quiet argument. The same address appears where Jacob redirects his grief and here, where he undertakes to intercede for the people who caused it. The chapter places both uses of the phrase in his mouth and lets the reader see that a complaint carried to God and a prayer offered for an offender belong to a single practice rather than to two temperaments.

**"The All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful"**

The two names close the verse, and their pairing is one the chapter has already used. Joseph ended his statement about the soul that commands evil with the same two names: *Surely my Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful* (12:53). Father and son both conclude a difficult speech with an assertion about God's capacity to forgive, and in both cases the assertion concerns offences they did not commit. The names are not a formula appended to a promise. They are the ground of the promise: a father can undertake to pray for the forgiveness of a deception because the One being prayed to is defined by forgiving. The two names are chosen for the situation rather than for their splendour. A deception sustained across decades requires a forgiveness that covers a long offence, and a household whose father was blinded by grief requires a mercy reaching the consequences as well as the act. Jacob names precisely the attributes the request depends on, and names them as the ground of his promise rather than as a hope.

**Both Injured Parties Have Now Prayed**

Joseph said: may God forgive you, and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful (12:92). Jacob says: I will pray to my Lord for your forgiveness, and He is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The two prayers cover the two halves of the offence and are recorded in adjacent scenes. The chapter's account of reconciliation is therefore complete before the family is physically reunited. Verse 99 reports the arrival in Egypt and verse 100 the prostration, but by the end of verse 98 nothing remains outstanding between the brothers and the people they wronged. The reunion that follows is the confirmation of a settlement already reached, not its occasion. The settlement is therefore complete before the family meets. Joseph has forgiven, the brothers have confessed twice, and Jacob has undertaken to ask God's forgiveness for them; nothing is outstanding when the household arrives in Egypt. The reunion that follows is not the moment the family is reconciled but the moment a reconciliation already achieved becomes visible to everyone in it.

**The Verse Ends the Family's Moral Arc**

From verse 8 to verse 98 the chapter has traced a single movement: envy, plot, disposal, false report, decades of grief, a second loss, a search, a disclosure, an admission, a forgiveness, a request and a promise of intercession. Verse 98 is the last of these, and after it the narrative has no further moral business to conduct. What remains is reunion, recognition of a dream, a migration and a prayer. The sūrah closes its ethical sequence here, in Canaan, in a sentence of twelve words, before the family has entered Egypt. The placement means that when the brothers appear in verse 100 they appear as forgiven men rather than as petitioners. That status is what the closing scenes require. Men still under accusation could not stand at a throne, prostrate, and hear a brother say there is no blame on you today; forgiven men can, and the chapter prepares the ending by settling the moral question three verses before it arrives. What remains after this verse is geography rather than ethics.

**Intercession Is Asked of a Father, Not of a King**

The brothers could have asked Joseph to pray for them. He had already done so in verse 92, and his standing with God is described throughout the chapter in terms of knowledge taught, mercy conferred and reward preserved. They ask their father instead, and the chapter records the choice without comment. What it reflects is the shape of the offence: the deception was directed at Jacob, the grief was his, and the intercession properly belongs to him. Joseph's role in the reconciliation was to waive his own injury and to explain the larger arrangement (12:100). Jacob's role is to pray. The two roles are not interchangeable, and the chapter assigns each to the person it belongs to.

**A Promise Made by the Man Who Was Deceived**

The verse is worth reading against verse 18, where Jacob answered the same sons with beautiful patience and the remark that God's help must be sought against what they described. He did not promise prayer then. The intervening eighty verses have produced a disclosure, a healing, a confession and a migration, and the promise arrives at the end of them rather than the beginning. What changed is not the offence, which was fully known to Jacob in verse 18, but the offenders. Men who lied with a bloodied shirt and men who ask their father to intercede for them are not the same men, and the chapter's decision to place the promise in verse 98 rather than verse 18 is its account of that difference. The prayer is given to people who have asked for it.
