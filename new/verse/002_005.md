## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:5

> It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict on Everything Just Said**

The sūrah's opening movement closes with a pronoun. Five attributes have been listed across two verses — belief in the unseen, prayer, giving, acceptance of every revelation, certainty about the end — and now all of them are gathered into the single word *those*, and a verdict is pronounced on the group. Nothing new is added. The verse exists to say what the preceding description amounts to.

The demonstrative is again the far one, *ulāʾika*, the same pointer used at the head of verse 2. The repetition frames the passage. The Book is pointed at from a distance because of what it is; the people are pointed at from a distance because of who they are. Distance in this sūrah's opening is consistently the language of honour, applied to revelation and to its recipients alike.

**"Upon" Guidance, Not Merely With It**

The Arabic says they are *upon* guidance from their Lord, and the preposition is doing more than grammar. Guidance is presented as something a person is mounted on or established upon — a position held rather than an object possessed — and its origin is stated in the same clause: it is *from their Lord*.

Two consequences follow, and both are argued throughout the Quran. The first is that guidance is not self-generated. The Prophet is told plainly that he does not control it: *You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills* (28:56). The second is that the people described here did not arrive by their own reckoning. The Quran states the same dependence as a prayer on the lips of the steadfast: *Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us* (3:8). Being upon guidance is a position that can be lost, which is why the request to be kept there is one of the few petitions the Quran puts in the mouths of the already-guided.

**Success as a Harvest**

The last word is *al-mufliḥūn*, from a root that means to split — specifically to split the earth in ploughing. The related noun *fallāḥ* is a farmer. Success in the Quran's vocabulary is therefore closer to a harvest than to a victory: something that takes a season, grows out of ground that was prepared, and is gathered at the end.

That sense explains the shape of the word's use. The Quran does not call anyone successful for a single good outcome. It reserves the term for people whose whole course has borne out: *Successful indeed are the believers* (23:1); *Successful indeed are those who purify themselves* (87:14); *Successful indeed is the one who purifies their soul* (91:9). The definition is stable across fourteen centuries of commentary because the metaphor is in the word: the successful person is the one whose life produced what it was planted for.

The same word is applied to the ordinary business of a life, which is what makes the harvest image exact rather than decorative. The Quran describes people as trading, buying, and selling throughout, and it uses the vocabulary of profit and loss for the result: some trades are called *profitless* and their holders are left with nothing to show for the season (2:16). Success and failure in this vocabulary are not moments of triumph or disaster but the yield of a whole field, which is why the Quran can describe a man's entire career in a single commercial sentence and call the outcome what it is.

**Cause First, Outcome Second**

The sentence has two halves and they are ordered: guidance, then success. The Quran keeps that order everywhere it joins them, and the reason is not stylistic. Guidance is the condition; success is what it yields. Where the two are separated, both fail.

The contrast the Quran draws makes the point from the other side. Its opponents are not described as unfortunate; they are described with this exact word negated: *Oh, indeed! The disbelievers will never succeed* (28:82). What they lack is not effort — the sūrah will say they bought and sold energetically — but direction. A life that produces nothing at the end is described in commercial language elsewhere in this sūrah, and the terms there are the same ones used here: a trade, a profit, a loss.

**Whose Guidance, and the Dispute About It**

The phrase *from their Lord* has been read in two ways that mattered enough to divide schools. One reading takes *hudā* here as *bayān* — the showing of the way, which God gives to everyone through messengers and signs, so that "their Lord" guided them by making the path clear and they took it. The other takes it as *tawfīq* — the enabling, the creating of willingness in the heart, which God grants to some. The first reading protects human responsibility and the justice of the reckoning; the second protects the plain sense of a sentence that credits the guidance to God rather than to the guided.

Both readings lean on real evidence, and the weighing is closer than either side's partisans allow. The universal address of the Quran — warnings sent to whole cities, to humanity, to jinn as well — supports the first: the call goes out to all. The specific ascriptions support the second: *Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. But whoever He leaves to stray, you will never find for them a guiding mentor* (18:17). The verse under commentary says that the guidance these people stand on came *from* their Lord, and it says this about the reverent specifically rather than about people generally. On the evidence of this sentence alone, the second reading has the stronger claim.

**What the Opening Has Built**

Five verses have established a great deal in a short space: a Book whose reality is asserted in the very letters it is made of, a claim about doubt, a condition placed on benefit, and a description of the people who meet that condition in five particulars. Everything that follows in the sūrah — its laws, its arguments with the People of the Book, its narratives, its rulings on marriage, trade, war, and inheritance — is addressed to that description.

The method of the sūrah, visible from its first movement, is to place two groups side by side and let the reader see what each produces. That pairing is the sūrah's structure for its full length: the reverent and the defiant, the believers and the hypocrites, those who trade and those who refuse the trade, each described in the same terms so that the comparison does the work of the argument.

The five verses also function as a preface in the strict sense. A reader who takes them as a statement of whom the sūrah is for will read its longest and most legal passages differently from one who takes them as decoration: the rulings that follow are addressed to people who already accept an unseen God and an audit, and they are unintelligible as bare commands to anyone who does not. The sūrah turns to the opposite case in the verse that follows.
