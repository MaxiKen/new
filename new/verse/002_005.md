## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:5

> It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pronoun That Closes a Portrait**

*Ulāʾika*, rendered here as *it is they*, is the demonstrative Arabic uses to deliver a verdict. The sūrah has spent three verses listing four marks — belief in the unseen, established prayer, giving, and acceptance of both revelations along with the Hereafter — and then closes the list with a single sentence that assigns the outcome. The verdict is God's, and it is stated before any of the sūrah's opponents have been presented.

The same construction appears throughout the Book in both directions. It pronounces on the guided here, and it pronounces on the losers elsewhere: *“Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.”* (63:9). The device is symmetrical. The Qur'an uses identical grammar to identify the two ends of the same road, which means the sentence is a decision rather than a compliment.

**Guidance Traced Back to Its Source**

The verse does not say the believers are guided; it says they are guided *by their Lord*. The phrase names the source of the guidance rather than the quality of the guided, and it makes the sentence a statement about God before it is a statement about people. The Qur'an uses the same construction whenever it explains who finds the road: *“He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path”* (2:142), and of the Book itself, *“Ramaḍân is the month in which the Quran was revealed as a guide for humanity”* (2:185).

Attribution to God also rules out a reading in which guidance is self-generated. The people described in the previous verses have behaved in specific ways, but the sentence does not reward their behaviour with a promotion; it says they are guided *by their Lord*, which means the road they are walking was opened for them. The marks listed in 2:3–4 are the shape their guidance takes, not the price they paid for it.

**Success Is Cultivation, Not Escape**

The word rendered successful, *mufliḥūn*, comes from a root associated with farming. Its sense is thriving, ripening, producing; Arabic uses the same root for the farmer who breaks ground. The Qur'an applies it to those who have brought something to harvest, and it keeps the word close to the action that produced it: *“Successful indeed are the believers: / those who humble themselves in prayer”* (23:1–2), and *“Successful indeed are those who purify themselves”* (87:14).

That etymology matters for how the sentence is read. Success in this vocabulary is not escape from a disaster or a verdict delivered at the end; it is what a life yields when it is worked properly. The community that prays, gives and believes is described as a crop that comes in, and the future tense of the second clause places the harvest at the end of the process rather than at its beginning.

**Two Clauses, Two Times**

The sentence divides into a present standing and a future outcome. The first clause speaks in a nominal sentence: the demonstrative and the participle describe a condition that is true now. The second clause announces what those people will be. The structure is one of the Qur'an's most common arrangements, and it holds a practical balance: guidance is available in the present, and its result is delivered later.

Nothing in the verse offers a schedule or a guarantee of visible reward in this world. The Book makes the same distinction elsewhere and warns against reading prosperity as a verdict: comfort in this life often accompanies rejection, while the reward of the patient is held back. The two clauses of this verse correspond to the two worlds, and the sentence that names success does so in the future because the harvest has not yet been brought in.

**Why the Pronoun Is Repeated**

Arabic could have said they are guided and successful in a single clause. Instead the demonstrative is repeated with a separate pronoun: it is *they*, and again it is *they*. The construction restricts the statement to the group just described and excludes anyone else from the claim. It answers in advance the kind of claim that the sūrah will shortly put in the mouths of others.

That exclusivity is a burden and a protection at once. It means the marks in the preceding verses are not one path among many, and it also means the verdict is not open to revision by the crowd. The Book has already used the same emphatic construction for guidance in the Fātiḥah; here it uses it to attach a specific future to a specific way of living, and it leaves the reader to decide which clause he is standing under.

**A Verdict With No Inherited Qualification**

None of the four marks in the preceding verses can be inherited. A person cannot inherit belief in the unseen, a maintained prayer, generosity from his own means, or certainty about the Hereafter; each one is done by the person who does it. The verdict in this verse therefore ignores the criteria that usually determined standing in the society the sūrah was addressing, where tribe, descent and wealth decided most questions.

That is the point of the demonstrative. The Book identifies its successful people by what they do rather than by who they come from, and it does so before it turns to the people who claim a place in the community without accepting its obligations. The sentence that follows this one begins the sūrah's second portrait, and the contrast rests on the plain fact that the first group's identity was entirely a matter of practice.
