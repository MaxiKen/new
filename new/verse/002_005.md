## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:5

> It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Pointers and One Restriction**

The verse pronounces its verdict with unusual grammatical weight, and the weight is the meaning. It opens with *ulāʾika* — "those," "it is they" — a demonstrative of distance that Arabic rhetoric uses to elevate: pointing at people as one points at something raised on a platform. Each of the two clauses then locks in a restriction. "It is *they* who are truly guided" fronts the pronoun so that guidance is confined to the described group — they, and not the claimants who will follow in the sūrah's next thirteen verses. "It is *they* who will be successful" repeats the lock on a second prize. The doubled structure is not ornament; it is exclusion by syntax. The same sūrah will describe disbelievers and hypocrites at length, and none of them receives either pointer. The opening portrait of al-Baqarah ends, therefore, not with a summary but with a verdict sealed twice — once over guidance, once over success — and the seal is grammatical.

**Falāḥ: Success With Roots in a Plowed Field**

"Successful" renders *mufliḥūn*, from *falāḥ*, and the root's memory is agricultural: to plow, to cleave the earth, to farm. The *fallāḥ* is the cultivator, and *falāḥ* is what cultivation eventuates in — the harvest at the end of long labor. The etymology colors every Quranic use of the word. Success in this vocabulary is not luck and not seizure; it is the yield of a field that was broken open, seeded, watered, and guarded through seasons. Read back into the verse, the metaphor quietly recapitulates verses 3–4: the plowing was taqwā, the seeding was faith in the unseen, the seasons were prayer and donation. The harvest named here belongs to that labor and to nothing else. The Qur'an's choice of an agricultural word for the soul's ultimate attainment is exact — farming is the human activity that most visibly combines effort with dependence, where the worker does everything and the increase comes from elsewhere.

**The Word the Adhān Carries Into the Street**

*Falāḥ* is not confined to scripture; it is shouted into Muslim streets five times a day. The call to prayer, after the testimony, rings out *ḥayya ʿalā al-ṣalāh, ḥayya ʿalā al-falāḥ* — come to prayer, come to success — welding the word to the act the previous verses described. The adhān's origin is fixed in history: when the Madinan community gathered for prayer by guesswork, some proposed a Christian bell, others a Jewish horn, and ʿUmar suggested a human caller; the Prophet ﷺ then ordered Bilāl to rise and call (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 604). Every recitation of al-Baqarah's opening thus has an echo in daily sound: the mosque announces in the same vocabulary that success is reached through prayer. The verse and the adhān mutually interpret each other — the call is the verse in the imperative mood, and the verse is the call with its grammar explained.

**Not Rescue but Arrival**

Translators sometimes render *falāḥ* as "salvation," and the substitution imports a frame the word does not carry. Classical usage does not sort humanity into saved and damned as its first division; it sorts the thriving from the lost, the arrived from the failed. *Falāḥ* is attainment of the good a thing was made for — in this world and in the next — rather than rescue from a verdict. The difference shows in what the word promises. Rescue is negative: escape from danger. Arrival is positive: possession of the harvest, nearness to the Provider, the settled state of one whose labor bore fruit. The Qur'an spreads the term across both horizons; the believer prospers in his soul here and inherits the Garden there. Reading verse 5 with this frame keeps its two clauses in proportion: guidance is the arriving life, success is the arrived one.

**Guidance They Ride, Not Guidance They Made**

The middle phrase deserves exact attention: they are *upon* guidance *from* their Lord — *ʿalā hudan min rabbihim*. The preposition "upon" pictures guidance as a mount or a vehicle the travelers ride; it is under them and carrying them, not something they manufactured or own. The source is then named: *from their Lord* — using *rabb*, the Lord who nurtures and manages, the same title that headed al-Fātiḥah's second verse. Possession is carefully refused. The guided do not hold their guidance as property; they receive it as provision, continuously, the way the field receives rain. The echo of the Qur'an's opening prayer is exact — the petition *"Guide us along the Straight Path"* (1:6) is answered here in the declarative: the God-conscious are the people that petition describes in motion. Verse 5 of al-Baqarah can thus be read as the first fulfillment of al-Fātiḥah, the Book showing what an answered prayer looks like from outside.

**Description, Then Verdict: The Method of the Opening**

The five-verse unit now displays its whole shape. Verse 2 names the Book and the posture it requires; verses 3–4 describe the people in four qualities; verse 5 judges them. The sequence — describe, then seal — is the Qur'an's characteristic method, and it teaches by contrast: when the disbelievers and the hypocrites appear next, they too will be described before any verdict falls, and their portraits will end in loss rather than *falāḥ*. The double reward in this verse also distributes across time: guidance is granted now, in this life, as a state the traveler inhabits; success is promised at the end, as an arrival. Neither is earned as wages — both are pronounced over people whose defining act was belief in what they could not see. The constitution of the community is complete before al-Baqarah turns to its histories and its laws, and everything after verse 5 addresses the people this verdict has defined.
