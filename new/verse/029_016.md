## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:16

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said to his people, “Worship Allah, and fear Him. This is better for you, if only you knew.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Summons Built From Two Words**

The address opens with two imperatives that cover the whole of religious life and then gives a reason for obeying them. The first is the command to worship, which in Arabic carries the sense of submitting to what one acknowledges as worthy of submission. The second bids the hearer to guard himself, to place himself behind a barrier of caution with respect to God. Together the two words describe the internal and the behavioural sides of one posture: the heart's allegiance and the conduct that follows from it. Abraham does not begin with a catalogue of his people's mistakes. He begins with what he wants them to do, and his first sentence is short enough to be memorized by a child. This is the Book's usual method in these prophetic addresses, and it is the opposite of the polemical opening a reader might expect from a man surrounded by idols.

**"Better for You," Said to People Who Disagree**

The reason attached to the command is a claim about benefit rather than an appeal to raw authority. Abraham tells his people that worship and guarding themselves are better for them, and the qualifier at the end — if only you knew — locates the obstacle in their knowledge rather than in his argument. The Book uses this idiom when it wants the hearer to weigh an option rather than flinch from a threat, and it uses it about worship specifically: believers are told to eat from the good things they have been provided and to give thanks to God if it is truly Him they worship (2:172). The reasoning is that the creature who lives in dependence on a provider is already in a relationship, and the command is an invitation to stop pretending otherwise. What Abraham offers his people is not a demand for surrender but a description of the arrangement they are already in.

**Abraham as the Book's Standing Witness**

The Qur'an returns to Abraham more than to any other figure of the ancient world, and it uses him as the proof that the new message is not a novelty. Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but a man of pure submission who was never a polytheist (3:67). The Prophet is commanded to follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who did not associate partners with God (16:123), and when the followers of other scriptures press their claims, the answer given is that the community follows the faith of Abraham instead (2:135). The Book even asks who could reject the faith of Abraham except a fool, noting that God chose him in this life and that he will be among the righteous in the next (2:130). Placing Abraham in this sūrah, immediately after the account of Noah's long effort, makes him the second witness to a single pattern: the tested prophet who stands alone against the customs of his town.

**How Abraham Argued**

The content of Abraham's reasoning appears across several chapters and always follows the same method. He is shown the wonders of the heavens and the earth so that his certainty can be complete (6:75), and he turns his face to the One who originated the heavens and the earth, upright and not one of the polytheists (6:79). He asks his father why he worships what can neither hear nor see nor benefit him at all (19:42), and the Book gives the same challenge in the mouth of the Prophet: how can anyone worship what can neither harm nor benefit him, when God is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing (5:76)? The argument is from evidence and from plain capacity. Objects that cannot perceive their worshippers cannot be relied on by them, and Abraham asks his people to notice the mismatch between what they call upon and what a god would have to be. The invitation to worship is therefore followed, later in the conversation, by a demonstration.

**What the Verse Hands to the Preacher**

Every generation of callers inherits the problem Abraham faced: how to speak to people who consider their inherited practice unremarkable. This verse supplies the model. Begin with the alternative rather than the accusation; state what you are asking and who is being asked; give a reason that a hearer can test; and leave the ignorance of the listener on the table as something to be cured rather than something to be punished. The chapter will shortly record how the town answered, and the answer will be an offer to burn the man alive. The Book reports that response without surprise, and it takes care, before it reports it, to show that the call itself was patient, reasonable, and brief. Noah spoke for nine hundred years; Abraham needed one sentence to state his case, and both were working under the same command.

**What the Summons Cost Abraham's Hearers**

A town that organizes its calendar, its trade, and its standing around a shrine is not being asked to change a private opinion when a prophet tells it to worship God alone; it is being asked to accept a loss. Abraham's reasoning in this verse anticipates that, which is why he attaches a claim about benefit to a command that appears, on the face of it, to be a demand. The Book elsewhere records the resentment such a change produced, and it answers those who would reject the faith of Abraham by asking what kind of person would do so and noting that God chose him in this life and that he will be among the righteous in the next (2:130). The argument from benefit and the argument from honour are two ways of saying that what the town was being asked to surrender was already worth less than what it was being offered.

**The Two Duties That Remain One**

The two imperatives of this verse are not two religions, and the Book treats them as a single obligation. Worship without caution becomes ritual with no consequence; caution without worship becomes fear with no direction. Abraham holds them together and then adds the word *better*, which assumes that the hearer is a person who can compare outcomes and act on the comparison. Everything that follows in the sūrah — the idols that provide nothing, the fire that cannot consume him, the migration he undertakes — is the working out of this one sentence in the life of the man who spoke it. The Book tells the story to a community under pressure, and its first instruction to them, delivered in the voice of their ancestor, remains the sentence that starts everything.
