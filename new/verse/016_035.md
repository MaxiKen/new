## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:35

> **The polytheists argue, “Had Allah willed, neither we nor our forefathers would have worshipped anything other than Him, nor prohibited anything without His command.” So did those before them. Is not the messengers’ duty only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Using Divine Will As Excuse For Shirk**

Wa-qāla al-ladhīna ashrakū law shā'a Allāhu mā ʿabadnā min dūnihi min shay'in naḥnu wa-lā ābā'unā wa-lā ḥarramnā min dūnihi min shay' — and those who associate partners said, had Allah willed, we would not have worshipped anything besides Him, neither we nor our fathers, nor would we have prohibited anything besides Him. This is argument of polytheists who claim that since Allah allowed them to worship idols and prohibit certain animals as ḥarām, therefore Allah wills and approves their shirk and prohibitions. Al-Ṭabarī explains that they argued: if Allah did not like our worship of idols and our prohibiting cattle, He would have prevented us, so since He did not prevent, He approves. This is misuse of divine will to justify disobedience. Al-Rāzī notes that they confused divine will that allows human choice with divine approval. Allah wills to allow humans to choose, but does not approve shirk. The same argument appears in 6:148: *Those who associate will say, had Allah willed, we would not have associated nor would our fathers, nor would we have prohibited anything* (6:148). The Qur'an answers that this argument was used by those before them and is invalid.

**Our Forefathers And Prohibitions**

Naḥnu wa-lā ābā'unā, we nor our forefathers, indicates that they appealed to tradition: our fathers did shirk, so it must be acceptable, and if Allah willed otherwise, He would have stopped our fathers. Wa-lā ḥarramnā min dūnihi min shay', nor would we have prohibited anything besides Him, refers to their practice of prohibiting certain cattle as ḥarām for idols, as 6:139-144 describes: they prohibited certain camels, cattle, saying this is for idols. They claimed that since Allah allowed them to prohibit, He approves prohibition. Al-Qurṭubī explains that they made unlawful what Allah made lawful, like certain cattle, and claimed it was by Allah's command, when in fact it was by their own invention, as 16:116 will say *Do not say what your tongues falsely describe, this is lawful and this is forbidden, inventing lie against Allah* (16:116). Their argument uses divine will to justify both shirk in worship and shirk in legislation.

**So Did Those Before Them**

Kadhālika faʿala al-ladhīna min qablihim — so did those before them. Those before them also argued that had Allah willed, they would not have worshipped others, using same excuse. Al-Ṭabarī says previous nations also used divine will as excuse for persistence in shirk. The phrase indicates that this argument is not new; it is old pattern of those who refuse responsibility. Al-Rāzī notes that kadhālika faʿala indicates that their action was same as previous: they associated, prohibited, and used will as excuse. The past precedent shows that argument leads to destruction, as previous nations were destroyed despite using it. Therefore current polytheists should not rely on it.

**Is Messengers' Duty Only To Deliver Clearly?**

Fa-hal ʿalā al-rusuli illā al-balāghu al-mubīn — is not messengers' duty except clear delivery? Balāgh means delivery, conveyance, communication. Mubīn means clear, manifest, clarifying. The verse says messengers' duty is only to deliver message clearly, not to compel guidance or prevent shirk by force. Al-Ṭabarī explains that messengers are not responsible for guiding all; they are responsible for conveying clearly, and they have done so. The excuse *had Allah willed we would not have worshipped others* is answered by saying messengers have delivered clear message that Allah does not approve shirk, so you cannot claim you did not know. The duty of clear delivery has been fulfilled, so responsibility now lies with listeners. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that al-balāghu al-mubīn includes both clarity of content and clarity of evidence, so that no one can say message was unclear.

**Why This Argument Is Invalid**

The argument *had Allah willed we would not have worshipped others* is invalid for several reasons. First, divine will that allows choice does not equal divine approval. Allah allows humans to choose disbelief, but does not approve it, as 39:7 says *If you disbelieve, indeed Allah is free from need of you, and He does not approve disbelief for His servants* (39:7). Second, if argument were valid, it would justify all sins: had Allah willed, we would not have stolen, killed, etc., which would eliminate moral responsibility. Third, Allah has willed to show straight way and to send messengers to clarify, so excuse that He did not will guidance is false: He did will to show way, as verse 9 said *upon Allah is to show straight way*. Fourth, they themselves do not consistently apply argument: they do not excuse theft against themselves by saying had Allah willed thief would not have stolen; they demand justice. So they apply determinism selectively to excuse own shirk.

**The Connection To Guidance And Will**

Verse 9 said upon Allah to show straight way, and had He willed He would have guided all. Verse 35 shows polytheists misusing that idea: if He could guide all, why did He not guide us, so our shirk must be His will. The answer is that He willed to allow choice and to send messengers with clear delivery. Guidance has been shown, messengers have delivered, so responsibility remains. The verse thus clarifies that divine will to allow choice does not remove human accountability. The messengers' duty is clear delivery, which has been done, so excuse fails.

**Linguistic Note On Law Shā'a And Balāgh**

Law shā'a, had He willed, from sh-y-ʾ, to will, wish. Mā ʿabadnā min dūnihi min shay', we would not have worshipped anything besides Him, with min shay' indicating anything at all. Ḥarramnā from ḥ-r-m, to prohibit, make unlawful. Kadhālika faʿala al-ladhīna min qablihim, so did those before them, same as verse 33. Fa-hal ʿalā al-rusuli illā al-balāghu al-mubīn, is not upon messengers except clear delivery, with hal for interrogative expecting negative: is there anything upon messengers except clear delivery? No, only clear delivery. Rusul plural of rasūl, messenger. Balāgh from b-l-gh, to reach, deliver. Mubīn from b-y-n, to be clear, manifest. The verse's structure — polytheists' argument, past precedent, messengers' duty — moves from false excuse to historical pattern to clarification of responsibility, guiding listener to reject excuse and accept clear message.
