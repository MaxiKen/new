## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:1

> Ḥâ-Mĩm.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter That Opens With a Sound, Not a Statement**

The first thing a reciter pronounces in this sūrah is not a sentence to be argued over but a pair of letters: ḥāʾ and mīm. Twenty-nine sūrahs of the Qur'an begin in this way, and the community had a name for the group early — al-muqaṭṭaʿāt, the cut-off letters. A book that works hard everywhere to be understood opens seven of its chapters with a sound that no dictionary defines. That belongs to the revelation, not to a reciter's taste. The Book itself sorts such verses into a class of their own, standing beside the precise verses that carry the rulings: “He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive.” (3:7). A scripture holding only what a reader can master at first hearing would be a manual. This one is a sign, and signs include what stops the hand. The reader's first act here is not interpretation but reception.

**The Sixth of the Ḥawāmīm**

Sūrahs forty to forty-six stand together in the order of the Book: Ghāfir, Fuṣṣilat, al-Shūrā, al-Zukhruf, al-Dukhān, al-Jāthiyah, al-Aḥqāf. Six of the seven open with ḥāʾ mīm; al-Shūrā adds three more letters before its formula closes. The tradition calls the group the ḥawāmīm, and the closeness of the family is not only a matter of spelling. These chapters return to the same arguments — the oneness of God against the many gods of the Quraysh, the ruin of nations that dismissed their warners, the defended authority of the Prophet, and a Resurrection that the comfortable found easy to deny. Al-Jāthiyah sits in the middle of the block in tone as well as in position. Its sentences are short, its cases are put quickly, and it does not linger over an image or a story. What a longer sūrah unfolds across twenty verses, this one states once and leaves standing. The letters shared with its neighbours say only that the same Voice is speaking; the argument that follows is this chapter's own.

**Why the Commentators Left the Letters Alone**

Early explanations of these letters fall into three lines, each with a reason behind it. One line reads them as abbreviations of words or names: a contracted reference to God, to the pen, or to something the text does not spell out. Another takes them as oaths, since the Qur'an swears elsewhere by created things and by the Book itself. A third, and the one carried by most of the classical commentaries, holds the meaning to be known to God alone and stops there. The abbreviations require a key that no sound report supplies; the oath reading assumes a grammatical pattern that is absent when the letters stand by themselves. The third line claims nothing that cannot be checked, and it matches the Book's own division of its verses into the precise and the elusive. Of the elusive, the verse continues, the well-grounded in knowledge say only, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” (3:7). Declining to guess is here a position, not a failure of nerve.

**An Alphabet as the Ground of the Challenge**

The letters are the raw material of Arabic speech, which is why they can carry an argument. The Book that uses them issues a standing demand: “And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.” (2:23). Nothing in that challenge withholds a tool. The opponents of the Prophet had the same alphabet, the same metres, the same market of eloquence in which reputations were made and destroyed, and the same language — none of it in short supply. Muslim scholars later built the doctrine of iʿjāz, the inimitability of the Qur'an, on this ground: the demand is total, and the means to meet it are common property. A chapter opening with two letters is therefore not hiding a message; it is laying the materials on the table. What is claimed is not secret knowledge but an effect that no combination of the available parts has produced. The smallest unit of the language stands at the head of the largest claim made for it.

**From Letters to Names to Signs**

The opening line sets a movement that the verses after it complete. Two bare letters appear first; then those letters give way to names — the Almighty, the All-Wise; then the sky and the earth are called signs. Speech grows from its smallest part to its greatest subject in three steps, and it never announces the ladder it is climbing. The same two names return at the chapter's close, where “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” (45:37) seals a chapter that began with ḥāʾ and mīm, so that one pair of words frames everything between them. The subject is placed first on purpose. Before the chapter tells anyone what to believe about the Resurrection, the Reckoning, or the Law, it establishes who is speaking. A reader who takes the letters as noise loses the first rung of that ascent. A reader who treats them as an unexplained sound stands exactly where the chapter wants him: listening to a Speaker whose authority is accepted before a single proposition has been proved.

**Reading a Sign Requires More Than Decoding**

A sign works by pointing, and a reader can hold a pointer without following it. The letters are the purest case of a sign whose whole function is to refer: they are undeniably present in the Book, undeniably recited, and they carry no claim that a translator can move into another language. That makes them a test of the reader's posture rather than his skill. The Qur'an draws attention elsewhere to people who read a text and miss it, who hear recitation and remain untouched, and it never treats this as a shortage of intelligence; it treats it as a decision about what to do with what lies in front of you. Two letters at the head of a chapter teach what the sun in the sky and the body of the reader himself teach equally well. None of these facts forces belief. Each of them, taken seriously, leaves a person facing a decision he can no longer pretend he has postponed.
