## Sūrah an-Nūr 24:61

> There is no restriction on the blind, or the disabled, or the sick. Nor on yourselves if you eat from your homes, or the homes of your fathers, or your mothers, or your brothers, or your sisters, or your paternal uncles, or your paternal aunts, or your maternal uncles, or your maternal aunts, or from the homes in your trust, or ˹the homes of˺ your friends. There is no blame on you eating together or separately. However, when you enter houses, greet one another with a greeting ˹of peace˺ from Allah, blessed and good. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will understand.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dismantling Social Stigmas Around Disability: laysa ʿalā al-aʿmā ḥaraj**

The revelation intervenes with profound compassion to reform dining etiquette and eradicate social discrimination against the vulnerable: *laysa ʿalā al-aʿmā ḥarajun wa-lā ʿalā al-aʿraji ḥarajun wa-lā ʿalā al-marīḍi ḥaraj*. In pre-Islamic Arabia, and among various religious factions in Madinah, individuals afflicted with physical disabilities—such as blindness, lameness, or chronic illness—were subjected to painful social ostracization.

People felt aversion toward sharing food from a single platter with disabled individuals, fearing contagion or finding their eating habits unappealing. Simultaneously, conscientious disabled individuals frequently withdrew in humiliation, fearing they might inadvertently take more than their share due to impaired sight or mobility. The Qur'an decisively shatters these superstitious stigmas and social anxieties. By declaring that there is no restriction (*ḥaraj*) upon the blind, the lame, or the sick, divine law integrates them fully into the communal table, affirming that physical disability carries zero moral defect or spiritual impurity.

**The Web of Kinship and Communal Table Fellowship**

The verse systematically maps out an expansive network of domestic kinship where food may be shared freely without formal invitations: *wa-lā ʿalā anfusikum an taʾkulū min buyūtikum aw buyūti ābāʾikum aw buyūti ummahātikum*. The text enumerates eleven categories of households: parents, siblings, paternal and maternal uncles and aunts, homes held in trust, and intimate friends.

In traditional tribal societies, extreme legalism often led individuals to obsess over whether taking a piece of fruit or bread from a brother's or uncle's kitchen constituted illicit consumption of another's property (*akl amwāl al-nās bi-al-bāṭil*). The Qur'an relaxes this neurotic anxiety, establishing that genuine family bonds and close friendships rest upon mutual affection, generosity, and implicit consent (*al-riḍā al-ʿurfī*). A believer does not need a formal legal contract or explicit permission to eat wholesome food in the home of close relatives, fostering an atmosphere of generous domestic hospitality and familial solidarity.

**Homes Held in Trust and the Bounds of Friendship**

The legislation extends table fellowship to two unique non-kinship categories: *aw mā malaktum mafātiḥah* (homes whose keys you possess in trust) and *aw ṣadīqikum* (or the homes of your friends). The phrase concerning keys refers to caretakers, neighbors, or domestic trustees who have been entrusted with the oversight of an absent person's property, orchard, or residence.

Islamic law permits such a trustee to consume reasonable, customary sustenance from the food stores in their care without committing embezzlement. Furthermore, the inclusion of *ṣadīqikum* elevates authentic friendship into a sacred relationship bordering on kinship. True friendship in Islam is grounded in mutual love for the sake of God, generosity, and shared values. An honorable friend rejoices when their brother enters their home and partakes of their table; to require stiff, formal transactional permission between sincere friends would stifle the spontaneous warmth and fraternal joy that divine law seeks to cultivate.

**Flexibility in Table Fellowship: jamīʿan aw ashtātā**

The divine law resolves another cultural anxiety surrounding the mechanics of dining: *laysa ʿalaykum junāḥun an taʾkulū jamīʿan aw ashtātā*, there is no blame on you eating together or separately. In pre-Islamic Arabia, certain tribes, such as the Banū Layth, maintained rigid superstitions: a man would refuse to eat alone, remaining hungry until a guest or companion arrived to share the platter.

Conversely, other groups felt awkward sharing communal platters with guests of differing appetites or social ranks. The Qur'an strips away both cultural neuroses, granting total behavioral flexibility. While dining collectively (*jamīʿan*) attracts special prophetic blessings (*barakah*) and strengthens social fellowship, eating alone (*ashtātā*) when circumstances require is completely permissible and carries zero religious fault. Divine law accommodates human diversity, liberating daily routines from irrational cultural taboos.

**The Liturgy of Domestic Entry: taḥiyyatan min ʿindi Allāh**

The verse links dining privileges to a mandatory liturgical etiquette upon crossing any threshold: *fa-idhā dakhaltum buyūtan fa-sallimū ʿalā anfusikum taḥiyyatan min ʿindi Allāhi mubārakatan ṭayyibah*. The command to greet "yourselves" (*fa-sallimū ʿalā anfusikum*) conveys that the community of believers is a single spiritual body; greeting your brother is greeting yourself.

Even if a house is empty, classical commentators like Ibn ʿAbbās noted that one should say: *al-salāmu ʿalaynā wa-ʿalā ʿibād Allāh al-ṣāliḥīn* (Peace be upon us and upon the righteous servants of God). The Islamic greeting of peace (*al-Salām*) is not a secular pleasantry; it is a sacred invocation descending from God (*min ʿindi Allāh*), characterized as *mubārakah* (blessed, attracting divine increase) and *ṭayyibah* (wholesome, pleasing to the soul). By initiating every domestic encounter with this divine greeting, the believer sanctifies the space, banishes demonic discord, and establishes an atmosphere of mutual security.

**Cognitive Maturity and the Ethic of Inclusion: laʿallakum taʿqilūn**

The verse concludes with a profound pedagogical seal: *ka-dhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakumu al-āyāti laʿallakum taʿqilūn*, this is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will understand. The verb *taʿqilūn* calls upon the human intellect to grasp the profound ethical coherence of divine law.

True intellectual maturity (*al-ʿaql*) is not measured by abstract philosophical speculation, but by the ability to cultivate an inclusive, compassionate, and dignified community. God clarifies these domestic, dietary, and social ordinances so that humanity may transcend the petty superstitions, social snobbery, and emotional anxieties that routinely poison human interaction. By integrating the disabled, honoring kinship, celebrating sincere friendship, and crowning every home with the greeting of peace, Sūrah an-Nūr constructs a radiant social order where human beings live in mutual dignity beneath divine grace.
