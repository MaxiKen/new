## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:107

> Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Kingdom Behind the Legislation**

The verse arrives as the ground of the ruling before it, and the connection is exact. Verse 106 announced Allah's power to abrogate and replace; verse 107 announces why the announcement needs no further argument: *do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs only to Allah?* Legislation is an attribute of sovereignty — the owner of a domain sets its law — and the domain in question is not a province but the whole: the heavens and the earth, the visible order and the unseen, every jurisdiction in existence gathered under one proprietor. The objection to abrogation, whatever form it wore in Madina — the mockers' charge of fabrication, the objectors' scandal at sequence — presumed a standard above the legislator, some fixed code against which divine rulings could be judged inconsistent. The verse removes the presumption at its root: there is no code above the King, no parliament over the sovereign, no statute limiting the owner's disposal of his own property. What Allah replaces, He replaces inside His own kingdom, by His own right, toward ends His own wisdom holds. The question-form — *do you not know?* — is the recital's device for truths already possessed, and its use here marks the doctrine's status: the kingdom of Allah is not a contested premise to be argued but a known fact to be applied, and the entire abrogation-debate resolves the moment the fact is actually brought to bear.

**From the Prophet to All People**

The exegetes mark a structural feature of the verse that carries interpretive weight: its address begins with the Prophet ﷺ and expands to human beings generally — a shift of addressee the Qur'an performs elsewhere, as in the opening of Sūrah al-Aḥzāb. The singular *do you not know* opens on the Messenger, to whom the knowledge is certain and the application immediate; the plural of the second clause — *you have no guardian or helper besides Allah* — opens the sentence to everyone within earshot. The expansion does real work. The kingdom-doctrine is not only the answer to a polemic about abrogation; addressed to all people, it becomes the description of every person's actual situation: creatures inside a domain wholly owned by another, with no protector and no ally in the ownership's place. The shift converts the verse from argument into address — from the defense of a legislative act into the statement of a universal condition — and the recital's method throughout this sūrah explains the conversion's purpose: the Muslims of Madina, the People of the Book, the polytheists, every later reader all stand in the same relation to the same kingdom, and the verse files that relation in the second person plural so that no audience can receive it as information about someone else.

**No Guardian, No Helper**

The two names the verse negates cover the whole field of recourse, and their pairing is deliberate. The *walī* — guardian, patron, protector — is the one who holds your affairs: who manages, defends, and provides as a matter of standing responsibility, the patron whose clientship is a permanent relation. The *naṣīr* — helper, supporter — is the one who arrives in the contest: the ally at the moment of conflict, the reinforcement when forces are joined. To have neither besides Allah is to be stripped of both the standing protection and the emergency aid that every other source supplies, and the negation's scope — *besides Allah* — leaves the two functions entirely in divine hands. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the same deprivation as the condition of the evil-doer's judgment: whoever commits evil is requited, *and they will find no protector or helper besides Allah* (4:123) — and the verse there does the work this verse's placement does, tying the negation to accountability. The sūrah has been building the patronage-theme across its whole passage: the traders of verse 86 told *nor will they be helped*; the covenant-breakers left with no advocate; the numbered-days claimants without a pledge. Verse 107 states the general law those instances illustrated: patronage beside Allah is a vacancy, and every reliance placed into the vacancy is reliance on nothing. The verse is not, however, a statement of abandonment — it is a statement of monopoly. No guardian *besides Allah* means one guardian, wholly sufficient, whose guardianship is not shared because it is not limited: the same sentence that empties the creature's roster of false patrons installs the true one, unopposed.

**The Kingdom Verses as One Declaration**

The declaration this verse makes is the Qur'an's most repeated, and its repetition is its emphasis. Sūrah al-Māʾidah's confrontation with the claim of divine sonship closes on the same sentence: *to Allah alone belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, and to Him is the final return* (5:18). The sūrah's own verse of the Throne describes the same dominion from its interior — the Footstool extending over the heavens and the earth, their preservation no burden to its Keeper (2:255) — and the pairing of the two verses in this sūrah frames the kingdom-doctrine with its governance: ownership in verse 107, administration in verse 255. The declaration's polemical targets are consistent across the Book: every claim of rival jurisdiction — sonship, partnership, intercession by permission-less intermediaries, the numbered-days tariff set by a community against its own King — and its positive content is equally consistent: the heavens and the earth are not a commons with absentee ownership but a kingdom with an active sovereign whose disposal includes its law. Read in the abrogation-context, the repetition tells the Muslims what their legislation-debate actually was: not a technical question about texts but a live question about the kingdom — whether its owner may govern it in stages, and the answer the repeated declaration gives is that governing in stages is what owners of kingdoms do, from the first ruling to the last replacement, without consulting the property.

**The Knowledge the Question Assumes**

*Do you not know?* — the verse's opening presumes its content is already held, and the presumption locates the verse's pastoral work precisely where the recital's epistemology has placed every failure this passage documents: not in the supply of knowledge but in its application. The kingdom of Allah was not news to the verse's hearers — the People of the Book confessed it in creed, the polytheists confessed a high god above their idols, the Muslims confessed it five times daily — and the verse's question therefore asks not whether the fact is held but whether it is operative. Does the person who knows the kingdom belongs to Allah legislate his own exceptions inside it? Does he seek guardians where the kingdom has none to give? Does he grieve replacements whose author is the King? The recital's diagnosis of the numbered-days claim was exactly this practical unbelief — a creed affirmed and a conduct arranged as though the affirmation were false — and verse 107 hands the same diagnostic to its own community in the form of the doctrine it must apply. The verse's two clauses are the application's two directions: toward Allah, the kingdom is His alone, so every law He sets and resets is within His right and every stage of His legislation is within His wisdom; toward everything else, there is no guardian and no helper, so every reliance placed beside Him is misplaced, and every fear of His disposal is a fear of the only disposal that exists. The knowledge is possessed; the question asks it to govern.
