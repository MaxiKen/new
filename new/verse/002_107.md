## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:107

> Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question of Sovereignty**

The verse continues the argument of the previous sentence by asking about ownership. The heavens and the earth belong to God, and the consequence the verse draws is that no one else can act as a protector or a helper for human beings. The question form assumes that the answer is known and asks the hearer to follow it to its conclusion.

The sūrah has been answering objections about the revelation, and this sentence supplies the foundation for the answers. A community that questioned why a ruling had changed was questioning the authority of the one who legislates, and the verse restates the scope of that authority in its widest terms. **The Meaning of Kingdom Here**

The Arabic term describes both the territory under a ruler and the rule itself, and the Book uses it for God's authority over creation in passages that describe His provision, His judgement, and His sending of the messengers. The phrase in this verse covers the two realms the reader can observe, and the sūrah's later passages extend the same claim to the unseen.

The statement is not a general affirmation of belief in God's power; it is the premise of the argument the verse is making. If the whole of creation belongs to God, then the choices He makes about the revelation, including the replacement of an instruction, are decisions about His own property, and no third party has standing to object.

**No Guardian and No Helper**

The second half of the sentence names two roles that people look for: someone to take charge of their affairs and someone to assist them when they cannot manage alone. The Book uses the pair elsewhere in describing the helplessness of those who rely on anything other than God, and the verse states that both roles belong to Him alone.

In the context of this passage the statement answers a specific hope. The community had been looking for intermediaries whose standing would secure its position, whether through lineage, through a scripture, or through a favoured angel. The verse removes every candidate at once, and it does so without elaborating on the status of any of them. **The Shifting Addressee**

Commentators noted that the verse begins by addressing the Prophet ﷺ and then widens to address people generally, since the question about knowledge in the opening clause is followed by a statement that applies to everyone. Similar shifts occur elsewhere in the Book, and the sūrah uses them when it moves from a particular dispute to a universal principle.

The shift matters for how the verse is read. What begins as a reply to an objection about the revelation becomes a statement of the human condition, and the reader who follows it is included in the second half of the sentence. The Book often handles its arguments this way, moving from the occasion to the principle so that the passage retains its force beyond the dispute that produced it.

**What the Sentence Rules Out**

The verse excludes the possibility that any creature can guarantee a person's security or supply what God has withheld. The Book's later passages describe the day when every connection of kinship, alliance, and patronage will be of no use to those who relied on them, and the vocabulary used there matches the two roles named here.

For the community that had been told its standing was assured, the sentence is a direct answer. No protector outside God can secure it, and no helper can change the terms on which the Hereafter is entered. The sūrah has already stated that the community would never wish for death and has attributed the reluctance to its own record; this verse supplies the reason no other course is available.

**The Two Questions and Their Sequence**

The previous verse ended with a question about God's capability, and this one opens with a question about His ownership. Capability without authority would not settle the objection, and authority without the ability to exercise it would leave the community where it was. The sūrah places the two claims together so that the reader holds both.

That pairing is a feature of the Book's teaching about God's names and attributes, which are usually presented in pairs in the passages that argue with objectors. What the sūrah establishes in these two sentences is a complete answer to the question raised about the revelation, and the verses that follow turn to the believers with a caution about the way they should ask. An argument from power alone would not satisfy the objectors, and a claim of ownership without the means to act on it would leave them where they began. Both claims are given in a single breath so that neither is considered apart from the other.

**A Foundation for What Follows**

The next verse warns the believers against questioning their Messenger in the manner that Moses had been questioned, and the statement about sovereignty here prepares that warning. **Ownership and the Freedom to Legislate**

The claim that the heavens and the earth belong to God carries a consequence the sūrah draws out in the argument about the revelation. If the whole of creation is His property, then the terms on which He addresses His creatures are His to set and to revise, and no community has standing to require that a ruling remain as it was. The Book's teachers used this reasoning when they explained the place of abrogation in the divine legislation, and they placed it beside the statement that God is capable of everything. What the two sentences together establish is that the revelation comes from a source with both the right and the ability to order human affairs as it wills. The verse's second half then removes the hope that some other party might take charge or lend assistance, and it leaves the reader with the sovereignty stated in the first half as the only ground of confidence.
