## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:10

> Allah sets forth an example for the disbelievers: the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot. Each was married to one of Our righteous servants, yet betrayed them. So their husbands were of no benefit to them against Allah whatsoever. Both were told, “Enter the Fire, along with the others!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Women Chosen to Make a Point About Everyone**

The chapter's closing section presents four women, and it opens with the two negative cases. The choice is pointed. Of all the disbelievers in the Qur'an's repertoire — Pharaoh, Abū Lahab, the chiefs of ʿĀd and Thamūd — the example held up for those who reject faith is a pair of women married to prophets. Nothing about their situation suggested that outcome. They lived in prophetic households, heard revelation at first hand, and shared a table with men God calls *“Our righteous servants”* (66:10).

That is exactly why they were selected. An example works by removing excuses, and these two remove the most respectable one available: that a person's religious fate is largely set by their circumstances. These women had the best circumstances imaginable for belief. The verse states the outcome without softening it, and the address is to disbelievers generally rather than to women — the point being made concerns proximity to righteousness, not gender. Placed at the end of a chapter that corrected the Prophet's own wives, the example is unmistakable in its local application and larger than that application in its meaning.

**The Betrayal Was Religious, Not Marital**

*“yet betrayed them”* (66:10) has been misread in a particular direction often enough that the commentators addressed it explicitly, and the classical consensus is firm: the betrayal was not sexual infidelity. No prophet's wife committed adultery. What these two did was work against their husbands' mission from inside their homes. Noah's wife is reported to have told the people that he was mad. Lot's wife is reported to have informed the townsmen when guests arrived at the house — the guests the men of Sodom came to assault.

Read this way, the word *khānatāhumā* describes a breach of trust of the gravest practical kind. Each woman possessed something only a spouse possesses: private knowledge, daily access, and the credibility that comes from being the person closest to the man. Each used it against him. Lot's wife's disclosure in particular converted the household from a refuge into a trap, which is the exact inversion of what a home is supposed to be in a chapter that has just told believers to guard their families from the Fire.

**Marriage to a Prophet Saved Neither of Them**

*“So their husbands were of no benefit to them against Allah whatsoever”* (66:10) states the doctrine the examples exist to establish. The Arabic ends with an absolute negation: nothing at all, in no respect. Two men whose intercession mattered enormously could do nothing for the women in their own houses.

The case of Lot's wife is the more documented of the two, and the Qur'an returns to it repeatedly. When the angels instruct Lot to leave by night, they specify the exception: *“do not let any of you look back, except your wife. She will certainly suffer the fate of the others”* (11:81). The same exclusion appears when he is rescued — *“We saved him and his family except his wife, who was one of the doomed”* (7:83) — and again in the negotiation over Sodom: *“We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed”* (29:32). Noah's wife has no comparable dossier; this verse is the only explicit Qur'anic statement of her betrayal, which is one reason it is cited so often. The rescue of Noah's household likewise excluded a member, and the Book is consistent across both stories: the ark and the escape were extended to a family, and membership in the family was not the qualification.

**A Principle That Cuts Both Ways**

The commentators draw from these two examples a rule stated in both directions: the righteousness of one person does not benefit someone who works corruption, and the corruption of one person does not harm someone who is righteous. The second half of that rule is what the next verse will illustrate with Pharaoh's wife, and the pairing is the reason the chapter presents its examples in this order.

Stated so baldly, the principle is harder than it first sounds. It dissolves the entire economy of inherited religious standing — the assumption that a family, a lineage, a community or a marriage carries its members along with it. The Qur'an is dismantling something that every traditional society runs on, and it does so using the most sympathetic possible cases. These were not women who rejected an obscure message from a stranger; they rejected the men they lived with. If that relationship did not transfer merit, no relationship does. What the individual is left with is a file in their own name, which is the arrangement the chapter's warnings about hearts and repentance have assumed throughout.

**The Sentence Delivered in the Plural**

*“Both were told, ‘Enter the Fire, along with the others!’”* (66:10) ends the verse with a command that contains its own commentary. The two are not given a separate destination reflecting their unusual position; they are sent in *with those who enter*. Whatever distinction their marriages had conferred in the world evaporates at the threshold.

The phrasing answers an unspoken expectation. A reader following the story might assume that a prophet's wife, even a treacherous one, would be handled as a special case — judged more severely because she knew more, or more leniently because of her household. Neither happens. She joins the general body of those entering, sorted by what she did rather than by whom she married. This is the same principle the previous verses applied to the living: excuses are not accepted, requital matches conduct, and the light a person walks by on that Day is the light they arrived with. The chapter now turns to a woman whose household offered her every reason to fail.
