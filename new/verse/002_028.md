## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:28

> How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Put to People Who Already Knew the Answer**

The verse opens with the Arabic interrogative of astonishment rather than of information, and its force depends on its audience. These were not people arguing that the world had no maker; the sūrah has already shown them naming God as the creator of the sky when asked. What the question exposes is the distance between an admission they were happy to make and the loyalty that admission requires, and the Book expresses the gap as disbelief.

The same device appears throughout the Qur'an's arguments with its first audience, and it always works the same way. The sentence does not begin by proving God's existence. It takes the existence as the common ground and asks how a person can hold it while living against it, which is a question about consistency rather than about evidence.

**Lifeless, and Then Alive**

The first stage describes a condition before life, and the Arabic word used is the ordinary word for being dead. Commentators explained it in three ways: existence as material in the earth or in one's ancestors, existence as a thing unremembered, and non-existence before this life altogether. Their explanations agree on the essential point, which is that the human being was not the author of his own beginning and did not bring himself into the world.

The Qur'an states the condition of that stage elsewhere with an image: *“Is there not a period of time when each human is nothing yet worth mentioning?”* (76:1). The comparison in this verse is between a state with no capacity at all and a state of full vitality, and the Book presents the transition as a single act performed by somebody else. What the audience is being asked is not a metaphysical question but the simplest question available: who did that?

**The Two Deaths and the Two Lives**

The verse then runs forward: He will cause you to die, then bring you to life again, and then to Him you will return. The full sequence appears in other passages with the same accounting. Sūrah al-Mu'min records the confession of those who have seen the outcome: *“Our Lord! You made us lifeless twice, and gave us life twice. Now we confess our sins. So is there any way out?”* (40:11). Another verse names the same three events in order without the final return: *“And He is the One Who gave you life, then will cause you to die, and then will bring you back to life.”* (22:66).

The pattern of life, death and life again is one of the Book's most frequently stated teachings, and it is presented as an ordinary exercise of the same power that produced the first life. Sūrah al-Rūm states the principle in nature before it states it about human beings: *“He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And He gives life to the earth after its death. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺.”* (30:19). Resurrection, on this argument, is not a novelty requiring a separate proof. It is the same act performed a second time.

**Why the Argument Runs Through Death**

The sequence is chosen so that death cannot be used as an escape clause. A person can tell himself that the world came into being by processes he does not need to think about, and that the question of what happens after death is beyond verification. The verse makes the two questions one question by placing the giver of life at both ends of the sequence, so that the power which began the story is the power which ends it.

The Book returns to this reasoning when it wants to answer denial of the Hereafter, and it does so with the same vocabulary of giving life and causing death: *“And He is the One Who gives life and causes death.”* (53:44). What the Book does not do is describe the mechanics of the process or offer a timetable. It states that the events are acts of the same agent, and it moves directly to their consequence.

**"To Him You Will Be Returned"**

The final clause names the destination rather than the event. Return, in this Book, carries the sense of arriving back at the source from which one came, and it is used for the whole of humanity without exception. The same sūrah states the theme repeatedly, and one of its most direct statements addresses the reader's own journey: no creature is outside the arrangement.

That clause is where the verse's argument lands, and it explains why the astonishing question at its opening is an argument about responsibility rather than about metaphysics. A person who accepts that his life was given, that it will be taken, and that he will be brought back is not in a position to treat his conduct as a private matter. The verse brings those facts together in a single sentence, and it does not add a threat to them.

**A Question Addressed to the Sūrah's Whole Audience**

The verse follows a passage about covenant-breakers and precedes a description of the earth and the heavens. It belongs to the stretch of the sūrah in which the Book is arguing with everyone rather than describing the three groups from its opening pages, and its question is put to the same audience that was called to worship its Maker two verses earlier. Nothing in it restricts the address to the hypocrites or to the rejecters.

That breadth is consistent with the way the sūrah builds its case. It names what people have received, asks them to account for their response, and then, in the next verse, points at the world around them as further evidence of the same generosity. The argument accumulates, and the astonished question is one step in a case that is being assembled rather than an isolated rebuke.
