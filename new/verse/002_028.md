## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:28

> How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is an Argument**

The verse opens with an interrogative of impossibility: *kayfa takfurūna bi-llāh* — how can you disbelieve in Allah? Not "will you," not "do you dare," but *how* — the question that challenges the mechanics rather than the audacity of a position. The implication is that disbelief, examined against the believer's own biography, becomes incoherent: not wicked, which it also is, but unperformable with open eyes. And the argument the question unfolds is not cosmological or scriptural but autobiographical — it is built entirely from things that happened *to the addressee*. You were dead. You were given life. You will die. You will live again. You will be returned. Every clause is a stage of the reader's own record, known by direct experience or by the experience of every person he has ever lost. The verse thereby relocates the debate about God from the sky to the self: the evidence is not out there at a distance to be assessed; the evidence is the assessor. The "how" now has its full force — with what instruments, drawn from whose life, is this denial being conducted?

**Four Stages and a Return**

The commentators count the verse's itinerary in stages: lifelessness before birth; life given for the world; death at the term; life restored at the resurrection — and then, governing all four, the return to Allah. The Qur'an states the same arithmetic from the other side, in the confession the dead will make: *"Our Lord! You made us lifeless twice, and gave us life twice. Now we confess our sins. So is there any way out?"* (40:11). Two deaths, two lives, one unanswered question. The symmetry is the verse's engine: resurrection is not a new kind of divine act requiring new evidence but the repetition of an act the addressee has already been the object of. Whoever finds the second giving of life incredible must first explain the first — and the first is not in dispute between the verse and its hearer, since the hearer exists. The argument's structure is thus *a fortiori* in the oldest sense: if the harder thing (life from no life at all) has been done to you, the easier thing (life again from a life already lived) cannot coherently be denied by you.

**What “Dead” Means Before the Beginning**

The first stage — *kuntum amwātan*, you were dead things — required the exegetes to define a pre-biographical death, and their readings converge on hiddenness. One strand takes it materially: the person's existence in the substance of the body — in the loins of the parents, in the dust of the earth, as raw material not yet a someone. Another takes it lexically: the Arabs called a thing "dead" when its traces were effaced and it was not mentioned or remembered — death as unremembrance — and the Qur'an supplies the gloss in its own idiom: *"Is there not a period of time when each human is nothing yet worth mentioning?"* (76:1). Ibn ʿArabī read the stage metaphysically: not sheer nothingness but non-manifestation — the person held, unexpressed, in divine knowledge, "dead" only to the world, fully real to the Knower. The readings agree on what matters for the argument: before the first life there was a true state of absence from the world, and the passage from that absence to a speaking, denying self was not performed by the self. Every person's first predicate, in the verse's grammar, is *was not* — and every subsequent claim to independence is made from borrowed existence.

**Life as a Loan From the Hand Being Denied**

The hinge of the verse is its second clause: *fa-aḥyākum* — then He gave you life. The verb is Allah's, the object is the reader, and the gift is the very capacity being used to refuse it. There is a compressed indictment in the sequence: the life with which the denial is thought, spoken, and defended is the gift whose Giver the denial targets. Disbelief, on the verse's showing, is not a neutral cognitive stance but an act performed with embezzled funds — the denier's existence is the denier's chief evidence against denial, and he cannot set it down while he uses it. The Qur'an elsewhere generalizes the point into a doctrine of total dependence: provision, hearing, sight, and hearts are all loaned faculties whose loan can end (as in 67:23's inventory — He produced your hearing, your sight, your faculties, and little thanks you give). Verse 28 keeps the inventory to its two largest items — being and being-again — because two are enough: a creature who did not originate himself and does not terminate himself is bracketed at both ends by the Giver, and the middle he calls his own is a span between two donations.

**“To Him You Will Be Returned”: The Preposition of Destination**

The final clause compresses the whole eschatology into a grammar: *thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn* — then *to Him* you will be returned. The passive verb removes the traveler's control: returns of this kind happen to people, like the first life did. And the destination is not a place but a person — *ilayhi*, to Him. The commentators note the Qur'an's habit of describing the Last Day as return precisely because return implies an origin and an owner: you go back to where you came from, to whom you belonged. The phrase re-reads everything before it — the four stages were never a cycle but a trajectory with an address, and every stage was movement toward the meeting. Judgment, in al-Fātiḥah's name for its Day, is the settling of accounts; here it is the arrival of the loan at the Lender. The preposition also answers the denial named in the verse's first word: disbelief in Allah is, at bottom, living as though the return were to nowhere — as though the trajectory had no address. The verse's last word says otherwise, and says it as grammar rather than threat: *to Him*.

**Why the Personal Argument Comes Last**

The verse's placement completes the sūrah's evidential architecture, and the placement is the argument's final turn. Verse 21 commanded; verse 22 proved from the world — earth, sky, rain, fruit; verses 23–24 proved from the Book and its unmet challenge; verse 25 painted the two destinations. Only then does verse 28 arrive with the evidence no one can dispute at arm's length, because it is not at arm's length: your own death and your own beginning. The order moves from the furthest evidence to the nearest — cosmos, scripture, eschatology, self — and reserves the self for last, where arguments are strongest. The address remains the plural of verse 21, all humanity, and it includes, without exception, everyone who has ever lost a parent or been lost by one: the middle two stages of the verse are public knowledge in every family on earth. The denial the verse calls incoherent is therefore not a philosopher's position but a lived contradiction, and the verse leaves its hearer holding it — a being who cannot explain his own first morning, arguing against the explanation offered for it.
