## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:28

> How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Its Own Proof**

“How can you deny Allah?” (2:28) is not a request for information; it is an astonishment. Kayfa takfurūn — how is denial even possible given what you have already seen in yourselves? The verse then answers its own astonishment with a biography in four clauses: you were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will be returned (2:28). The proof is not brought from afar; it is your own passage through death and life and death and life.

The rhetoric matters. The sūrah has spent verses diagnosing denial that hides behind mockery of parables and breach of covenant; now it turns that denial back on the denier’s own existence, which cannot deny the Giver without denying itself.

**Four Stages, Not Two**

The stages are counted in the source as: before birth, earthly life, earthly death, and resurrection. The Qur’an elsewhere says, “Our Lord, You have caused us to die twice and given us life twice” (40:11). The “first death” is the state before animation — dust, loins, unremembered matter — the second is the end of this worldly life; the “first life” is this worldly breathing, the second is the raised life before God.

The opening “You were lifeless” (2:28) — kuntum amwātā — renders a “death” before life. Early lexicographers gloss it as being a thing unremembered (76:1, “a time when man was nothing to be remembered”) or being matter without animation — dust effaced of trace. Ibn ʿArabī, cited in the source, reads this lifelessness as non-manifestation within God’s Knowledge rather than sheer nothingness: the form (ṣūrah) known to God before its worldly unfolding. In either emphasis — material or metaphysical — the point stands: there was a “you” that had no power to give itself life, and then life was given.

To call pre-existence death is to collapse pride’s distance from need. If you were once not alive and then made alive, you are not self-caused. The Qur’an chooses amwāt rather than “non-existent” to keep the parallel with the second death sharp: the same One who turned first death into first life can turn second death into second life. Ability is proved by having been done once. Resurrection is not argued as a new kind of act, but as a repetition by the One who already enacted its like.

**“He Gave You Life” — Gift, Not Achievement**

Fa-aḥyākum — “He gave you life” (2:28) — centers agency. Life arrives as gift and assignment, not as self-creation. The giver is named in the same sentence that recalls reception: you were lifeless and *He* gave you life. The sūrah’s ethic grows from this grammar: if life is given, it is owed. Covenant (2:27), gratitude, and reform are responses to a prior bestowal.

**Then He Will Cause You to Die — The Certainty That Organizes Life**

“Then He will cause you to die” (2:28) is placed immediately after gift to prevent the illusion of ownership. The life given is not indefinite; it is loaned with a term. The certainty of return shapes how the interim is lived. The Qur’an elsewhere pairs this certainty with moral consequence precisely to make time count: death is not a rumor but an appointment that reorders priorities before it arrives.

“Then He will again bring you to life” (2:28) — thumma yuḥyīkum — is the hinge of the whole passage. If the first giving from lifelessness is accepted, the second is no harder. The verse does not argue resurrection from biology; it argues from biography. One who has already been made alive from “death” cannot claim such remaking is impossible without claiming his own life is impossible.

**“To Him You Will Be Returned” — The End Is a Return, Not a Drift**

“Then to Him you will be returned” (2:28) closes the movement not with annihilation but with return — turjaʿūn. The verb suggests a destination that was also an origin. Return gathers both accounting and belonging: accounting because the given life will be asked about; belonging because the One who gave it is the One to whom it reverts. The “all” in “you will all be returned” — emphasized in the translation — prevents substitution: no one sends a representative in his place.

2:27 ended with losers who break covenant, sever ties, and corrupt the land. 2:28 asks how denial is possible given the continuous dealings of the Lord with each person: lifelessness, animation, death, re-animation, return. Corruption in the land (2:27) is made more blameworthy when weighed against the mercy that gave the land’s inhabitants life in the first place. Covenant-breaking (2:27) looks more astonishing after remembering that the covenant-breaker himself was once nothing and was made someone.

- Remember the first death when pride swells: you did not author yourself.
- Remember the first life when gratitude thins: you were given what you could not earn.
- Remember the second death when delay tempts: the terminus is not speculative.
- Remember the second life and return when justice seems absent: the account has a court, and the court has a Judge who is also the Giver.

These four memories do not require technical cosmology; they require honesty about one’s own passage.

**A Quiet Reply to Reluctance About Resurrection**

If the heart says resurrection is far-fetched, the verse does not pile on physics; it returns the heart to the first animation it already accepts. The One who did that is the One who will do this. The distance between “dust” and “a speaking, remembering human” is not less than the distance between “a dead human” and “a raised human.” Who grants the greater grants the lesser. To accept the first giving and deny the second is to assert power in the Creator for the beginning while denying it for the end, without cause.
