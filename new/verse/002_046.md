## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:46

> those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Humble Identified by What They Expect**

The previous verse named a group who find prayer and patience manageable, and this verse says who they are. The defining mark the Book chooses is not a practice but a conviction about the future. What distinguishes these people is that they are settled about a meeting and a return, and everything else in the sentence follows from that.

The Book's method here is to attach a behaviour to a belief rather than the other way round. The verse does not say that people who fast and give will come to believe in the Hereafter; it says that people who are certain of meeting their Lord find the practices light. The order tells the reader where the sūrah thinks the difficulty of religious practice actually lies.

**Certainty, and the Word the Book Uses for It**

The Arabic verb rendered as being certain also carries the sense of supposing and of expecting, and the Book uses it across that range depending on context. In this verse the meaning is conviction, and the qualifying construction gives it the strength of a settled conclusion rather than a hopeful guess. Commentators were alert to the ambivalence of the verb, and they read the context as determining which sense applies.

The Book uses the same root elsewhere for the suppositions of people who are wrong, which shows that the word itself does not guarantee the quality of what is thought. What makes the usage in this verse significant is its object. The thing these people are certain about is not a forecast or an opinion but an appointment, and the Book presents that certainty as the axis on which a whole life turns.

**"Meeting Their Lord": the Book's Term for the Hereafter**

The phrase recurs throughout the Qur'an as one of the standard names for the world to come, and the Book uses it in promises and in warnings alike. It tells the Prophet to announce that whoever hopes for this meeting should act rightly and worship God alone (18:110), and it states that the appointed time for it is certain to arrive (29:5). Against those statements it sets the loss of those who deny it: *“Lost indeed will be those who denied the meeting with Allah, and were not ˹rightly˺ guided!”* (10:45), and a sūrah that describes such people as carrying their burdens on their backs (6:31).

The phrasing is worth noting because of what it says about death. The Book does not describe the end of life as an ending; it describes it as an arrival before someone, which is why the vocabulary of meeting appears so often where a modern reader would expect the vocabulary of death. What is being denied by the deniers in those verses is a confrontation, not an extinction.

**"And to Him They Will Return"**

The second half of the verse states the direction of the journey, and the Book uses the image of return for the whole of human existence: *“O humanity! Indeed, you are labouring restlessly towards your Lord, and will ˹eventually˺ meet the consequences.”* (84:6). The term implies that the creature has come from where it is going, so that the end of life is neither a novelty nor an exile but a movement back toward its source.

That image governs how the Book evaluates everything else. Wealth, standing and difficulty are all temporary because the traveller is temporary, and the sūrah's earlier argument about provision and the earth being arranged as a dwelling for a limited stay rests on the same conception. The people described in this verse live with the destination in front of them, and the verse presents that arrangement as the reason they can carry what others cannot.

**Why This Conviction Makes Discipline Bearable**

The connection between this verse and the previous one is causal. Prayer requires a person to interrupt his day and present himself before a God he cannot see; patience requires him to absorb losses without retaliation. Both are only rational if the story continues past death and if the account is settled by someone with authority over the whole of it.

Strip the certainty away and the arithmetic changes. A person who believes that this life is all there is has good reason to treat prayer as an interruption and forgiveness as a surrender. The Book does not argue that such a person lacks motivation; it states plainly that the practices it recommends are a burden except for those who know how the story ends, and it leaves the reader to work out which description fits him.

**The Portrait Completed in Two Clauses**

The verse describes one group of people with two statements, and together they rule out the two most common ways of getting the future wrong. A person who expects a meeting but does not expect a reckoning has only exchanged one form of comfort for another. A person who expects judgement in general but not a return to a particular Lord has not understood who is doing the judging. The verse states both facts and attaches them to the same people.

What the sūrah has done with these two verses is to answer the question its earlier rebuke raised. The previous passage accused a community of teaching what it did not practise, and the Book's response was to name two practices and the state of mind that makes them possible. The portrait in this verse is the standard the reader is invited to measure himself against, and the sūrah moves from it to the further favours owed to the community it has been addressing.
