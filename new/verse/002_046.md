## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:46

> those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse That Begins Mid-Sentence**

Verse 46 has no verb of command, no vocative, no independent standing in the grammar of the passage: it opens with *alladhīna*, "those who," and completes the sentence begun in verse 45. The burden of patience and prayer is heavy *except for the humble* — and the humble are defined by a certainty with two contents: the meeting with their Lord, and the return to Him. The entire verse is a relative clause doing definitional work, and the definition is the passage's hinge: verses 40–45 issued the covenant's demands and named the disposition that can carry them; verse 46 states what produces that disposition. Humility before Allah — *khushūʿ* — is not temperament, upbringing, or mood. It is an inference held with certainty, and the inference has two premises: the meeting is real, and the return is His. The Qur'an's method here is characteristic: it does not command the inner state directly, because inner states cannot be commanded into being; it commands the knowledge that generates them. To become humble, on this verse's account, one becomes certain — and certainty has contents, which the verse supplies.

**Ẓann: The Verb That Carries Both Doubt and Certainty**

The verb rendered "are certain" is *yaẓunnūn* — from *ẓann*, the Arabic's most double-edged word for thinking. *Ẓann* ordinarily occupies the middle ground between knowledge and ignorance: supposition, conjecture, opinion — the region the Qur'an repeatedly condemns as a foundation for belief (the disbelievers follow nothing but conjecture, as the refrain runs in 6:116 and 10:36). Yet the same verb also carries full conviction when the heart's assent is complete, and classical Arabic lexicography records both poles under one head. Here the context settles the sense decisively: the object of the *ẓann* is the meeting with Allah, and the verse uses it to define the people for whom the heavy things are light — a role conjecture could never fill. Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah puts the verb's certainty-pole in the mouth of the saved man receiving his record: he *ẓann* — he was sure — that he would meet his reckoning (69:20). The double register is theologically productive rather than confusing: certainty of the unseen is exactly the act of holding as known what the senses cannot show, and ẓann at this strength is the nearest thing Arabic has to naming faith's specific epistemology — conviction past demonstration, steadier than sight.

**Mulāqā: The Meeting as an Appointment**

The content of the certainty is *liqāʾ* — meeting: *annahum mulāqū rabbihim*, that they are meeting their Lord. The word choice makes the future an encounter rather than an event. A meeting has parties, a face-to-face structure, an appointment quality: someone will be *met*, and the meeting will happen *to* the self in person. The Qur'an's eschatology in this verse is therefore not primarily cosmological — no scales, no bridge, no scenery appears — but personal: you will meet Him. That framing does the definitional work the verse needs. Certainty of a meeting reorganizes conduct the way certainty of an audience with a king reorganizes a petition: the rehearsed words, the examined record, the posture practiced in advance. The humble of verse 45 are humble because they live before a meeting already scheduled; *khushūʿ* is the body's knowledge of the appointment. The exegetes connect the meeting to its instrument — the reckoning each soul will face alone, since verse 48 will announce the day when no soul avails another — and the connection sharpens the certainty's object: not a general afterlife but *my* meeting, *my* account, unavoidable and personal.

**Rajīʿūn: The Return as the Verse's Second Premise**

The second clause — *wa-annahum ilayhi rājiʿūn*, and that to Him they will return — adds direction to the meeting. The participle *rājiʿūn* names return, and return presupposes departure: the human being is here understood as one who came *from* Allah and is going back — the descent-and-return arc the sūrah has been tracing since Adam. The formula became Islam's most repeated sentence of grief: when disaster strikes, the believer says *"Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return"* (2:156) — the same two claims in the same order, belonging and returning, which verse 46 installs as the certainty underlying all steadiness. The Qur'an generalizes the clause beyond the humble: every soul tastes death, is tested with good and evil, *"then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned"* (21:35). The return is universal; what distinguishes verse 46's people is not the destination but the certainty — they hold the universal return as a fixed premise of daily reasoning, while others file it as doctrine never consulted. The difference between the two is the difference the whole passage is about: knowledge that changes the load-bearing of a life, and knowledge that merely sits in it.

**Khushūʿ Explained by Eschatology**

Read as the completion of verse 45, this verse answers a practical question: how does a burden become light? The answer is that weight is perceived against horizons. Patience and prayer are heavy to the person whose horizon is this life — within that frame, abstention is loss and standing in worship is time removed from visible returns. Move the horizon to the meeting and the return, and the same acts change mass: patience becomes the short cost of an endless settlement, prayer becomes preparation for an audience that cannot be rescheduled. The humble are not people with more willpower but people with a longer frame — the *ẓann* of verse 46 is precisely that frame held as certainty. This is why the Qur'an treats heedlessness (*ghaflah*) as the root condition of unbelief: not ignorance of the doctrine but failure to consult it. And it explains the verse's placement at the end of the covenant unit: the demands of verses 40–45 are load-bearing only for a person whose calculations already include the meeting. Legislation without eschatology produces exactly the failures the coming recital will document — commands known, deferred, sold.

**The Definition as a Silent Summons**

Verse 46 is the only verse in this opening unit that issues no command, and its commandlessness is its force. The unit began with imperatives — remember, fulfil, dread, believe, do not deny, do not sell, do not mix, establish, pay, bow, seek help — and closes with a portrait: these are the people who carry it. A portrait cannot be ordered; it can only be recognized, and recognition is its own summons. The Qur'an's addressees in Madina — scholars weighing the cost of confessing a new prophet, communities weighing the price of a changed allegiance — are being shown the interior of the people who pay such costs easily: certainty of the meeting, certainty of the return. The verse also does quiet work against the recital that follows. The history about to begin is the history of a people who had every sign and lost the certainty — who saw the sea part and still built a calf to meet instead of the Lord they were returning to. Verse 46 stands at the head of that history as its diagnostic: whatever else went wrong, the humble certainty named here is what went missing first, and it is the one thing no sign can substitute for, because signs are met with it, not the reverse.
