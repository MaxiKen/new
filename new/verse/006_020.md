## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:20

> Those to whom We gave the Scripture recognize him ˹to be a true prophet˺ as they recognize their own children. Those who have ruined themselves will never believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Recognition, Not Information**

The verb in this sentence is not the one used for intellectual knowledge in general; it is the one for recognizing something already known. The People of the Book are not described as encountering a new claim and being persuaded by it, but as seeing someone they had been expecting and identifying him. Their scriptures had described a messenger to come, and the sūrah reports that the identification is not difficult for them.

A covenant is on record elsewhere in which God took a pledge from the prophets that if a messenger came confirming what they had, they would believe in him and support him (3:81). Whether the audience of this verse were rabbis, monks, or traders who had picked up the language of the earlier scriptures, the Book's claim is that the tools for recognition were already in their hands.

**As They Recognize Their Own Children**

The comparison chosen is deliberately domestic. Nobody mistakes his own child for someone else's, and nobody needs an argument to establish the fact. The Book could have said that the People of the Book know him to be true, and it says something stronger: that the recognition is of the same order as the recognition of a face seen every day in one's own house.

The sentence is aimed at the Quraysh more than at the People of the Book. The Makkans were used to dismissing the Prophet as one more local preacher among people who had grown up with him. The verse points across the city to the neighbours who possessed the older books and reports what those books had prepared them to see.

**The Same Claim With an Uncomfortable Addition**

In the sūrah of the Cow the statement is repeated with a second sentence that this verse omits: a group of them hides the truth knowingly (2:146). Recognition and concealment appear side by side in the same report, and the pairing defines the accusation. It is not that the learned among the People of the Book failed to notice the signs; it is that some of them noticed and chose silence.

That is why the sūrah does not rest the argument on their testimony. A witness who has decided not to speak is worthless in court, and the Book never presents the recognition of the People of the Book as the proof of the message. It presents it as evidence against the people who recognized and said nothing.

**Why Recognition Is Not the Same as Acceptance**

Every reader of scripture brings interests to what he reads, and in Makkah those interests were social and economic as much as theological. To acknowledge a new prophet was to fold one's own standing into his, to accept correction from a man of a different clan, and to lose the authority that came from being the custodian of the older books. The Book names the motive plainly as knowledge, which makes the concealment a decision about status rather than a difficulty about evidence.

The verses about the People of the Book are read wrongly when they are taken as a complaint about scholarship. The complaint is about a class of experts who held their position by knowing more than others and who recognized in the new message a claim that would redistribute that position.

**Those Who Ruined Themselves Will Never Believe**

The sentence closes with the refrain the sūrah has already used once: those who have ruined themselves will not believe (6:12). Placed after the recognition of the People of the Book, the clause reads as an explanation of how recognition can fail to become faith. Something in the person is spent, and the loss is his own — the ruin comes first, the unbelief follows, and both are described as the same event seen from two angles.

The Book is careful not to make this a general verdict on every Israelite or Christian who did not accept the message. It speaks of those who have ruined themselves, a category defined by what it does rather than by what it is born into. The verse leaves the category open and lets each hearer decide whether the description fits.

**What the Verse Does for Its First Hearer**

A man being rejected by his own city is told that another community recognizes what his own does not. Read coldly, that could sound like a threat; read in the context of the sūrah's consolations, it is closer to a diagnosis. The refusal of his people is not proof that he is wrong, because the criteria for judging him are available to anyone who has read the earlier scriptures.

The sūrah will return to this comfort more than once, but its placement here matters. The verse stands directly after the great testimony, in which God is named as the witness between the Prophet and his people. In that courtroom the People of the Book supply the material evidence, and this verse reports, flatly, what the evidence shows.

It also settles a question the sūrah's audience would have found confusing. If the earlier scriptures came from the same God, why should a new messenger be a surprise? The verse answers that the earlier books themselves created the expectation, so that the arrival of the messenger is a fulfilment rather than an innovation. The Quraysh could dismiss the Prophet as a man from their own town, but they could not dismiss the fact that the communities with older books had been told to expect someone like him.
