## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:167

> They threatened, “If you do not desist, O Lot, you will surely be expelled.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Community Answers Correction With Threat**

Lot has warned his people about a practice they have made part of their public life. Instead of answering the moral claim, they threaten him with expulsion. Their response reveals how a society protects wrongdoing: it treats the person who names the harm as the danger and seeks to remove him from the community.

The threat is not an argument. It is an attempt to make the cost of truth so high that Lot will become silent. A people confident in its conduct should be able to answer a warning with reasons. The resort to exile shows that the community's confidence is more fragile than its public posture suggests.


To threaten removal is to say that belonging depends on agreement with the dominant practice. The people are not merely rejecting Lot's theology; they are trying to control who may remain among them. Exile becomes a warning to everyone else: speak as Lot speaks, and you too may lose home, family ties, and safety.

Such pressure can be more powerful than a direct blow. It makes neighbors police one another and persuades the uncertain to remain quiet. A community can claim to value freedom while denying it to the person who refuses its moral demands. Lot's threat exposes that contradiction.

**The Messenger's Place Is Not Granted by the Crowd**

Lot belongs to the community by human relation, but his mission comes from God. The people may control access to their town, yet they cannot decide whether God's command is true by voting on it. Their threat can endanger Lot's worldly residence, but it cannot cancel his status as a messenger.

This distinction gives courage to anyone who loses social approval for obeying God. Belonging to a place is valuable, and expulsion is painful, but no community is the final owner of a servant's conscience. The believer seeks to remain just and peaceful, yet he cannot purchase membership by surrendering truth.


The people behave as if Lot's warning is the shameful act and their practice is the norm that must be protected. Their response reverses the moral positions. Instead of examining themselves, they label the corrector intolerable. This is a common feature of entrenched wrongdoing: correction is experienced as aggression because it threatens comfort.

The Qur'an does not ask the reader to accept every accusation made by a dissenter. Truthful correction requires knowledge, wisdom, and justice. In Lot's case, the surrounding verses establish his trustworthiness and the revealed nature of his warning. The threat therefore confirms resistance; it does not establish the people's innocence.


Quran 7:82 records Lot's people responding to his warning by saying that they should be expelled from the town. That parallel confirms that expulsion was not an isolated metaphor but a stated social response to Lot's moral opposition.

The repetition across the accounts shows the cost of challenging a community's identity. When wrongdoing becomes a badge of belonging, the people may prefer to remove the truthful person rather than reconsider the practice. Lot's faithfulness is tested not only by the content of his warning but by the pressure to leave.

**A Threat Cannot Make Wrongdoing Right**

A community may have the power to remove a person, but power does not create moral legitimacy. If a crowd threatens the messenger, the truth of the message must still be judged by God. The threatened person should not answer oppression by becoming oppressive, but he should not call the threat justice simply because it is popular.

This principle applies beyond religious dispute. Institutions sometimes protect their reputation by expelling whistleblowers, critics, or victims who name abuse. The act of exclusion can hide a problem for a time, but it does not repair it. Lot's story warns against confusing silence with peace.


A healthy community can survive correction because its identity is not built on denial. It may disagree with a messenger while preserving room for honest examination. Lot's people have made belonging conditional on moral surrender, so their community becomes a cage rather than a source of mutual protection.

The threat asks the reader what kind of belonging he wants. Shared place and custom are valuable, but they must remain under God's authority. A community that demands disobedience as the price of membership is asking for loyalty that no servant can rightly give.

**Lot's Warning Has Already Changed the Balance**

The people threaten expulsion because Lot's words have exposed a vulnerability. They cannot silence the question by answering it, so they try to remove its speaker. The threat is evidence that the warning has reached the social structure, not proof that the warning failed.

A truthful word may appear weak when the powerful can punish its speaker. Yet it can continue to work in the conscience of those who hear it. Lot remains responsible for speaking faithfully; God remains responsible for judging the people who threaten him. No expulsion can erase that accountability.


The verse does not command every person to seek conflict or abandon lawful social ties. It teaches that belonging cannot be purchased through surrender of God's command. A wise servant speaks with patience, uses lawful means, and protects others, but he does not accept exile as proof that falsehood has become true.

Lot's people threaten him; the reader is asked not to become one of those who make truth homeless. Make room for correction, distinguish the person from the wrongdoing without approving the wrongdoing, and remember that the Lord's judgment is higher than the crowd's decree.

A community that expels every honest critic eventually loses the ability to correct itself. It may keep its outward unity, but its unity becomes the silence of fear. Lot's threatened removal shows that moral health requires room for a truthful voice, even when that voice unsettles the majority.
