## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:182

> Whoever suspects an error or an injustice in the will and brings about a ˹fair˺ settlement among the parties will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Guards the Exception**

The previous verse made the will inviolable and charged every alteration to the alterer; this verse opens the one door through which alteration may lawfully pass. Whoever *fears* — suspects, apprehends, has ground to worry — an error or an injustice *from the testator himself*, and then sets matters right between the parties, carries no sin. The structure is deliberate and rare: a prohibition (2:181) immediately qualified by its necessary exception (2:182), so that the law's severity against changers cannot become a shield for a corrupt will. The sin the previous verse assigned to the changer is here lifted from the corrector, and the distinction between the two actors is the verse's entire content. Both change the document; one changes it against justice for gain and bears the sin alone; the other changes it toward justice against a wrongful instrument and bears none. The verse thus completes the bequest-legislation by installing a remedy inside the rule — the dead man's word is protected, but only insofar as his word was just.

**Janaf: The Error and the Injustice**

The two words the corrector may fear — *janaf* and *ithm* — cover the will's two failure-modes. *Ithm* is sin proper: a bequest commanding something forbidden, directing wine or stolen property, exceeding the third with no heir's consent. *Janaf* is subtler, and the exegetes illustrated it with the case that defines it: a dying man leaves property to his son's wife, meaning thereby to enrich the son himself — a will that looks lawful on its face (the son's wife draws no fixed share) but is engineered to route property to someone the law excludes, violating the rule that legal heirs receive no bequests. Al-Qurṭubī records a prophetic report built on the verse's own word, branding *janaf* in wills a grave sin. The classification matters: the testator who commits janaf is not breaking a rule in plain sight but manipulating the rules' gaps — using the bequest's freedom to defeat the inheritance's fairness. It is the deathbed's version of the concealment the passage behind this one prosecuted: form preserved, substance subverted, the letter honored while the intent is smuggled. Against this engineered will, the verse arms the community.

**The Duty of the Living**

The correction the verse authorizes is not optional housekeeping but a communal obligation. Al-Qurṭubī states it as a communal charge: where a will's evident purpose is to sidestep the Quran's fixed shares of inheritance, the community must revise or void it. The actors are named with legal precision: *between them* refers to the heirs; *him* — the one who fears and sets right — is the trustee administering the will, its attesting witnesses (5:106), or whoever holds the charge, though some readings place the initiative with the heirs themselves. The no-sin clause then explains itself against the general rule: altering a will would ordinarily incur the sin of 2:181, but here equity outranks the dead man's directive — the correction is not alteration in the forbidden sense because it does not defeat the dead man's lawful word; it defeats his unlawful one, on behalf of the very system his word attempted to outmaneuver. Al-Rāzī's formulation gives the principle its edge: the corrector acts *for* the testator's real interest and *for* the heirs' right simultaneously, and a change that serves both against a documented injustice is the law repairing itself, not a person overriding the law.

**The Names That Close the Triad**

The bequest-legislation ends where it began, with mercy named: Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The two names do specific work here. Forgiveness covers the testator — the man whose will erred or inclined, who perhaps repented too late or never knew his bequest was janaf; the verse corrects his instrument without adding his sin to the changers' accounts, and the forgiveness named leaves his case with God rather than with the estate. And forgiveness covers the corrector — the executor who must judge a dead man's intentions, who may fear wrongly, who may adjust a will that was innocent after all; the no-sin clause is generous precisely because the judgment is hard, and the merciful names assure the hesitant intervener that an honest correction made in fear of injustice stands under pardon even where his fear proved mistaken. The triad of verses thus closes as a complete legal-moral unit: the command to bequeath in fairness (2:180), the protection of the bequest against the greedy living (2:181), and the correction of the bequest against the unjust dead (2:182) — each guarded from the abuse its own emphasis invites, and all three framed by the same names.

**Charity While the Blood Is Warm**

The commentators closed this passage with a caution that protects the verse from a misreading: none of this bequest-legislation diminishes the superior virtue of giving while alive. The tradition prizes the charity given when the giver is *ṣaḥīḥ shaḥīḥ* — healthy and avid, in the full grip of life's attachment to wealth — and reports the Prophet's comparison: the dying donor resembles a man who gives only once his own appetite has been sated. The deathbed gift comes cheap; the healthy gift costs. The jurists fixed the practical boundary: during life a person may dispose of his property freely, in any measure he wishes — the one-third restriction binds only the bequest, the posthumous gift, not the living hand. The distinction keeps 2:182 in perspective: the will is the law's provision for the dying, a bounded instrument fenced against janaf; it is not the main channel of generosity, and the believer who waits for his deathbed to be fair has already spent the years when fairness was costly. The verse forgives the corrector and regulates the testator; the tradition beside it exhorts the healthy — and the three together describe a community in which property is disciplined at death, corrected after death, and given away, ideally, long before.
