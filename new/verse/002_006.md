## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:6

> As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict on Persistence, Not on Possibility**

The verse swings from the guided to a second company, and its opening word fixes the charge: *kafarū* — those who disbelieved, in the settled sense the translation marks with "persist." The grammar describes a state achieved rather than a door being shut. When the sentence adds "they will never believe," it reports an end reached through its own road, the way a physician reports a disease that has passed its treatable stage. Nothing in the verse claims these people were barred before they chose; the negation of belief is the sum of their negations. The Qur'an elsewhere keeps the two ideas in tension without collapse — guidance offered to all, acceptance determined by each — and this verse states the second term only. Read as description rather than decree, it answers a pastoral problem rather than a metaphysical one: what should a warner do when warning demonstrably no longer works on particular hearts?

**Nadhīr: An Office Measured by Delivery**

Warning is one of the two names the Qur'an gives the prophetic office, and its logic is contractual: the messenger delivers, the hearer decides, Allah judges. The verse releases the Prophet ﷺ from the burden of outcomes by defining his commission in terms he can always fulfill: *"We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a deliverer of good news and a warner to all of humanity"* (34:28). The adverb "only" does real work — the office does not include manufacturing belief in other people's chests. Prophetic biography supplies the cost of forgetting this: the Makkan years show a messenger grieving over every refusal, and revelation repeatedly consoles him with the limits of his jurisdiction. A warning that changes no one has still succeeded as a warning, because the act was owed to the hearer and to Allah, not to the result. The verse is the doctrine of the office stated at its sharpest edge — the case where delivery and reception fully part company.

**Consolation in a Hard Shape**

There is mercy in the sentence's bluntness, addressed to a man who grieved over every soul that refused him. The precedent runs back to Nūḥ, told after nine and a half centuries of refusal: *"None of your people will believe except those who already have. So do not be distressed by what they have been doing"* (11:36). The information arrives as relief — once the closed cases are known to be closed, the heart stops paying for them daily. Expectation honestly lowered is a form of care for the one who carries the mission, and the Qur'an applies that care here without softening the fact. The verse does not say warning is worthless; it says warning has done all it can do with these particular hearts, and the warner may set that portion of his grief down. Consolation and verdict arrive in one sentence, and the consolation depends entirely on the verdict being true.

**A Sentence Spoken Over Many Peoples**

The formula is not unique to al-Baqarah, and its repetitions show that the Qur'an is describing a type rather than a roster. Of the Makkan elite who mocked the messenger it says: *"It is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe"* (36:10) — nearly the same words, a generation and a city away. The exegetical lists gather further parallels where warning is declared spent (7:193; 26:136; 63:6). Each recurrence marks a threshold crossed by a community: repeated refusals compacted into a fixed disposition, after which new evidence no longer reaches the faculty that weighs evidence. The generality matters for reading this verse inside the sūrah's opening. The five-verse unit before it described the guided as a type too — qualities, not names — and now the second type appears in the same style. Al-Baqarah is building a taxonomy of souls, and "the ones for whom warning has closed" is its second entry.

**Warning Still Reaches the House Next Door**

The futility declared here is local, and the verse does not license silence. The command to warn stands; what changes is the distribution of results. In any assembly the same words fall on three kinds of ground — the guarded heart of verse 2, the settled refusal of this verse, and everything between — and no warner can see the boundaries from where he stands. The call is therefore universal while the outcome remains per soul. This arrangement also protects the message from becoming salesmanship. If the warner's success were measured in conversions, he would temper the warning to make it sellable; measured in delivery, it can stay exactly as sharp as it was sent. The Qur'an records the futility openly, in the same Book that commands the warning, so that neither truth is lost: some hearts will never open, and the call must still reach every ear.

**Foreknowledge That Does Not Bind the Will**

A difficulty remains, and honesty requires stating it: if Allah declares they will never believe, in what sense were they free not to? The classical answers distinguish God's knowledge from God's coercion — knowledge of a choice is not a chain on it, any more than a witnessed event is caused by the witness. The verse's own framing supports the distinction: it is spoken *about* people who have already persisted long enough to be predictable, the way a prognosis follows a history. The Qur'an never presents this knowledge as the mechanism of their refusal; the mechanism, in the sūrah's coming verses, will be shown as something they do — and something that is then confirmed over them. How knowledge and confirmation relate to the will is a question the revelation states rather than systematizes. The following verse shows what this settled refusal looks like from the inside.
