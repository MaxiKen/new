## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:6

> As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Warning That Does Not Change a Fixed Stance**

The sūrah has just sealed the portrait of the mindful with a double *it is they* (2:5). It now opens the second portrait with a parallel *indeed* — *inna alladhīna kafarū* — “as for those who persist in disbelief.” The shift is not from one doctrine to another but from one human posture to another. The same Book that was a guidance for the mindful (2:2) is now described as a warning that leaves another group unmoved: *“it is the same whether you warn them or not — they will never believe”* (2:6). The sameness does not describe the warning’s quality; it describes the hearer’s fixedness. A warning is by definition an attempt to avert by foretelling consequence, and the office of the warner (*nadhīr*) is central to the Prophet’s self-description. To say the warning is “the same” to these hearers is to say their stance has calcified before the warning arrives. The verse is therefore less about the inefficacy of prophetic speech and more about a moral condition that renders even the clearest speech inert.

**Why the Grammar Allows “Will Not Believe”**

The source notes that *lā yuʾminūn* can be read as “they do not believe” or “they will not believe.” The difference is temporal, not theological. In Arabic the imperfect (*yuʾminūn*) can denote present habit or future expectation, and the context of warning favors the future-facing sense: no matter what is done now, the outcome in the horizon remains non-belief. That nuance collects the cross-references the source gathers — *“Had you warned them they would not believe”* in 7:193, the comparison in 26:136 that warnings and reminders are treated alike, *“it is all the same whether you warn them”* in 36:10, and the Medinan echo in 63:6. Each instance uses the same idiom of indifference to mark not a property of the message but a property of the recipient who has decided, before hearing, that hearing will not alter him. The translation’s “will never believe” rightly preserves that forward-leaning finality.

**Nadhīr and the Office of Warning**

To be a *nadhīr* is not to be a compeller. The Qur'an distinguishes the warner from the controller (*muṣayṭir*, 88:22), the guardian (*wakīl*, 6:66), and the compeller (*jabbār*, 50:45). The Prophet warns; he does not force assent. That distinction is why the verse can be true without charging the warner with failure. The same distinction explains why the Qur'an couples warnings with leeway: *“We do not punish until We have sent a messenger”* (17:15) and *“Every community has had a warner”* (35:24). Warning is the form divine justice takes before it becomes judgment, and the verse marks the tragic case where the form is offered and the function is refused. The listener who treats warning and its absence as the same has, in effect, closed the interval that revelation opened for return.

**The First Portrait After the Prosperous**

Placed directly after the seal on the mindful (2:5), this verse performs a sorting that the sūrah will elaborate over the next fifteen verses. Humanity is not divided here by tribe, wealth, or lineage, but by response to the same Book. One group is upon guidance and shall prosper (2:5); another, described here, is upon a settled refusal. A third, beginning in (2:8), will claim to be upon guidance while harboring disease. The second portrait is therefore deliberately brief — two lines — because there is less to describe in a fixed refusal than in the elaborate self-deception that follows. The brevity is itself a rhetorical judgment: the disbeliever, unlike the hypocrite, does not require a long anatomy; his stance is stated and then judged.

**Disbelief as Persistence, Not a Momentary Slip**

*Alladhīna kafarū* — “those who disbelieve” — uses the perfect *kafarū* to denote an entrenched act, not a passing mistake. Classical commentators distinguish *kufr* as a covering — the same root that yields *kāfir* for a farmer who covers seed in the soil — so that disbelief is the covering of a truth that is already present enough to be covered. That etymology informs the verse’s logic. A warning is useful to one who has not seen the danger; it is useless to one who has seen it and chosen to cover it. The verse does not say “those who have not yet believed”; it says “those who have persisted in covering,” and that persistence, not the Prophet’s eloquence, is what renders the warning equal to silence.

**It Is the Same — An Equality That Judges**

*Sawāʾun ʿalayhim* — “it is the same upon them” — places the equality *upon* them as a burden. The phrase recurs in the Qur'an only when the equality marks a closed door. To say “it is the same whether you warn them or not” is not to counsel the warner to be silent; elsewhere the warner is told to *“warn, for warning benefits the believers”* (51:55). It is to tell the warner that for this particular group, the benefit has been refused in advance, so that the warning becomes a witness rather than an invitation. The same wording in 36:10, where the Prophet’s warning to his own townspeople is described as equally unheeded, shows that the sameness is not an ethnic verdict but a moral one, tied to a stance that can be adopted in Mecca, Medina, or any city where the Book is recited.

**The Same Warning Given Everywhere**

That the idiom recurs in 7:193 (addressed to those who call on idols that cannot create), in 26:136 (from the lips of ʿĀd to Hūd), and in 63:6 (about the hypocrites for whom seeking forgiveness is the same as not), suggests the verse in al-Baqarah is not an isolated pronouncement about a specific Medinan faction but the Qur'anic statement of a principle: there is a kind of refusal that neutralizes the very instrument designed to cure refusal. The principle does not cancel divine mercy; it establishes that mercy operates through warning, and that to render warning equal to its absence is to elect to meet the consequence head-on. The next verse will describe that election in physiological terms — a sealing — but this verse first states it as a human fact: they will not believe.

**A Definite Group, Not a Speculative Category**

Finally, *alladhīna* — “those who” — retains its demonstrative force. The verse is not a speculative determinism about unknown hearts; it is a description of a present, observable class whose response to warning has already been tested. The Prophet could see among his audience those who remained unmoved whether he spoke at dawn in the mosque or at night in their homes. The verse names what he saw, and it authorizes the community to recognize the same pattern without claiming to know the unseen decree behind it. That balance — descriptive, not prescriptive — will be critical when the next verse attributes the sealing to Allah, lest the diagnosis of the present be mistaken for a denial of possibility.
