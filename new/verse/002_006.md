## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:6

> As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse That States the Objection Rather Than the Answer**

Taken in isolation, this verse reads like a reason not to preach. If warning and silence produce the same result for the people in question, the work of a warner is pointless, and the sūrah is about to spend 280 verses arguing with people it has already declared unreachable. The difficulty is real, and it has occupied commentators for as long as the verse has been recited.

The verse should be read as a diagnosis rather than a sentence. It describes a condition that has already developed in the people it names, and every element of the statement points that way: the subject is *those who persist in disbelief*, not a list of individuals; the verb can be read in the present (*they do not believe*) as well as the future (*they will not believe*); and the sentence's function in context is to tell the Messenger something about his audience, not to tell the audience something about their destiny.

**Who Is Being Described**

The formula recurs four times in the Quran, and the recurrence settles who is meant. In one sūrah the identical sentence is used: *It is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe* (36:10). In another it is applied to something other than warning: *It is the same whether you pray for their forgiveness or not, Allah will not forgive them. Surely Allah does not guide the rebellious people* (63:6).

The decisive instance is the one where the sentence is not God's description of the rejecters but their own description of themselves: *They responded, “It is all the same to us whether you warn ˹us˺ or not”* (26:136). There the indifference is plainly a stance the people have adopted, stated in the first person, and the Quran is recording it rather than imposing it. The same structure appears where idols are addressed: *It is all the same whether you call them or remain silent* (7:193). In every occurrence the subject is a party that has already taken up a settled position — which is why the warning no longer reaches them, not the other way round.

**The Warner's Task Ends at Delivery**

The title the Quran gives the Messenger throughout is *nadhīr*, warner, and the office is defined by what it delivers rather than by what it achieves. The limitation is stated repeatedly and in nearly identical words: *The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺* (5:99); *your duty ˹O Prophet˺ is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly* (16:82); *your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. Judgment is for Us* (13:40).

The point of this verse, in its context, is to release the Messenger from responsibility for an outcome he cannot produce. He had been warning for years and seeing refusal, and the natural conclusion for a preacher in that position is that the message is defective or the preaching was bungled. The verse removes both conclusions: the warning is intact, and so is the warner. A sūrah that opens by describing the people who *do* benefit (2:2–5) and then states that others will not is drawing the boundary that makes the Messenger's labour intelligible. Another sūrah records the opposite expectation being disappointed: *They swore by Allah their most solemn oaths that if a warner were to come to them, they would certainly be better guided than any other community. Yet when a warner did come to them, it only drove them farther away* (35:42).

**Veils That Follow a Choice**

The mechanism behind the closed response is described repeatedly, and the descriptions are ordered in a way that matters. *And who does more wrong than those who, when reminded of their Lord’s revelations, turn away from them and forget what their own hands have done? We have certainly cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend this ˹Quran˺—and deafness in their ears* (18:57). The turning away comes first in the sentence; the veiling is named after it.

That order is the substance of the argument and it is consistent across the Quran's accounts: *There are some of them who ˹pretend to˺ listen to your recitation ˹of the Quran˺, but We have cast veils over their hearts* (6:25); *We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears. And when you mention your Lord alone in the Quran, they turn their backs in aversion* (17:46). The veil is presented as the consequence of a settled refusal, not as its cause — the condition of a person who has heard enough to have lost the capacity to hear. This sūrah names the sealing in the line that follows.

**Foreknowledge Is Not Coercion**

The distinction the tradition drew is between what God knows and what God compels. A statement that someone will not believe is a statement about the future, and if it is made by God it cannot be mistaken; but an infallible prediction is not a cause. The Quran itself refuses the deterministic inference when it is offered as an excuse, and it does so by quoting those who made it: *The polytheists argue, “Had Allah willed, neither we nor our forefathers would have worshipped anything other than Him, nor prohibited anything without His command.” So did those before them. Is not the messengers’ duty only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly?* (16:35).

The reply is instructive. The Quran does not refute the premise — it declines the use being made of it and turns the argument back to the only question that is the arguer's business, which is delivery. The same refusal grounds the clearest statement of the matter anywhere in the Book: *Had your Lord so willed ˹O Prophet˺, all ˹people˺ on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them! Would you then force people to become believers?* (10:99). God could have made a world of compelled believers and did not; the existence of refusal is therefore part of what He willed, and the refusal is still the refuser's own.

**What the Verse Removes**

Read as a diagnosis, the sentence takes two things away. From the Messenger it removes the despair that comes from measuring faithfulness by results. From the hearer it removes the assumption that the warning will always be available in the same form — that a person can defer the response indefinitely and still find the door as open as it was the first time.

That is why the sūrah continues to argue, command, narrate, and warn after saying this. The warning is not withdrawn from anyone; it is declared ineffective only for a posture that has become permanent, and no reader is told that he occupies it.
