## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:6

> As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence the Book Repeats**

The Qur'an states this proposition more than once, which is why it reads as a rule rather than as a report about particular people. Sūrah Yā Sīn has it almost word for word: *“It is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe.”* (36:10). A prophet's people answer in the same terms: *“It is all the same to us whether you warn ˹us˺ or not.”* (26:136), and elsewhere the warning is said to be useless for another reason — the ones addressed cannot respond at all, *“It is all the same whether you call them or remain silent.”* (7:193).

The repetition is deliberate. Each occurrence attaches the statement to a different situation — a prophet's frustration, a community's defiance, a futile appeal to idols — and each time the sentence closes a door that the address itself had opened. The Qur'an is teaching its messenger that the outcome of his work is not proportional to the effort he puts into it.

**Disbelief as Covering Something Over**

The Arabic verb *kafara* means to cover or conceal, and Arab farmers used it for burying seed in the ground. Those who *kafarū* are not people with unanswered questions; in the Qur'an's usage they are people who have covered over what they know. The verse's opening construction, *those who persist in disbelief*, describes a settled condition rather than a momentary doubt.

That settled quality is what makes the sentence in the second half possible. Warning addresses a person who is still in motion, and the verse describes people who have arrived at a position. The Book elsewhere draws a line between those who have not yet accepted and those who will not: an invitation stands open, and a decision has been made. The Qur'an treats the second category as a fact about the world that its messenger must learn to work with rather than as a problem to be solved by better argument.

**The Warner's Task Is Announcement**

The Qur'an repeatedly limits the messenger's responsibility to delivery. *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. / You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:21–22). The same boundary appears in the instruction to the Prophet in argument: *“your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺”* (3:20), and again at 42:48 and 64:12, where the messenger is told he is not a keeper over the people he addresses.

The verse in al-Baqarah is the negative version of the same principle. If the warning changes nothing for a particular group, the message has still been delivered and the messenger's commission is complete. The Qur'an never presents the refusal of an audience as a failure of the message, nor does it ask the messenger to accept responsibility for the spiritual condition of another person.

**Not-Yet-Believing and Never-Believing**

The sentence is narrower than it sounds, and the rest of the Book shows the difference. Al-Baqarah itself will state that there is no compulsion in religion, since the truth has become distinct from falsehood (2:256), and the Qur'an addresses an uncommitted audience with an open choice: *“Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve”* (18:29). Invitation, argument and warning all assume that the person addressed can still move.

The verse therefore does not describe everyone who has not yet believed. It describes people whose rejection has become a settled feature of their character, and the Book's word for that condition is the same one it uses for a seed buried under earth. What is covered becomes inactive, and the verse's purpose is to tell the messenger in advance that such a condition exists and that his grief cannot alter it.

**The Grief the Sentence Was Meant to Prevent**

Telling a messenger that his warning will do no good sounds like cruelty, and the Qur'an shows why it is a mercy. The Prophet ﷺ is addressed twice with an image of self-destruction: *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief.”* (26:3), and *“Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial”* (18:6). A third passage removes the burden directly: *“So do not grieve yourself to death over them ˹O Prophet˺.”* (35:8).

The verse in al-Baqarah belongs to that pattern. It is given at the head of the longest sūrah, before the long history of rejection that the sūrah will narrate, so that the messenger knows from the outset that some doors will not open. The statement is not a verdict on the messenger's effectiveness but a limit on his responsibility, and it frees the work from the impossible demand of universal agreement.

**Who the Words Were First Said Of**

Commentators connected the passage with the leaders of rejection in the sūrah's setting: the Qurashī notables who had settled into hostility and the Jewish leaders of Madinah who had decided against the Prophet before hearing him. The Book does not list names here, and the absence of names is what makes the statement usable across generations. The condition is described; the people who fit it change.

That anonymity also protects the community from using the verse as a label for its opponents. The verse cannot be applied by a believer to anyone except in the terms it sets out — a rejection so complete that warning makes no difference — and no human being can inspect that condition. The sentence is addressed to the messenger about his work, not to the community about its neighbours.

**Two Kinds of Failure at the Head of the Sūrah**

The place of this verse in the sūrah's opening sequence clarifies what it is doing. Verses 2:2–5 describe people who accept guidance they cannot verify; 2:6–7 describe people who have covered over what they knew; 2:8–20 describe people who say one thing and hold another. Three portraits, three failures, and the middle one is the plainest.

The distinction between the second and third portraits is the reason the sūrah spends so much more time on the third. A rejection that is out in the open can be answered, argued with or avoided. A profession without conviction cannot be answered at all, because the person making it has already agreed with everything that could be said against him. The Book gives the settled rejecter two verses and the hypocrite thirteen, and the proportion measures the difficulty rather than the guilt.
