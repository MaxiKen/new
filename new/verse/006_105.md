## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:105

> And so We vary our signs to the extent that they will say, “You have studied ˹previous scriptures˺,” and We make this ˹Quran˺ clear for people who know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Statement About Varying the Signs**

And so We vary our signs. The sūrah has used this vocabulary three times before. It described the varying of the signs when the audience turned away from the argument about hearing and sight (6:46); it used the same word when the explanation of the signs was said to make the way of the wicked distinct (6:55); and it used it in the warning about the punishments that could come from above, from below, or through the community's own divisions (6:65). The recurrence marks the stages of the sūrah's argument, and each occurrence states a different purpose.

The Book uses the same term elsewhere for the presentation of the message in many forms. The sūrah of the Heights and the sūrah of the Bee describe the signs as varied so that people might be mindful and give thanks, and the sūrah of the Ant speaks of the varying of the signs for a people who understand. What the term describes is a method of instruction rather than a rhetorical flourish, and the sūrah presents it as a deliberate provision for an audience that needed the argument in more than one shape.

**The Accusation That Will Follow**

To the extent that they will say, You have studied previous scriptures. The sentence states what the varying of the signs will produce in the audience, and it records the accusation in advance. The charge is that the Prophet had learned his material from the People of the Book, and the Book records the same charge in several passages: the sūrah of the Bee quotes it and answers that the person the accusers have in mind speaks a foreign tongue while the Qur'an is in eloquent Arabic (16:103); the sūrah of the Discernment records that they said the message was a fabrication made up with the help of others and that it consisted of ancient fables written down and rehearsed morning and evening (25:4–5).

The sūrah's inclusion of the accusation inside a statement of God's own activity is significant. The verse does not present the charge as an unexpected development but as something the varying of the signs would produce, which means the Book treats the accusation as part of the response its method would receive rather than as a refutation of that method.

**Why the Accusation Was Predictable**

The charge was predictable because the Qur'an contains the stories of earlier prophets, and the audience knew that the People of the Book possessed accounts of those figures. The Book's answer to the charge is not to deny that the stories are known but to point to what the revelation contains that no human source could have supplied, and to the fact that the man it accuses speaks a foreign language while the Qur'an is in the most eloquent Arabic.

The sūrah's own method makes the accusation inevitable in another way as well. The varying of the signs means the same truth is presented through the created order, the history of the earlier communities, the arguments of Abraham, and the conditions of the present. An audience determined to resist could always claim that one of those forms had been borrowed, and the Book responds by presenting the accusation rather than disputing it at length.

**The Clarity Given to People Who Know**

And We make this Qur'an clear for people who know. The sentence closes the verse with the same qualification the sūrah has attached to its other accounts of the signs: the clarity is real, and it is realised in people who know. The sūrah has used the phrase about the stars and the signs (6:97), and it has used the same construction at the end of the passage about the varying of the signs earlier (6:55).

The Book's use of this qualification consistently prevents the reader from turning the accusation into an argument against the message. If the audience says the Prophet has studied the earlier books, the answer is that the message has been made clear to those who know, which places the question of response back with the hearer. The clarification is God's act, and the knowledge of the clarified material is the reader's responsibility.

**The Sequence in Which the Verse Stands**

The verse follows the statement that the insights have come from the Lord, that the choice is the reader's, and that the messenger is not a keeper over the people (6:104). Read as a unit, the two verses describe the delivery of the message, the freedom of the response, the limits of the messenger's office, and the accusation that the Book knew would follow. The sūrah is preparing the reader for the rest of the chapter, which continues the argument with a people who had been shown the signs in every form.

The verses that follow turn to the subject of following what has been revealed and of the community that had been given the Book, and the sūrah will return to the guidance of Abraham once more. The pattern established in this passage governs the treatment of those subjects as well: the message is clarified, the response is counted to the person who makes it, and the messenger is a warner rather than a keeper.

**What the Verse Says About the Revelation's Source**

The verse's most important claim is contained in its first three words, since the varying of the signs is attributed to God. The audience's accusation concerned the human source of the message, and the answer given here is that the presentation of the message in different forms is itself the work of the One who sent it. Every new argument the Prophet brought was a fresh instance of that work.

The Book's confidence in this arrangement is part of what it says about the message's authority. A revelation that adapts its arguments to its hearers while keeping the same content is not a text that has been assembled from other texts; it is the address of a speaker who knows whom He is addressing. The sūrah states that in one sentence and leaves the accusation to be recorded beside it.
