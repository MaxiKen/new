## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:6

> **They are also pleasing to you when you bring them home and when you take them out to graze.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Beauty As A Recognized Blessing**

Verse 5 mentioned warmth, benefits, and food. Verse 6 adds jamāl — beauty. Wa-lakum fīhā jamālun ḥīna turīḥūna wa-ḥīna tasraḥūn — and for you in them is beauty when you bring them home in the evening and when you send them out to pasture in the morning. This is the only place in the Qur'an where cattle are explicitly described as a source of aesthetic pleasure. Elsewhere they are described as useful, but here they are pleasing to look at. Al-Rāzī highlights this as evidence that the Qur'an does not reduce creation to mere utility. Beauty is itself a benefit, a gift that nourishes the eye and heart. The Makkan who owned herds knew this beauty firsthand: the sight of camels returning at dusk, full, slow, dust rising, is a scene of prosperity and calm. The Qur'an validates that feeling and attributes it to Allah. Your pleasure when you see them is part of what He created for you. This reframes aesthetics as part of gratitude, not vanity.

**Evening Return And Morning Departure**

Turīḥūna comes from r-w-ḥ, to return in the evening, to bring home. Tasraḥūna comes from s-r-ḥ, to send out to pasture, to let go free in the morning. The two verbs capture a daily rhythm that structured Bedouin life: at sunrise herds leave for grazing, at sunset they return. Between those two moments the owner experiences two different beauties. In the morning, the sight is of hope and abundance as animals go out lean and eager. In the evening, the sight is of fulfillment as they return full-bellied, milk heavy. Al-Zamakhsharī notes the word order: turīḥūna before tasraḥūna, evening before morning, though chronologically morning comes first. He explains that evening return is more beautiful to the owner because it shows profit, the result of the day's grazing, and because evening is a time of gathering, rest, and counting. Al-Ṭabarī adds that evening is when neighbors see each other's herds and recognize Allah's favor, making beauty a social, visible blessing, not hidden.

**Why Beauty Matters In An Argument For Tawḥīd**

Sūrat al-Naḥl is arguing against shirk, association of partners with Allah. Why mention beauty? Because beauty reveals intention. A purely utilitarian animal could be ugly and still provide milk and meat. That cattle are also beautiful, that their movement, symmetry, and presence please the human eye, suggests that the Creator considered human delight, not only human survival. Al-Rāzī argues that jamāl cannot be explained by material necessity; it points to a Creator who is Himself beautiful and loves beauty, as in the prophetic saying *Allah is beautiful and loves beauty* (Muslim 91). The idols, by contrast, were often crude stones, unable to create beauty or to appreciate it. By directing attention to the beauty of living creatures, the verse invites comparison: which deserves your devotion, the living beautiful creation that benefits you, or the lifeless stone that benefits nothing? The aesthetic argument is gentle but firm. It does not threaten; it asks you to look.

**The Human Need For Beauty And Its Place In Gratitude**

Modern readers may pass over this verse as minor, but for its first audience it named something they felt but had not been taught to count as divine favor. In a harsh environment, beauty is not extra; it sustains morale. The Qur'an's inclusion of jamāl among blessings teaches that gratitude should include what pleases the eye, not only what fills the stomach. This has practical consequences. If beauty is a divine gift, then caring for animals so that they remain healthy and beautiful is part of gratitude, and mistreating them is ingratitude. Classical jurists used this verse to argue for good treatment of livestock, since Allah mentions their beauty as a gift to humans, implying they should be kept in a state where that beauty is preserved. The verse also subtly counters a worldview that sees religion as only about hardship. Here, pleasure in a beautiful sight at dusk is affirmed as legitimate and divinely given, provided it leads to recognition of the Giver.

**The Pairing Of Benefit And Beauty**

The sequence from verse 5 to 6 moves from necessity to delight. First difʾ, warmth, which preserves life; then manāfiʿ, general benefits; then ta'kulūn, food; then jamāl, beauty. This is a rising scale from survival to enjoyment. Al-Rāzī sees in this a hierarchy of human needs that the Qur'an respects: first the body is protected, then it is fed, then the soul is delighted. The same hierarchy appears later in the sūrah where houses are mentioned as places of rest, then garments for protection, then garments for adornment. The pattern shows that Islam does not demand denial of beauty but places it after necessity, so that beauty does not become a god. When beauty is seen as coming from Allah, it leads to gratitude; when it is seen as coming from self, it leads to pride. The verse therefore trains the eye: when you see your herds at evening, say, this is from Allah.

**Linguistic Note On Jamāl And The Daily Cycle**

Jamāl in Arabic denotes beauty that is pleasing in appearance and proportion, distinct from ḥusn which can include inner goodness, and from zīnah which often denotes added adornment. The Qur'an uses jamāl only twice, here and in 15:16 where it describes stars as beautiful. Both uses connect beauty to creation that guides or sustains. Turīḥūna and tasraḥūna are both in the form IV with plural address, indicating a habitual, collective action. The pronouns lakum and fīhā anchor the blessing: for you, in them. The benefit is not abstract; it is located in the animals themselves and directed to you. The verse's brevity — a single sentence — mirrors the simplicity of the scene it describes: evening, morning, herds, pleasure. No theological term is needed. The image does the work.
