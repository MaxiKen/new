## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:26

> They turn others away from the Prophet and distance themselves as well. They ruin none but themselves, yet they fail to perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Refusals in One Sentence**

The verse describes a double movement: turning others away from the Prophet, and standing back from him. The first half is social and the second is personal, and together they form a policy rather than a mood. The leaders of Makkah did not simply decline the message; they made sure the people around them did not get near it, and then kept their own distance so that no association with the Prophet could be held against them.

The Book records the operational version of this policy in another sūrah: the disbelievers advised one another not to listen to the Qur'an, but to drown it out with noise so that they might prevail (41:26). That is a meeting, a decision, and a method. Noise was the earliest instrument of censorship available in Makkah, and the verse in front of us describes the two halves of the same decision, one aimed outward and one aimed inward.

**Why Blocking the Road Was a Business Decision**

Makkan hostility to the message was not detached theological dislike. The standing of the city rested on the sanctuary, the pilgrimage seasons, and the network of shrines and offerings that made Quraysh a broker between tribes. A message that made the idols functionally worthless threatened revenue, prestige, and the regional arrangement in which the clan's authority was grounded. The Book describes the pattern in general terms when it speaks of those who hinder others from the way of Allah, striving to make it appear crooked (14:3), which is deliberate distortion rather than passive unbelief.

The refusals in the verse therefore belong to people with something to protect. The tribal notables were not free to accept the Prophet without dismantling the basis of their own standing, and the sūrah's argument repeatedly exposes the interests behind the doctrine.

**The Reading That Points to Abū Ṭālib**

Some early exegetes read the first clause differently, taking the thing forbidden to be harm to the Prophet rather than the message itself, and applying the verse to Abū Ṭālib, the Prophet's uncle and early protector, who shielded him from the notables while not accepting the message. The interpretation is transmitted from several commentators.

Its weakness is the sentence that follows. The verse censures those it names as people who ruin none but themselves, and shielding the Prophet was a service, not a ruin. The commentators who report the Abū Ṭālib reading also note that it sits awkwardly in a passage aimed throughout at the leaders who persecuted the Prophet, and the Shia tradition rejects the attribution altogether, holding that Abū Ṭālib died a Muslim. The obstructionist reading is the stronger one, because it gives both halves of the verse a single hostile meaning and keeps the censure where the passage has placed it.

**"They Ruin None but Themselves"**

The verse then turns the policy back on those who designed it. Whatever they believed they were protecting by keeping the message away from their people, the Book reports the loss as their own. The followers they turned away are not excused, and the leaders are told elsewhere that they will carry their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment along with some of the burdens of those they misled without knowledge (16:25).

A principle stated repeatedly in the Book holds the two truths together: no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another (6:164; 17:15; 35:18). The led answer for their own choices and the leaders answer for theirs, which include the choices they cultivated by blocking the road. What no leader can do is hand his loss to the people he obstructed.

**Why the Damage Stays Invisible**

The closing phrase explains how such a policy survives for years: they fail to perceive it. Nobody assembles in a council and resolves to ruin himself. Those notables believed they were defending the standing of the city, the customs of their fathers, and the position of their families. The Book's diagnosis is that a man can spend a lifetime defending an arrangement without noticing that the arrangement is consuming him.

That is what makes the verse permanent rather than local. Every generation produces people who cannot be shown their own losses because the losses are dressed as loyalty. Naming the damage plainly is a mercy toward them, and the difficulty of the verse is that the people it describes would not have recognised the description as theirs.

**The Barrier That Trapped Its Builders**

A wall built across a road keeps other people out, and it also fixes the builder on one side of it. The notables who turned others from the Prophet had committed themselves to a position they could no longer leave without humiliation, since a man who spends years discrediting a message cannot quietly accept it afterwards without conceding that his whole campaign was wrong. The Book describes the same trap elsewhere in the story of those who planned their strategies and found that God's own planning was ahead of it (86:16).

This is the practical sense in which they ruined none but themselves. Their followers could still hear the message from another quarter, and many of them eventually did, while the leaders had staked their authority on the message being false and were therefore unable to reconsider it. The turn away in the verse is not only an act they performed on others; it is the door they closed on themselves.

A question hangs over the sentence. If the notables turned the people away and the people went, what becomes of the ones who obeyed? The Book answers elsewhere by distinguishing those who followed without knowledge from those who taught them error, and it assigns responsibility to both rather than excusing either. This verse names only one party because its subject is the turning away itself.

What it leaves is the picture of a road with a barrier across it, guarded by men who think the barrier is protecting them. The verses that follow turn to a different group, people who came to argue after listening, carrying conclusions that had been settled before they arrived, and the two passages describe the same city from two floors of the same building.
