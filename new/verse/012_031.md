## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:31

> When she heard about their gossip, she invited them and set a banquet for them. She gave each one a knife, then said ˹to Joseph˺, “Come out before them.” When they saw him, they were so stunned ˹by his beauty˺ that they cut their hands, and exclaimed, “Good God! This cannot be human; this must be a noble angel!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Her Response to Gossip Is an Invitation**

The woman does not deny, explain, withdraw or protest. Hearing that the city's leading women are discussing her, she hosts them. The choice is characteristic and it is strategic: an accusation answered in words can be answered again, but an accusation answered with a demonstration settles the argument in a single moment and in front of the accusers themselves. She converts her defence into an event. The commentators note that the reports put her guest list at forty women, of whom five had been the source of the talk, and that the setting was a meal with fruit and knives distributed to each guest. The detail of the knives is what makes the scene possible, and the verse is careful to record that she gave them out before she called Joseph in.

**The Order of Operations Is Recorded Deliberately**

Three actions in sequence: she prepared the banquet, she gave each woman a knife, she said come out before them. The knives precede the entrance. A reader who knows the order understands that the women were holding blades when the object of the city's gossip walked into the room, and that their hands were already occupied with cutting. Nothing in the verse suggests she intended an injury. What the arrangement guarantees is that any loss of self-possession among her guests will be visible, physical and immediate. She has constructed a test of her own, and the test measures not Joseph's conduct but her guests' composure. The sūrah reports the preparation without comment, which leaves the reader to notice what the hosts of the banquet did not.

**The Beauty of Joseph Is a Reported Fact, Not a Described One**

The Qur'an never describes Joseph's appearance. No adjective for his face, no comparison, no measurement. What the text supplies instead is the effect of his appearance on other people, and it does so repeatedly. The tradition adds a statement of its own: the Prophet ﷺ reported meeting Joseph in the third heaven during the Night Journey (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 164a), and the narrations of that journey describe him as having been given half of beauty — understood by the commentators either as half the beauty that exists among people, or as half of a beauty whose remainder belongs to someone the speaker excluded from the comparison out of humility. The chapter's method is more economical than any description: it shows a room of women cutting their own hands and calling him an angel, and lets the reader infer what it declines to state.

**They Cut Their Hands Without Noticing**

The verb form indicates that they cut themselves larger than the wound required, and the commentators gloss it as a complete loss of ordinary self-awareness: they were cutting fruit, saw him, and the knife continued while the attention went elsewhere. Al-Maybudī's image is that the light of his beauty struck their inner sight and they fell into bewilderment. Whether or not the exaggeration is literal, the narrative function is exact. The women who said she was in manifest error for desiring this young man have, within a minute of seeing him, wounded themselves for looking. Their verdict of verse 30 is reversed not by argument but by their own hands, and the reversal is public, physical and impossible for them to explain away afterwards.

**"This Is Not a Human Being" Is Also an Acquittal**

The exclamation has two functions and the commentators attend to both. The first is amazement at God's creative power — *ḥāsha lillāh*, God be praised, God forbid — used here as an expression of astonishment at the existence of something so unlike the ordinary. The second is exculpatory. By likening Joseph to a noble angel the women place him outside the range of ordinary human appetite; an angelic being does not commit lewdness. Al-Rāzī draws the inference explicitly: their vision of him made clear to them that he was untainted by the passions that characterise the human condition, which means the accusation their own gossip repeated has just been withdrawn by the gossips. The verse therefore completes the case the shirt began. The husband found him truthful in private; the city now finds him incapable of the act in public.

**The Scene Is Constructed, and the Construction Is the Point**

What the woman staged is a demonstration of her own excuse, and it works so well that she says so in the next verse. But the demonstration has a second result she did not arrange: it establishes Joseph's innocence before the entire female establishment of the city, in a room she filled and a moment she chose. The chapter allows her plan to succeed at what she intended and to fail at what she could not control. This is the same structure that governs the brothers' plot — a scheme that achieves its immediate object while producing an outcome its authors never contemplated — and it is why the sūrah can describe concealment and manipulation as real forces without ever treating them as decisive. The knives were hers. The hands were theirs. What was proved in the room was not what she had planned to prove.

The wounds are not forgotten afterwards. When Joseph is finally summoned out of the prison, he refuses to leave until the case is examined, and the case he names is this one: *“Go back to your master and ask him about the case of the women who cut their hands. Surely my Lord has ˹full˺ knowledge of their cunning”* (12:50). The evidence he asks the king to investigate is not the torn shirt or the witness but the banquet, because that is where the city's own women testified against themselves with their hands. The reports describe the fruit they were given as citrons, which required a knife and attention; the verse records only the cutting. Either way the scene produces a group of witnesses who cannot afterwards claim that the woman's desire was unfounded or that Joseph was a man capable of the act he was accused of, and it is that group Joseph sends the king to.
