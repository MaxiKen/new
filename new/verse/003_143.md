## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:143

> You certainly wished ˹for the opportunity˺ for martyrdom before encountering it, now you have seen it with your own eyes.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Who the Verse Addresses**

Al-Ṭabarī reports that the verse is understood to address believers who had not taken part in the Battle of Badr and who had wished for an opportunity to fight the idolaters in the way of God. The verse reminds them that the wish they had expressed was now being answered in the hardest possible form, and it does so without any softening of the contrast between the hope and the event.

The address is therefore not to cowards being taunted but to people who had asked for something and then met it. The sūrah's method in the passage is to hold the community to its own words, and this verse does so in two short sentences whose brevity gives them their force.

**Longing for Death and What It Meant**

The commentators explained the longing for death as a longing for the fighting that could lead to it. The wish was for the honour and the test, not for extinction, and the Book's language reflects the way such wishes were expressed by people who expected a decisive engagement.

That understanding keeps the verse from a misunderstanding that has sometimes attached to it. The Book does not praise the desire to die as such; it addresses the gap between what a person declares when the danger is distant and how he behaves when the danger arrives. The verse is a reminder about that gap, and the community's own history supplied the illustration.

**The Emphatic Reading**

One reading of the closing phrase takes it as a form of emphasis comparable to telling a person that he heard something with his own ears. On this reading the sentence means that the community did not merely hear about death or see it from far off; the matter was placed before its eyes.

The Book uses this kind of insistence when it wants a point to be undeniable, and the point here is that the opportunity the community had wished for was granted. The listeners could not claim afterward that they had been spared the test they had asked for, since the test had come to them in the plainest form.


Al-Qurṭūbī records other ways of taking the phrase. On one, the community saw death with an untrammeled vision, without any barrier between itself and the fact of it. On another, the words carry an implied question: the community had seen the fighting face to face, so why did it fall back?

The second reading connects the verse directly with the rout that the sūrah describes later. On that understanding the sentence is not only a reminder about a wish but an inquiry into the conduct of the day, and the question is left unanswered in the verse so that the next verse can supply the remedy.

**The Retreat at Uḥud**

The sūrah's later verses refer to those who turned back on their heels, and the commentators connected the two passages. During the fighting, the ranks broke, and some of the believers left their positions and withdrew.

The verse's closing words therefore function as a mirror held up to a specific afternoon. The community that had wished for a chance to prove itself had its chance, and the record of what it did with that chance is preserved in the sūrah rather than passed over, and the sūrah draws the lesson for the community that would come after it. The Book's willingness to preserve such material is part of what makes its account of the community's formation credible.

**Wishing for Death in the Book's Other Passages**

The Book addresses the subject of wishing for death in other places as well, and in those passages the wish is treated as a claim that a person is invited to substantiate. It tells those who assert that the next life is theirs alone to wish for death if their claim is true, and it tells others who spoke about their fallen brothers that they should try to avoid death if what they say is true.

The pattern is consistent across the Book. A claim about one's standing is tested by whether the person is prepared to accept the consequences of it. The verse before the reader belongs to the same family of statements, and its application to the community at Madinah was immediate.

**The Difference Between a Wish and a Turning**

The verse exposes a distinction that the community had not fully appreciated: a wish expressed in safety is not the same act as a decision made in danger. Both are real, but one costs nothing and the other costs everything.

The Book does not treat the wish itself as hypocrisy. Al-Ṭabarī's report describes people who wanted an opportunity, and the verse acknowledges the wish while insisting on the difference between it and the event. What the sūrah does with the difference is to teach the community how to read its own record, not to condemn the people who had expressed a hope, since the verse acknowledges the wish as something that had genuinely been felt.

**The Verse's Function in the Passage**

The verse stands between the question about the garden and the announcement that the Prophet is only a messenger. It provides the concrete memory the community needed in order to hear both of those statements properly.

The sequence gives the passage its emotional shape. First the community is asked what it expected, then it is reminded of what it had asked for, and then it is told the truth about the messenger it had followed. The verses work together as an examination of the community's assumptions, and the sūrah proceeds from that examination to the instructions that follow, which turn the community's attention from the memory of the field to the conduct of its ordinary affairs.
