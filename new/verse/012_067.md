## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:67

> He then instructed ˹them˺, “O my sons! Do not enter ˹the city˺ all through one gate, but through separate gates. I cannot help you against ˹what is destined by˺ Allah in the least. It is only Allah Who decides. In Him I put my trust. And in Him let the faithful put their trust.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Instruction, Practical in Form**

The oath of verse 66 concerned whether Benjamin would travel. This verse concerns how the party will arrive. Jacob gives a specific operational direction: do not enter by one gate, enter by separate gates. The instruction is concrete and it is about a city's architecture — Egypt's capital had multiple gates, and eleven men arriving together through one of them would be conspicuous. The chapter places the direction immediately after the covenant, which means the reader receives both the religious bond and the practical measure as parts of a single act of fatherly care. The pairing is characteristic of the man. Jacob secures an oath and then gives directions about gates, and the chapter places both in his mouth without distinguishing their weight. Care for his sons' souls and care for their physical safety are treated as one continuous responsibility, which is the same understanding of providence Joseph has been working from in Egypt since verse 47.

**The Reading That Names the Evil Eye**

The commentators' primary explanation is protection from the evil eye. In the traditional cultures of the Mediterranean and Near East the evil eye is associated with harm that reaches a person because he is an object of envy, and Jacob's sons were conspicuously handsome. One account adds that they had already fallen victim to it on their previous visit to Egypt. Entering by different gates would disperse the group, make their arrival less noticeable, and reduce the exposure to envious attention. The reading is not exotic; it belongs to a world in which a band of well-favoured strangers attracts attention that can turn into harm, and a father who has already lost one son takes that possibility seriously.

**The Reading That Names Ordinary Caution**

Other commentators decline the evil-eye explanation and read the instruction as straightforward prudence. The brothers had become recognisable after their first journey, and a large group of foreign men entering a famine-stricken capital together might attract hostility, suspicion or jealousy. Dispersal is the obvious countermeasure. On this reading the instruction is about security rather than about envy, and it belongs to the same category as the oath: a father taking every available step to protect a son he is obliged to send. Both readings are recorded in the tradition and the Qur'an's wording does not decide between them, which is characteristic of a verse reporting an action without reporting a motive. The ambiguity is preserved rather than resolved. Jacob's instruction is reported in his own words, and the reason attached to them concerns God's judgment, not crowds, gates or security. Whatever practical wisdom lies behind dividing eleven travellers at a city entrance, the verse declines to make it the explanation, and it declines without denying it either.

**"I Cannot Avail You Anything Against God"**

The clause is the verse's centre and it qualifies the instruction before the instruction has been acted on. Jacob tells his sons what to do and tells them, in the same breath, that the doing cannot protect them from what God has decreed. Nothing is withdrawn by the qualification. The precaution stands; its efficacy is limited and the limit is stated by the person imposing it. This is the chapter's clearest formulation of a distinction Islamic thought has returned to constantly: the taking of means is obligatory, and the outcome of means is not in the hands of the person who takes them. Jacob does not resolve the tension between the two claims. He holds both in one sentence.

**"Judgment Belongs to God Alone"**

*In al-ḥukm illā li-Llāh* — the decision is God's and nobody else's. The same formula appears in Joseph's own preaching, where it grounds the demand for exclusive worship: *“It is only Allah Who decides. He has commanded that you worship none but Him”* (12:40). Father and son use identical words about divine authority in different registers — Joseph in a theological argument with two prisoners, Jacob in a practical instruction to eleven sons. The repetition is one of several that tie the two halves of the chapter together, and it shows that the conviction Jacob teaches is the conviction Joseph preaches. The correspondence is exact in substance. Joseph told two prisoners that judgment belongs to God alone and that He commanded the worship of none other; his father, sending ten sons into a famine, states the same principle as the ground of his own composure. Neither knows the other has said it, and the chapter arranges for the reader to hear both.

**Trust Stated and Then Generalised**

The verse closes with two clauses: *upon God I rely*, and *let those who rely, rely upon Him*. The first is personal; the second turns it into a rule addressed to believers generally. The movement from the singular statement to the general injunction is characteristic of prophetic speech in the Qur'an, and here it converts a father's parting words into a formulation available to every reader. The chapter has shown trust tested in a well, in a prison and now in a departure from home with a youngest son. The generalisation at the end of verse 67 is the sūrah's way of stating what those tests have produced: not a private consolation but a principle. The generalisation is what lifts the instruction out of its occasion. A father protecting his sons in Egypt is a particular case; the principle that judgment belongs to God alone is not, and it is the principle Joseph has already used to refuse a prison door, to answer two dreamers and to decline a king's first summons. The verse gives the family's shared conviction in a form that outlasts the journey.

**The Verse Is Followed Immediately by Its Own Limitation**

Verse 68 begins by reporting that the brothers entered as their father had commanded and then states that it did them no good whatever against God. The chapter therefore places the instruction and its ineffectiveness in adjacent verses. Nothing in verse 67 is contradicted — Jacob said himself that he could not avail them anything — but the sequel confirms it in the narrator's voice. The precaution was taken, the outcome was unaffected, and the trial that followed came anyway. That arrangement is what makes verse 67 a statement about trust rather than about technique: the means are prescribed, performed and shown to be insufficient, all within two verses. That structure makes the verse a statement about reliance rather than about technique. Entering by separate gates is presented as obedience to a father's instruction, and the verse that follows records that it availed nothing against God's decree. What survives the failure is the intention, and the chapter preserves it by showing the measure taken seriously and then exceeded.

**Eleven Men Dispersed at Their Father's Word**

The practical content of the instruction deserves a moment. Eleven travellers arriving at a city's walls would naturally enter together, since travelling as a group is safer in a famine and the group carries the documents, the money and the cargo. Jacob asks them to separate at precisely the point where separation is least convenient, and the chapter records their compliance without comment in verse 68. That compliance is itself informative: these are men who once disposed of a brother and lied about it, and here they follow an instruction whose purpose their father has explained only partly. The oath of verse 66 and the obedience of verse 68 together describe a family that has been changed by what happened at the well, even before the change is complete.
