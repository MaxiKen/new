## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:33

> Say, “My Lord has only forbidden open and secret indecencies, sinfulness, unjust aggression, associating ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺—a practice He has never authorized—and attributing to Allah what you do not know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The List of the Forbidden Things**

The verse gives the Prophet the statement of what his Lord has actually forbidden, and the list that follows contains five items. The enumeration answers the audience's system of prohibitions, and it presents the divine commands in their true scope. The Book provides similar lists in the sūrah of the Cattle and the sūrah of the Night Journey, where the prohibitions are set out in sequence, and the passage in front of us belongs with them.

The length of the list is part of its message. The audience had surrounded its diet, its pilgrimage, and its livestock with rules that it attributed to God, and the replacement list is short and specific. The sūrah's next verses state that the terms of the communities are fixed and that the messengers are sent with the revelation, and the brief list in front of us is the standard against which the community is measured.

**Open and Secret Indecencies**

The first items are the shameful deeds committed openly and in secret, and the phrase covers both the public act and the hidden intention that precedes it. The Book's treatment of the two categories appears in the sūrah of the Cattle, where the prohibition of open and secret indecency is stated in its own list, and in the sūrah of the Night Journey, where the prohibition of approaching indecency is joined to the prohibition of killing and of taking the orphan's property.

The pairing of the outward act and the inward intention gives the verse its reach. The audience could regulate its public conduct while leaving its intention unexamined, and the verse includes both. The sūrah's teaching about the heart and the outward act appears in its treatment of the two groups that the previous verses described.

**Sin and Unjust Aggression**

The second and third items are sin and unjust aggression, and the commentary tradition explains the first as the transgression of the divine limits and the second as the oppression of people by the misuse of power. The Book's descriptions of aggression appear in its accounts of the communities that used their strength against the weak, and the sūrah's narratives of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh supply the examples. The term also carries the sense of arrogance, and the sūrah's account of the refusal of the adversary uses the same language.

The two items together cover the wrongs done against God's limits and the wrongs done against people. The Book's lists of the major sins generally include both categories, and the sūrah's earlier verses on the weighing of deeds and the questioning of the messengers place the judgment of both in the reckoning.

**Associating Partners With God**

The fourth item is the association of partners with God in worship, and the verse states that no authority has been sent down for it. The Book identifies this sin as the one that is not forgiven if a person dies without repenting, and the sūrah of the Women states the principle twice in its passage on the matter. The sūrah's own argument has been directed at the association from its opening verses, and the narrative of the adversary has shown the first refusal that produced it.

The phrase about the absence of authority returns to the challenge that the verse has made to the audience's claims. The partners are worshipped without any warrant, and the Book's question about what knowledge the audience possesses is answered by the statement that no authority has come down for them. The passage in front of us places the association at the head of the forbidden things in a chapter that has devoted its narratives and arguments to it.

**Attributing to God What Is Not Known**

The fifth item is the attribution to God of what people do not know, and the phrase covers the false claims about His commands that the audience made in the previous verses. The sūrah's answer to the claim that God commanded the shameful deeds has already been given, and the verse in front of us names the practice itself among the forbidden things. The Book's warnings about fabricating lies against God appear in the sūrah of the Cattle, the sūrah of the Table, and the sūrah of the Heights itself, and the sūrah's later verses name those who do it among the greatest wrongdoers.

The prohibition closes the list where the passage began, since the audience's false attributions were the source of the rules that the sūrah has been correcting. The reader is shown the connection between the invented prohibition and the false report about God, and the sūrah's argument against both is complete.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands thirty-third in the chapter, and it follows the question about the good things and precedes the statement about the appointed term of every community. The passage on dress, food, and prohibition is thus closed by the list of what is actually forbidden, and the sūrah then continues with the warning about the term and the messengers.

The reader has been given the permission, the limit, the provision's destination, and the list of the forbidden things. The sūrah's later narratives show the communities that transgressed the list and the messengers who delivered it, and the reckoning that the opening verses described awaits both.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that every community has an appointed term that can neither be delayed nor advanced (7:34). The statement of the forbidden things is followed by the statement of the fixed term, and the passage then turns to the messengers who come to the children of Adam.

The two verses together set the boundaries and the duration in which they apply. The community that has received the revelation has a term in which to follow it, and the verse in front of us states that the term is not subject to negotiation.
