## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:81

> And He ˹is the One Who˺ will cause me to die, and then bring me back to life.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Death Belongs to the Same Lord as Creation**

Abraham's account of God moves from creation and provision to death. The transition is deliberate. The Lord who gives life does not lose authority when life reaches its appointed end. Death is not an outside power that interrupts God's rule. It occurs under the command of the One who created the person and sustained him throughout his days.

This conviction changes the meaning of mortality. Abraham does not speak as though death is a rival deity or a meaningless accident. He places it within the relation between servant and Lord. The human being receives life for a time, then returns to the One who gave it. Idols cannot claim that authority because they neither began Abraham's life nor determine its true end.

**The One Who Ends Life Also Restores It**

The verse joins death and renewed life in one sentence. The pairing rejects the idea that death is an absolute disappearance. If God causes death, then death remains within His power; if He brings life again, resurrection is not a problem for the Creator. The second act is not harder for Him than the first. Both belong to the same sovereign command.

Abraham's wording also preserves continuity between the person who dies and the person who is raised. The God who knows and sustains the individual in this life is able to restore him after death. Resurrection is therefore not the creation of an unrelated replacement. It is the return of a servant to the Lord before whom his life already stood.

**Resurrection Makes the Present Life Accountable**

Life would have a different moral weight if death were the final boundary. Abraham's mention of being brought back to life places every choice under a future horizon. Worship, truthfulness, oppression, and repentance are not matters that vanish when the body is buried. The Creator who gives life again will also reveal what the servant made of the life first given.

This is why resurrection belongs in Abraham's argument against idolatry. A powerless object cannot judge the dead or restore them. A shrine may preserve a memory, but it cannot call a person from the grave. Only the Lord who governs life and death can make the moral history of a human being reach its appointed judgment.

**Death Is Not Proof of Divine Abandonment**

People often experience death as loss, silence, and separation. Abraham does not deny that pain. He gives it a theological place. The Lord remains the Lord over the servant even when the servant can no longer act, speak, or protect himself. Death removes human control, but it does not remove divine knowledge.

That belief can produce grief without despair. The dead are not imagined as self-sustaining, and the living are not asked to pretend that parting is easy. They are asked to trust that the One who receives the life of a servant has not ceased to govern it. Death becomes a passage under God's authority rather than evidence that the world has fallen outside it.

**The Personal Pronoun Keeps the Doctrine Near**

Abraham says that God will cause me to die and bring me back to life. He does not hide behind a general statement about humanity. The doctrine touches his own body, his own future, and his own accountability. Personal faith becomes stronger when it includes the self rather than remaining a claim made about other people.

The pronoun also prevents resurrection from becoming a distant theory. Abraham expects to stand within the event he describes. His worship is shaped by the certainty that the Lord's power concerns him directly. Each believer must eventually make the same recognition: the One who gives life to the world will also address me as a particular servant.

**Death Breaks the Illusion of Self-Sufficiency**

Food and drink can make a person feel independent because daily needs are often met through familiar means. Death exposes the limit of that confidence. No amount of wealth, skill, medicine, or social power can make a creature its own source of life. The hour of death reveals what was true from the beginning: the human being is dependent.

Abraham does not use this dependence to demean human effort. He places effort in its proper scale. A person may care for the body and plan for the future, but he cannot grant himself immortality. The Lord alone controls the boundary that no created means can cross. Remembering death therefore turns competence into humility rather than pride.

**The Two Acts Announce a Complete Divine Care**

God's care is not limited to keeping Abraham alive. It includes receiving him at death and restoring him for the final meeting. The full arc of the servant's existence remains in God's hands. This makes divine care larger than comfort in the present. It reaches through the grave and gives the future a meaning that the idols cannot supply.

A parallel prayer in 14:39 praises God for granting Abraham children in old age. That account and this verse share the same outlook: human life is received at every stage, whether beginning, continuing, ending, or being restored. Abraham's worship is therefore grounded in the Lord's complete authority over the servant's journey.

**The Lord of Life and Death Is Worthy of Worship**

The verse completes a direct test of the idols. They cannot create Abraham, guide him, provide for him, heal him, end his life, or restore him. They are surrounded by worship, but they have no share in the acts that define the servant's existence. The Lord alone performs them and therefore alone deserves ultimate allegiance.

Death and resurrection together leave no room for a divided trust. Abraham's people must choose whether their hope rests on powerless objects or on the One who governs the whole passage from life to death and back to life. The choice is not between competing styles of devotion. It is between reality and an object that cannot act.
