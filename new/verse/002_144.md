## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:144

> Indeed, We see you ˹O Prophet˺ turning your face towards heaven. Now We will make you turn towards a direction ˹of prayer˺ that will please you. So turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque ˹in Mecca˺—wherever you are, turn your faces towards it. Those who were given the Scripture certainly know this to be the truth from their Lord. And Allah is never unaware of what they do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet's Turning**

The verse opens with an observation of the Prophet's conduct: he was looking up at the sky, and the Book reports that God had seen it. The gesture is one of waiting, and the sūrah presents the change of direction as an answer to it rather than as an administrative decision.

The commentators described the situation that produced the waiting. The Prophet ﷺ was displeased with the taunt that he followed the direction of the Jewish community and shared its religion, and he hoped for a direction of his own. Other reports add that he loved the House of Abraham and desired that the community's prayer be directed toward it. The verse records the hope and the answer in a single sentence.

**The Direction That Would Please Him**

The middle of the sentence states the answer: he would be turned toward a direction that would satisfy him, and the Book then names it. The phrase joins the divine decision to the servant's desire in a way that is unusual in the Book, and the commentators noted it as a mark of the regard in which the Prophet ﷺ was held.

The direction named is the Sacred Mosque, and the sūrah's earlier passages have told the reader who built it, who purified it, and what prayers were offered at its foundation. The command to face it is therefore the completion of the narrative the sūrah has been developing, and the change of direction is presented as a return to the practice of Abraham rather than as a departure from the practice of the earlier prophets.

**Wherever You Are**

The command is extended beyond the Prophet ﷺ to the community and to every place: wherever they are, they are to turn their faces toward the House. The Book states the instruction three times in the space of a few verses, and the repetitions emphasise a matter on which the community would have had questions.

The extension also gives the direction a universal character. The earlier direction had been a place associated with one community's history; the new one is a site connected to the founder of the community that both earlier groups honoured, and the command applies wherever a Muslim may be, from the region of the sanctuary to the far reaches of the earth.

**The Knowledge of Those Given the Scripture**

The sentence states that those who were given the Scripture know this to be the truth from their Lord, and the commentators explained the clause as a statement about their recognition of the Prophet's standing rather than only about the direction of prayer. Their books contained the marks of the messenger who was to come, and the sūrah has already described them recognising him as they recognise their own children.

The statement removes the objection from the realm of scholarship and places it in that of honesty. The two communities were not arguing from a position of uncertainty about the matter; the Book presents them as knowing, and the verse's closing clause about God's awareness of what they do turns the discussion back to conduct.

**The Report of the Turn in Prayer**

The change is associated with a mosque near Madinah where a messenger is reported to have arrived during the prayer with the announcement that the direction had been changed, and the congregation turned during the prayer itself to face the new direction. The building has been known since then as the mosque of the two directions.

The report illustrates how the community received the instruction, and it also shows the character of the practice. The direction of prayer is a command to be followed rather than a subject for deliberation, and a community that understands it that way can alter its orientation in the middle of an act of worship without the worship being interrupted.

**Prayer and Community Identity**

The direction of prayer carries a social meaning in addition to its ritual one, since the Book and the reports treat the qiblah as one of the marks distinguishing the community. A well-known report states that whoever prays the community's prayer, faces its direction, and eats what it slaughters is a Muslim under the protection of God and His Messenger.

The verse's insistence on the new direction therefore concerns identity as well as worship. The community was being given a distinctive orientation, and the sūrah's earlier account of the two communities' claims about Abraham furnishes the reason: the direction of the House of Abraham belongs to the religion of Abraham, and the community that follows him faces what he built.

**The Closing Attribute**

The sentence ends with the statement that God is never unaware of what the people do, and the sūrah uses this attribute at the end of several of its charges against this community. Here it follows the statement about their knowledge, so that the reader understands that their recognition and their concealment are both seen.

The attribute also applies to the community's own response. The believers who turned toward the new direction and the groups that objected are both within the same knowledge, and the verse leaves the reader with the sense that nothing in the dispute escapes notice.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence gives the community a direction, states the authority behind it, and reports the knowledge of those who objected. What it does not do is argue the case at length, since the sūrah has supplied the material in the preceding passages. The reader who has followed the account of the House from its foundations to Abraham's prayers is expected to recognise the direction as the answer to a petition offered long before the community existed, and the verse's brevity assumes that recognition.
