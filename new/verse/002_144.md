## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:144

> Indeed, We see you ˹O Prophet˺ turning your face towards heaven. Now We will make you turn towards a direction ˹of prayer˺ that will please you. So turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque ˹in Mecca˺—wherever you are, turn your faces towards it. Those who were given the Scripture certainly know this to be the truth from their Lord. And Allah is never unaware of what they do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Face Lifted to Heaven, Seen and Answered**

The verse begins with an image unique in the Qur'an's legal passages: God watching the Prophet's face turn upward in longing. *We see you turning your face towards heaven* — the gesture of a man who hopes for a revelation he has not been given, who cannot ask in words for what he wants and so asks with his whole posture. And the answer matches the gesture: *Now We will make you turn towards a direction that will please you*. The phrasing is tender in a way legislative verses rarely are — the new qiblah is defined not first by its sanctity or its history but by the fact that it will please the one who hoped for it. The sequence teaches something about prophethood that the polemics around the qiblah had obscured: the Prophet does not legislate his preferences, but God attends to them, and when the preference is for God's House the answer comes as revelation rather than initiative.

**Why He Hoped**

The exegetes preserve several accounts of the hope behind the lifted face, and they compose rather than conflict. Al-Ṭabarī reports that the Jews of Medina were saying, "He follows us in our religion and uses our qiblah" — and the Prophet was displeased by their arrogance, not because the direction was wrong but because the boast made obedience look like discipleship to men. Al-Rāzī adds two further motives: love for the House of Abraham, the sanctuary his forefather in faith had built, and the hope that the Arab tribes would incline toward Islam if their ancestral sanctuary became the direction of prayer. On the origin of the Jerusalem orientation itself the sources differ: a few hold that the Prophet chose it on his own judgment to build a bond with Medina's Jewish population, while most maintain that he was instructed by God to face Jerusalem and then commanded to change. The majority reading is the stronger, because the verse's own logic — a divinely seen longing answered by a divinely decreed turn — presumes that both directions were decrees, and because the sūrah treats the earlier qiblah as something God assigned (2:143), not as a diplomatic choice.

**From Your Face to Your Faces**

The command moves in one verse from the singular to the plural: *turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque* — and then, *wherever you are, turn your faces towards it*. The first clause establishes the Prophet's own direction; the second universalizes it across the community and across geography. "Wherever you are" is deliberately unqualified: the command travels with the believer to every climate and coordinate, which is what made the qiblah a problem in spherical geometry rather than a local custom. The object is the Sacred Mosque — not merely the Kaʿbah's stone cube but the sanctuary around it, described in Āl ʿImrān as the first House of worship established for humanity at Bakkah, a blessed sanctuary and a guide for all people (3:96). The community is turned, in other words, toward the oldest house of God on earth — the House whose foundation Abraham and Ishmael raised, and whose claim to precedence the sūrah has already argued (2:127).

**A Command Given Before the Mosque Was Free**

One historical fact sharpens the verse: at the moment this command was revealed, the Sacred Mosque was in the hands of the very idolaters who had expelled the community — and would remain so for several more years, until the conquest of Mecca in the eighth year after the emigration. The believers were ordered to face a sanctuary they were barred from, held by enemies who had profaned it with idols. The command therefore functioned as prophecy and promise before it functioned as geography: to pray toward the Kaʿbah while the Kaʿbah was occupied was to enact the certainty that the House would return to those who faced it. The qiblah became a daily rehearsal of a future the community could not yet see, which is one reason the change could serve as the sorting test described in the previous verse — only those guided by Allah could keep praying toward a distant, occupied, promised direction.

**The People of the Book Know**

The verse then turns on the objectors with an assertion about their knowledge: *Those who were given the Scripture certainly know this to be the truth from their Lord*. Al-Ṭabarī explains the content of what they know: they know that Mecca is the true qiblah for this community — which means, in his reading, that they know the Prophet is right in his claims generally, so that their opposition to the change of direction is not a scholarly objection but one more instance of intransigence. The knowledge claim rests on the Islamic premise that the earlier scriptures carry descriptions of the coming prophet and his community, including the tokens of his mission. The verse does not argue the point here; it asserts it and moves to the closing clause. The assertion sets up the next verses, which will describe this same recognition in the simile of a father knowing his own children, and the concealment of it by a party among them.

**Never Unaware**

The verse ends with the passage's recurring signature: *And Allah is never unaware of what they do*. The clause answers the specific frustration embedded in the qiblah controversy. The objectors' weapon was speech — boasts, taunts, insinuations that the change of direction proved instability or hankering after homeland — and speech can be deniable, adjustable, aimed at different audiences. The closing clause withdraws that protection: the Speaker behind the revelation is tracking the doings behind the words. For the community the clause is the other kind of assurance, the one it needed when its prayers toward Jerusalem were questioned and its dead were worried over — nothing done in obedience is unseen, and the same surveillance that records the objectors' evil records the believers' turn. The verse thus completes the movement begun two verses earlier: the fools' question was answered theologically (the directions belong to God), the community's rank was declared (witnesses, middle, tested) — and now the longing of the Prophet is satisfied with a direction that will please him, a command binding on all his followers wherever they stand, and a warning to everyone who knows the truth and argues against it anyway.
