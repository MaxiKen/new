## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:23

> And the lady, in whose house he lived, tried to seduce him. She locked the doors ˹firmly˺ and said, “Come to me!” He replied, “Allah is my refuge! It is ˹not right to betray˺ my master, who has taken good care of me. Indeed, the wrongdoers never succeed.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Subject of the Sentence Owns the Building**

The woman is introduced by a relative clause: the one in whose house he was. The construction is legal rather than romantic, and it fixes the power relation before anything happens. Joseph is inside her property, under her authority, in a country where he has no kin, no status and no exit. The verse establishes the setting as a trap rather than an occasion. It also identifies her by position rather than by name — the Qur'an never calls her Zulaykhā, though the Islamic sources commonly do, along with Rāʿīl. The anonymity keeps the scene general: this is not a particular Egyptian woman's failing but the behaviour available to anyone who holds a house and a person inside it.

**Rāwada Describes Pressure, Not a Single Advance**

The verb *rāwadat-hu* comes from a root meaning to seek something from someone persistently, with repeated urging and manoeuvre. It is not the word for a momentary temptation or a single proposition. The form implies an extended campaign, attempts met by refusals and renewed attempts, which is why the Qur'an uses the same verb again in verse 30 for what the city's women say she has been doing over time, and again in verse 51 when she confesses it herself. Joseph's later prayer speaks of what *they invite him to* in the plural and over a period (12:33), confirming that verse 23 opens a process rather than an incident. The chapter's moral realism depends on that reading: what he resists is not one moment of weakness but a sustained effort to wear him down.

**The Doors Are Locked in the Plural**

*Wa-ghallaqati al-abwāb* — she locked the doors — uses a verb form indicating thoroughness and a plural noun. Not one door but the doors of the rooms between them and the household, fastened properly. The detail eliminates any suggestion of opportunity or accident. This is preparation, carried out before the summons, and the plural tells the reader how much of the house she controlled. It also explains why verse 25 has both of them running toward a door: only one remains, and it leads to the person she least wants to meet. The locked doors serve the plot twice, first as her instrument and then as the reason the encounter ends with her husband standing in the only exit.

**"Come to Me" Carries No Argument**

Four words in the translation and three in the Arabic: *hayta lak*. There is no persuasion in them, no promise, no threat, no appeal to feeling. After the prolonged manoeuvring the verb described, the summons itself is bare. That economy is realistic and it is also the reason Joseph's answer can be equally short. Neither party is negotiating. She has moved past argument to command, and he has moved past deliberation to refusal. The verse thus records a collision of wills rather than a debate, which is why nothing said in it addresses the merits. She does not justify herself and he does not reason with her. Both state a position.

**His First Word Is a Refuge, Not a Refusal**

Joseph begins with *maʿādha Llāh* — God be my refuge, I seek shelter in God. The words are not a rejection of her but a relocation of himself. He places the encounter inside a larger frame before answering anything in it, and the frame is God rather than consequence. The same expression returns from his mouth much later, when his brothers ask him to take another man in place of the one accused of theft: *“Allah forbid that we should take other than the one with whom we found our property”* (12:79). Both uses mark the moments where Joseph is asked to do something possible and profitable and answers from a position he cannot be moved out of. The phrase is also the traditional formula of refusal in the Qur'anic idiom, used by the women of the city in verse 51 in a different sense, and its presence here tells the reader that this refusal is not improvised.

**"He Is My Lord" Has Two Referents**

The clause *innahu rabbī* — he is my lord, who has made my lodging good — is the crux of the verse and the exegetes divide on it. On one reading *he* is the ʿAzīz: Potiphar honoured me and I will not betray him. On the other, *he* is God: my true Master is the One who saved me from the well and placed me in this man's favour, and it does not become me to disobey Him. Both readings fit the Arabic and both fit the sentence that follows about the good lodging, which was the ʿAzīz's own instruction in verse 21. The second is the stronger, because Joseph's next clause appeals to God as refuge and because the sūrah elsewhere shows him refusing to rest his case on human favour. The first reading is nevertheless preserved in the tradition and adds a real moral layer: loyalty to a benefactor is itself a reason, and the chapter allows both reasons to stand.

**"The Wrongdoers Never Succeed" Closes a Private Refusal With a Public Law**

The verse ends not with a personal statement but with a general one: *innahu lā yufliḥu al-ẓālimūn*. Wrongdoers do not prosper. The shift from the particular to the universal is the mark of prophetic speech in this chapter, and Joseph will use it again in the prison when he turns a request for interpretation into an argument about God. Here the maxim does specific work. It refuses to treat the proposal as a private transaction between two people in a locked room and reclassifies it as an instance of a category with a known outcome. That reclassification is what makes refusal sustainable. A man who weighs an offer against his desires can lose; a man who weighs it against a law can wait. The sentence also functions as the chapter's verdict on the whole episode, delivered before the event has finished.
