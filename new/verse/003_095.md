## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:95

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah has declared the truth. So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Declaration of Truthfulness**

The verse instructs the Prophet to say that God has spoken the truth, and the statement follows the demonstration about the food laws and the verdict on those who fabricated a claim about God. The declaration is short and its placement gives it weight.

The phrase affirms what the revelation has just reported about Jacob and the Torah. It is also, in the practice of Muslims, a sentence recited when a passage of the Book has been read outside the formal prayer, which shows how the community has taken it. The verse gives the reader a formula for the moment when the truth of what has been recited has just been established.

**Following the Way of Abraham**

The command that follows the declaration is to follow the way of Abraham, and the choice of Abraham at this point in the sūrah's argument is the whole point. The communities addressed had been claiming him, and the passage has shown that the practices they attributed to him did not belong to his lifetime.

What remains of the claim to Abraham when the attributions have been removed is his way, which the verse names and then defines. The Book calls him upright — the word describes a person turned away from falsehood toward what is straight — and adds that he was not one of those who associated others with God. The two halves of the description, positive and negative, are the Book's standard way of identifying his position.

**Why the Word "Upright" Matters**

The Arabic term describes a natural orientation toward the truth, and the commentators understood it as the original disposition on which Abraham was formed. The Book uses it only where an ancestral claim is being settled, which is why it appears here, in the sūrah of the Cow in a comparable command, and in the passage that says Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian but upright and submitting.

The word's function in this sūrah is to move the discussion away from labels and back to a stance before God. A community might dispute which of them Abraham belonged to; the verse answers by naming the thing he actually was. Neither of the two names the sūrah has been discussing covers him, and the name the verse uses is the one that does.

**Submission as the Content of a Legacy**

The Book elsewhere describes Abraham with the vocabulary of submission and reports that God told him to submit, and that he answered that he had submitted to the Lord of all worlds. It also reports his advice to his sons and Jacob's advice to his own sons, both urging the same stance and warning them not to die except in that state.

Read together with the verse above, the picture is of a legacy that is a disposition rather than a set of rituals. The Book's treatment of him consistently separates what he did from what later communities made of him. What is being carried forward in the verse's instruction is a way of facing God, and the sūrah's discussion of the prayer direction and the House of worship a few lines later belongs to the same argument about what may legitimately be called Abraham's practice.

**The Command Addressed to the Community**

The verse is phrased as a command to the Prophet, and its content is for everyone who accepts him. The Book's style here is worth noticing: a command given to the messenger in the second person becomes the community's obligation by virtue of their following him.

The tradition records that the community has taken the verse this way, and the same structure recurs throughout the sūrah where commands addressed to the Prophet concerning belief or fidelity are the community's own instructions in practice. What the community is told to follow is not a person's lineage but a way, and the description of that way in the verse is short enough to be remembered and precise enough to be tested against conduct.

**The Remaining Business of the Passage**

The argument about Abraham's law is now complete. The verse has stated what is true, named the way that remains after the false attributions have been removed, and set aside the two community labels that the dispute was about.

The sūrah then turns to the place where that way was established and the obligation attached to it, and it does so in the language of a house and a pilgrimage. The verse above is the hinge between the two discussions, and its brevity is what makes the turn visible: the reader has left a dispute about texts and arrives at a description of a sanctuary and a practice, with Abraham named at both ends of the transition.

**Declaring and Following: The Verse's Two Verbs**

The sentence contains a statement and a command. In the first, God is described as having spoken the truth; in the second, the listener is told to follow a way. The pairing embodies the Book's treatment of knowledge as something that terminates in movement rather than in agreement.

A person may concede a proposition and remain where he is, and the Book's construction leaves no room for that position. The truth has been stated, and the next word is an imperative. That is why the verse's content is the way of Abraham rather than the family of Abraham: a lineage can be claimed without any change in conduct, and a way cannot. The community reciting the sentence is answering a dispute about ancestry with a commitment about conduct, and the two verbs make the commitment unavoidable.
