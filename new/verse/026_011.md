## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:11

> the people of Pharaoh. Will they not fear ˹Allah˺?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mission Is Directed at Pharaoh's Whole Order**

The second half of the command identifies the wrongdoing people as Pharaoh's people. The name is more than a label for a ruler's household. Pharaoh represents a political order in which authority has become an object of loyalty and oppression has become normal. Moses is not being sent to challenge a private sinner in isolation. He is sent into a society whose public life has been shaped by the ruler's claim and by the harm that claim permits.

The question about fear is therefore placed before any demand about policy. Yattaqūn, translated here as fear, is related to taqwā, a guarded awareness that makes a person protect himself from wrongdoing by remaining conscious of God. The call asks whether this people will develop that moral guard. It is not merely asking them to feel terror. Terror can make a person submit for a moment; taqwā changes what he is willing to do when power and desire press upon him.

**Fear Means Becoming Morally Awake**

Taqwā contains fear, but fear alone does not exhaust it. A person who fears God in the Qur'anic sense recognizes that no rank places him outside judgment. He watches his conduct because he knows that power, wealth, and public praise cannot hide injustice from the One who sees. For Pharaoh's people, this awareness would expose the gap between their splendor and the suffering maintained beneath it.

The question is phrased as a possibility rather than a verdict. Will they not fear? That form leaves a path open. The people are not told that repentance is impossible before the first word of warning reaches them. They are invited to recover a disposition that tyranny has suppressed. A warning can be severe and still be merciful when it asks the wrongdoer to stop before the full consequence arrives.

**The Ruler's Name Does Not Absorb Individual Responsibility**

Calling them Pharaoh's people could tempt them to hide behind the ruler. A subject may say that orders came from above, a courtier may say that policy was already established, and a crowd may say that everyone accepted it. The verse refuses that shelter. Belonging to a political order does not remove the need for personal taqwā. Each person must decide whether he will assist injustice, profit from it, remain silent before it, or resist it within the limits God has given.

At the same time, the verse does not pretend that every person possesses equal power. Pharaoh's officials and the vulnerable Israelites do not stand in the same position. Moral responsibility is measured with knowledge and ability in view. Yet the call still reaches the whole people because every society contains choices, even under pressure. The command names a collective wrong without erasing the different degrees of participation within it.

**The Question Tests Their Idea of Security**

A powerful society often treats security as protection from enemies, loss of status, or economic decline. The question proposes a deeper security: guarding the soul from God's punishment by refusing injustice. That standard reverses Pharaoh's order. The ruler believes his control keeps the kingdom safe, while revelation asks whether the kingdom is safe from the consequences of its own arrogance.

This is why the call to fear God is not an invitation to social passivity. Taqwā demands that a person see the victim as a servant of the same Lord and recognize that domination is not ownership. A courtier who becomes mindful of God may have to stop a command, refuse a lie, or speak a truth that threatens his position. Spiritual fear becomes public ethics when it is real.

**Earlier Calls Use the Same Moral Beginning**

The wording recalls other prophetic summonses in which fear of God comes before obedience. Moses is later told, in 20:43–44, to approach Pharaoh and speak gently while inviting him to remembrance and fear. Gentleness there does not weaken the indictment here. It shows that the purpose of warning is to awaken, not to satisfy anger. A hard truth can be carried in measured speech.

The repeated beginning also links the mission to the basic call of every prophet. Before detailed law, political reform, or debate over signs comes the recognition that God is Lord and that human action is answerable to Him. Pharaoh's people are not exempt because their customs are old or their ruler is strong. Their first duty is the first duty of every community: guard yourselves from injustice by returning to the Lord.

**The Question Leaves a Door Open for Change**

A command that ended with a fixed sentence would announce only doom. This one ends with a question. The question does not guarantee that Pharaoh's people will accept, but it shows that the warning is addressed to their capacity to choose. Even a society formed by oppression can be called to fear God before it is judged.

Moses therefore goes not simply to pronounce a verdict but to make a summons audible inside the court of tyranny. The issue will soon become a confrontation between claims of lordship, yet the first appeal is moral and direct. Will they become guarded, humble, and just before the Lord who gave them time? Their answer will show whether power has erased conscience or merely covered it.

The question also gives the mission a measured tone. Moses is not told to begin by insulting Pharaoh's people or by describing them as incapable of reform. He is told to ask whether they will become mindful. Even when a community is deeply compromised, the invitation to fear God recognizes that people can still change their stance. Warning is strongest when it tells the truth about wrongdoing without turning the wrongdoer into a being beyond repentance.
