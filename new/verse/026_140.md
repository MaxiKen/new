## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:140

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain Interprets the Destruction**

After stating that ʿĀd were destroyed, the verse closes with the names of the Lord: the Almighty, Most Merciful. The judgment is therefore not a chaotic disaster outside divine wisdom. It occurs under the power of the One who sent warning and made repentance possible. The names tell the reader how to understand both the severity and the care in the account.

Power explains why ʿĀd's castles, attacks, and confidence could not protect them. Mercy explains why Hūd came to them as a brother, why he asked for no payment, and why he continued to warn them before the final consequence. Quran 46:24–25 likewise places the approaching punishment after Hūd's warning and describes the remains of ʿĀd's dwellings, reinforcing the contrast between divine power and human security. The refrain holds these meanings together.


ʿĀd measured greatness through landmarks, fortifications, physical strength, and the ability to strike others. The title Almighty dismantles that measure. No created power can compete with the Lord who gives existence to people, land, water, and time.

This does not make human strength meaningless. It makes it dependent. A strong society can act beneficially when it knows that its strength is borrowed and accountable. It becomes dangerous when it treats borrowed power as a rival to the One from whom it came.

**Mercy Came Before the Sentence**

The judgment should not be isolated from the warning that preceded it. Hūd did not arrive after the people had been destroyed; he came while they could still respond. His calls to reverence, obedience, gratitude, and restraint were openings of mercy.

Mercy can be present even when a people refuses it. The fact that ʿĀd rejected the warning does not turn the warning into cruelty. It shows that divine care had been offered and that the people chose to close themselves against it. Their destruction confirms the seriousness of that choice.


Some imagine that mercy requires allowing powerful people to continue harming others. The story gives a fuller account. Mercy toward the oppressed and toward the moral order can require ending the power of those who persist in tyranny. The Almighty's judgment protects the truth that violence is not entitled to continue forever.

Yet human beings must not claim God's complete knowledge for themselves. The refrain calls them to oppose injustice with lawful means and humility, not to declare every personal enemy condemned. Divine mercy includes wisdom about hidden circumstances that no human tribunal fully possesses.

**The Names Correct Two Errors**

One error sees only power and imagines God as a distant force whose judgment gives no room for hope. The other sees only mercy and assumes that no wrongdoing will have a final consequence. The refrain rejects both distortions. God is mighty enough to judge and merciful enough to invite return.

For the believer, this balance produces courage without cruelty. He does not fear tyrants as though they were ultimate, and he does not enjoy judgment as though he were its owner. He trusts the Almighty and seeks the mercy that every servant needs.


The same pair of names closed Noah's account and now closes the account of ʿĀd. The repetition teaches that the Lord of different peoples is one Lord with one consistent moral character. He saves, warns, delays, judges, and forgives in wisdom; the changing communities do not change Him.

The pattern also helps the reader compare responses. Noah's people rejected and were drowned; ʿĀd rejected and were destroyed. The forms of judgment differ, but the lesson is stable: a messenger's warning is mercy, and persistent denial cannot make the Almighty powerless.

**The Refrain Guards Against Worshipping the Story**

A reader might become absorbed in the vanished civilization, the wind, or the ruins. The final names redirect attention. The true subject is not ʿĀd's grandeur but the Lord whose power exposes its limit and whose mercy gave it an opportunity to repent.

This redirection matters because historical fascination can become another form of vanity. People may admire ancient strength while repeating its spiritual error. The refrain asks them to leave the story praising God, not envying the people who were judged.


It is not enough to say that God is Most Merciful while refusing the guidance He sends. Mercy appears in the messenger, the reminder, the provision, and the time given before judgment. The hearer's response is gratitude expressed through obedience.

The title Most Merciful also gives hope to someone who recognizes the pattern in his own life. A person need not wait until security collapses to return. He can abandon vanity, repair harm, and seek forgiveness while the door of repentance remains open. Fear of the Almighty should move him toward the mercy already offered.

**Power and Mercy Meet in Divine Justice**

Divine justice is not a compromise between power and mercy. It is the wise action of the Lord who knows what every person has done and what opportunity each received. ʿĀd's destruction is not evidence that mercy failed; it is evidence that mercy does not erase truth or permit unending oppression.

The human task is to live under both names. Be firm against wrongdoing without becoming tyrannical, and hopeful for repentance without excusing harm. Remember power so that pride falls, and remember mercy so that correction remains compassionate.


The account of ʿĀd ends, but the reader remains. The next community may have different buildings, technologies, and customs, yet it will still receive gifts from the Lord and face the temptation to treat them as proof of independence. The final refrain places every age under the same reality.

Your Lord is Almighty: no false security can resist Him. Your Lord is Most Merciful: no sincere return is meaningless while life remains. To understand the story is to carry both truths into worship, stewardship, and the way power is used.
