## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:86

> How will Allah guide a people who chose to disbelieve after they had believed, acknowledged the Messenger to be true, and received clear proofs? For Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question About Guidance**

The verse asks how God could guide a people who chose disbelief after believing, acknowledged the messenger as truthful, and received clear proofs. The question is rhetorical in form and precise in content: it names the three things the people had, and their presence is what makes the question unanswerable.

The Book's habitual way of explaining the loss of guidance is visible here. Guidance is not described as something withheld arbitrarily from a person who wanted it; it is described as withdrawn from a course that has been abandoned in the presence of evidence. The three clauses are the evidence, and their sequence moves from the community's initial faith, through its acknowledgement of the message's truthfulness, to the proofs it received. Each step raises the level of responsibility, and the question arrives after the third.

**What the Three Clauses Establish**

The first clause places the community's starting point: they had believed. The second reports an acknowledgement that the messenger was true, which the commentators noted is a conscious act rather than a verbal formula. The third names the proofs, which the Book uses for the signs and evidences that accompanied the message.

Together the three describe a situation in which nothing was lacking except the will to continue. A person who has believed, admitted the truth of the messenger, and seen the proofs has no unanswered question left, and the verse's picture of the refusal is of a decision made with every available means of verification already held. The Book's arguments about the earlier communities follow the same pattern: the accusation is never a shortage of information.

**Who Is Being Described**

The commentators offered two main identifications for the people in view. Some took them as apostates who left the community after having accepted the message, and they connected the verse with a report about a group that departed for Makkah and later returned. Others took the passage as continuing the sūrah's address to the People of the Book, who had received revelations and had then refused the message that confirmed them.

The two readings share the elements the verse names. In both cases the people had believed — the People of the Book had believed in the revelation they received — and in both cases the refusal arrived after knowledge. The Book's terminology here is drawn from its own account of the covenant, and the verses that follow state a consequence in the language of the Day of Judgment, which suits either identification. What matters for the reader is that the verse excludes the sympathetic explanation of ignorance and leaves the refusal standing as a choice.

**Why Guidance Is Related to Conduct**

The sentence says that God does not guide a wrongdoing people, and the clause is the Book's standard way of expressing the connection between a person's conduct and his access to guidance.

The connection runs in both directions in the Book's account, and the sūrah has already stated it in the negative: those who persistently deviated had their hearts caused to deviate, as the Book says of the people of Moses (61:5). The Book's language of guidance describes a process rather than a verdict, and its warnings are addressed to people who still have the ability to turn. What the verse above states is what happens when the turning is unrepented: the guidance that was available is not offered again in the same way, and the condition the verse describes is that of people who have used the means of knowledge to settle their decision.

**The Closing Description**

The verse ends by naming the people wrongdoers, which is the description the Book attaches to conduct that damages both the actor and others. The word is not a synonym for the disbeliever as such; the Book applies it to those whose acts are a wrong done against the truth they have received.

The choice of description fits the passage's argument about the covenant. A person who has accepted an obligation and then set it aside has not merely failed to reach a conclusion; he has acted against something due. The passage will go on to describe the penalty in terms of cursing and lasting punishment, and the reader who has followed the sequence is meant to see the severity as a proportionate response to a wrong that was committed with knowledge and in the presence of proof.

**The Door the Next Verses Leave Open**

The verses that follow state the punishment of the people described, and then the passage provides for those who repent and mend their ways, with a declaration of forgiveness and mercy. The provision is stated in a single sentence and it comes after the severity, which is the Book's usual arrangement.

That arrangement is what keeps the verse above from being a verdict on any person alive. The Book's descriptions of what happens to those who turn away are written to be read by people who have not yet turned away, and the sūrah's own passage will show that the door stays open for anyone who returns. The severity and the offer belong to the same paragraph, and the reader who takes one without the other has misread the passage.

**The Question Form and Its Work**

The verse asks how God could guide such a people, and the form invites the reader to supply the answer himself. The Book uses this method at points where a proposition is so evident to its audience that stating it would be redundant.

What the reader supplies is a conclusion about the connection between knowledge and responsibility. A community that has believed, acknowledged, and seen the proofs is not in a position to plead that it lacked the means of knowing; what it lacks is the willingness to stay. The following verses describe the outcome, and the reader who reaches them has already made the inference the question was designed to produce.
