## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:145

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṣāliḥ Removes the Motive of Profit**

The messenger has asked Thamûd to fear Allah and obey him. The people might answer that he is pursuing wealth or a position of influence. Ṣāliḥ removes that suspicion: he seeks no reward from them for delivering the message. His call is not a bargain in which acceptance purchases his favor.

This matters because his audience is divided by status and resources. If guidance could be bought, the wealthy would receive the best access and the poor would be left outside. Ṣāliḥ presents a message that cannot be priced by the people. Every person hears the same claim of God's authority.


Ṣāliḥ does not describe his work as unpaid labor with no value. He locates its reward with the Lord of all worlds. That answer changes the direction of his hope. The approval of Thamûd is temporary, and its wealth is limited; God's knowledge and recompense encompass every people and every hidden intention.

A messenger who expects his reward from God is not free from concern for his audience. He wants Thamûd guided and fears its destruction. But love for the people is not the same as dependence on their praise. He can continue warning when they turn against him because his service is not sustained by their applause.

**No Payment Keeps the Message Open**

Ṣāliḥ's refusal of a reward prevents religion from becoming a private gate controlled by money. A poor family does not need to purchase the right to hear, and a chief cannot purchase a milder standard. The message is public and universal because its source is the Lord of all worlds, not the household of the messenger.

This principle does not forbid lawful support for ordinary teachers, workers, or community servants. Their human labor may be compensated. The verse concerns the messenger's motive and authority. Acceptance of revelation is not a financial transaction, and God's favor cannot be sold by any human being.


The title Lord of all worlds is especially important in a local dispute. Ṣāliḥ is not appealing to a private deity who belongs only to Thamûd or to his own family. He speaks of the Lord who owns every people, land, and generation. The community's customs cannot limit God's claim.

That title also explains why the reward is secure. A tribe may withhold wealth or honor, but it cannot withhold God's recompense. The messenger's real audience is not confined to the people standing before him. His work is part of a divine order that reaches beyond one settlement and one age.

**A Messenger Must Not Sell Silence**

A paid advocate may adjust his speech to preserve his contract. Ṣāliḥ cannot do that. He must identify idolatry, entitlement, and the misuse of the land even when the truth threatens powerful interests. Refusing payment protects the independence needed for truthful warning.

The absence of a fee does not make his task easy. He pays through patience, rejection, and the risk of being misunderstood. His statement separates worldly compensation from faithful service. He gives the message because God entrusted it to him, not because Thamûd has promised to make the work comfortable.


If Ṣāliḥ asks nothing from the people, their rejection cannot be explained by the cost of conversion. The issue is not a demand on their treasury. It is whether they will surrender the right to define worship and conduct for themselves.

This is why accusations of hidden motives can be convenient. They shift attention from the truth of the warning to a suspicion that has not been proved. Ṣāliḥ's declaration asks Thamûd to be fair. Examine the message, the messenger's character, and the evidence rather than protecting pride through an invented financial explanation.

**A Parallel From Hūd Clarifies the Pattern**

Quran 11:51 records Hūd telling ʿĀd that he asks no reward and that his reward is with the One who created him. Ṣāliḥ's statement follows the same prophetic ethic. The repetition establishes that the messengers do not come to create personal empires. They direct all reward and worship back to God.

The parallel also gives Thamûd a comparison it should understand. Another powerful people heard the same refusal of payment and still rejected its messenger. Ṣāliḥ's mission will test whether Thamûd learns from that earlier failure or repeats it under a different name.


After Ṣāliḥ removes the charge of profit, the people are left with the message itself. They can accept God's oneness and submit to His limits, or reject the call because they prefer their established way. His lack of personal demand makes the moral decision clearer, not less demanding.

For the reader, the verse honors service that expects its deepest reward from God. It also warns against judging truth by the wealth or popularity of the person who conveys it. Ṣāliḥ's authority does not arise from what Thamûd can give him. It arises from faithful service to the Lord of all worlds.

The absence of a demand also protects the poor from a second humiliation. They need not prove their worth by funding the messenger, and the wealthy cannot claim that their contribution gives them control over the message. Divine guidance is not a monument financed by patrons. It is a trust announced to every person under the same Lord.

For Ṣāliḥ himself, expecting reward from God steadies the heart when the audience is ungrateful. A servant may feel the pain of rejection without measuring his worth by the reaction. He continues to speak with clarity because the final judgment of his work belongs to the Lord who knows why he spoke and what the people chose. That dependence keeps service sincere even when visible results are small.
