## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:261

> The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Resurrection to Agriculture**

The life-and-death sequence closes with birds returning at a call, and the sūrah's great spending-section opens with a seed. The transition is the verse's first lesson: resurrection and charity run on the same divine logic — the small thing given over to the earth's keeping comes back multiplied, and the arithmetic in both cases belongs to God. The parable's form is agricultural through and through: one grain, planted, becomes seven ears, and each ear carries a hundred grains — a seven-hundredfold return stated not as a miracle but as a harvest, the ordinary mystery of the seed performed at its most generous reported yield. The commentators note the parable's precision: it describes what spending *in the cause of Allah* is like, and every element maps — the grain is the deed, small and single; the planting is the giving, which buries the deed where it cannot be retrieved or displayed; the multiplication is God's, who owns the increase the way He owns the rain. The spent dirham, in the verse's picture, is not lost but sown.

**The Multiplication Beyond the Parable**

The seven-hundred is a floor, and the verse says so in its second sentence: *Allah multiplies even more to whoever He wills*. The commentators connect the clause to the Qur'an's wider reward-arithmetic: the sūrah of Light promises that God rewards the best of what they did *and increases them from His bounty*, providing without reckoning (24:38); the sūrah of the Cattle sets the baseline — the good deed rewarded tenfold, the evil deed with one (6:160). Between ten and seven hundred and beyond-reckoning, the parable's field has no fence, and the will named in the clause is the same will that grows the seed: the increase is not a wage computed but a harvest willed. The tradition preserves the Prophet's own statement of the range — God multiplies the son of Adam's good deed tenfold up to seven hundred times, and beyond, except the fast, which is God's own and He rewards it as He wills (in the ḥadīth collections) — and the exception proves the parable's point from the other side: the deed most hidden, most purely between the doer and God, has the one reward no arithmetic is printed for.

**Seven Hundred Camels**

The narrations gathered around this verse include one that translates the parable into the desert's own currency. A man came to the Prophet leading a camel and said: this camel is given in the way of God. The Prophet's answer, Ibn Kathīr records: on the Day of Judgment you will have seven hundred camels. The report does the exegetical work of the parable in a single exchange — the gift of one animal answered by the promise of the parable's exact multiplication — and it fixes the arithmetic's direction: the seven hundred is not a spiritualized metaphor for satisfaction but a real ledger-entry in the currency given. The commentators read the concrete promise alongside the more abstract ones deliberately: the Qur'an's reward-language moves between the garden's rivers and the camel's count because the spenders it addresses hold both kinds of wealth, and the parable of the grain is itself the bridge — a promise stated in seeds that applies to camels, dates, dirhams, and the day's labor alike. Whatever is sown in the cause, the verse's arithmetic runs on it: one becomes seven hundred, and the will of God runs past the number.

**All-Bountiful**

The verse's first closing name is al-Wāsiʿ — rendered here *All-Bountiful*, and the commentators note the word's double register: the Wide, the Encompassing, and the Generous whose giving has width. The name answers the parable's implied question — where does the seven hundred come from, when the seed contains one? — by naming the source's capacity: the increase comes from the One whose bounty is wide enough that a single grain's return does not narrow it. The exegetes connect the name to the multiplication-clause structurally: God multiplies for whoever He wills *because* God is Wāsiʿ — the will to increase is not constrained by scarcity on His side, and the clause's open-endedness (whoever He wills) is the width's grammatical form. The name also answers the giver's secret fear, which every spending-verse in this section will address in turn: that the gift diminishes the giver. The parable's field belongs to the Wide One; what is sown in it is received by a capacity that the sowing cannot deplete.

**All-Knowing**

The second closing name pairs the width with precision: *All-Knowing* — ʿalīm, the name the verse's agricultural image requires, since a harvest is a reckoning. The Knower knows the grain sown — its size, its quality, the hand that released it — and knows what it became: which of the seven hundred belongs to which act of giving, and which giving was for the cause and which was for the eyes of men. The pairing of Wāsiʿ and ʿAlīm is the section's founding statement, and the spending-verses that follow are its unfolding: the reminders that annul charity (2:264), the secret giving that is better than the public (2:271), the recipients who do not beg and are known by their mark (2:273) — every clause presupposes a God who is wide enough to multiply every gift and knowing enough to distinguish every gift's interior. The parable thus ends where the sūrah's whole ethics of spending begins: the deed is buried like a seed, invisible after its planting, and the invisibility is not a loss but the condition of the harvest — the All-Knowing keeps the account the earth keeps the grain.

**The Parable's Place in the Section**

This verse is the section's title, and its position sets the terms for everything that follows. The next verses will legislate the manner of giving — no reminders, no injury, kind words better than wounded charity — and the quality of what is given, from the good things earned and produced; they will rank the secret over the public, name the recipients too dignified to beg, and warn against the rock that yields nothing. All of it elaborates the parable's single claim: spending in the cause is planting, and planting has conditions — the seed must be good grain rather than chaff, the soil must be receptive rather than stone, the sower must not dig the seed back up to show the neighbors where it went. The verse's agriculture is thus the section's ethics in one image: what is sown in sincerity, from the good, and left in the earth of God's knowledge, returns at seven hundred to one — multiplied further for whoever He wills — and the will that multiplies is the will that knows exactly what was planted, by whom, and why.
