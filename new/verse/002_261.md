## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:261

> The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Parable Opened**

The sentence presents the spending of wealth in the cause of God under the figure of a seed that produces seven ears of grain, and the community's teachers read the image as the answer to the anxiety that accompanies the loss of property. The Book has been addressing the obligations of charity through the passage, and the parable here supplies the account of what the giver receives.

The choice of a grain as the object of the image has been noticed by the commentators, since the seed is the smallest part of the harvest and the multiplication belongs to the order that God has established in the earth. The teachers drew the lesson that the reward of charity follows the pattern of the creation rather than the measure of the gift.

The sentence opens with the comparison of those who spend, so that the parable describes their condition rather than the nature of the wealth they part with. The giver is named first, and the harvest follows.

**The Seven Ears**

The commentators discussed the number of the ears and the grains that each of them contains, and they described the abundance that the figure presents. The image was explained as the account of a reward that exceeds the act many times over, and the interpreters noted that the Book's promises regularly use the language of multiplication.

The teachers also observed the consideration that the figure gives to the sower, since the seed is not consumed but planted, and the wealth that is given for the sake of God is described as remaining in the hand of its owner in a better form. The community's preachers used the image when they answered the argument that charity reduces a person's property.

The passage's earlier verses have named the reward of those who spend, and the parable here supplies the figure by which the community pictured it. The two statements belong together in the sūrah's treatment of the subject.

**The Multiplication Stated**

The sentence adds that God multiplies for whom He wills, and the community's scholars read the clause as the statement that the increase belongs to the divine decision rather than to the measure of the act. The teachers noted that the same wording appears in the descriptions of the provision that God grants, so that the reward of the giver is placed within the general bounty of the Creator.

The reports of the community describe sayings of the Prophet ﷺ about the measure of the reward, and one of them states that a good deed is written tenfold or up to seven hundredfold, with the exception of the fast, which the Book reserves for its own reward. The teachers gathered the material when they explained the sentence.

The clause also serves the community's understanding of generosity, since the person who gives does not calculate the return himself. The multiplication is left to God, and the sentence supplies the assurance that the calculation is in better hands.

**The Reports About the Reward**

The reports of the community preserve the sayings that describe the reward of charity in concrete images, and the teachers used them when they taught the subject. One report describes a man who brought an animal to the Prophet ﷺ and declared that he gave it in the way of God, and the answer described the reward that the giver would receive in the like of what he had offered many times over.

The material shows the manner in which the community received the promise of the sentence. The parables of the Book were explained through the images of the daily life of the people, and the teachers regarded the two forms of statement as the confirmation of one another.

The commentators also noted the difference between the reward that the Book promises and the increase that belongs to the dealings of the world. The first is granted by the decision of God, and the second follows the measures that He has placed in the creation.

**The Name All-Encompassing**

The verse closes with the description of God as bountiful and knowing, and the community's scholars explained the pair as the ground of the promise. The first name, which is also rendered as all-encompassing, carries the sense of a generosity that no measure limits, and the second concerns the knowledge of every act and every intention.

The teachers noted that the two names answer the two doubts that a giver may hold. The fear that the gift is too small for a reward is answered by the bounty that has no limit, and the fear that the act will be forgotten is answered by the knowledge that records it.

The Book uses the pair in the passages that treat the provision of the creatures, and the community's scholars gathered the instances when they explained the attributes. The verse therefore ends with the statement about the One who receives the gift.

**The Parable in the Sūrah**

The sentence belongs to the long passage on charity that runs through the middle of the sūrah, and the teachers read the parables of that section as a series that presents the subject from several sides. One image describes the reward of the giver, another describes the charity that is ruined by the reminder of it, and a third describes the giver who has lost his harvest at the end of his life.

The arrangement of the material gives the community a complete treatment of the subject. The sentence in this place supplies the encouragement that answers the reluctance of the giver, and the verses that follow supply the warnings that protect the act from what destroys it.

The teachers of the community drew the lesson that the passage addresses the intention as well as the act, since the reward of the gift depends on the state of the person who gives it. The parable of the grain is therefore read with the verses about the manner of giving.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the figure of the seed and the harvest, the multiplication of the reward by the decision of God, the generosity that no measure limits, and the knowledge that records the act. The community's teachers used the verse to encourage the believers in their giving, and the sūrah's later verses were read with it when the community examined the conditions of the charity that God accepts.

The clause about the will of God also supplies the answer to those who called the promise into question after their own experience of the world. The harvest of the giver belongs to the life to come, and the sentence places the whole matter within the bounty of the One who gives it.
