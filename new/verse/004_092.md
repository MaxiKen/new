## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:92

> It is not lawful for a believer to kill another except by mistake. And whoever kills a believer unintentionally must free a believing slave and pay blood-money to the victim’s family—unless they waive it charitably. But if the victim is a believer from a hostile people, then a believing slave must be freed. And if the victim is from a people bound with you in a treaty, then blood-money must be paid to the family along with freeing a believing slave. Those who are unable, let them fast two consecutive months—as a means of repentance to Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**It Is Not Lawful for a Believer to Kill a Believer**

The verse opens *wa mā kāna li muʾminin an yaqtula muʾminan illā khaṭaʾan* — it is not lawful, it is not for a believer to kill another believer except by mistake. *Mā kāna* — it is not, it is not fitting — was explained as a negation of permissibility, not only of occurrence, the same construction as *“it is not for a believer”* to disobey after God and Messenger decide. The exception *illā khaṭaʾan* — except by mistake, error — was explained as unintentional killing: a slip of aim, a misreading of identity, a cause where intent to kill a believer was absent, as when the sūrah earlier forbade killing yourselves and devouring wealth unjustly (4:29). The rule follows directly after the permission to seize and kill the double-faced who do not withdraw or offer peace (4:89–91); where that permission might be widened, the sūrah narrows it to its limit: the believer’s blood is not lawful to the believer. The Book records the gravity elsewhere: *“the shedding of blood”* is paired with corruption — *nufsid* (2:30) — and with the sanctity of a soul — *“whoever kills a soul it is as if he killed all mankind”* (5:32). The verse anchors that sanctity in law: no believer kills a believer on purpose; if it happens, it is only by error, and error itself has a heavy remedy.

**Freeing a Believing Slave and Paying Blood-Money**

The remedy *fa taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin wa diyatun musallamatun ilā ahlihī illā an yaṣṣaddaqū* — then freeing a believing slave and blood-money delivered to the family, unless they waive it charitably as alms — was explained as two rights restored. *Taḥrīr raqabah* — freeing a neck — was read as liberating a believing bonds-person, a life returned for a life taken, recalling that to free a life is to keep humanity alive. *Diyah musallamah* — blood-money delivered, handed over — was explained as wealth paid to the victim’s people, delivered fully, as the return of trusts commanded earlier (4:58). The clause *illā an yaṣṣaddaqū* — unless they waive, give it as charity — was read as remission by the family, who may forgo it charitably; the sūrah has made gracious waiver a virtue where dowry is waived willingly (4:4) and where charity is given from what God provided (4:39). That a mistake still requires freeing and payment shows that the law does not treat error as nothing; it treats it as a harm whose maker must still repair, though without the curse of intention. Early reports noted this freeing applies where the killer has means; where he does not, the alternative is named at the end.

**If the Victim Was From a Hostile People or From a Treaty People**

The two cases *fa in kāna min qawmin ʿaduwwin lakum wa huwa muʾminun fa taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminah, wa in kāna min qawmin baynakum wa baynahum mīthāqun fa diyatun musallamatun ilā ahlihī wa taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminah* — but if the victim is a believer from a hostile people, then only freeing a believing slave; and if from a people bound with you in a treaty, then blood-money to the family along with freeing — were explained as the sūrah’s consistent respect for treaty — *mīthāq* — the firm word seen in marriage (4:21) and in trusts (4:58) and in the exception of 4:90. If the mistakenly killed believer was from hostile people — living among those at war while himself a believer — there is no payment to his people who are hostile, lest it strengthen the hostile; the right to God remains as freeing. If he was from treaty people — *baynakum wa baynahum mīthāq* — then the blood-money is delivered to his family, fulfilling the treaty, together with freeing for God. The former was understood to cover a believer who hid belief among the opponent or who joined without being known; the latter covers the covenanted protected person whose blood the treaty guarantees, as 4:90 had spared those joined to treaty people. The threefold pattern — full atonement where family can receive, freeing alone where payment would aid hostility, and payment plus freeing where treaty binds — shows the law weighing life, treaty, and the community’s security together without contradiction.

**Those Unable — Fast Two Consecutive Months**

The alternative *fa man lam yajid fa ṣiyāmu shahrayni mutatābiʿayni tawbatan mina Allāhi* — those who are unable, let them fast two consecutive months as repentance, as turning from God — was explained as expiation for the one who cannot free a slave. *Man lam yajid* — whoever does not find, cannot afford — was read as lacking a bonds-person to free or the means to acquire one; for him the bodily worship of consecutive fasting replaces the freeing, as fasting also replaces what is lacking elsewhere in atonements. *Mutatābiʿayni* — consecutive, successive — was stressed: two months without break, showing that the error’s repair requires continuity, not fragments. *Tawbatan mina Allāhi* — repentance from God, a turning granted by Him — was explained as His acceptance of this replacement; the same root *tāba* appears in the sūrah’s promise that if wrongdoers came seeking forgiveness the Messenger prayed for them they would find Him ever Accepting of Repentance (4:64). The closing *wa kāna Allāhu ʿAlīman Ḥakīmā* — and God is All-Knowing, All-Wise — was read as the seal that He knows error from intent and judges with wisdom where means fail. The verse was also linked to the wider law where killing a believer intentionally carries Hell and curse (4:93), while error carries freeing, payment, or fasting — a distinction the Community kept when evaluating reports of accidental killings on campaigns.

**A Verse That Guards Blood After Giving Permission Over Hostility**

Placed after the clear warrant over the double-faced (4:91), this verse guards blood where permission might be misread. The sūrah that began by creating from a single soul and commanding mindfulness of ties (4:1), that forbade consuming orphans’ wealth, that said *“do not kill yourselves, God is ever Merciful to you”* (4:29), and that promised Hell for intentional killing (4:93), here converts the sanctity of the believer into procedure: freeing, payment, treaty fidelity, and fasting where there is no means. The order itself teaches that permission over hostility (4:89–91) is not permission over belief: the believer’s life is unlawful to the believer except by mistake, and even mistake requires repair. For the weak for whom burdens were lightened (4:28), the closing *tawbatan* is the lightest of burdens: where the hand slipped without intent, the law opens a path back through freeing or, where there is nothing to free, through consecutive fasting as repentance accepted by the All-Knowing, All-Wise. The next verse will set the opposite face — the intentional killing whose reward is Hell — so that mistake and purpose are never confused.
