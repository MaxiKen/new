## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:4

> previously, as a guide for people, and ˹also˺ revealed the Standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. Surely those who reject Allah’s revelations will suffer a severe torment. For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guidance Sent "Previously" Is Still Guidance**

The verse opens with a word of time — "previously" — and then states the purpose of the older books: they were *a guide for people*. Not for one tribe, and not only for the generations that received them. The Qur'an describes revelation as a universal benefit that happened to arrive in stages, and the timing is incidental to the function. The verse names the Book first and the earlier scriptures afterward, and the order presents the later revelation as the confirmation of what preceded it rather than a departure from it.

The Standard mentioned alongside them carries the same force. In the month of fasting, the Book is described as *“a guide for humanity with clear proofs of guidance and the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺”* (2:185), which places the criterion inside the guidance rather than beside it. A guide that tells a person where to walk and a criterion that tells him what is right are two services of one revelation.

Reading these lines together produces a claim about religious history that is easy to state and hard to live with: God has never left human beings without a way of telling right from wrong. Communities differ in what they received and when, and the difference is a matter of divine scheduling, not of divine preference.

**Al-Furqān: One Name, Several Books, One Function**

The word translated "Standard" is *al-furqān*, and the Qur'an uses it of more than one revelation. Moses receives it: *“And ˹remember˺ when We gave Moses the Scripture—the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺ that perhaps you would be ˹rightly˺ guided.”* (2:53), and Aaron is joined with him in the same gift (21:48). It is used of the Qur'an itself: *“Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant, so that he may be a warner to the whole world.”* (25:1). Even the day of Badr is called *yawm al-furqān*.

The dictionaries separate the word from "Qur'an" sharply. *Furqān* means the act of separating, of making a distinction, built on the pattern that Arabic uses for intensity and instrument; *Qur'an* means recitation. A book is named for what it says and what it does — recited when read aloud, separating when applied. Some commentators, noticing that the Qur'an is already called the Book in this sentence, took the Standard to be another scripture, even the Psalms; others took it as a description of all three books together. Both readings respect the same underlying claim: revelation's first job is discrimination.

**The Self-Description of the Book That Verifies Books**

Nothing here is a claim about the text of any library. The verse says what revelation from God does when it arrives: it guides, and it distinguishes. The Qur'an's later arguments with the People of the Book never turn on what might have been lost or gained in transmission; they turn on what the recipients did with what they had, and the accusation is consistently about the will rather than the archive.

That is why the same Book that calls itself a confirmation also says of itself, *“We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ this Book with the truth, as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority on them.”* (5:48). Confirming and arbitrating are not in tension once the subject is revelation rather than manuscripts. A judge who upholds a valid contract is not denying it.

The verse's placement, in a paragraph listing descents, makes the claim concrete: every one of these Books was given for the same two purposes, and the newest one stands in that line, not against it.

**Why Denial of Revelation Draws the Harshest Warning**

The sentence that follows the list of books does not say "those who reject this Book". It says *those who reject Allah's revelations* — the plural that includes every descent just named. The warning is attached to a refusal that covers the whole series, and the punishment is described as severe.

The Qur'an keeps this charge in view elsewhere with unmistakable phrasing: *“And who does more wrong than the one who is reminded of Allah’s revelations then turns away from them? We will surely inflict punishment upon the wicked.”* (32:22). The same threat is repeated to the Prophet's opponents when they imagine the messenger's removal would free them: *“Even if We take you away ˹from this world˺, We will surely inflict punishment upon them.”* (43:41).

Rejecting a sign is not an intellectual misjudgement in this vocabulary; it is a decision made in the presence of evidence. What protection would remain for anyone, in heaven or earth, once that decision has been taken?

**"Almighty, Capable of Punishment" — an Attribute, Not a Threat**

The Arabic attribute *dhū intiqām* is often rendered "Avenger", which imports a mood of personal injury that the Arabic does not require. The translation here, "capable of punishment", keeps the statement on the level of capacity. The verse does not say God is angry with them; it says He can do what He warns about, and that no defence exists.

The Qur'an attaches the same pair of attributes to very different contexts so that the reader learns to hear it as a fact rather than as a flourish. It closes a verse about hunting during pilgrimage with it (5:95) and stands as a rebuke to doubters elsewhere: *“So do not think ˹O Prophet˺ that Allah will fail to keep His promise to His messengers. Allah is indeed Almighty, capable of punishment.”* (14:47). Revelation was refused by people who assumed that the refusal carried no cost. The cost is stated in the same breath as the gift.
