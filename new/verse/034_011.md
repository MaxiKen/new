## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:11

> instructing: “Make full-length armour, ˹perfectly˺ balancing the links. And work righteousness ˹O family of David!˺. Indeed, I am All-Seeing of what you do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Miracle Is Issued as a Work Order**

Softened iron arrives with instructions attached, and the instructions are technical: *an iʿmal sābighāt* — make ample, full-length coats. *Sābighāt* comes from the root *s-b-gh*, to be complete or to reach fully; the same root produces *isbāgh al-wuḍūʾ*, performing the ablution thoroughly, and *niʿmah sābighah*, a favour that covers everything. Applied to mail, it means armour long enough to protect the whole body rather than a fashionable half-piece. Nothing in the commission is ceremonial. A prophet-king has been handed control of the hardest substance available and is told to produce defensive equipment to a specified standard. This is a distinctive feature of how the Qur'an handles the miraculous. The extraordinary capacity is not the story's climax; it is an input to ordinary work, and the story's interest lies in what the work is for. Al-Naḥl lists armour among the favours of God to humanity generally, alongside shade and clothing: *"and armour shielding you in battle"* (16:81). David's gift lets him supply, at scale, a protection the Book treats as a mercy.

**Measure the Links: Craftsmanship Given as a Command**

The second half of the instruction is the striking one: *wa qaddir fī al-sard*. *Sard* is the technical term for the interlinking of rings in mail, the row-upon-row structure of chain armour, and *qaddir* means to measure, proportion, get the quantity right. The command is about gauge. Rings too heavy and the wearer cannot move; too light and a spear point passes through. Rivets too thick tear the plate, too thin and they shear. What is being given the force of a divine instruction is accurate workmanship in a specific trade. This sits oddly beside the assumption that revelation concerns itself only with belief and ritual, which is exactly why it repays notice. A badly made coat of mail is not a technical failure with a moral remainder; it is a man killed in a battle line because his armourer was careless. Precision in the workshop is being treated as a religious obligation and placed in the same sentence as righteousness, joined by nothing more than *and*.

**"Work Righteousness" — The Same Verb, Extended**

The instruction continues *waʿmalū ṣāliḥan* — and do good work. English hides what the Arabic makes obvious: the verb is the same one used a clause earlier for making the armour, *iʿmal*. Make coats; make good. The repetition welds craft and conduct into one activity rather than two. The root of *ṣāliḥ* reinforces this, since *ṣ-l-ḥ* means to be sound, fit, in working order — a word as natural for a well-made object as for a well-lived life, with *fasād*, rot, as its opposite in both senses. So the passage moves from a soundly built coat to a soundly built life using vocabulary that does not change gear. Notice also the shift from singular to plural. The armour command is addressed to David alone; *work righteousness* is plural, and the translation supplies the addressee the Arabic implies from the following words: the family of David. The gift was personal, the responsibility is collective, and the household that benefits from the privilege is folded into its obligations.

**A Household Named, and Held to Account**

*Āl Dāwūd*, the family of David, becomes a standing phrase in the Qur'an's treatment of this dynasty, and it recurs two verses later when Solomon's workforce is told to *"Work gratefully, O family of David!"* (34:13). Naming a house rather than an individual does something particular in a tribal setting, where a family's standing was its most valuable asset and its faults were absorbed by its status. Here the household is addressed directly and given duties, which cuts against the assumption that a great lineage is itself a form of merit. The sūrah will return to that assumption with hostility when it quotes men boasting of wealth and children as proof of divine approval (34:35). Against that, the family of David is presented as a house whose distinction consists in the work it is told to do. The line of the prophet-king does not inherit protection from the command; it inherits the command.

**The Closing Clause Is a Supervisor's Sentence**

*Innī bimā taʿmalūna baṣīr* — I am All-Seeing of what you do. The verb again is *ʿamal*, work, for the third time in two lines, so the sentence reads as an inspection notice on the very activity just commissioned. The name invoked is *Baṣīr*, from *baṣar*, eyesight: not knowledge in the abstract but watching. This is a workshop clause. An armourer working for a distant king can cut corners in the certainty that the king will never count his rivets, and the man who discovers the shortfall is a soldier too far away to complain. The clause removes the distance. The same formula closes verses on charity — *"Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do"* (2:110) — and stands as a warning to those who abuse the revelations (41:40), which shows it is not a decorative name but a standing statement about unobserved conduct. Placed here, it supplies the reason why measuring the links is a religious matter rather than a professional preference.

**Power Handed Over With Its Conditions Attached**

Reading the two verses together, the sequence is: a favour given, a capacity conferred, a task specified, a standard of quality imposed, a moral instruction attached, and a reminder of supervision. At no point is the power left free-standing. This is the Qur'an's consistent handling of what it grants to prophets — the capacity arrives inside a set of obligations, and the obligations are as much a part of the gift as the capacity. The pattern also sets up the contrast that governs this whole section of Sabaʾ. David is given control over the hardest material on earth and told to make protection and to work well. The people of Sheba will be given two orchards and told to eat and be grateful (34:15), and the difference in their outcomes will turn on nothing more complicated than whether the instruction attached to the gift was obeyed. Solomon's inheritance follows next, and with it a larger command over the natural order.
