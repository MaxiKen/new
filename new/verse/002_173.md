## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:173

> He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity —neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—they will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word "Only" and the Short List**

The verse opens with a restriction on restriction: *He has only forbidden you* — four items, and the four exhaust the divine prohibition of food. The "only" is the verse's argument in one word. Against the tribes that forbade whole classes of their cattle to themselves, against the ancestral customs that bound what God had loosed, against the footsteps that walk the pious toward invented prohibitions, the revealed law presents a list so short it can be memorized in a breath: carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in another's name. Everything else on the earth's table stands under the permission of the previous verse. The brevity is jurisprudence and theology at once — a religion whose food law is four exceptions to a universal permission cannot be accused of strangling life with scruple, and the contrast with the surrounding polemic is exact: the human additions were many, anxious, and false; the divine prohibitions are few, reasoned, and final. The tradition's summary of the verse's effect on the table is that God loosed the earth and marked four doors closed.

**The Four, One by One**

Each prohibition carries its own logic, and the classical readings drew it out. Carrion — *al-maytah*, the dead thing — is flesh whose death no one governed: an animal that died of disease, accident, or age, its body a site where the processes of decay and unknown illness have already worked; the prohibition of the unslaughtered dead is the prohibition of the uncontrolled. Blood — the flowing blood, as the sūrah of the Cattle will specify — is the life-fluid the ritual draining removes, prohibited in its gathered form. Swine is the single animal named as forbidden in its own flesh, a prohibition the Qur'an repeats without elaboration and the community received without exception. And what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah is the prohibition whose object is not hygiene but worship: the meat is physically sound, but the act that produced it was an offering to another — idolatry performed at the animal's throat. The four thus span the categories of concern: the unsafe, the unfit, the excluded by decree, and the consecrated to a rival. The last is the deepest, which is why the many jurists' permission of the food of the People of the Book turns on its absence: their slaughter names God, not an idol.

**The Name Over the Throat**

The fourth item establishes the positive ritual the community performs at every slaughter: the pronouncing of God's Name. The sūrah of the Pilgrimage legislates the same act over the sacrificial camels — *"pronounce the Name of Allah over them when they are lined up ˹for sacrifice˺"* (22:36) — and Islamic practice fixed the formula as the *basmalah* spoken at the cut. The theology is precise and answers the meal's deepest question: who made this death lawful for my life? The animal dies because a human needs it; the Name declares that the taking happens under God's permission and in His direction, converting a killing into a receipt. Meat from which the Name is absent is not thereby carrion in the physical sense; it is meat whose killing belongs to no one's stated accountability — or worse, to a named rival. This is why the verse's list ends with the dedication-prohibition rather than beginning with it: the first three items protect the body and the decree; the fourth protects the meaning of the act, and meaning is the verse's real subject. The table of the believers is a table at which every death has been declared to its Owner, and the declaration is what separates eating from mere consumption.

**The Necessity Clause**

The second sentence bends the law toward life: *but if someone is compelled by necessity — neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need — they will not be sinful*. The clause is the Qur'an's standing principle that the prohibition serves the human being and not the reverse: when the choice is the forbidden food or death, the food is commanded by the same law that forbade it. The two qualifiers prevent the clause from becoming a loophole. *Neither driven by desire* — *ghayra bāgh*, where the word carries both coveting and rebellion: the eater must not be using necessity as a cover for appetite, reaching for the forbidden because it is forbidden or because he wants what others may not have. *Nor exceeding immediate need* — *wa-lā ʿād*, transgressing the measure: enough to survive, not enough to feast; the necessity opens the door exactly as wide as the danger requires and no further. Al-Ṭabarī recorded a third reading of the transgression-qualifier with social teeth: some understood it to exclude the rebel and the highwayman — the person journeying in disobedience to God, for whom even necessity earns no license, since he may not provision his crime with the law's mercy. The mainstream reading keeps the clause general — necessity is necessity, whatever road the starving person is on — and the disagreement itself shows how carefully the tradition guarded the balance: mercy wide enough to save a life, discipline tight enough to survive the mercy.

**A Ruling the Qur'an States Three Times**

This verse is one of the Qur'an's most exactly repeated laws, and the repetitions mark its constitutional status. The sūrah of the Bee states it in near-identical words: *"He has only forbidden you carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful"* (16:115). The sūrah of the Cattle states the principle without the list: *"Why should you not eat of what is slaughtered in Allah's Name when He has already explained to you what He has forbidden to you—except when compelled by necessity?"* (6:119). And the sūrah of the Table, in the Medinan period's final legislation, expands the list to its full detail — carrion, blood, swine, the dedicated-to-others, the strangled, the beaten to death, the fallen, the gored, the partly eaten by predators, the altar-sacrificed, the divining arrows — with the same necessity exemption standing (5:3). The expansion confirms rather than revises: the later verse elaborates the carrion category into its modes of death and keeps the fourfold core intact. A law repeated verbatim across Meccan and Medinan revelation, then detailed without disturbance, is the tradition's picture of a ruling beyond abrogation — the food law's permanent floor.

**Forgiving and Merciful as the Law's Frame**

The verse closes with the two names — All-Forgiving, Most Merciful — and their placement is the final lesson. They follow the necessity clause rather than the prohibitions, covering the eater who crossed the line under compulsion, the one who erred in measuring his necessity, the one whose past meals were eaten before the law arrived. A food legislation could have closed with vigilance: Allah watches what enters your mouths. This one closes with absolution, and the absolution is attached precisely where human beings will fail — at the boundary cases, the famines, the mistakes, the years of ignorance before the permission and prohibition were revealed. The whole passage from the address to humanity to this verse is thereby framed by mercy at both ends: the enemy declared (2:168), his methods exposed (2:169), the ancestral refusal refuted (2:170), the uncomprehending flock parabled (2:171), the believers' grateful table set (2:172), and the four doors closed with the key of necessity in the lock and forgiveness on the frame. The community that receives this law is told, in the act of receiving it, that its Lawgiver expects to be thanked at the table and expects to forgive at its edges — and both expectations are mercy.
