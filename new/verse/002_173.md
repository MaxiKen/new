## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:173

> He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity —neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—they will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prohibitions Named**

The verse names four things that have been forbidden: carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and what has been slaughtered in the name of anything other than God. The list is short and specific, and the community's scholars noted that the Book elsewhere adds to it in a passage that names further categories.

The sentence's opening states that these are the only things forbidden, and the wording stands as a correction to any practice that added restrictions on its own authority. What the reader receives is a statement of what the standard amounts to in practice, and the list follows the command to eat what is lawful and good.

The community’s scholars compared the list in this verse with the longer list in another chapter and concluded that the two together describe the same set of prohibitions in different circumstances. What the sentence supplies here is the standard in brief, and the community’s practice has preserved it in the rulings on what may be eaten and what may be shared with others.

**The Terms of the List**

The commentators explained each item. Carrion is the flesh of an animal that has died without being slaughtered in the manner the law requires, blood is the fluid that is not to be consumed, and the pig is excluded by name. The fourth category covers animals that are slaughtered as an offering to something other than God.

The Book's treatment of the list elsewhere includes additional items, and the community's scholars gathered the passages into a single teaching. The verse in this place states the categories that concern the ordinary circumstances of a community recently addressed on the subject, and the list's brevity reflects the practice of dealing with the matter as it arises.

**The Practice of Slaughter**

The Book's teaching connects the permissibility of meat with the invocation of God's name at the time of slaughter, and the community's practice has preserved the invocation as a part of the act. The reports describe the Prophet ﷺ instructing those who were sent to teach about the proper manner of slaughtering animals.

The rule also bears on the relation between the community and those around it. The commentators noted that the meat of the People of the Book is not included in the prohibition, since the passage concerns what has been offered to something other than God, and the sūrah states that permission elsewhere. The sentence therefore defines the boundary of what the community may share with its neighbours, and the reader is shown that the regulation is practical rather than ceremonial.

**The Permissions of Necessity**

The sentence then states that a person who is compelled by necessity, without desiring what is forbidden and without exceeding what the situation requires, commits no sin. The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere states the same permission, and the community's scholars derived from it the rule that preservation of life takes precedence over the ordinary prohibition.

The commentators explained the two qualifications in the sentence. The person must not be pursuing the forbidden thing out of desire, and he must not take more than the necessity requires, and the community's teachers applied the pair to the amount a person may eat and to the length of time over which the concession lasts.

**The Conditions Explained**

The term rendered as one who covets carries the sense of rebellion, and the commentators discussed whether the qualification concerns a person's intention or his conduct. Some explained it as a description of someone who goes beyond the limit, and others applied it to the means by which the food is obtained, including the taking of what is not lawful.

What the community's jurists concluded is that the concession is narrow and that its conditions concern both the internal disposition and the outward act. The sentence therefore does not open a general permission to eat the forbidden; it provides for a person who has no other means of preserving his life, and the community's teachers treated the conditions as part of the ruling.

**The Names at the End of the Sentence**

The sentence closes with the statement that God is forgiving and merciful, and the community's teachers read the pair as the ground of the concession that has been granted. The names appear at the end of rulings elsewhere in the sūrah, and their placement indicates that the legislation is presented as an expression of mercy rather than as an arbitrary restriction.

The reader who has followed the passage from the address to humanity through the address to the believers arrives at a statement about what is not permitted, and the sentence leaves him with the attributes of the one who made the rule. What the verse establishes is that the law's limits and its concessions belong to the same source, and the community has carried both together in its practice.

The community’s teachers noted that the names appear at the close of rulings throughout the sūrah, and that the practice of ending a legal statement with an attribute of God gives the reader the disposition in which the ruling is to be received. The concession described in the sentence is therefore presented as an instance of mercy rather than as an exception to a rule, and the reader is shown that the law and the attributes of the lawgiver belong together. What the verse establishes is that the limits and the permissions of the passage come from the same source, and the community has carried both in its practice.

**The Passage Completed**

The sentence completes the section on food and brings the discussion that began with the address to humanity to its conclusion. The Book has stated the principle, described the practice that it corrects, addressed the believers, and set out the prohibitions along with the concession for necessity. The next verses take up the subject of what is required in return for what has been forbidden and the fairness of the recompense, and the reader is carried from the regulation of a person's table to the account of the one who gives and takes away.
