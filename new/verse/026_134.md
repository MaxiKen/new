## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:134

> and gardens, and springs.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Landscape Itself Is Named as Provision**

Hūd completes his immediate list with gardens and springs. The gifts are not confined to objects owned by a household. They include a living landscape: cultivated growth and the water that makes growth possible. ʿĀd's prosperity is therefore surrounded by signs of sustaining care.

Gardens display the fruit of time, soil, labor, and water. Springs suggest a source that continues to feed life beyond a single moment. By naming them, Hūd asks the people to look at the environment that supports their comfort and to recognize that its order is not an achievement of their pride alone. Quran 16:10–11 similarly connects rain with drinking water, grazing, crops, and fruit, presenting the chain of provision as a sign for reflection.


A garden can be enjoyed, cultivated, and shared, but it cannot be treated as an endless possession. Its fertility depends on conditions outside the gardener's total control. A grateful society protects water, soil, and access to nourishment instead of exhausting the land for immediate display.

This is not a technical rule about one agricultural method. It is a moral principle. What sustains life is a trust. The people who receive gardens should ask whether their use leaves the land capable of benefiting others and future generations. Vanity consumes resources to make the present look powerful; gratitude preserves them as gifts.

**Springs Point to Dependence**

A spring gives a visible image of provision that precedes human labor. People can channel water, build around it, and distribute it, but they do not command the source into existence. The spring reminds the people that their skill works with a gift already present.

That reminder should temper claims of ownership. Control over access to water can make rulers feel indispensable, but the source remains God's. A just community manages the means of provision for life rather than turning necessity into a tool of humiliation or domination.


When gardens remain green and springs continue to flow, a generation may assume that prosperity is automatic. The more stable the blessing appears, the easier it is to forget the One who sustains it. Hūd's list restores the origin of what the people see every day.

Forgetfulness is not corrected merely by repeating the word “gift.” The people must let the recognition affect their conduct. They should show gratitude in worship, restraint in consumption, fairness in distribution, and mercy toward those whose access to provision is less secure.

**The Gift Is Collective, Not Only Private**

Gardens and springs shape the life of a whole people. Their benefit can pass through families, workers, travelers, and neighbors. The image therefore challenges an economy in which a small group claims the entire blessing while everyone else serves its display.

A community that fears God treats common goods with public responsibility. It does not allow the powerful to make abundance a reason for exclusion. Nor does it assume that shared provision removes individual duty. Each person must act honestly, while leaders must prevent the strong from consuming what belongs to the community's welfare.


A garden is useful and beautiful. The verse does not ask the people to despise pleasure or to regard cultivated beauty as vanity by definition. The problem lies in forgetting the Provider and converting beauty into a proof of superiority. Enjoyment becomes honorable when it is joined to gratitude and does not require oppression.

This balance protects against an austere misunderstanding of piety. Fear of Allah does not require people to destroy every attractive thing. It asks them to enjoy created beauty without worshipping it, hoarding it, or using it to announce that they are beyond accountability.

**Provision Does Not Excuse the Oppression of Others**

The gardens and springs cannot justify the attacks mentioned earlier. A prosperous land is not a license to take neighboring people's resources or to treat outsiders as obstacles. If ʿĀd's abundance leads to cruelty, the gift has become evidence in the case against them.

God's provision should widen compassion. A person who knows dependence can recognize the dependence of others. He does not ask only whether his own garden is watered; he asks whether his power is blocking another person's access to life. Gratitude is measured by that widening of concern.


The cultivated garden can awaken hope for a greater, lasting reward, but it can also make people cling to the temporary world. Springs flow and seasons change; even the richest landscape remains within time. The beauty is real, but it is not final.

Hūd's reminder asks ʿĀd to use present provision without making it an idol. Let gardens encourage gratitude, and let springs teach dependence. When the people fear the Provider, the land becomes a sign that leads them to worship. When they forget Him, the same land feeds their illusion of self-sufficiency.


Cattle, children, gardens, and springs form a picture of sustenance at several levels: living creatures, family continuity, cultivated food, and water. Hūd is showing the people that mercy has surrounded them in ordinary forms. Their refusal is not a response to deprivation but to a call they hear amid abundance.

The proper response is not to abandon the gifts. It is to return them to their right meaning. Everything they know and enjoy comes through the generosity of the Lord, so it should lead to reverence, justice, and obedience rather than vanity.

The list also joins nourishment with beauty. Gardens give sustenance and delight, while springs make continued life possible. A faithful community can appreciate both dimensions without confusing enjoyment with entitlement. It receives pleasure as a reason to praise God and shares the conditions of that pleasure as a responsibility toward others.

The land's generosity should make the people more aware of dependence, not more contemptuous of those who have less. If the same water and soil are used to concentrate privilege or support aggression, abundance has been severed from its moral purpose. Hūd's reminder restores that connection before he warns of the coming day.
