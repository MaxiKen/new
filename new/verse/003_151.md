## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:151

> We will cast horror into the hearts of the disbelievers for associating ˹false gods˺ with Allah—a practice He has never authorized. The Fire will be their home—what an evil place for the wrongdoers to stay!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Terror Cast Into Hearts**

The verse announces that terror will be thrown into the hearts of those who deny, and the form of the sentence presents the event as settled. The Book describes this casting of horror among the opponents of the prophets in several passages, and the wording is consistent across them.

One passage reports the instruction given to the angels on the day of Badr, in which God states that He will cast horror into the hearts of those who disbelieve. Another describes the expulsion of a group from their homes and the horror placed in their hearts, so that they destroyed their own houses with their own hands. A third describes the same treatment of those who supported an alliance against the community. The sentence in this verse belongs to that family.

**The Reason Given for the Terror**

The verse supplies a reason for what happens to those who deny: they associated partners with God for which no authority had been sent down. The charge is the central one of the sūrah's argument with its opponents, and the wording adds the element of warrant.

The Book's objections to the association of others with God are frequently accompanied by this observation, that the practice rests on no divine authorization. The point is not merely that the practice is wrong but that those who defend it cannot produce the grounds on which it would rest. The verse states the charge in the form of a refusal to accept a claim that had never been supported.

**The Weight of the Word for Authority**

The term rendered as authority covers proof, warrant, and the power that accompanies a legitimate claim. The Book uses it for the evidence that the prophets brought and for the license that rulers possess, and it uses it in its challenges to the opponents of the messengers.

One of the Book's recurring demands is that those who associate partners with God should bring their proof, a demand that the verse before the reader implicitly records. The absence of such authority is presented here as part of the case against the practice, and the punishment announced in the first half of the verse follows from it.

**Their Refuge Is the Fire**

The sentence then states where those who deny will end: their refuge is the Fire. The word describes a place to which a person retreats and in which he remains, and its use here forecloses the hope of escape.

The sūrah uses the same term in the passage about the reward of the reverent, where the garden is described as their refuge. The two destinations are set out in the sūrah with the same vocabulary, so that the reader meets the pair in consecutive contexts and learns to read them as the two ends of the same road.

**The Abode of the Wrongdoers**

The verse closes by declaring that the abode is evil, and it describes its inhabitants as wrongdoers. The Book applies the term to those who deny the truth and to those whose conduct damages the rights of others, and the two usages are connected.

The sūrah has used the word repeatedly in its account of the community's opponents, and it uses it in the passage about the people who fabricate claims about God. The designation in this verse places the opponents of the community, the people who associate partners with God, and those who falsify revelation in a single category, and the sentence about their abode is stated as a conclusion of the whole.

**The Report From the Return to Makkah**

The commentators connect the verse with an event after the fighting at Uḥud. The idolaters, on their way back to Makkah, regretted that they had not finished the community off, and they resolved to return and complete what they had begun. The reports state that God then cast terror into them, and they lost their resolve and continued home.

The report gives the verse a concrete occasion and shows the community what the sentence meant in its own history. The army that had just defeated them was turned back without another engagement, and the sūrah attributes the outcome to the terror placed in their hearts rather than to any change in the community's strength.

**The Book's Realism About Danger**

The verse does not tell the community that its enemies would cease to threaten it, and the sūrah's other passages acknowledge the hostility it continued to face. What the sentence establishes is where the decisive factor lies when the community is defended.

The Book describes the fear of its opponents as one of the means by which the community is protected, and it treats that fear as an arrangement rather than an accident. The community's task in such passages is to hold its position and to keep its own conduct straight, and the removal of the threat is left where the sūrah has placed it, in the hands of the one who casts dread and grants safety.

**What the Verse Establishes for the Community**

The verse follows immediately after the statement that God is the master of the believers and the best of helpers, and it shows what that guardianship had already accomplished. The community was spared a second attack by the same army that had beaten it, and the sūrah records the sparing as the work of its protector.

The placement matters for how the whole passage reads. The community is given a claim about God's protection and then an instance of it from its own recent memory, and the joining of the two leaves the believers with a basis for confidence that does not depend on their own condition after the battle.
