## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:3

> **Certainly, We have made it a Quran in Arabic so perhaps you will understand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Content of the Oath**

This verse is the answer to the oath in the previous one. God swore by the clear Book, and what He swore is this: We have made it an Arabic recitation so that you may understand. The particle innā with the emphatic lām on the verb marks the clause as the sworn statement. So the structure is: by the clear Book, We truly made it Arabic for your understanding. The content of the oath is not a doctrine about God or the Hereafter but a fact about the language of the revelation. That tells us what the passage is arguing. The issue in Makkah was not whether Arabic was a fine language; it was whether a text in the Quraysh's own tongue, recited by one of their own, could be from heaven. The verse insists that the Arabic is deliberate. The Book was not Arabic by accident of where the Prophet ﷺ happened to be born. It was made Arabic, chosen to be Arabic, for a purpose stated in the last clause.

**"Made" Rather Than "Created"**

The verb is jaʿalnā, we made or appointed, not khalaqnā, we created. Sunnī theology has always been careful about this distinction in this verse, because the Muʿtazilah cited it to argue that the Qurʾān is a created thing. Their argument was simple: whatever is made is created, and God says He made the Qurʾān. The reply of Ahl al-Sunnah, given at length by al-Rāzī and others, is that jaʿala in Arabic has two uses. With one object it can mean to create, as in 6:1, where God made the darkness and the light. With two objects, as here, where the object is "it" and the second object is "an Arabic Qurʾān", it means to render something into a state or to appoint it as something. God did not create the Qurʾān; He caused it to be in Arabic. The difference matters for the verse itself, apart from the theological quarrel. The verse is not about the Qurʾān coming into existence but about the form in which the eternal speech of God was expressed for a particular people.

**Arabic as an Argument Against Excuse**

Al-Ṭabarī reads the purpose clause as closing a door. The Qurʾān is in the Arabs' own tongue, and so they of all people have no excuse for failing to grasp it. This links to a wider theme. The Qurʾān repeatedly says it was sent down in a clear Arabic tongue (26:195), that it was sent as an Arabic recitation (12:2, 20:113, 39:28, 41:3, 42:7), and that every messenger was sent in the language of his own people so that he might make things plain to them (14:4). The Makkans could not claim that the message was foreign to them, obscure, or in need of translation. They heard it in the dialect they used in the market. Their rejection was therefore not a failure of comprehension but a refusal after comprehension, and a refusal after comprehension is exactly what the Qurʾān calls kufr, the covering over of what one has already seen. This is why the sūrah proceeds, in the verses that follow, to warn them using the examples of earlier peoples. Those peoples also received messengers who spoke their language, and their rejection carried the same weight.

**Laʿallakum: Hope, Purpose, or Both**

The clause laʿallakum taʿqilūn is usually rendered "so that you may understand" or "so perhaps you will understand". The particle laʿalla in ordinary Arabic expresses hope or expectation, and hope is not something one attributes to God, Who knows all outcomes. The exegetes handle this in two ways. Some say that when laʿalla is used by God it takes the meaning of purpose: the Qurʾān was made Arabic in order that you understand. Others, following Sībawayh, say the hope is on the human side. The situation is set up such that the listener may hope for understanding; the language is his, the words are plain, and the only thing missing is his willingness. Both readings yield the same practical sense, but the second is more faithful to the ordinary meaning of the particle and it says something true about how revelation works. God provides the conditions of understanding; the act of understanding remains the listener's own. The verb is taʿqilūn, from ʿaql, which means to bind or restrain, and by extension to reason. To understand the Qurʾān in this sense is to let it bind one's thinking to the truth, so that the mind stops wandering after every rumour and settles on what has been shown to it.

**Understanding Requires Language, Not the Reverse**

There is a plain lesson in this verse for how the Qurʾān is to be approached. It was made Arabic so that people would understand it. The goal was understanding; the Arabic was the means. A reading of the Qurʾān that stops at the sound of the Arabic and never reaches the meaning has taken the means for the end. The verse also establishes something for those who do not know Arabic. The Qurʾān came in a human language because meaning has to travel through a human language to reach a human mind. Translation, commentary, and teaching are the ways that same meaning reaches people who were not born in Makkah, and they serve the purpose the verse states, even though none of them is the Qurʾān itself. The Arabic text remains the revealed speech; the understanding is what it was revealed for. That balance, text and meaning together, is what the oath in the previous verse promised when it called the Book clear, and it is what this verse now makes explicit.
