## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:63

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who rescues you from the darkest times on land and at sea? He ˹alone˺ you call upon with humility, openly and secretly: “If You rescue us from this, we will be ever grateful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prayer of the Tight Corner**

The verse asks the Makkans about a moment every one of them knew: the point at which a situation has become hopeless and the only remaining act is to call out. The Book returns to this scene repeatedly because it is the one place where a human being's actual beliefs become visible. The idolaters could argue in the market about their gods, and in the dark they abandoned them without discussion.

The Book describes the same behaviour elsewhere, sometimes with the vivid detail that a person in danger calls on God with sincere devotion but then returns to his old associations once he is safe (29:65), and sometimes with the observation that when people are asked who saves them, they answer that it was God (31:32). The verse in front of us turns that knowledge into a question and lets the audience supply the answer the Book already knows they will give.

**Land and Sea**

The pairing of land and sea covers the two great fears of a trading city, and the merchants of Makkah knew both. A caravan crossing the desert faced thirst, sandstorms, and raiding; a ship faced the possibility that the water would simply close over the crew, with no grave and no witness. The Book uses the sea in several places as an image of helplessness, describing the drowning of the people of Noah and the rescue of Jonah, and it describes people in a ship as calling upon God with sincere faith until He brings them safely to land, at which point they begin to associate others with Him (17:67).

Commentators note that the verse names the two settings because each produced a different kind of fear. On land a person may still run, and at sea there is nowhere to go. The question is designed to reach a memory and not an argument, and the Book's method with its stubborn audience is often exactly this.

**Calling With Humility, Openly and Secretly**

The verse describes the manner of the call before it describes its content, and the description is precise. The person in danger prays with humility, meaning with a lowered voice and a subdued self, and he prays openly and secretly, which the commentators explain as praying aloud in company and quietly alone, or as the visible and hidden forms of supplication.

The two adverbs reveal how thin the Makkan position was. A person who prays in secret has admitted where the power lies, and he cannot then claim that his public position represents his belief. The Book recorded this inconsistency long before: those who call upon God in fear and hope, and those who cry out when harm befalls them, and then attribute a share of what they have been given to others (16:53; 39:8).

**The Vow Made in the Dark**

The verse gives the actual words used in that moment: if You rescue us from this, we will be ever grateful. Gratitude is the promise, which is the opposite of the response the Book says most of them produced. The sūrah of Yūnus describes the pattern exactly, saying that when God rescues people they immediately transgress in the land unjustly, and then addresses them with the reminder that their transgression is against their own souls (10:23).

The vow is the proof the verse needs. Nobody promises gratitude to a stone in a storm, and nobody offers a bargain to an image. The words used at the moment of danger are the audience's own testimony that they know who the rescuer is, and the Book uses their testimony against them rather than against an outsider.

**Why the Question Is Put to the Prophet to Ask**

The command begins with Say, which places the question in the Prophet's mouth rather than in God's. The effect is that the audience is being answered by the man they were disputing with, using material they cannot deny. The sūrah uses this device repeatedly, as it did with the question about hearing and sight (6:46) and the question about being overwhelmed by the punishment (6:47).

The interrogative form also keeps the audience responsible for the conclusion. The Book does not say that every one of them knows the answer; it asks them who rescues, and the answer arrives in the next verse as a verdict rather than as a speculation.

**What the Verse Establishes Before the Rebuke**

By the time the rebuke arrives, the audience has already granted the whole premise of the message. They have been asked who alone can answer a call from a sinking ship, and the answer they must give is the same one the Prophet gives. The following verse states it and then names the contradiction that their own religious practice represented, which is why the rebuke lands where it does.

The sequence is worth noting for how the Book argues with an unwilling audience. It does not begin by declaring them wrong; it leads them to the edge of a conclusion they already believe and lets them stand there, and the sentence that follows is short because the work has already been done.

**The Same Scene in the Sūrah of Yūnus**

The Book tells this story elsewhere with details that make the Makkan setting unmistakable. In the sūrah of Yūnus, people are described boarding a ship that sails with a favourable wind while the passengers rejoice, and then the wind turns, the waves come from every side, and the travellers are certain they are about to die. At that point they cry out to Allah with sincere devotion and promise that if they are saved they will certainly be grateful (10:22). The wording of the prayer in that account is virtually the same as the wording preserved in this verse, which is a sign of how closely the two passages are related in the Book's argument.

The sūrah of the Ant carries the same question in the form of a challenge: who responds to the distressed when they cry to Him and relieves their affliction, and who makes people successors in the earth? Is it another god besides Allah, asks the verse, before noting that people are hardly mindful (27:62). The Book's audience is asked to answer its own question in three separate sūrahs, and the answer is never disputed.

**The Mercy Hidden in the Question**

There is a further point in the way the verse is framed. The question does not ask what a person believes in general, nor does it test him on the fine points of doctrine that the Makkans were disputing. It asks only about the one moment when no human being can maintain a pretence, and it treats the honesty of that moment as a foothold.

The following verse rebukes the inconsistency, and the rebuke would be unanswerable if there were nothing else in the passage. But the Book's habit of returning to that same scene across many sūrahs suggests something further. The call made in the dark is treated as the recollection of a truth the person already carries, and the reminder of it is meant to make the recovery of that truth possible before the next crisis arrives.
