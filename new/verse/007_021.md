## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:21

> And he swore to them, “I am truly your sincere advisor.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Sworn to the Pair**

The verse states that the adversary swore to the pair that he was their sincere adviser, and the oath is the element that distinguishes the temptation from a simple false statement. The commentary tradition reports that the oath was sworn in the name of God, and that the pair took it as evidence of sincerity because they could not imagine that anyone would swear by God in order to lie. The narrative presents the deception at its strongest, since the promise of the previous verse has now been guaranteed by an appeal to what is most sacred.

The Book's warnings about the oaths of the wicked appear in its accounts of the hypocrites and the deniers, and the sūrah of the Bee describes those who make God the subject of their oaths for a small price. The verse in front of us gives the first instance of the pattern, and the sūrah's instruction to the children of Adam about the enemy's deception is addressed to people who will meet the same kind of guarantee.

**What Adam Is Reported to Have Said**

The commentary tradition reports a defence offered by Adam after the fall: that the adversary swore to him by God, and that he could not have believed that any creature would swear by God in order to deceive him. The report is used to explain how the pair were taken in by an oath that they had no reason to doubt. The narrative in front of us supplies the oath, and the report supplies the answer to the question of why it worked.

The Book's teaching about the enemy's methods follows from the same observation. The enemy does not come with an open declaration of hostility; he comes with the appearance of good counsel, and the sūrah's later passages show the audience being deceived in the same manner by claims about the practice of their ancestors and about what God has commanded. The verse in front of us states the first and clearest case.

**The Sincere Adviser**

The word the verse puts in the adversary's mouth describes a counsellor who wants only the good of the one he advises, and the sūrah's later verses give the audience instructions about whom to take as a counsellor. The Book's descriptions of the guidance that comes through the messengers present them as the sincere advisers of their peoples, and the sūrah of the Poets reports the prophets addressing their peoples with the declaration that they are trustworthy messengers. The verse in front of us shows the title being claimed by the one who intends the opposite of what it means.

The Book's treatment of the counterfeit adviser appears in its warnings about the leaders of misguidance who will disown their followers at the reckoning, and the sūrah of the Heights will describe the followers blaming their leaders in the Fire (7:38–39). The oath in front of us is the beginning of the relationship whose end the sūrah describes in its later verses.

**The Oath as a Model of Deception**

The Book's language for the enemy's activity includes the promise, the whisper, and the oath, and the three are joined in the account of the first temptation. The sūrah of the Night Journey states that the adversary makes promises and raises hopes, and the sūrah of the Women states that his promises are deception (4:120). The oath in front of us adds the strongest available guarantee to the false promise, and the narrative shows that it was believed.

The sūrah's instruction to the children of Adam in its following verses is shaped by the account. The children are told not to let the enemy deceive them as he deceived their parents, and the instruction names the deception as the thing to be recognized. The oath is recorded so that the method can be identified when it is used again.

**The Pair and Their Descendants**

The account in front of us presents the first human pair as the recipients of a deception that their descendants will meet in other forms, and the sūrah addresses the descendants directly in the passage that follows. The verses that call on the children of Adam to take their adornment at every place of worship, to eat and drink without excess, and to avoid the forbidden things are addressed to people who have been told this story.

The Book's practice of making the story of the first pair a lesson for later generations appears in the sūrah of the Cow, where the descent from the Garden is followed by the promise of guidance to those who follow it (2:38). The verse in front of us supplies the point at which the deception succeeded, and the sūrah's later instructions supply the means by which the descendants can avoid it.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands twenty-first in the chapter, and it follows the false promise and precedes the fall. The narrative then records that the pair were brought down by the deception, that their nakedness was exposed, and that they began to cover themselves with the leaves of the Garden. The oath in front of us is the last element of the temptation before it takes effect.

The reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening has been given the revelation, the command, the warning, the account of the reckoning, the adversary's story, and now the first temptation. The verses that follow complete the narrative and bring the pair to the confession that the sūrah commends.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that the adversary brought about the fall of the pair through deception, that they tasted the tree and their nakedness was exposed, and that they began to sew the leaves of the Garden together to cover themselves, and it reports the call of their Lord asking whether He had not forbidden them the tree and told them that Satan was a manifest enemy (7:22). The oath in front of us is followed by the act and by the question that names what the pair had been told.

The two verses together complete the account of the temptation. The guarantee is given, the boundary is crossed, the covering is lost, and the Lord who had warned them asks the question that establishes their responsibility. The sūrah then records their answer.
