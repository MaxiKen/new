## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:113

> The Jews say, “The Christians have nothing to stand on” and the Christians say, “The Jews have nothing to stand on,” although both recite the Scriptures. And those ˹pagans˺ who have no knowledge say the same ˹about people of faith˺. Surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mutual Accusation**

The verse reports each community denying that the other stands on anything, and it sets the two statements beside each other so that the symmetry is plain. Each side makes the same charge against the other, and the sentence's arrangement shows that the accusation cannot be true of both.

The Book has just answered the claim that only one community would enter the Garden, and this sentence supplies the other half of the picture. The communities were not merely confident about their own standing; they had concluded that the other's was void, and they had been teaching the conclusion to their followers. **The Occasion Reported**

Commentators connect the verse with a dispute between a group of Jews and the Christian delegation from Najrān that came to Madinah, though others read it as a description that applies to the two communities generally and does not require them to have been present before each other. The reports agree that the exchange the verse records had an actual setting.

**"Although Both Recite the Scriptures"**

The middle clause supplies the Book's objection to the mutual charge. Both communities recited the scriptures, and the Book holds that each scripture affirms the truth that the other community was denying. The Torah speaks to the prophethood of Jesus, and the Gospel affirms Moses and the Book given to him, so the argument each side used to deny the other could be read in support of the other.

The Book's method here is to use the opponents' own texts as evidence against their use of them. The sūrah has already told the community that its scripture contained the marks of the messenger it declined, and this verse extends the same reasoning to the mutual denials of the two groups.

**Those Without Knowledge**

The sentence adds that those who had no knowledge said the same thing about people of faith, and the clause widens the diagnosis. The habit of denying that others stand on anything is not a peculiarity of communities with scriptures; it belongs to whoever assumes that his own position is the only possible one.

The Book mentions the polytheists in this connection, and the commentators noted that the clause also extends to nations before the two communities. What the verse identifies is a human tendency that revelation is meant to correct, and the addition of the pagans prevents the reader from treating the previous clause as a verdict on two particular groups. **Judgment Left to the Day of Resurrection**

The verse closes by stating that God will judge between the communities on the Day of Judgment concerning what they differed about, and the Book takes the same position in several places where religious disagreement is described. The Qur'an states in other chapters that the truth about such matters will be disclosed when the parties return to their Lord, and it repeats the formula in the chapter of the table and elsewhere.

That positioning serves two purposes. It removes the believers from the position of final judge between communities, and it affirms that the disagreements are not beyond resolution simply because the parties cannot resolve them. The Book's answer to a dispute it does not settle in this world is a statement about the next.

**Al-Kāshānī's Observation**

A commentator cited in the tradition observed that the two communities were veiled from the truth by opposite attachments: one by its fixation on the outward form and the other by its absorption in the inward meaning, and he added that similar problems appear among Muslim schools of thought. The observation does not settle the doctrinal questions the verse describes, and it identifies a pattern the Book's readers can recognize in their own disputes.

The sūrah's placement of the verse at this point in the chapter supports that reading. The communities being described had scriptures and recited them, and what divided them was not a lack of text but the way each selected from it. The Book's own community is warned against the same practice throughout the passage, and the warnings become explicit in the verses that follow.

**What the Verse Teaches About Disagreement**

Read with the surrounding verses, the sentence establishes the Book's manner of handling religious disputes among communities it addresses. It states the claims, identifies the contradiction, notes the scriptures that both parties recite, and leaves the verdict to God. The believers are instructed to pardon and bear with those who oppose them, and the argument is carried on by presenting what has been revealed rather than by pronouncing on the fate of others. **Leaving the Verdict to the Day of Judgment**

The Book states in several places that God will inform people of their differences when they return to Him, and the repetition of the formula indicates how consistently the Qur'an declines to settle religious disputes within this world. That refusal is not a concession that the questions are unanswerable; it is a statement about who is competent to decide them. The believers are told what has been revealed to them and are instructed to present it with wisdom and kind advice, and the fate of those who decline is left where the verse leaves it. The same passage commands the believers to pardon and bear with those who oppose them, and the two instructions work together: the community states its case, conducts itself well, and leaves the judgment to the day when every dispute will be settled by the One who knows what people differed about and why.
