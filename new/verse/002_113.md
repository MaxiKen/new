## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:113

> The Jews say, “The Christians have nothing to stand on” and the Christians say, “The Jews have nothing to stand on,” although both recite the Scriptures. And those ˹pagans˺ who have no knowledge say the same ˹about people of faith˺. Surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mutual Nullification**

The verse documents an exchange, and the exchange's symmetry is its subject: *the Jews say, "The Christians have nothing to stand on," and the Christians say, "The Jews have nothing to stand on."* The formula — *laysa ʿalā shayʾ*, is not upon anything — is total nullification: not error in particulars, not deviation in degree, but the absence of any ground at all, the other's religion declared foundationless in its entirety. Each voice, quoting the other's exact sentence back, gives the verse its structure of mirrored absolutes, and the mirroring is diagnostic: the two communities' judgments of each other are identical in form, which suggests the form itself — total nullification of the rival — is doing the work, rather than any examination of the rival's actual foundations. The exegetes divide on the occasion, and the division is instructive. Some tie the verse to a concrete event: the exchange between Jews and the Christian delegation of Najrān in Madina, the two parties arguing before the Prophet ﷺ, each nullifying the other on the spot (the reports of Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī). Others read the verse generally — Jews and Christians saying this of each other across time, without needing to be in each other's presence or even each other's century. The general reading fits the verse's grammar better: the present-tense *says* describes a standing speech, not a single debate — but the Najrān report supplies the scene's texture, two scriptural communities performing, before the recital's own Messenger, the exact mutual verdict the verse quotes.

**Although Both Recite the Scriptures**

The verse's irony-clause is its sharpest instrument: *although both recite the Scriptures*. The nullifiers are not illiterate rivals trading ignorant contempt — they are Book-people, and their Books contradict their sentence. The exegetes state the contradiction from both sides: the Torah the Jews recite testifies to truths Christianity holds — its own prophetic line, its messianic expectation, the standing of Jesus's forebears; the Gospel the Christians recite affirms Moses and the Torah it emerged from. Each community's recitation is therefore a daily witness against its nullification: the very act the verse names — reciting the Scripture — carries inside it the affirmation the speakers' verdict denies. The clause also fixes the nullification's mechanism. A religion's claim to totality is maintained by selective recitation: the Book read, but the passages that ground the rival read past — the casting-behind-the-backs of verse 101 in its liturgical form. Al-Ṭabarī adds the historical note that sharpens the irony: in the beginning of their religious histories each community did stand on something true, and the divergence grew through difference and alteration — so the nullification is not only contradicted by the Books as recited but by the communities' own origins, each rooted in a truth the other's root shares. The verse's structure — claim, mirrored claim, irony-clause — indicts the exchange without adjudicating its content: whatever the two parties' actual differences, a mutual sentence of foundationlessness pronounced by two reciters of grounding Scriptures is self-refuting in the pronouncing.

**“Those Who Have No Knowledge Say the Same”**

The verse widens its lens in a third clause, and the widening is its sociology: *and those who have no knowledge say the same*. The identification of this third group runs two ways in the tradition — earlier peoples who made the same mutual nullifications before Jews and Christians existed (Ibn Kathīr's reading), or the idolaters of Arabia saying the equivalent about all people of faith (al-Rāzī's) — and both readings carry the same finding: the nullification-sentence is not a product of scriptural knowledge, because it appears identically where no scripture is present. The unlettered say *the same*, in the verse's exact phrase — the same total verdict, the same form, without the Books to recite or contradict. The leveling is the verse's deepest cut: the speech of the informed nullifier and the speech of the ignorant one are indistinguishable, which means the information was never the sentence's source. Total nullification of the rival is a human default posture, produced by partisanship rather than study, and scripture's presence adds to it only the irony-clause — the recitation that contradicts what the reciter says. The clause extends the verse's jurisdiction past its two named communities to every partisan exchange in every tradition: the sectarian verdicts pronounced with a Book in hand, and the same verdicts pronounced with none, one sentence, three populations, identical form — and the verse's judgment falls on the form itself.

**The Judgment Deferred**

The verse's final sentence declines to settle the dispute and assigns it instead: *surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute*. The deferral is not evasion but doctrine, and the Qur'an states the doctrine as a consistent theme: Allah will judge between you all on Judgment Day regarding your differences (22:69); the same deferral filed across the Book — at 3:55 over Jesus's fate, at 5:48 over the communities' divergent laws, at 10:93, 16:124, 32:25, 39:3 and 46 — each dispute named, each verdict reserved to the Day. The reservation has three consequences the recital's audience is meant to hold. First, the dispute is real: deferral is not dismissal, and the differences the verse files will be adjudicated rather than dissolved — the Day's judgment is between positions, which means positions differ in truth. Second, the adjudicator is fixed: not the parties, not their mutual nullifications, not the loudest recitation — Allah judges *between them*, the two-sided verdict belonging to neither side. Third, the deferral disciplines the parties' present conduct: the community that knows its case is scheduled argues differently from the community that believes it must win now, and the verse's audience — the Muslims hearing the Najrān exchange or its equivalents — receives the discipline as instruction: hold what your own revelation makes clear, and leave the reserved questions in the reserved court. The sūrah supplies the deferral's frame in its own earlier grammar: mankind was one community, then it differed, and the differences await a prior decree's unfolding (10:19); difference itself is inside the divine arrangement — *they will always choose to differ* (11:118) — which makes its final adjudication a divine act rather than a human achievement.

**The Inward Reading, and the Movement's Close**

The esoteric commentators add a reading that converts the verse into a mirror for its own tradition: the Christians, in al-Kāshānī's note, are veiled from the outward by their attachment to the inward — spirit exalted, law attenuated — while the Jews are veiled from the inward by their attachment to the outward — law maintained, spirit's reach contracted — and the same one-sidedness besets schools within Islam, each nullifying what its own emphasis has let atrophy. The reading is the verse's permanent transfer: the mutual-nullification form reproduces inside every tradition as the exchange between its emphases, each party's ground real and each party's sentence on the other's ground false, the whole dispute awaiting the Judge who holds both sides' foundations in one knowledge. The verse thereby closes the recital's long second movement on its widest note. The movement began with a charter and a covenant addressed to one community (83–84) and ends with a dispute between two communities, an ignorance shared with a third, and a verdict reserved by the Owner of all of them — the progression from one people's record to humanity's standing case. The exclusivism of verse 111 was answered by the universal formula of verse 112; the mutual nullification of verse 113 is answered by the deferred judgment — and between the answer-forms the recital's position is complete: entry by whoever submits and does good, disputes held for the Day, nullification of rivals pronounced by no one the verse approves. The believers hearing the sūrah keep both instruments — the formula for their own standing, the deferral for every argument they cannot close — and the recital moves on, its eschatological court now formally in session for every claim it has filed.
