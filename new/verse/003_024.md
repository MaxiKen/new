## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:24

> This is because they say, “The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.” They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim That Made the Refusal Comfortable**

The previous verse described people turning away from a book that could settle their disputes. This verse gives the reason: a doctrine about their own future. They believed that whatever fire awaited them would be brief, and the belief was doing steady work in their lives, because a person who expects to be scorched lightly can afford to ignore the corrections he is offered.

The Qur'an quotes the claim in an earlier sūrah in nearly the same words: *“˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, ‘The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days.’ Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?’”* (2:80). The formulation there exposes the logic. An exemption from punishment is a promise, and promises are made by the one who owns the punishment. Nobody had been told.

**Where the Number Came From**

Commentators searched for the origin of the "few days" and came back with more than one report. Some said the figure referred to the forty nights during which the Israelites worshipped the calf, on the theory that a matching term of punishment had been fixed. Others reported a scheme of seven days, one for each millennium of the world's duration as they calculated it.

The reports are not agreed, and the disagreement itself is the interesting part. What the Book records is not a fixed arithmetic but the habit of producing one. A community that has decided it will not be punished permanently will find a text, a calculation, or a tradition to justify the conclusion, and the calculation will look precise enough to be repeated from the pulpit. The verse names the builder of the doctrine as *wishful lying*, a phrase that locates the origin in desire rather than in evidence.

**A Doctrine That Serves Its Owners**

This is the reason the Book treats the claim as serious. A limited sentence for one group is not a private opinion; it is a theory of privilege, and its practical effect is to remove the fear that would otherwise enforce the commandments. If the punishment is brief, the rules are recommendations.

The Qur'an answers the theory elsewhere in the same tone: *“The Jews and the Christians each say, ‘We are the children of Allah and His most beloved!’ Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Why then does He punish you for your sins? No! You are only humans like others of His Own making.’”* (5:18). The answer does not argue about days. It denies the premise that any community's ancestry or standing changes the terms of the accounting, and it puts the claimants back where the sūrah put everyone: human beings made by the same hands.

**"Deceived in Their Faith" — the Grammar of Self-Deception**

The second sentence says they have been deceived *in their faith* by their own fabrications, and the phrasing keeps the responsibility where it belongs. The Book does not present them as victims of a trick played by someone else. What has deceived them is what they invented.

The Qur'an uses the vocabulary of deception for the world's false promises in other places, and it always names the deceiver plainly. The life of this world is a delusion (57:20); the promise of God is true, so do not let the life of this world deceive you (35:5); what emboldened a person against his Lord if not the assumption that generosity meant approval? (82:6). The pattern is consistent: the texts of the Book treat self-deception as an act a person performs on himself, with his own desires supplying the material.

**Why a Borrowed Certainty Feels Like Knowledge**

The doctrine had one more quality that made it powerful: everyone around the person believed it. A claim repeated by neighbours, teachers, and ancestors acquires the texture of a fact, and few people audit the things their community considers obvious.

The verse supplies the audit that was never performed. It asks about the source of the guarantee — whether a pledge had been received, or whether the claim came from the community's own storytelling. The test is portable. It can be applied to a person's expectations about his own standing, and it usually returns the same answer: what felt like knowledge had no owner behind it.

**A Warning the Book Repeated for Its Own**

The Book does not restrict this warning to the people addressed here. It warns those who recite it against the same disease, because the mechanism does not belong to any one religion. Whenever a group concludes that its identity settles its account, the verse above is the diagnosis, and the cure offered is the same: hold no promise except the ones God has made, and make no claim about the Day that the Day's owner has not made.

Notice how much of the sūrah's medicine is contained in one sentence. The Book does not respond to the claim with a longer calculation of its own, or with a list of the texts that might support a stricter reading. It asks a single question about the source of the guarantee and then leaves the claim where it found it — in the mouth of the person making it. That restraint is itself an argument. A doctrine that depends on its own repetition does not need to be refuted; it needs to be identified.
