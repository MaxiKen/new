## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:27

> And it is Allah’s Will to turn to you in grace, but those who follow their desires wish to see you deviate entirely ˹from Allah’s Way˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Wills Facing One Another**

This verse places two intentions side by side in one sentence. *Wa Allāhu yurīdu an yatūba ʿalaykum* — and it is Allah’s Will to turn to you in grace — repeats the promise of the previous verse that He wishes to make things clear, guide to the noble ways, and turn to you in mercy (4:26). The repetition is not redundancy; it isolates the turning as the focus. Immediately beside it stands a contrary wish: *wa yurīdu alladhīna yattabiʿūna al-shahawāt an tamīlū maylan ʿaẓīmā* — but those who follow their desires wish to see you deviate entirely from Allah’s Way. The verb *yurīdu* — to will, to want — is used for both, making the contrast a contest of wills. On one side is the will that clarifies limits and then pardons those who keep them; on the other is the will that blurs limits and draws people into gross deviation. The Qur’an elsewhere says that Satan wants to lead astray and that human whims invite to the same, and the sūrah has shown what that deviation looks like in practice: consuming orphan wealth (4:10), exceeding inheritance limits (4:14), treating women as inheritable (4:19), reclaiming dowries by slander (4:20), and marrying where God said do not. Those who follow desires benefit from each blur, because in each the strong gain at the weak’s expense. The verse tells the believer that the line he keeps is not a private preference but a front between two larger intentions.

**Who Are Those Who Follow Desires**

The phrase *alladhīna yattabiʿūna al-shahawāt* — those who follow their desires — was read broadly by early authorities. Some identified them as those who pursue unlawful sexual relations and want others to join them so that their own choice feels less isolated. Others read it as those who follow cravings in general — for wealth, dominance, and display — and therefore want the community’s law to bend so that their cravings can be satisfied without censure. The sūrah’s immediate context supports both, because the preceding laws covered both *fāḥishah* and property. Those who committed indecency and needed four witnesses to prove it (4:15–16) would wish the barrier of proof lowered or the act normalized; those who ate orphan wealth or seized dowries would wish the limits on property lifted. In each case the wish is not only to do wrong but to see the community deviate — *an tamīlū* — to incline away, to swerve off the path that was just made clear (4:26). The addition *maylan ʿaẓīmā* — a great, profound deviation — was explained as not a small slip but a wholesale turn, where the exception becomes the norm and what was once called *maqt* — despicable (4:22) — is renamed as freedom. The verse warns that the pressure to deviate will be presented as tolerance or as alignment with desire, but its size is great and its direction is away.

**Turning in Grace Against the Pull of Deviation**

The contrast between *yatūba* — to turn in grace — and *tamīlū* — to tilt, to deviate — is spatial. One turn brings the community upright; the other tilts it off balance. The Qur’an uses *mayl* elsewhere for tilting toward one wife at the expense of another where justice is required, cautioning *“do not incline completely toward one, leaving another suspended”* (4:129), and that caution shows how *mayl* operates in family life as well. Here the tilt is entire — *ʿaẓīm* — a comprehensive swerve from the noble ways just mentioned. The sūrah’s answer to that pull is not to deny desire but to place it inside limits where it can be chaste and sheltered. The lawful field beyond the prohibited degrees — sought with wealth, in protection, not in fornication (4:24) — and the permission for a believing bondwoman where means fail (4:25) — already showed that desire has a lawful home. The wish of those who follow desires is to remove the walls of that home and call the open field liberation. The divine will to turn in grace offers the opposite: walls that protect, clarity that distinguishes, and pardon that follows when the walls are kept. That is why the sūrah pairs the two wills in one breath; the believer must choose which will he aligns with, knowing both are inviting.

**Why the Verse Repeats Allah’s Will to Turn**

Repeating *Allah wishes to turn to you* immediately after saying He wishes to make things clear and guide to the ways before (4:26) isolates grace as the purpose of law. Law without the promise of turning would be heard as burden alone, and the next verse will say explicitly that Allah wishes to lighten your burdens because you were created weak (4:28). Here the lightening is already implied: to be turned toward in grace after clarification is to be given a way back when the law has been broken. That way back was already detailed: repent soon after acting ignorantly (4:17), repent and mend and be relieved (4:16), give back what was taken, and speak equitably (4:9). The wish of the followers of desire is to keep people from that return by normalizing deviation until the return no longer seems needed. The sūrah has already defined the failed repentance that comes only when death is present (4:18); the deviators’ wish is to keep people persisting until that failure becomes inevitable. By naming both wishes together, the verse makes the choice urgent. One will has prepared a painful punishment for persistence (4:18) but has also prepared Gardens for keeping limits (4:13) and forgiveness for soon return; the other will has no garden to offer, only the satisfaction of seeing others swerve greatly with them.

**A Community Invited to Stay on the Clear Path**

The verse’s practical effect is to steady a community that has just received many boundaries. New boundaries attract critique — that they are too strict, too traditional, or too lenient — and that critique often comes from desire dressed as advice. The sūrah answers by personifying the critique: it comes from those who follow desires, and it wants not a small adjustment but a great inclination. To stay on the path that was made clear is therefore not rigidity; it is loyalty to the will that wills to turn toward you. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“Allah does not wish to place hardship upon you”* (5:6) and here it says He wishes to turn toward you, both statements showing that the limits are not meant to burden but to shelter. A community that keeps the orphan’s wealth with witnesses (4:6), honors fixed shares after debts (4:11–12), and seeks marriage with wealth in chastity (4:24–25) will appear distinct from those around it; that distinctness may feel like deviation to outsiders, but the verse reframes it. The great deviation would be to tilt away from those limits at the invitation of desire. Remaining upright, with the help of the One who wills to turn toward you when you do, is the sūrah’s definition of staying on the way.
