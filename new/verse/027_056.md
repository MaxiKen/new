## Sūrah an-Naml 27:56

> But his people’s only response was to say, “Expel Lot’s followers from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Answer They Had**

The verse marks the reply as the whole of what they produced: *fa-mā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā an qālū* — the answer of his people was nothing but that they said. The construction is restrictive. Out of everything available to them, this is what came.

Lūṭ had asked two questions. A community with an argument would have made one; a community that disagreed could have said so. What they generate instead is a motion of expulsion, which is not a reply to either question. The Qur'an uses this same restrictive formula for other peoples confronted by their prophets, and it functions each time as a verdict on the quality of the response. Nothing was answered. The objection was treated as a presence to be removed rather than a claim to be met, and the formula records that the removal was the entirety of their contribution.

**Purity Turned Into an Accusation**

The charge is astonishing when read closely. They call for the expulsion of Lūṭ's household on the grounds that *innahum unāsun yataṭahharūn* — they are people who keep themselves pure.

The verb is *taṭahhara*, and the Qur'an uses it elsewhere as unambiguous praise. God is said to love those who purify themselves, and a mosque is commended because men in it love to be purified. Here the same word is used as an indictment. Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī both gloss the sense as a sneer: they are trying to keep themselves clean of what we do. The people of Lūṭ have not disputed that his household is pure; they have agreed, and made the purity the offence. That inversion is the most complete moral reversal recorded in this chapter, and it is delivered in a single word. The Qur'an reports the same sneer from these people in another chapter, in almost identical wording, which indicates it was their settled formulation rather than a remark made once in anger.

**When Restraint Becomes an Insult**

The mechanism behind the insult is worth setting out, because it is not peculiar to this city. A community that has normalised a practice finds abstention intolerable in a way that mere disagreement is not.

Disagreement can be argued with and dismissed. Abstention is a standing demonstration that the practice is optional, performed daily by people living next door, and it therefore functions as a judgment regardless of whether the abstainers say anything. Lūṭ's household is not accused of preaching, obstructing or organising. They are accused of being clean. That is enough to require their removal, because their existence is the argument. The chapter's earlier verses described people whose deeds were made to look good to them; this is what such a condition does when it meets a counter-example — it cannot leave the counter-example in place.

**"From Your Land"**

The expulsion is framed possessively: *min qaryatikum*, from your town. The speakers address one another, and the town belongs to them.

The pronoun does the political work. Lūṭ is a prophet sent to his own people and has lived among them, and the phrasing writes him out of the community he belongs to. Once his household is *them* and the town is *yours*, the removal becomes tidying rather than exile. This is the same operation Thamūd performed when they told Ṣāliḥ he and his followers were an ill omen, converting an internal disagreement into an external contamination. The Qur'an records the move twice in ten verses, and the repetition marks it as a recognised pattern: communities that will not answer an objection reclassify the objector as a foreign body.

**The Sentence They Passed on Themselves**

There is a grim accuracy in their proposal. They ask for the righteous to be removed from the city, and within two verses exactly that happens — Lūṭ and his household are taken out, and what follows falls on those who remain.

The Qur'an does not point this out, and the restraint is more effective than a comment would be. Their demand is granted in form and reversed in meaning. The same request that was meant to purge the town of an irritant becomes the description of its rescue operation, with the difference that the people leaving are the ones who survive. This is the second time in the chapter that a plan has arrived at the opposite of its intention; Thamūd's nine swore an oath that ended their nation. Both cases make the same structural point without stating it. The rescue is reported in the next verse, along with the one exception to it.

**A Response With No Content**

Strip the reply to its components and nothing remains but a motion and a justification. The motion is expulsion. The justification is that the people to be expelled are clean. There is no denial, no counter-claim about the practice, no defence of it, and no attack on Lūṭ's standing to speak.

That emptiness is what the restrictive opening formula was marking. The Qur'an could have reported an argument and refuted it; what it reports is that no argument was offered. This matters for the chapter's larger case, which has been measuring communities by the quality of their response to evidence. Sheba's queen received a demand and built a test around it. Sulaymān received a report and verified it. Pharaoh's court produced at least a classification, however dishonest — they called the signs sorcery, which is a claim capable of being wrong. Lūṭ's people produce nothing that can be assessed at all, which places them at the bottom of the chapter's scale. The reply is recorded in their own words, so the assessment that follows rests on what they said rather than on a characterisation of it.
