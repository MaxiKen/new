## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:52

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Sufficient is Allah as a Witness between me and you. He ˹fully˺ knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And those who believe in falsehood and disbelieve in Allah, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Dispute Handed to the Only Competent Witness**

The demand for a sign in the previous verses was a demand for evidence that the audience would accept on its own terms, and the answer given here declines the tribunal and names another. The Prophet is told to state that God is sufficient as a witness between him and his opponents, which is the language of a case that cannot be settled by the parties themselves. The Book uses the phrase in other disputes as well, and the pattern is consistent: when the argument reaches the point where nothing the messenger produces will satisfy the other side, the matter is transferred to the One who knows what has been said and what has been concealed. The sentence does not concede defeat. It changes the venue.

Handing the dispute over is also a practical instruction for a messenger who cannot follow every argument to its end. The sūrah gives him words that place the case in a record he does not administer, which frees him from the demand to win every exchange and leaves his task at the level of delivery.

**Why a Witness Is the Right Category**

The dispute in Makkah was not primarily about astronomy or arithmetic; it was about whether the man speaking was telling the truth about what he had received. A witness is exactly what such a question requires, and the Book is careful to name one whose competence no party can question. The verse goes on to give the ground of that competence: God knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth, which means He has full access to the fact at issue and to the motives of everyone discussing it. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the same sufficiency in the context of the Prophet being denied, noting that God bears witness to what He has sent down, that He sent it with His knowledge, and that the angels also bear witness, while God alone is sufficient (4:166).

**"Between Me and You"**

The phrase is forensic, and it fixes the two parties to the disagreement precisely. One man brings a message and a community denies it, and there is no third party on the ground with jurisdiction to determine who is right. The Book records the same impasse elsewhere when the disbelievers tell the Prophet flatly that he is no messenger, and he is told to answer that God is sufficient as a witness between him and them, along with those who have knowledge of the scripture (13:43). The addition in that verse is instructive, since it shows that the witness in this arrangement is not silent: people who have studied earlier revelations count as testimony in the Book's understanding of evidence, and the sūrah has already reported that some of them believed.

**Believing in Falsehood**

The sentence closes by describing the opponents with a phrase that repays attention: they believe in falsehood and disbelieve in God. The order is deliberate. The Book does not present these people as neutral skeptics holding their verdict in reserve; it presents them as people who have already committed themselves to something else, and the term rendered as falsehood covers idols, fabricated claims, and every authority set up in opposition to God. That prior commitment explains the demand for signs discussed in the preceding verses, since a person who has something to defend will not be persuaded by an argument that removes it. The Book's diagnosis of unbelief is consistently that it is a loyalty rather than a conclusion.

The phrase also defines the alternative rather than merely condemning the people who chose it. What the opponents were placing their confidence in was a set of claims about objects that could not verify them, and the Book's word for that material covers both a lie and everything built upon one.

**The Vocabulary of Loss**

The final word of the verse comes from the language of trade, and the audience addressed was composed largely of merchants who would have felt the weight immediately. A person described as being in loss has seen his capital disappear; in the Book the term describes a life spent on goods that turn out to have no value at the settlement. Sūrah al-Kahf states the principle in its sharpest form when it reports the complaint of those who discover that their works have been scattered, and the sūrah under discussion has already described false protectors as a spider's web. The two images agree: what the losers purchased with their lives could never have held, and the bill for it is presented at the only moment when the purchase cannot be reversed.

The register is drawn from the market the audience knew best. Profit and loss describe outcomes a trader measures in a ledger, and the Book uses that vocabulary for the Hereafter because the calculation involved is of the same kind: something given up in the present against something received later.

**Why the Sentence Suits the Chapter's Ending**

This verse stands near the end of the sūrah's long argument, and its vocabulary gathers what has gone before. The witness answers the demand for evidence; the knowledge of the heavens and the earth echoes the sūrah's appeals to creation and to travel; and the description of the opponents as believers in falsehood recalls the idols that cannot provide and the protectors who cannot protect. What the sentence does not do is pronounce a punishment. It states a relation of commerce between what people invest in and what they receive, and it leaves the reader to place himself on one side or the other of the account.

The sentence also gives the reader a place to stand before the closing verses begin. Everything after it in the sūrah is instruction addressed to the believers, and the transition works because the dispute has been formally handed to the authority that will settle it.

**The Prophet as a Witness in His Turn**

The Book assigns the Prophet the same office the verse assigns to God, and it does so in passages that define his mission: he has been sent as a witness, a deliverer of good news, and a warner (33:45; 48:8). The two uses of the word are connected. In this life the messenger serves as a witness over the community that received him, and the Book describes a moment when every community will produce its witness and the Prophet will be brought as a witness over his own (4:41). The verse under discussion names God as the witness between the two parties in the present dispute, which places the Prophet's testimony and his opponents' denial inside a record that is already complete. The sentence does not need to argue about who is right, because the administration of the case has been described from both ends.

**The Difference Between a Witness and a Referee**

One further distinction belongs to the sentence, and it concerns what a witness is for. A witness does not settle a dispute at the moment he is named; he establishes what happened, and the settlement comes later. The Book names God as witness here and elsewhere without promising that the disagreement in Makkah would be resolved in the Prophet's lifetime, and the arrangement tells the reader where the outcome is stored. The community being addressed is not asked to win; it is asked to conduct itself in the knowledge that the record is being kept by someone who cannot be misled. That is a harder discipline than victory, and the sūrah's closing verses will turn to the practical consequences of it for believers living under pressure.
