## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:45

> Those ˹destroyed˺ before them denied as well—and these ˹Meccans˺ have not attained even one-tenth of what We had given their predecessors. Yet ˹when˺ they denied My messengers, how severe was My response!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Argument From Proportion**

The verse runs a comparison and draws a conclusion from the ratio. Earlier peoples denied their messengers and were destroyed. The present opponents have not reached a tenth of what those peoples possessed. Therefore the confidence of the present opponents is worth less than the confidence of people who had ten times the grounds for it and were still overtaken. This is a different move from the sūrah's earlier appeals to history, which pointed at Sheba's ruin as an example (34:19). Here the argument is quantitative. It does not merely say that a precedent exists; it says the precedent involved a stronger party, and reasons downward from there. Nothing about the argument requires the audience to accept any theological premise. It requires them only to compare two sets of resources, which is the kind of comparison a trading people made professionally.

**"Not a Tenth" — Of What, Exactly?**

The Arabic is *mā balaghū miʿshāra mā ātaynāhum*, they have not reached a tenth of what We gave them. The verse does not itemise what was given, and the commentators have filled it out in the obvious directions: power, wealth, numbers, length of life, the scale of what earlier civilisations built. The sūrah has supplied one of these examples itself, in detail, a few verses earlier — Sheba with its two irrigated plains, its chain of way-stations, its safe roads by day and night (34:15, 34:18). Against that, the Quraysh were a merchant community of moderate size in a barren valley, with no agriculture, no monuments, and wealth that depended on other people's goodwill along a trade route. The fraction is not flattery in reverse; it is roughly accurate, and the audience knew it. This is what gives the comparison its force. It is not telling them they are small in the eyes of God. It is telling them they are small by their own standards of measurement.

**Why a Fraction Rather Than a Comparison**

The verse could have said the earlier peoples were greater, or mightier, or longer-lived, and the sense would have carried. Choosing a numerical fraction instead does something a qualitative comparison cannot. It invites the hearer to perform a calculation, and the calculation has to be done with his own estimate of his own resources on one side of the ratio. A man told he is weaker than his predecessors can dispute the assessment. A man told he holds under a tenth of what they held has been handed an arithmetic problem, and working it honestly means first totalling what he actually has. This is the same technique the sūrah used when it measured divine knowledge by the smallest weight on a merchant's scale (34:3) and Solomon's wind by a month of caravan travel (34:12). Quantities drawn from the audience's working life are harder to argue with than adjectives.

**Denial Named as the Common Factor**

*Wa kadhdhaba alladhīna min qablihim* — those before them denied. The verb *kadhdhaba* means to declare something a lie, and it is the Qur'an's standard term for the response of every destroyed community. What the verse isolates is the one variable that stayed constant while everything else changed. The civilisations differed in wealth, technology, location and religion; their endings differed in method — flood, blast, stones, drowning (29:40). The denial was identical. This is the sūrah's second use of the classification argument in twelve verses, after the observation that every warned town's luxury class said the same thing (34:34). Both verses do the same work: they remove the opponent's sense of being a special case, which is the assumption every confident party operates on.

**The Exclamation That Ends the Verse**

*Fa-kayfa kāna nakīr* — how, then, was My rejection! The word *nakīr* comes from the root *n-k-r*, to disown, to refuse to recognise, to find something objectionable; the same root gives *munkar*, what is wrong or disavowed. So the divine response to their denial is named with a word that mirrors the offence: they refused to acknowledge, and they were refused in turn. The phrase can also be rendered *how was My reproach* or *how terrible was My punishment*, and the recurrence of the formula elsewhere shows it functioning as a fixed closing exclamation — at al-Ḥajj, at Fāṭir, and at al-Mulk, where it follows the reminder that earlier deniers were seized. Grammatically it is an interrogative used as an exclamation, and the effect is to invite the hearer to supply the answer from evidence he already has. The ruins were available for inspection along the roads these men travelled.

**Why the Question Is Left for the Audience to Answer**

The verse does not describe what happened to the earlier peoples. It asks how severe the response was and stops. This is the second time the sūrah has used an unfinished construction, after the *if only you could see* that broke off before naming what would be seen (34:31). The technique is consistent with the chapter's method throughout: the opponents are repeatedly handed the materials for a conclusion and left to assemble it. Told to call their gods and see what answers (34:22); told to produce the partners they attached (34:27); told to stand apart and think, two verses from now (34:46). A revelation that argued by assertion would have described the punishments in detail. This one relies on the audience's own knowledge of a landscape full of abandoned sites.

**The Logic Runs Backwards From Security**

There is a specific complacency this verse targets, and it was stated openly a few verses earlier: *we are far superior in wealth and children, and we will never be punished* (34:35). That sentence treats resources as insurance, and insurance is a claim about causation — that having more of something reduces exposure to a risk. The comparison here dismantles the assumption by running it backwards. If wealth protected people, the wealthiest communities in history would still be standing; they are not, and the men making the claim are poorer than the ones who fell. What remains of the argument after this verse is nothing at all, which is why the sūrah's next move abandons debate entirely and prescribes a procedure instead. Having shown that the opponents have no scripture, no prior warner, no precedent in their favour and less than a tenth of the resources of those who failed, the chapter turns to what a person should actually do.
