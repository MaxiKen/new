## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:7

> Had We sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ a revelation in writing and they were to touch it with their own hands, the disbelievers would still have said, "This is nothing but pure magic!"

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Condition Settled Before It Happens**

The verse grants the demand of the doubters and then cancels the result in advance. Let the revelation come as a physical book, let it be placed in their hands, let the fingers confirm the paper: the verdict is already prepared. This is a counterfactual aimed at a specific complaint. The disbelievers asked why the Qur'an had not been sent down all at once (25:32), why the Prophet had not been given the like of what Moses received (28:48), and they set belief on a book they could read with their own eyes (17:93).

By answering the demand hypothetically, the verse exposes what the demand was doing. A request that can be satisfied in principle and dismissed in practice was never a test of the evidence; it was a way of keeping the question open while the answer was postponed.

**What the Hands Would Actually Have Touched**

The clause about touching is not decoration. It removes every mediating doubt a sceptic could raise: no reciter to question, no memory to distrust, no chain of transmission to attack. What arrives is matter he can feel, in a script he can read, in a language he speaks. Nothing in the apparatus of belief is missing except belief.

That is why the sentence is so bleak. The Book is not saying the Makkans lacked data; it is saying that data was never the variable. Generation after generation is described in the same terms — seeing every sign and still not believing, then arriving to argue that the message is a collection of old stories (6:25). The objection travels from mouth to mouth without ever being examined.

**The Verdict That Was Waiting: Sorcery**

What the disbelievers would have said is recorded elsewhere in almost the same words. A group of them say of the revelation that it is nothing but pure magic (37:15); when Moses came with clear signs, they called it conjured tricks and denied that their forefathers had heard of such a thing (28:36); Pharaoh accused Moses of coming to drive them out of their land with magic (20:57); and the first Makkan reaction to revelation was to call the messenger a clear magician (10:2).

Sorcery is the perfect refuse bin for inconvenient evidence. It requires no engagement with content, no refutation, and no counter-argument, and it carries a social penalty for anyone who takes the message seriously. Once the category exists, any proof can be dropped into it.

**Why the Charge Refutes Nothing**

Naming a thing magic does not tell anyone what the thing says. The accusation presupposes that the accuser has read or heard the material well enough to judge it, and then declines to judge it. In the case of Moses, the men levelling the charge had already conceded the signs were clear; their objection was that the signs were unfamiliar, which is an argument about their own ignorance rather than about the message.

The same pattern holds for the Prophet's opponents. Their complaint concerned the medium — parchment instead of an angel, a man instead of a book — and never the content. The verse answers the medium in their own terms and finds the content has still not been discussed. A demand that can be met without the conversation ever beginning is the clearest proof that the conversation was not the objective.

**The Book Keeps Company With Other Impossible Requests**

The written scripture is one item in a cluster of demands, and the cluster is revealing. Alongside "let him bring us a book we can read" sits the wish for a treasure to be sent down with the messenger or an angel to accompany him as a co-warner (11:12), and the complaint that the messenger eats food and walks in the markets like everyone else (25:7). Different requests, same structure: each is satisfied by changing the messenger or the medium, and none enquires into what the message actually says.

Read together, they describe an audience shopping for a proof it has already decided to reject. That is why the answer in this verse can be granted hypothetically without cost to the argument. The Book is not defending an inadequate sign; it is showing that the objectors would have found the same sentence for a better one, and that the sentence was prepared before the sign arrived.

**The Line That Splits Two Kinds of Hearers**

Read carefully, the verse is addressed as much to the person who says he would believe with better evidence. If a written book delivered to the hand would not have converted the Quraysh, then the modern complaint that revelation is too old, or too mediated, or too unlike anything drawn from a laboratory, is the same complaint in newer clothing. The Book states the principle plainly: the hearts are all alike (2:118).

That leaves the reader with an uncomfortable symmetry. The obstacle in the verse is not the parchment but the disposition, and the disposition is not confined to seventh-century Makkah. Every hearer of the message is invited to ask whether he is waiting for a sign, or avoiding one already in his hands.

**What the Verse Says About Signs in General**

The Book never treats its signs as coercive instruments. They are sufficient for the one who is willing to be convinced, and inert for the one who has decided to explain them away. That is the whole of the verse's teaching, and it is why the Qur'anic argument relies on reasoning from what the audience already grants rather than on spectacle.

The Makkans had asked for the spectacular version. The answer they received told them that the spectacular version had already been priced and would be paid for, and that they would still be standing in the same place, holding the same book, saying the same sentence.
