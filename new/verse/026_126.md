## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:126

> So fear Allah, and obey me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Follows the Messenger's Claim**

Hūd has identified himself as a trustworthy messenger. This verse now states the response that trustworthiness requires: reverence God and obey the messenger. The command is not an invitation to admire Hūd's character while leaving life unchanged. His reliability matters because his guidance must be followed.

The order is also significant. Reverence for Allah comes before obedience to Hūd. The messenger is not the final object of loyalty; he directs the people to their Lord. They obey Hūd because he conveys God's command, and their obedience to him is therefore an expression of obedience to God rather than a transfer of worship to a human being.


To fear Allah is not to imagine Him as an unpredictable force waiting to harm the people. Reverence combines awareness of His greatness with caution about disobedience. It makes the heart conscious that power, wealth, and public honor remain under His judgment. The fear Hūd calls for is therefore a protection from arrogance.

A community that has become strong may fear only visible enemies. It measures danger by armies, droughts, rivals, or economic loss. Hūd redirects its attention to the Lord who gave it every capacity. When that awareness is restored, the people can use their strength without treating it as permission to do whatever they wish.

**Obedience Gives Taqwa a Shape**

Reverence cannot remain a private feeling with no effect on conduct. The command to obey Hūd gives it a visible form. The people must listen to the warning, abandon false worship, restrain their pride, and allow revelation to judge their customs. A claim of fear that leaves injustice untouched is only a word.

This does not mean that obedience eliminates thought. Hūd calls ʿĀd to use their understanding, but he also asks them to submit their conclusions to God's guidance. The obedient person thinks seriously and then accepts that human judgment is not the highest authority. Taqwa is intelligent humility expressed through action.

**The Messenger Does Not Compete With God**

Hūd's command is carefully bounded by the first phrase. He does not demand loyalty to his tribe, personality, or private ambition. He calls the people to fear Allah, then to obey him as the bearer of that call. A genuine messenger is not threatened when people remember that the Lord is supreme; his role depends on that supremacy.

This protects the community from two opposite errors. It must not reject a messenger because he is human, and it must not elevate a messenger into a divine rival. Hūd's authority is real but received. The people honor it by following the guidance he brings and by directing worship to God alone.

**A Repeated Prophetic Pattern**

The same pairing appears in the surrounding narratives. Noah tells his people to fear God and obey him, and Hūd now receives the identical duty. The repetition shows continuity in the prophetic mission. Hūd is not asking ʿĀd to accept a novel religious demand; he is renewing the summons that earlier peoples heard.

Quran 7:65 records Hūd calling ʿĀd to worship Allah and asking whether they will not fear Him. The parallel confirms that the present command joins worship, reverence, and obedience. The three belong together: worship identifies the true Lord, reverence guards the heart, and obedience proves the claim in life.


For a weak person, obedience may seem like a simple private choice. For a dominant society, it has public consequences. If ʿĀd obeys Hūd, its chiefs can no longer treat their power as unrestricted. They must answer for how they build, attack, judge, and distribute what they possess.

That is why a powerful people may resist an apparently short command. The words reach into the foundations of its identity. Reverence for God places limits on rulers, traders, builders, and warriors. It tells every one of them that success is not a license and that another authority stands above the social order.

**The Command Leaves the People a Clear Choice**

Hūd's sentence is brief because the issue is not obscure. The people have a trustworthy messenger, a clear Lord, and a direct response. They can continue to trust their strength, or they can submit that strength to God. The command does not promise that obedience will preserve every worldly advantage, but it promises that worldly advantage is not the measure of truth.

For the reader, the verse asks whether reverence has become obedience or remains a sentiment reserved for speech. God is feared truly when His guidance is allowed to correct desire, custom, and power. Hūd's call gives ʿĀd—and every later community—the same path: honor Allah, then follow the messenger who faithfully conveys His command.

Reverence also changes the way a person receives correction. Instead of asking whether a warning flatters his rank, he asks whether it is true and whether God is calling him back through it. This posture makes repentance possible before pride hardens into public policy.

For a people, the command creates a common moral center. Leaders cannot claim one standard while subjects bear another, and the strong cannot interpret obedience as a privilege reserved for the weak. Everyone stands beneath the same Lord, so every use of authority becomes answerable to the same command.

This shared accountability is especially important when a community is divided by rank. The chief and the laborer may possess different responsibilities, but neither can claim exemption from God's sight. The command disciplines the ruler and dignifies the person ruled, because both are servants before the Lord. It also prevents obedience from becoming mere loyalty to a faction: the measure is revelation, and the purpose is a society that returns its gifts to justice and gratitude.
