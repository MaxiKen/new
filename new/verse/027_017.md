## Sūrah an-Naml 27:17

> Solomon’s forces of jinn, humans, and birds were rallied for him, perfectly organized.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Army Made of Three Kinds of Creature**

The composition of Sulaymān's forces is the claim in this verse, and it is a strange one. Jinn, humans and birds are assembled under a single command. Two of the three categories are not normally subject to human authority at all, and the Qur'an reports their inclusion without any note of surprise, as a plain fact about this king's reign.

The jinn matter most for the theology. Arab tradition treated them as beings of a different order — invisible, fast, powerful, and above all not controllable. Soothsayers claimed to bargain with them; nobody claimed to command them. Their presence in the ranks establishes that Sulaymān's authority did not come from anything a human being can acquire. Al-Rāzī makes the related point that the inclusion signals both the miraculous character of the kingdom and the rank of the man holding it. A ruler who commands only what can be compelled by force has an ordinary kingdom, however large. This one includes creatures no force could compel.

**"Marshalled" — a Word About Holding Lines Together**

The final word, *yūzaʿūn*, is the technical term in the verse and the commentators divided over it. The root *w-z-ʿ* carries the sense of restraining and of arranging. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī report the primary reading as corralled or kept in check — the ranks held together, the front prevented from outrunning the rear, the columns kept from spreading. Others took it to mean they were moved forward in order, and a further group understood it of a rearguard set against attack.

The readings are close enough that the general picture is not in dispute: this was a disciplined body, not a crowd. What the restraining sense adds is an image of active management. An army of birds and jinn would naturally travel at wildly different speeds; holding such a force in formation is a continuous effort rather than a one-off arrangement. Ibn Kathīr's gloss, that officers went ahead to keep the ranks from breaking, describes an organisation with structure below the king. The word also returns in a surprising place two verses later, in Sulaymān's own prayer, where the same root means something quite different, and the shift is worth watching for.

**Organisation as a Form of Gratitude**

Sulaymān had just announced that he was given a share of all things. This verse shows the first thing he did with it: he ordered it. The gift arrived as capacity; what he added was arrangement.

That is the substantive claim in *yūzaʿūn*, and it separates this king from the picture of a man simply indulging in miraculous resources. Power without organisation dissipates; an army of jinn and birds moving as it pleased would be a spectacle rather than an instrument. The Qur'an mentions the marshalling in the same breath as the assembling, which makes discipline part of the description of the favour rather than an afterthought. There is a moral implication that the chapter will press further. The ant's warning in the next verse is triggered precisely by the size and momentum of this force — an organised army is also a dangerous one, and the danger it poses to small things is a function of how well it moves. Sulaymān's competence sets up the problem the following episode solves.

**Were These Ordinary Birds?**

A question the commentators raised is whether the creatures serving Sulaymān were like the ones anyone can see. Al-Rāzī reports the view that his hosts were particular to his time, the birds under his command being of a different order from birds in general.

He immediately qualifies it, and the qualification is the more interesting half. Even ordinary creatures, on his account, possess a measure of intelligence and of inspiration appropriate to their kind. That position has support in the Qur'an, which describes the bee as receiving revelation from its Lord and describes all created things as glorifying God in a manner human beings do not grasp. On this view Sulaymān's gift was not that his birds were unlike others but that he could hear what others already possessed. That reading is stronger, because it fits the next episode exactly: the ant that warns her colony is not presented as a special ant. She behaves as an ant would, with foresight, and the extraordinary element is entirely on the listening end.

**A Kingdom That Included Creatures With No Legal Standing**

The three categories named here are not equals in any human system. Humans have rights and obligations; jinn, in Islamic law, are addressed by revelation and accountable; birds are neither. Yet the verse lists all three as *junūd*, forces, under one command and one organising discipline.

Sulaymān's own request, recorded in Ṣād, was for a kingdom that would suit no one after him, and this verse describes what was granted. The unrepeatable element is not the size of the force but its membership. Any conqueror can assemble men; nobody else assembles species.

The inclusion says something about the scope of the authority described and, more quietly, about the responsibilities attached to it. Sulaymān is answerable for what he does with creatures that cannot petition him. The chapter tests this immediately. Within two verses the smallest possible constituent of his realm — an ant, outside all three named categories — will speak about the danger his army poses, and the king's response to that will be the measure of his kingship. An army of jinn and birds is impressive. What the narrative is actually interested in is whether the man at the head of it notices an insect. The next verse puts that question directly.
