## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:84

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord—we make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we ˹fully˺ submit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Confession of Faith That Names Its Contents**

The verse instructs the Prophet to state what the community believes, and the statement begins with God and then lists the revelations given to a series of named prophets. The list runs from Abraham through his descendants to Moses and Jesus and the other prophets, and it ends with a declaration that no distinction is made among them and a statement of submission.

What the Book is doing is giving its community a creed in the form of a list. The contents are not abstract propositions; they are the messengers and the books that the sūrah's disputes had been about, named one by one so that the community's position on each is public. The confession is therefore a way of answering the arguments of the preceding verses by means of a statement that includes everything the other parties claimed and refuses to rank it.

**Abraham and His Descendants Named First**

The list begins with Abraham and continues through Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes descended from him. The order follows the Book's habit of naming the patriarchs together, and the inclusion of Ishmael alongside Isaac is significant in a sūrah that has been arguing about who has the better claim to Abraham.

Placing Ishmael in the same sentence as Isaac settles the ancestral dispute in a single stroke. The two sons whose descendants became separate peoples are named as recipients of revelation in one clause, with no hierarchy between them, and the sūrah thereby refuses the competition it has been addressing. The community confessing this creed is not choosing one branch of Abraham's house against another; it is receiving what was given to them all, and the confession's shape makes the choice unnecessary.

**Moses, Jesus, and the Other Prophets**

The second half of the list names Moses and Jesus and then generalises: and the other prophets from their Lord. The two names carry the two books the sūrah has discussed at length, and the general phrase covers the rest of the messengers without requiring an inventory.

The Book's use of this construction, which appears in nearly identical form in the sūrah of the Cow, shows how it handles the history of revelation in a creed. It names the figures its audience knows and divides the whole history under one source, so that the confession has both the specificity of the disputes and the inclusiveness of the whole tradition. What is being recited is not a general principle about prophets; the names are those of the particular messengers whose communities stood in front of the first hearers.

**"We Make No Distinction Between Any of Them"**

This clause is the creed's centre, and it is the Book's standard formula for the relation between the missions. The distinction it forbids is not the recognition of differences in the laws given to different peoples, which the Book acknowledges elsewhere; it is the acceptance of some messengers and the rejection of others.

The same formula appears in the sūrah of the Cow's nearly identical verse and in the passage where the believers are described as accepting God, His angels, His books, and His messengers without distinction. The Book also states the reverse case with equal clarity, describing those who believe in some messengers and disbelieve in others as desiring a compromise that cannot be made. A community that accepts the whole line of prophets has abandoned the option of treating revelation as a set of competing properties, and that is precisely what the sūrah has been pressing toward through its arguments about Abraham and the People of the Book.

**Submission as the Closing Clause**

The sentence ends not with a claim about prophets but with a statement about the speakers: to Him we submit. The confession therefore closes on the same word the sūrah has used repeatedly for the response God requires, and it places the community's own position in the same category as the messengers it has just named.

The arrangement keeps the creed from becoming a list of honoured names. The prophets are named, and then those who name them describe themselves with the word that describes a prophet's own stance before God, which is what prevents the veneration of persons the earlier verses warned against. A community that recites this verse has put itself and the prophets of God in the same position of submission, and the sūrah's statement about worship follows from the grammar of the sentence.

**The Verse as an Answer to the Passage Before It**

The confession arrives after the discussion of the covenant with the prophets, the community that turns back from it, and the submission of everything in creation. Each of those themes appears in the verse: the prophets whose covenant it honours, the refusal of partial acceptance that the covenant bound them to, and the submission that the previous verse described as universal.

The Book's practice of placing a creed after an argument is visible throughout the sūrah, and it serves a purpose that the reader can use. A controversy conducted with an opponent leaves the community holding a set of positions; a creed leaves it holding a statement it can recite, and this one is constructed so that reciting it is itself an answer to the disputes that prompted it. The verses that follow it state the consequences of seeking another way and of abandoning a faith once accepted, and the two statements belong to the same creed: what the community holds is not a private preference among options but the acceptance of what God has sent through all of His messengers.

**The Counterpart Passage About Partial Faith**

The Book states the creed in nearly the same words in the sūrah of the Cow, where the community is told to believe in what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants, and to Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets, making no distinction among them (2:136). The same confession is reported once more among the qualities of the believers, together with their declaration that they make no distinction between any of the messengers (2:285).

The rejection of partial acceptance is stated in the sūrah of Women, where the Book describes those who wish to separate God from His messengers, saying that they believe in some and reject others and hoping to find a middle position, and then declares that those who accept all of them and reject none will receive their reward (4:150, 4:152). Read beside that passage, the clause in this verse about making no distinction is not a formality. It is the line the Book draws between a faith that takes the whole message and one that takes what suits it, and the community's confession is placed on the first side of that line.
