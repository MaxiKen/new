## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:49

> Pharaoh threatened, “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission? He must be your master who taught you magic, but soon you will see. I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, then crucify you all.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pharaoh Treats Faith as a Privilege He Can Permit**

Pharaoh's first question after the magicians' confession is how they dared to believe before receiving his permission. The sentence reveals his claim over conscience. He does not say that their conclusion is false because the sign was not real. He objects that they acted without authorization from the ruler. Worship and belief have been placed under the same permission system as public appointments and court decisions.

This is the clearest form of false lordship. A ruler may regulate public affairs within justice, but he cannot own the heart or decide whether a person may recognize God. Pharaoh's outrage shows that the magicians' confession has struck the center of his authority. They have not merely changed an opinion. They have refused to wait for the king to approve what they know to be true.

**Permission Language Exposes Pharaoh's Fear**

The demand for permission sounds confident, but it gives away fear. If the magicians' belief were harmless, Pharaoh would not need to control the moment at which it occurred. He fears that their public recognition will influence the crowd and weaken the royal interpretation. Permission is therefore a way to delay and contain truth until the ruler can decide how it may be spoken.

The phrase also turns the magicians' previous service against them. They had asked Pharaoh for reward, and he had promised closeness. Now he reminds them that proximity does not make them independent. In his view, even their inner judgment remains a privilege granted by him. The reward has become a claim of ownership, and their conversion is treated as betrayal of a patron rather than response to God.

**Pharaoh Invents a Conspiracy to Avoid the Sign**

He claims that Moses is their master who taught them magic. This explanation has a political advantage. It makes the magicians' recognition look planned from the start, so the ruler can dismiss their testimony as collusion. The court no longer has to ask why trained practitioners saw a difference between their craft and Moses' sign. It can say that Moses recruited them into a plot.

The accusation is contradicted by the sequence of the story. The magicians arrived seeking reward and confidently invoked Pharaoh's might. Their prostration came after the sign, not before it. Pharaoh knows the sequence, but he chooses a story that protects his power. Conspiracy language is useful when a ruler cannot refute witnesses honestly. It changes a spontaneous recognition into an alleged crime.

**Violence Replaces Interpretation**

After accusing the magicians, Pharaoh announces mutilation and crucifixion. The progression is stark. The court began with a request for evidence, moved to a contest, and now responds to the result with bodily punishment. Violence is being used to prevent the public from following the implication of what it saw.

The punishment is not a response to harm committed by the magicians. Their offense is believing without royal permission. Pharaoh's violence therefore exposes the weakness of his argument. A ruler who has proved his case does not need to cut the bodies of witnesses. He uses force because he cannot make his interpretation prevail by truth alone.

**Opposite-Side Mutilation Makes the Threat Deliberately Public**

The threat to cut off hands and feet from opposite sides is a severe form of mutilation designed to destroy movement and mark the body. It turns the magicians into warnings for anyone who might imitate them. Pharaoh wants the crowd to imagine the cost of public belief and to associate recognition of God with physical ruin.

The threat also reveals how the state uses bodies as messages. The punishment is not only meant to hurt the magicians. It is meant to be seen and remembered. Their bodies would become royal propaganda, communicating that Pharaoh can decide the fate of anyone who challenges him. Yet a threatened body can also become a testimony. The severity of the threat shows how seriously the ruler fears their words.

**Crucifixion Claims Control Over Life and Death**

The final threat goes beyond imprisonment. Pharaoh claims the right to prolong suffering and publicly execute the magicians. Such a claim imitates divine judgment. He cannot grant life, resurrection, or ultimate punishment, but he behaves as though control over the body gives him control over the person's final meaning.

The magicians' faith will challenge that illusion. A ruler can decide when a body is harmed, but he cannot decide whether the servant has returned to God or whether the confession was true. Pharaoh's power remains terrible in this world, yet it is not ultimate. His threat reveals a limited ruler pretending that the limits of flesh are the limits of reality.

**The Threat Tries to Make the Witnesses Retract Their Sight**

Pharaoh's aim is not only to punish past behavior. He wants the magicians to retract their interpretation of the sign. If they can be made to say that Moses' staff was merely magic and that their prostration was a mistake, the public story can be repaired. The threat is therefore an attempt to force false testimony after the event.

This is why the next response will matter. The magicians must decide whether bodily safety is worth surrendering what they have recognized. Their courage will not make Pharaoh's violence just or make suffering pleasant. It will show that truth can remain firm even when a ruler tries to purchase or terrorize it.

**Pharaoh's Accusation Reveals the Cost of a Controlled Society**

A society in which the ruler controls belief cannot permit experts to change their minds in public. Genuine recognition becomes a political offense because it demonstrates that the throne does not control reality. Pharaoh's response therefore tells the court what his kingdom is defending: not merely a religious formula, but the power to decide what every person may see, say, and worship.

The magicians' new allegiance exposes that project. They have been brought close with promises and now face mutilation when they refuse to remain close. The choice between favor and torture makes the moral structure plain. Pharaoh's court can offer status, but it cannot own the conscience that stands before God.
