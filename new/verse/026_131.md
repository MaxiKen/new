## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:131

> So fear Allah, and obey me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Call Returns to Its First Demand**

After exposing ʿĀd's vanity, imagined permanence, and violent attacks, Hūd returns to the central command: fear Allah and obey the messenger. The repetition is not a failure to advance the argument. It shows that every social problem he has named grows from the same root, a heart that no longer lives under God's authority.

The people may separate worship from architecture, politics, and warfare, as if religion concerned only private rituals. Hūd refuses that separation. Reverence must reach the way a society builds, protects itself, uses force, and treats those beyond its circle. The command returns because the whole of life must return to God.


A first summons can be dismissed as a general ideal. Repeated instruction removes that excuse. Hūd has already stated his trustworthiness and his freedom from payment. He has questioned their structures and attacks. Now the response is stated again in the same compact form so that no one can claim uncertainty about what repentance requires.

The people are not asked to decode a hidden program. They are asked to revere Allah and follow the messenger. Clarity is itself a mercy. It gives the powerful no room to pretend that they were confused about the moral direction demanded of them.

**Fear of Allah Reorders Ambition**

ʿĀd's ambition has become self-display. Their high landmarks announce status, their castles imply permanence, and their attacks impose their will on others. Fear of Allah does not erase ambition, but it places ambition beneath accountability. A project must serve a good that God permits rather than merely enlarge the reputation of its sponsors.

This reordering is difficult because pride can borrow the language of public benefit. A ruler may call vanity progress and call domination security. Taqwa asks a harder question: who benefits, who bears the cost, and would the project remain acceptable if the powerful had to answer before God for every part of it?


To obey Hūd is to accept his guidance when it challenges the habits that made ʿĀd impressive. It means that the messenger's warning can overrule custom and prestige. The command therefore has public consequences even though it begins in the heart.

A community's obedience can be seen in how it restrains its strongest members, protects the vulnerable, and uses provision with gratitude. It does not need to abandon every worldly achievement. It needs to stop treating achievement as a permission slip for injustice. Following the messenger turns power into responsibility.

**The Messenger's Role Remains Servanthood**

Hūd repeats the command without making himself the object of the people's devotion. They are to fear Allah first. His authority comes from carrying God's message, and obedience to him is valid because it leads the people back to the Lord rather than toward Hūd's personal glorification.

This order protects the community from personality cults. A sincere messenger does not ask people to admire his identity while ignoring the Lord's claim. He asks them to hear, understand, and submit to God. Hūd's repeated phrase is therefore an act of humility as well as authority.


The same summons has appeared in Noah's story and appears in the accounts of other messengers in this sūrah. Prophetic guidance is not a collection of competing demands. Each messenger calls his people to the One God, to reverence, and to obedience. The differences in their circumstances do not change the center of the message. Quran 7:65 presents Hūd's call to ʿĀd in the same connection between worship, reverence, and obedience, confirming that this repeated command is the mission's center rather than a passing refrain.

This continuity also exposes the excuse that Hūd's call is merely an unusual opinion. His people are being asked to return to a truth that has been brought before. Repetition across prophetic histories gives the command a wider witness than the approval or rejection of any one tribe.

**Repetition Is Mercy Before Judgment**

A warning repeated before punishment is not cruelty. It gives the hearer another opportunity to abandon pride. Hūd could have described the people's wrongdoing once and then left them to its consequence. Instead, he keeps placing the path of return before them.

Mercy does not always sound gentle. Sometimes it takes the form of a sentence that interrupts self-deception. The repeated command tells ʿĀd that their fate is not sealed merely because they have built high or struck others. They can still fear Allah and obey the messenger.


Because the words recur, a reader may pass over them quickly. That would repeat the error of a community that heard warning without allowing it to govern life. The verse asks whether the command has reached the place where pride decides, money is used, projects are approved, and opponents are treated.

Hūd's return to the same words is an invitation to return as well. The path is not hidden: recognize God as Lord, fear Him with responsible awareness, and follow the truthful guidance He sends. Everything else in the account tests whether that simple command has actually been obeyed.

The command also protects the heart from choosing only the parts of guidance that feel agreeable. A people might accept a teaching about worship while refusing its limits on violence, or praise gratitude while protecting its vanity. Hūd's repetition joins the pieces again. Fear and obedience must govern the whole life, not a carefully selected corner of it.

When a messenger repeats himself, the hearer should not measure the worth of the message by its novelty. Truth does not need a new shape in order to remain true. The test is whether the familiar instruction has become new in the listener through sincere response. A repeated call can expose a new layer of resistance each time it is heard. It also gives a sincere listener room to act before the warning becomes a record of what was refused.
