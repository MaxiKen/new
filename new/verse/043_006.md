## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:6

> **˹Imagine˺ how many prophets We sent to those ˹destroyed˺ before!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kam: A Number Too Large to Count**

The verse begins with kam, the Arabic particle of quantity. In a question it asks how many; in a statement, as here, it exclaims at how many. The construction wa-kam arsalnā means "and how many We sent", and it is an expression of abundance, not a request for a figure. The Qurʾān does not give a total of prophets, and the verse does not want one. Its point is that the sending of prophets was not an occasional event but the standing practice of God across human history. A report from Abū Dharr, recorded by Ibn Ḥibbān (361), gives the number of prophets as one hundred and twenty-four thousand, with three hundred and thirteen messengers among them; Ibn Taymiyyah notes that Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal did not consider it established, and Ibn al-Jawzī went so far as to call it fabricated, so it cannot be used as a fixed figure. Whatever one makes of that figure, the Qurʾān's own position is stated in 40:78: some messengers have been related to the Prophet ﷺ and some have not. Kam covers both groups. The Quraysh were not the first people to hear a messenger, and Muḥammad ﷺ was not the first man to be one.

**Sent to the Awwalīn, the Peoples of Old**

The recipients are al-awwalīn, the first ones or the ancients, which the translation renders with the added sense that they were destroyed. That addition is drawn from what follows in verse 8, but it is not in this verse's wording. Here the awwalīn are simply the people who lived before the Quraysh: ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Nūḥ, the people of Lūṭ, and the many nations whose names are not given. The Arabs of Makkah knew some of these by their ruins. They passed the dwellings of Thamūd on the road north to Syria and the remains of the people of Lūṭ near the Dead Sea, and the Qurʾān elsewhere tells them so directly (37:137–138). The word al-awwalīn places the Quraysh in a sequence. They were the latest audience of a message that had been delivered many times before to peoples who thought themselves, as the Quraysh did, secure in their customs and their gods. The verse's brevity is part of its force. It does not need to name the nations, because the audience knew them.

**Consolation Addressed to the Prophet**

Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn read this verse, together with the next, as consolation for the Prophet ﷺ. The logic runs like this. He was being mocked in Makkah and might feel that his situation was unusual or that the rejection reflected on him. The verse tells him that the sending of prophets is a long history and that his place in it is not exceptional. Al-Ṭabarī draws out the practical meaning: the derision he faced was no greater than what earlier prophets faced. The Qurʾān uses this form of consolation repeatedly. In 6:34 the Prophet ﷺ is told that messengers before him were denied and bore the denial with patience until help came. In 41:43 he is told that nothing is said to him that was not said to the messengers before him. The comfort offered is not that the pain is small but that it is shared. A man who knows that Nūḥ preached for centuries and Ibrāhīm was thrown into a fire can hold his own rejection in proportion. The verse gives him that proportion in five Arabic words, and it gives it before the mockery is even named, so that when the mockery arrives in the next verse it is already framed as old news.

**Warning Addressed to the Quraysh**

The same words carry a second message for a second audience. The Quraysh were listening, and to them the abundance of prophets says something different. If God sent so many messengers to the peoples before you, then His sending one to you is not strange, and your rejection of him puts you in the company of those earlier peoples. This is why the verse comes immediately after verse 5, which asked whether the Reminder should be withdrawn from a transgressing people. The answer implied there was that it should not be, and this verse explains why the question arose at all: God has a long practice of sending guidance, and a long record of what happened to those who refused it. The same sentence therefore comforts the Prophet ﷺ and cautions his opponents, and it does so without changing a word. That double address is characteristic of the Makkan sūrahs, which were recited in the hearing of both believers and mockers and had to speak to both at once.

**The Practice of God Does Not Change**

Behind the verse lies a principle the Qurʾān states in its own words elsewhere: you will find no change in the sunnah of God (33:62, 35:43, 48:23). The sending of prophets is one such sunnah, and the response of peoples to them has followed a pattern as regular as the sending. The verse asks its hearers to look at that pattern. If the history of prophecy is long and the outcome of rejection is known, then a people hearing a prophet is not in a new situation and cannot plead ignorance of how the story goes. The Makkans were proud of their memory. Their poetry preserved genealogies and battles across generations, and their trade routes passed through the ruins of peoples who had once been mighty. The verse turns that memory against them. How many prophets were sent to those before you? You know the answer better than most, and you know what became of the ones who laughed. The next verse names the laughter.
