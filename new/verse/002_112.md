## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:112

> But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Corrective to the Previous Claim**

The verse opens with a negation that cancels the exclusive claim of the previous sentence, and it replaces it with a criterion that is open to anyone. The Book's habit in such places is to answer a restricted position with a universal condition rather than with a competing restriction, and the sentence follows that pattern.

The sūrah used a similar opening earlier when it answered the community's claim about the duration of punishment, and in both cases the correction is stated in the form of a principle. What the Book refuses is the idea that the Garden is distributed by affiliation, and what it puts in its place is a description of the people who enter it. **Submitting the Face**

The Arabic expression rendered as submitting oneself is literally a surrender of the face, and the commentators noted both the idiom and the image. The face is the most honoured part of a person, and the prostration in prayer places it on the ground, so the expression conveys a submission that involves the whole person rather than an opinion held in the mind.

The Book uses the same figure in other passages where it describes the surrender of a believer to God, and it pairs the act with good conduct in each of them. What the phrase excludes is a submission that remains internal while the conduct goes its own way, and the second condition in the sentence confirms the reading.

**Doing Good**

The companion phrase describes conduct that is good in itself and well performed, and the Book uses it for the quality of a person's actions as well as their content. The two conditions in the verse therefore cover the inward and the outward aspects of religion: the heart's surrender to God and the life that expresses it.

The sūrah's descriptions of the believers elsewhere use the same pair, and its account of those who enter the Garden names belief and righteous deeds together. The verse in this place is a summary of the Book's standard, and the sūrah establishes it in the middle of a dispute about which community owns the Hereafter. **The Two Conditions and Their Explanation**

Commentators discussed what the phrase requires and how its two parts relate. Al-Ṭabarī took the submission to mean the surrender of a person's body and actions to God, and Ibn Kathīr added that the conditions for acceptance are sincerity and conformity to the law, which together exclude both the hypocrite who lacks sincerity and the ascetic who follows no revealed guidance.

Al-Rāzī carried the analysis further and argued that the physical conformity required by the phrase limits it to those who follow a revealed code. Others read the sentence in the wider sense the verse's own wording supports, since the Book states that whoever believes in God and the Last Day and does good will have his reward, and the passage does not name a community.

**No Fear and No Grief**

The sentence closes with the two assurances the Book attaches to those who meet its conditions. The first concerns what lies ahead and the second concerns what has passed, and the pair covers the anxieties that occupy a person who is uncertain about his standing.

The phrase appears several times in the Book, including in the sūrah's description of the believers who follow the earlier prophets, and the repetition gives the community a fixed formula for what the reward includes. What the verse offers is not a description of the Garden's contents but a statement about the condition of the people who enter it, and the two negations are the Book's way of describing a settled state. **The Verse's Place in the Sūrah**

The sentence stands between the exclusive claims of the two communities and their mutual accusations in the following verse, and it functions as the Book's own position in the dispute. The sūrah does not join either party; it states the terms on which the Garden is entered, and it leaves the claimants to their evidence.

The same criterion appears later in the chapter when the Book describes the believers as those who submit and do good, and it appears in the sūrah's account of Abraham as a person who surrendered himself to God. The verse therefore belongs to a theme the chapter develops rather than to a single dispute, and its position in the passage gives the sūrah's answer to a question the communities had been arguing about.

**A Statement About Reward With the Lord**

The middle of the sentence locates the reward with God, and the phrase matches the promise to the believers earlier in the passage that whatever good they send ahead they will find with Him. **A Criterion Open to Everyone**

The sentence replaces a dispute about ownership of the Hereafter with a description of the people who enter it, and the description names no community. That is the point of the corrective opening: the Book's answer to an exclusive claim is not a counter-claim on behalf of another group but a standard that any person can meet or fail. The two conditions it names cover the heart's surrender and the life that expresses it, and the sūrah's descriptions of the believers elsewhere use the same pair. The verse therefore stands as a test for whoever reads it, including the community that received the revelation last. What the passage establishes is that the question worth asking is not which group has a claim on the Garden but whether a person has met the terms on which it is entered.
