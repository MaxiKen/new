## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:112

> But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Balā Against the Monopoly**

The verse opens with the particle the recital uses to overturn: *balā* — but no, on the contrary, the same word that answered the numbered-days claim at verse 81. What it overturns here is the exclusivity just quoted: against *none will enter except those of our faith*, the verse sets the actual formula of entry, and the formula's first word after the particle is its whole revolution — *whoever*. The subject is universal in its grammar: not a people, not a lineage, not an affiliation, but the indefinite pronoun covering every person the pronoun can reach. The monopoly keyed entry to identity; the verse keys it to two acts, and the acts are available to whoever performs them. The recital has prepared this formula across its whole second movement: the paired verdicts of verses 81–82 — evil engrossing, belief with works — filed the deed-grammar against the tariff-claim, and verse 112 now states the grammar's positive side in its final form, as the answer to the salvation-monopoly. The structure of the exchange deserves notice: verse 111 dismantled the claim by demanding its absent proof; verse 112 installs the truth the claim usurped. Refutation and installation in adjacent verses — the desire classified, then the actual terms published — and the terms contain, in the whole of their statement, not one reference to who a person's fathers were.

**Submitting the Face**

The first condition is physical in its idiom and total in its sense: *whoever submits themselves to Allah* — literally, whoever submits his *face* to Allah. The exegetes unfold the metaphor from both of its ends. The face is the body's noblest member (al-Ṭabarī's point) — the seat of identity, the part turned toward others in address, the feature by which a person is known — and submitting it is the surrender of the self at its most dignified point: not the hand's labor offered but the person's frontage laid down. And the submission's enacted form exists in the worship itself: the prayer's deepest movement is the prostration, where the face — forehead and nose on the ground — performs the verse's sentence in the body (al-Rāzī's reading). Submission here is also specified as sincerity: the face turned to Allah alone, without division, the undivided direction of the self that the two-rooms people of verse 76 lacked. Sūrah Luqmān states the condition's full weight in the Qur'an's own image: *whoever fully submits themselves to Allah and is a good-doer has certainly grasped the firmest hand-hold* (31:22) — submission as the grip that cannot be broken, the *ʿurwa wuthqā* whose firmness answers exactly what the monopolies tried to guarantee by descent: security of holding. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ adds the condition's genealogy: *who is better in faith than those who fully submit themselves to Allah, do good, and follow the Way of Abraham, the upright?* (4:125) — the same pair of acts, anchored in the tradition's founding submitter, whose own standing the recital has filed as earned by obedience rather than inherited by anyone.

**And Does Good**

The second condition keeps the submission from being a posture: *and does good* — the face turned accompanied by the hands employed. The pairing is the sūrah's standing formula, and the exegetes give its two terms their technical precision. Ibn Kathīr's formulation sets the twin conditions of any deed's acceptance: sincerity — the submission to Allah alone, which excludes the hypocrite whose face turns two directions — and conformity, the deed done on the pattern the Sharīʿah prescribes, which excludes devotion however intense that runs outside the revealed form. Al-Ṭabarī reads the face more broadly as the body and its actions, the whole person submitted; the readings converge in requiring that the verse's subject be an actor, not a feeler. The exegetical tradition applied the twin conditions to its own landscape — ruling out sincerity-deficient profession on one side and, in al-Rāzī's discussion, forms of devotion whose practice he judged deformed on the other — while other authorities noted that the passage's plain sense does not itself require such exclusions, the verse's grammar welcoming *whoever* meets its two terms. The tension between the applications and the plain sense is the verse's permanent interpretive frontier, and its resolution belongs to the two conditions rather than to any list: sincerity is examinable by none but Allah, and conformity is defined by the revelation rather than by any community's boundary-drawing. What the verse itself fixes is the formula's shape — orientation plus action, the face and the hands together — and its openness: whoever, not whomever-we-approve.

**Reward With Their Lord**

The formula's outcome is stated in the sūrah's custody-language: *will have their reward with their Lord*. The preposition does the work — the reward is *with* Allah, held in the divine keeping, which is the verse's answer to every insecurity the monopolies exploited. A reward held with a Lord cannot be lost, devalued, or preempted by another claimant's lineage; the custody-location is the security the exclusivist claims tried to manufacture by closing the door, and the verse supplies it by opening the door and naming the Keeper. Verse 110 filed the same guarantee from the sending side — whatever good dispatched will be *found with Allah* — and the two verses describe one depot from its two ends: the good sent, the reward held, the finding certain because the holding is divine. The verse's final sentence completes the guarantee in its emotional register: *there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve*. The pair covers the two directions of the heart's unrest — fear facing what comes, grief facing what was left — and their joint negation is the sūrah's standing formula of the saved, filed at verse 38 for whoever follows the guidance, at verse 62 for the believers and the Jews, the Christians, and the Sabians who truly believe in Allah and the Last Day and do good, at verse 262 and 274 for the givers, and at verse 277 for the prayer-keepers. The formula's recurrence across those verses is itself the verse's argument against the monopoly: the no-fear-no-grief clause appears wherever the two conditions appear, attached at verse 62 to communities the exclusivists excluded — and the recurrence says that the security the monopolies claimed as property is distributed, throughout this sūrah, by formula rather than by franchise.

**The Formula Against Every Franchise**

Verse 112 is the recital's universal answer, and its universality is its transfer. Against every claim of reserved salvation — by ethnicity, denomination, lineage of teachers, or ancestral merit — the verse publishes the actual terms: submission, good, reward held with the Lord, fear and grief removed. The terms are examinable in a way the franchises are not: anyone can inspect his own face's direction and his own hands' output, while no one can inspect his lineage's standing in a divine ledger that does not have a lineage column. The verse thereby relocates assurance from the unexamiable to the examinable, and the relocation is mercy in both directions. Against despair: the door stands open to *whoever*, and the two conditions are within every person's beginning — the sinner's face can turn, the empty hand can start. Against presumption: the franchise-holder's pedigree saves nothing where the two conditions are missing, and the exclusivist of verse 111 is answered not with a rival exclusion but with a formula his own conduct can fail while a stranger's passes. The Muslims hearing the sūrah received the verse as their standing definition: not *we are the new chosen people* — the recital had just dismantled chosenness-as-property in a hundred verses of case law — but *we are the people of the formula*, holding security exactly as long as the face stays submitted and the hands stay busy with good. The no-fear-no-grief clause is their present tense as much as their future: the heart whose reward is held with its Lord grieves no loss and dreads no arrival, and the holding begins when the submitting does. The monopoly is overturned; the terms are published; and whoever is the widest word the recital ever files.
