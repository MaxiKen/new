## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:84

> He turned away from them, lamenting, “Alas, poor Joseph!” And his eyes turned white out of the grief he suppressed.

**Expanded Commentary**

**He Turns Away Before He Speaks**

The verse begins with a gesture and places it first. Jacob turns away from his sons — *tawallā ʿanhum* — and only then speaks. The order separates the two acts that occupy verses 83 and 84. The first was addressed to the brothers and contained a judgement about their souls, a declaration of patience and a hope. The second is addressed to nobody. What Jacob says after turning away is not part of the conversation and is not intended to persuade, explain or answer anyone. The chapter's arrangement makes the lament private without making it secret: the sons hear it, report it and rebuke him for it in the next verse, but the words were not spoken to them.

**The Name He Has Not Used**

*Yā asafā ʿalā Yūsuf* — alas, my grief for Joseph. This is the first occasion in the chapter on which Jacob names his lost son in the presence of other people. In verse 64 he referred to him only as *his brother*, inside an argument about a different child. In verse 83 he spoke of *them all* being returned without specifying who. Here the name arrives, in an exclamation made after he has turned away from the people whose report provoked it. The placement is exact. Jacob can say the name only when he is no longer answering his sons, which suggests that the name belongs to a grief he has been carrying separately from every conversation the chapter has recorded, including the conversations about the son who was lost.

**A New Loss Reopens the Old One**

The commentators note what the verse is doing. Jacob has just been told that Benjamin is detained in Egypt under a penalty his brothers supplied. What he mourns is Joseph. The second loss does not produce a second grief; it reopens the first, and the first is what he names. The structure of the exclamation makes the point: the grief is his, it is old, and its object is a son he has believed dead for decades. A reader who knows Joseph is alive and administering Egypt's grain watches a father mourn a living child by name at the moment when a second child is genuinely beyond his reach. The chapter supplies no commentary on the coincidence and does not treat it as a sign. It simply reports the words and the whiteness of the eyes.

**"His Eyes Turned White"**

The physical consequence is stated without explanation or elaboration. The commentators record two readings: that he wept so much he went blind, or that his eyesight became very weak. Either way the whiteness denotes a visible change in the eye produced by sustained weeping, and the chapter treats it as a fact rather than as a figure of speech. The injury is the only bodily harm in the sūrah that receives a stated cure. Verse 93 provides the remedy — a shirt carried from Egypt and cast over his face — and verse 96 reports the recovery in the same plain terms: he regained his sight. A grief described physically is healed physically, and the healing arrives by means of an object belonging to the son he is mourning.

**"He Was Choked With Grief"**

The closing word describes a person who holds sorrow in. *Kaẓīm* denotes one filled with grief who does not let it out, who restrains the expression of what he feels. The term qualifies everything else in the verse. Jacob's lament is a single exclamation; his weeping is prolonged but private; his complaint goes to God alone (12:86). The chapter's portrait of this man's sorrow is a portrait of sorrow contained, and the containment is what damages his sight. He does not rail, does not curse his sons, does not withdraw from worship and does not accuse God of injustice. What he does is turn away, say one name, and weep until he cannot see — and the verse reports all three acts in a single sentence without comment.

**Restraint as the Habit of This Family**

Verse 77 showed Joseph concealing a response inside himself and refusing to disclose it to the men insulting him. Verse 84 shows his father doing the same with grief. Verse 86 will have Jacob state the practice explicitly: I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to Allah. The chapter is describing a shared family discipline, and it presents the discipline in both of its prophets without praising or criticising it. What it does show is the cost. Joseph's suppression costs him years in a prison he could have left; Jacob's costs him his sight. Neither man is depicted as wrong to have endured silently, and neither is depicted as unharmed by the endurance. The sūrah's interest is in the price of restraint rather than in its virtue, and it records the price in the body rather than in argument.

**No Rebuke From the Narrator**

The verse reports the grief and stops. It does not say Jacob was excessive, does not remind him of God's decree or of the patience he declared in the previous verse, and does not contrast his conduct with an ideal of composure. The only criticism of his mourning anywhere in the chapter comes from his sons in verse 85, and the sūrah places that criticism in the mouths of the men who caused the mourning and who do not know that its object is alive. The narrator's silence is itself a judgement in favour of Jacob. A father who has lost two sons to the same errand, who does not know that the first is ruling the country holding the second, and who weeps until his sight fails is described in a handful of words and then left alone.
