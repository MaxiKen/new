## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:73

> He is the One Who created the heavens and the earth in truth. On the Day ˹of Judgment˺ He will say, ‘Be!’ And there will be! His command is truth. All authority is His ˹alone˺ on the Day the Trumpet will be blown. He is the Knower of all—seen or unseen. And He is the All-Wise, All-Aware.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation in Truth Rather Than in Play**

The verse opens by describing God as the One who created the heavens and the earth in truth, and the phrase answers a question the Makkans had never posed but which the sūrah has been building toward. The Book states that the creation was not made as play, and it repeats the point in several places: the sūrah of the Prophets asks whether God took the heavens and the earth and everything in them as a pastime, and it answers that He did not; the sūrah of Ṣād denies that the creation of the heavens and the earth was in vain; and the sūrah of the Family of Imran describes the people of understanding as those who reflect on the creation and conclude that it was not made without purpose (3:191; 21:16; 38:27).

The same claim appears in the sūrah of the Smoke, where God states that He did not create the heavens and the earth and what lies between them as play (44:38). Put beside the sūrah's account of the idols, which can neither benefit nor harm their worshippers, the statement makes a comparison the audience could not miss. The created order has a purpose, and the gods of Makkah had none.

**The Command That Brings Things Into Being**

The verse then names the mechanism of creation: on the day He says, Be! and it is. The Book uses this formula repeatedly for the divine command, and it attaches it to the original creation of the heavens and the earth (2:117), to the conception of Jesus (3:47), to the creation of Adam (3:59), and to resurrection (36:82; 40:68). The phrase's function is to deny any gap between God's intention and its execution.

The commentators drew a theological conclusion from the wording, since if everything was created by God's word then the word itself must precede creation and cannot be a created thing. The discussion belongs to the long debate among Muslim theologians over whether God's speech is created or uncreated, and this verse was cited in it by those who held the second position. What the verse settles for a reader is simpler than the controversy: the world owes its existence to a command, and the One who gave the command remains its owner.

**Sovereignty on the Day the Trumpet Sounds**

All authority is His alone on the day the trumpet will be blown. The Book mentions the trumpet in many places as the herald of the resurrection, and it describes the sequence: the trumpet is blown and everything in the heavens and the earth falls dead, then it is blown again and they rise up looking on (39:68). The sūrah of Ṭā-Hā states that on that day God will gather the wicked, blue-faced from horror (20:102); the sūrah of Yā-Sīn describes people rushing from their graves to their Lord (36:51); and the sūrah of the Troops gives the same scene in the same terms as this verse.

Some commentators read the word for trumpet as forms, giving the sense that sovereignty belongs to God on the day when forms are given their spirit, but the majority reading is the trumpet that announces the end. Either way the conclusion is the same, and it is the conclusion this verse draws: the authority that the Makkans distributed among their gods will be concentrated on that day in a single hand, and the Book states that the question will be asked aloud: who does all authority belong to this day? The answer it supplies is: to Allah, the One, the Supreme (40:16).

**The Knowledge That Covers Both Sides of Reality**

The verse closes by naming God as the Knower of all, seen or unseen, and the pairing appears across the Book as a summary of His knowledge. The sūrah of the Thunder calls Him the Knower of the seen and the unseen, the All-Great, the Most Exalted (13:9); the sūrah of the Prostration gives the name at the head of a passage about His mercy (32:6); the sūrah of the Troops places it in a prayer for judgment (39:46); and the sūrah of the Gathering and the sūrah of Mutual Disillusion repeat the same pair (59:22; 64:18).

The pairing matters here because the sūrah has spent a long passage on things that human beings cannot see. The keys of the unseen belong to God (6:59), the leaf and the grain are recorded (6:59), the soul is taken at night (6:60), and the record is kept by angels (6:61). The verse now states the principle that covers all of those cases: nothing is outside the knowledge of the One who is described in the same sentence as the maker and the sovereign.

**The Closing Word of the Declaration**

The verse ends with two names, the All-Wise and the All-Aware, which are the same two names that the sūrah used near its beginning when it described God as reigning supreme over His creation (6:18). The repetition closes a ring in the sūrah's structure: the argument that began with the wise and aware Creator ends with the wise and aware Sovereign.

That structure is worth noticing because it explains the placement of the Abraham narrative that follows. Having stated the case in terms of creation, command, sovereignty, and knowledge, the sūrah turns to the man whom the Book presents as the model of a person who reasoned his way from the created order to its maker. Abraham's argument with his people is the human counterpart of everything the last three verses have stated about God.
