## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:79

> So he turned away from them, saying, “O my people! Surely I conveyed to you my Lord’s message and gave you ˹sincere˺ advice, but you do not like ˹sincere˺ advisors.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turning Away**

The verse reports that the messenger turned away from his people and addressed them after their destruction, and the turning away states the end of his work among them. The sūrah's account of Shuʿayb uses the same opening and almost the same words, recording that he turned away from them and said that he had delivered his Lord's messages and given them sincere counsel (7:93). The verse in front of us supplies the third instance of the farewell, and the two passages together establish the form in which the sūrah's messengers take their leave.

The tradition gives the turning away two settings: the messenger's departure from the settlement with the believers before the punishment came, and his return to the ruins afterwards. The verse in front of us does not specify which, and the address that follows fits both, since it states the completed duty rather than any expectation of a response. The Book's narratives of the communities frequently close with the messenger's statement of what he did and what the people did with it.

**The Message Delivered**

The messenger states that he delivered the message of his Lord, and the phrase repeats the description of the prophetic work that the sūrah has already used for Noah (7:62) and for Hūd (7:68). The Book presents the delivery of the revelation as the substance of the office, and the sūrah of the Family of Imran states that the messenger's duty is only to convey (3:20), while the sūrah of the Table repeats the same limit twice. The verse in front of us states the delivery as a completed fact, and the perfect tense marks the end of the commission.

The message in the singular in front of us is the one that the messenger was given, and the sūrah's accounts of the prophets present each of them as the bearer of a single message. The Book's teaching that the messengers were sent with the same religion appears in the sūrah of the Consultation, and the verse in front of us states the third messenger's completion of his charge.

**The Sincere Counsel**

The messenger states that he also gave the people sincere counsel, and the phrase repeats the term that the sūrah has used for the same activity in the earlier narratives (7:62; 7:68). The Book's presentation of the prophetic work includes the two parts, the delivery of the message and the counsel that accompanies it, and the verse in front of us states them together at the close of the account. The messenger's counsel was offered to a people who had already refused the message, and the verse records it as a completed act.

The Book's treatment of counsel among the believers adopts the practice, and the sūrah of the Declining Day states that those who are not at a loss urge one another to the truth and to perseverance (103:3). This verse supplies the prophetic origin of the practice, and the account then states the people's response to it.

**The People Who Did Not Love Counsel**

The verse closes with the messenger's statement that the people did not love sincere advisers, and the phrase describes a disposition rather than a single refusal. The Book's accounts of the communities present the rejection of the messengers as the rejection of what they offered, and the sūrah's earlier accounts stated the leaders' charges and the people's demands. The verse here states the reason for the messenger's grief, and the sūrah's account of Shuʿayb uses the same closing words (7:93).

The Book's descriptions of the people who disliked the counsel include their preference for their own arrangements, and the sūrah of the Ornament reports the disbelievers stating that they found their forefathers on a way and were following in their footsteps (43:23). The present verse states the disposition in the mouth of the messenger, and the account that follows turns to the next people.

**The Address to a Destroyed People**

The messenger's words in front of us are spoken to a community that has already been removed, and the address states the truth about the past rather than appealing for a change. The tradition records that the Prophet addressed the dead of the Quraysh at Badr, asking whether they had found what their Lord promised them to be true, and that he answered ʿUmar's surprise by saying that the dead could hear what he said even if they could not reply (al-Bukhārī 3976). The verse under discussion supplies the earlier instance of an address to a destroyed people, and the tradition records the Prophet's return to the practice.

The Book's narratives of the messengers' closings state the completed work in this form in several accounts, and the sūrah's two other narratives state the rescue of the believers as the outcome of the same work. Here the verse states the farewell of the third messenger, and the account of the fourth people begins after it.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands seventy-ninth in the chapter, and it closes the account of Thamūd that began with the call of Ṣāliḥ (7:73). The verse that follows opens the account of Lot (7:80), and the series continues with the fourth people. This verse supplies the conclusion of the third narrative and the model for the farewells that follow.

The reader who has followed the third community from its call to its destruction now reads the messenger's assessment of his work. The message was delivered, the counsel was given, and the people are described by what they refused; and the sūrah's next verse turns to the people of Lot and the message their messenger brought them.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports Lot's address to his people, asking them whether they commit an indecency that no one in the world had committed before them (7:80). The farewell of the third messenger in front of us is followed by the opening of the fourth account, and the Book's series of the communities continues without a break.

The two verses together carry the series forward. One community has been ended and its messenger has stated the completion of his duty, and the next messenger is introduced with the question that names his people's offence; and the verses that follow record the argument between Lot and his people and the outcome of their refusal.
