## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:9

> **It is upon Allah ˹alone˺ to ˹clearly˺ show the Straight Way. Other ways are deviant. Had He willed, He would have easily imposed guidance upon all of you.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Physical Roads To The Straight Path**

After listing animals that carry bodies to distant lands and landmarks that show direction, the sūrah turns to the road that matters most: wa-ʿalā Allāhi qaṣdu al-sabīl — upon Allah is the direction of the way, to show the straight way. Qaṣd means straightness, directness, aiming for the target without deviation. Sabīl is way, path. The phrase indicates that guidance to the straight path is something Allah has taken upon Himself to provide. Al-Ṭabarī explains qaṣdu al-sabīl as the straight path that leads to Allah, the path of truth, as opposed to jā'ir, deviant paths that turn away. The verse comes right after horses and unknown creations to draw a parallel: just as Allah created carriers for your bodies, He has taken responsibility for guiding your hearts. Physical roads need landmarks; the spiritual road needs revelation. Both are from Him. The bracketed ˹alone˺ and ˹clearly˺ in translation reflect that the Arabic construction with ʿalā implies obligation or guarantee — it is for Allah, upon Him, to show — and that the showing is clear, not obscure.

**Other Ways Are Deviant**

Wa-minhā jā'ir — and among them, from those ways, are deviant ones. Jā'ir comes from j-w-r, to deviate, to turn aside unjustly. It describes a path that does not lead to the destination but veers off. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that the pronoun minhā refers to subul, paths, implied though not explicitly stated, meaning among paths there are those that are crooked. The Qur'an often contrasts one straight way with many crooked ways, as in 6:153: *This is My straight path, so follow it, and do not follow other paths, for they will separate you from His path* (6:153). The image is of a main road with side tracks that lead into desert or danger. The sūrah will later give examples of such deviant paths: attributing partners to Allah, saying Allah willed their shirk as excuse, calling revelation fables. Each is a jā'ir path. By mentioning deviant ways immediately after affirming that Allah shows the straight way, the verse makes clear that guidance is available but not automatic; people may still choose crooked tracks. The existence of deviation does not mean guidance failed; it means humans have choice.

**Had He Willed, He Could Have Forced Guidance**

Wa-law shā'a la-hadākum ajmaʿīn — had He willed, He would have guided you all together. This clause answers a hidden objection. If Allah alone shows the way, why are not all guided? The answer is that He could have forced guidance, but He willed to allow choice. Al-Rāzī explains that hadā here means compelled guidance, making everyone follow truth without option, which Allah is able to do but does not do because the purpose of creation includes testing. The same idea appears in 6:149: *Had He willed, He would have guided you all* (6:149) and 13:31 and 32:13. Ashʿarī theologians cite this to show that guidance is by Allah's will, not by human merit alone, while Muʿtazilī thinkers emphasize that Allah wills to leave room for human earning, so that reward has meaning. Both agree on the outcome: diversity of response to guidance is part of divine wisdom, not inability. The verse therefore preserves both divine power and human responsibility. Allah is able to guide all, He has shown the straight way clearly, yet He leaves the walking to you.

**Why This Verse Is Placed Here**

The placement after transport and before rain is not random. The sūrah is building a layered argument: Allah creates carriers, He creates what you do not know, He shows the way. The physical journey and spiritual journey are paired. A traveler needs a mount and a road and direction. Verses 5-8 gave mounts, verse 15-16 will give landmarks and stars, and this verse gives direction for the heart. Without qaṣd al-sabīl, even the best mount goes astray. The Makkan audience understood travel. They knew that a caravan with strong camels but no guide perishes. The verse says, for your ultimate journey, Allah Himself is the guide who shows qaṣd, the straight aim. This also reframes the earlier prohibition against hastening Allah's command. The one who hastens judgment wants to skip the road; the one who follows qaṣd walks it patiently. The sūrah will close with *Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom* (16:125), echoing this same sabīl.

**The Meaning Of Straightness In Arabic**

Qaṣd in classical lexicons means moderation, middle, directness. It is the opposite of excessive deviation to either side. A qaṣd path is one that is direct to its goal without crookedness. Jā'ir is from the same root as jawr, injustice, because deviation from truth is injustice to oneself. The Qur'an uses qaṣd and jā'ir as moral terms, not only geographic. A straight path is just, balanced, leading to Allah; a deviant path is unjust, leading away. This moral geography is important for al-Naḥl, which repeatedly contrasts those who are grateful with those who cover favors, those who reflect with those who are proud. The straight way is the way of gratitude, which recognizes the Giver. Deviant ways are ways of ingratitude, which attribute gifts to others or deny them. The verse thus condenses the sūrah's ethical vision into one sentence: one straight way, many deviant ways, all under divine knowledge and will.

**A Verse That Guards Against Two Errors**

This verse guards against two opposite errors that still divide people. The first error is to think guidance is not from Allah, that humans find it alone by reason. The verse says wa-ʿalā Allāhi qaṣdu al-sabīl — upon Allah is the showing, not upon reason alone. The second error is to think that since Allah could guide all, those who are not guided are excused. The verse says He could have guided all, but He willed to show clearly and leave choice, so responsibility remains. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the verse therefore establishes both tawakkul and taklīf: reliance on Allah for guidance and accountability for following. In practical terms, the believer is taught to ask Allah for guidance constantly, as in al-Fātiḥah *Guide us to the straight path* (1:6), while also striving to walk, because the path has been shown. The balance is precise: Allah shows, you walk; He could compel, but He invites.
