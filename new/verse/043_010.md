## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:10

> **˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for you, and set in it pathways for you so that you may find your way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Their Admission to His Works**

The previous verse recorded the Quraysh's admission that the Almighty, All-Knowing created the heavens and the earth. This verse begins to describe what that Creator did, and the grammar ties the two together. Alladhī, "the One Who", is a relative pronoun whose antecedent is the God just named. What follows is a list of relative clauses that runs through verse 12: Who laid out the earth, Who sends down rain, Who created the pairs. Each clause is an act of the Creator the Quraysh had just acknowledged. The passage is not proving that God exists; the previous verse showed that the Makkans granted it. It is reminding them of what they owe the God they granted, and it starts with the ground under their feet. The earth is the first item because it is the most taken for granted. Nobody in Makkah thanked anyone for the fact that the ground held them up and could be walked on. The verse asks them to notice that this, too, was made for them.

**Mahd: A Cradle, Not Merely a Floor**

The word for what God made the earth is mahd, and its ordinary meaning is a cradle, the smoothed and prepared bed on which an infant is laid. The Qurʾān uses the same image in 20:53, and a related form, mihād, in 78:6. The choice of word is doing more than describing a flat surface. A cradle is made for someone; it is prepared, soft enough to rest on, firm enough to hold, and sized to its occupant. Ibn Kathīr explains mahd as the earth made smooth and easy to travel over. Al-Rāzī adds that it is made tractable for cultivation, so that seed can be put into it and food drawn out. The cradle image includes both. The earth is a place of rest and a place of nourishment, and it was arranged that way for a creature who could not have arranged it himself. The phrase "for you" appears twice in the verse, once with the earth and once with the paths, and the repetition insists that the arrangement was intentional. The Quraysh did not build the valley they lived in. They were laid in it.

**Subul: Roads That Were Not Built by Men**

The second gift is subul, paths or roads, set into the earth. The Qurʾān mentions this gift in 21:31, where mountain passes are made as paths, in 71:19–20, where the earth is spread out so that people may travel its broad roads, and in 67:15, where the earth is made subservient so that people may walk in its tracts. The Arabs were travellers. The Quraysh's wealth came from the two caravan journeys of winter and summer (106:2), and the routes they followed were not roads they had paved but ways that the land itself made possible: passes between mountains, valleys with water, coastal plains, fords across wadis. Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn read subul as exactly these navigable routes. The verse points out that the possibility of travel is built into the shape of the earth, and that a merchant who profits from the road did not make the road. The land was cut to be crossed before anyone crossed it.

**"That You May Find Your Way": Two Kinds of Guidance**

The verse ends with laʿallakum tahtadūn, so that you may be guided or find your way. The verb is from the same root as hudā, guidance, and the exegetes read it on two levels. The first is literal. The paths were set into the earth so that travellers could find their way from one place to another without being lost, and a people who lived by caravans knew what it meant to find the way and what it meant to lose it. The second level is the one the word hudā always carries in the Qurʾān. The paths in the earth are signs; the God who made physical guidance possible is the God who sends revelation, and the person who notices the first should be led to the second. The passage as a whole, verses 10 through 14, supports this double reading. It moves from the earth to rain to the resurrection to the ships and animals to the traveller's prayer, and it ends with a return to the Lord. The physical journey and the journey toward God run together through the whole passage, and this verse's last word opens both.

**Gratitude as the Argument's Goal**

The verse belongs to a form of argument that the Qurʾān uses more often than any other: the enumeration of blessings. It does not say "therefore worship Him alone". It lists what He did and lets the listener feel the weight of the debt. The technique works because the Quraysh had already granted that He was the Maker. Once that is granted, every item on the list becomes a charge against the idols, since none of them laid out the earth or cut a pass through a mountain. Al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt had no valleys to their credit. The verse thus has a polemical edge that is felt without being stated. The Makkans walked the roads God made to fetch the goods they sold to fund the shrines of gods who had made nothing. The same charge is laid explicitly in 16:17: is the One who creates like one who does not create? Here it is only implied, and the implication is left to work on a listener who is being asked, item by item, to remember who made the world he lives in. The next verse adds rain to the list.
