## Sūrah an-Nūr 24:48

> And as soon as they are called to Allah and His Messenger so he may judge between them, a group of them turns away.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Judicial Summons: idhā duʿū ilā Allāh**

The verse isolates the precise moment of testing where the hypocrisy of the disaffected was laid bare: *wa-idhā duʿū ilā Allāhi wa-rasūlihī li-yaḥkuma baynahum idhā farīqun minhum muʿriḍūn*. The phrase *duʿū ilā Allāh* (when they are called to Allah and His Messenger) refers to a formal summons to judicial arbitration in civil, commercial, or property disputes within the Madinan community.

In the nascent state of Madinah, the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ was not merely a spiritual preacher; he was the head of state and supreme constitutional magistrate. Submitting a legal dispute to him meant subjecting personal claims to divine revelation and unyielding prophetic integrity. For the sincere believer, such a summons was an occasion of relief, peace, and gratitude, knowing that their rights would be adjudicated with immaculate fairness and profound wisdom. For the hypocrite, however, the summons was an existential threat, because divine justice recognizes no social bribery, tribal favoritism, or elite entitlement, holding all parties strictly accountable before the law.

**The Singular Authority of the Prophet: li-yaḥkuma baynahum**

The syntactic phrasing of the purpose clause contains an exquisite theological nuance: *li-yaḥkuma baynahum* (so he may judge between them). The singular masculine pronoun *yaḥkum* (he judges) refers grammatically to the Messenger ﷺ, yet the summons was explicitly to *Allah and His Messenger*.

Classical commentators, including al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī, and Ibn Kathīr, observed that this syntactic fusion affirms the absolute inseparability of divine law and prophetic judgment. The Messenger ﷺ does not judge according to personal whim, cultural convention, or subjective bias; his rulings are direct applications and embodiments of divine revelation. To accept the judgment of the Prophet is to accept the judgment of God; conversely, to reject or evade prophetic adjudication is an explicit rejection of divine sovereignty itself. The court of the Messenger was the terrestrial manifestation of cosmic justice, leaving no space for selective allegiance, tribal evasion, or opportunistic obedience.

**The Reflexive Evasion: idhā farīqun minhum muʿriḍūn**

The reaction of the hypocrites to the judicial summons is portrayed with dramatic immediacy: *idhā farīqun minhum muʿriḍūn*, behold, a group of them turns away. The surprise particle *idhā* (behold / immediately) indicates instantaneous, reflexive aversion that bypasses rational deliberation and reveals their panic.

They do not pause to deliberate, investigate the evidence, or hear arguments; the mere prospect of standing before the Prophet's bench triggers immediate evasive maneuvers. The active participle *muʿriḍūn* depicts entrenched psychological and physical recoiling. They seek excuses, manufacture jurisdictional objections, feign illness, or attempt to divert the case toward alternative tribal or pagan arbitrators whom they hope to bribe, manipulate, or intimidate. Their retreat exposes their complete lack of confidence in the objective righteousness of their own legal claims, proving that their real loyalty was to illicit gain rather than impartial justice, unmasking their deceit.

**Historical Incidents of Evasion in Madinah**

Classical exegesis records several historical disputes that illuminate the concrete context of this revelation. Exegetes such as al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī document a dispute over land between a hypocrite named Bishr and a Jewish resident of Madinah.

The Jewish claimant, fully confident in the legendary fairness and incorruptibility of the Prophet ﷺ, insisted: Let us take our dispute to Muḥammad! The hypocrite Bishr, knowing that his claim was fraudulent and that the Prophet would never rule in favor of injustice regardless of religious identity, adamantly refused, insisting instead: Let us go to Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf (a corrupt tribal chieftain who took bribes). Other accounts narrate a property dispute between Mughīrah ibn Wāʾil and ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, where Mughīrah openly expressed fear that the Prophet would judge against him. These incidents demonstrated that religious minorities often had greater trust in prophetic equity than hypocrites who claimed to be Muslims, shaming the duplicity of the disaffected.

**Aversion to Impartial Justice as an Index of Corruption**

This verse provides an eternal moral diagnostic: aversion to an impartial, incorruptible judiciary is the definitive hallmark of an unjust heart. A person who genuinely loves justice welcomes an objective tribunal that examines facts, verifies evidence, and protects legitimate rights, even if the verdict requires them to make restitution or concede error.

In stark contrast, the corrupt individual despises transparent courts precisely because corruption cannot survive impartial scrutiny. They prefer opaque legal environments, compromised judges, political interference, and systems where wealth, status, or tribal connections dictate outcomes, shielding their unjust gains from exposure. When an individual or a faction evades an incorruptible legal process, their evasion is a tacit confession of guilt. They avoid the light of justice because they know that their claims are grounded in extortion, theft, or deceit, which wither under the sun of truth and crumble before honest scrutiny, leaving the wrongdoer exposed.

**The Sovereign Character of Divine Adjudication**

Verse 48 establishes that authentic faith is ultimately validated in the courtroom of obedience. A religious tradition that confines itself to private contemplation while abandoning the public administration of justice is an incomplete faith that fails the test of practical life.

The Qur'anic vision insists that the law of God must govern commerce, contracts, marriage, inheritance, and civic conflict across every stratum of society. When believers are summoned to divine arbitration, their response must be swift, unequivocal surrender to truth, regardless of whether the ruling grants them property or strips them of an unjust advantage. By condemning those who turn away from prophetic arbitration, the Qur'an protects the integrity of Islamic governance, establishing that the rule of divine law is supreme, inviolable, and binding upon every member of society without exception, privilege, or exemption.
