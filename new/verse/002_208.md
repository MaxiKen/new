## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:208

> O believers! Enter into Islam wholeheartedly and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. Surely he is your sworn enemy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Enter Completely**

The diptych of the two men gives way to a direct address, and the address gathers the whole community: O you who believe, enter into the silm — completely. The command's first word raised the exegetes' longest question: silm literally means peace, and many read it here as submission — islām itself — acknowledging, as al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī do, that this is not the word's plain lexical sense but its contextual one. Others take it as obedience in the general sense: enter into the state of obeying God wholly. The readings converge on the act commanded: a total entry, the believer's full-person immersion in the religion rather than a partial occupancy of it. The adverb kāffah doubles the totality: it can be read as all together — the community entering as one body, undivided — or as altogether, modifying the entry rather than the entrants, so that the command reads enter into all of it, omitting none of its rites and commands. Al-Rāzī preserves both, and the verse holds them together without strain: the entry is complete in extent (all of the religion) and complete in company (all of the community), and either incompleteness is the foothold the second clause warns against.

**The Occasion: The Converts Who Kept Their Sabbath**

The reports behind the verse give the command its first case-study. Some narrations describe a group of Jewish converts — ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām and his companions are named in the traditions — who wished, after embracing Islam, to keep observing the Sabbath and other specifically Jewish practices that did not explicitly contradict Islamic teaching: continuing to abstain from foods the new law permitted, honoring the old holy day alongside the new. The verse answered the wish with the command to enter completely: the new submission is not an addition to the old observance but a replacement of it, and the convert who keeps one foot in the superseded law has not entered — he has leaned in the doorway. Al-Qurṭubī extends the reading to the People of the Book generally: the verse urges Jews and Christians, believers in Moses and Jesus, to convert en masse — the kāffah reading — to embrace Islam's totality rather than retain a partial continuity. The occasion and the extension agree on the principle: partial entry is no entry, and the religion admits no installment plans, because every retained reservation is a room of the house left unlit.

**The Entry of the Already-Believing**

For the believers already inside — the address's primary addressees — the command reads as deepening rather than conversion. Al-Rāzī states the sense: enter more deeply and completely into islām, omitting none of its rites and commands. The verse thus legislates against the most common failure of the established believer: the selective practice, the religion kept in its comfortable provinces while its demanding territories are left unvisited — the prayer maintained and the zakāh negotiated, the fast kept and the tongue unfasted, the hajj performed and the interval corrupted. The silm-as-peace reading adds a social dimension: the community that enters together enters into peace with one another, unfactionalized, the Ḥums-type separatisms of rank and lineage dissolved in the single entry — the same leveling the pilgrimage-verses just enacted at Minā. The command's grammar reinforces the totality: the entry is into the silm, not toward it or near it; the preposition of immersion, not approach. The believer addressed by this verse is told that his membership is not a status but a movement — an entering that is never finished, a going-in that must reach all the rooms, together with all the other entrants, until the house is fully inhabited.

**The Footsteps of Satan**

The second clause names what partial entry actually is: do not follow the footsteps of Satan. The phrase is the Qur'an's fixed formula — the same command the food-legislation carried (2:168), the cattle-rulings of the sūrah of the Cattle repeat it (6:142), and the sūrah of Light legislates it for the believers directly: whoever follows Satan's footsteps, he commands immorality and wickedness (24:21). The word khuṭuwāt — footsteps, tracks, the prints of a walked path — carries the formula's whole psychology: Satan's method is not the leap but the step. He does not invite the believer from the mosque to the Fire in one motion; he lays a track — the small concession, the reasonable exception, the reservation kept from the old life, the room left unentered — and each step is short enough to take. The verse's two clauses are therefore one argument: the incomplete entry *is* the first footprint. The convert keeping his Sabbath, the believer skipping a rite, the community splitting into factions — each has placed a foot on a track that leads, step by undramatic step, away from the house he thinks he still lives in. The manifest-enemy clause at the verse's end confirms the reading: an enemy declared, an enemy known, is countered by watching the path rather than the horizon — the footsteps, not the face.

**The Enemy Manifest**

The closing declaration — he is your manifest enemy — states the enmity's two properties at once. Manifest: the hostility is not hidden, not debatable, not subject to reassessment — Satan declared his program at the beginning, in the Qur'an's own narration of his expulsion: to sit for them on the straight path, to come from before and behind, to prove them ungrateful. The enemy who has published his methods is the enemy whose footsteps can be recognized, and the verse's command to watch the tracks presupposes the declaration's clarity. And *your* enemy: the enmity is personal and collective — aimed at each believer and at the community's entry as a whole, which is why the two clauses address the individual's completeness and the community's togetherness together. The sūrah's earlier food-legislation ended its own footsteps-command with the same declaration (2:168), and the repetition is the verse's pedagogy: the believer is reminded, at each legislative station, that the track exists and the enemy is known. Between the impressed speech of the hypocrite (2:204) and the total entry commanded here, the sūrah has drawn the whole geography of the heart's danger — the eloquence from outside, the footsteps from below — and stationed one command against both: enter, completely, together, and watch the ground you walk on.

**The Verse at the Passage's Hinge**

The placement of this command completes the movement's architecture. The hajj-legislation formed the community through rite (2:196–203); the diptych displayed the heart's two economies (2:204–207); and this verse issues the summons that both prepared: enter completely. The summons is addressed to believers — to people already inside the door — which is its sharpest feature: the rite-formed, portrait-warned community is told that its membership is still an entry, a movement not yet completed, a house with rooms it has not walked into. The verse that follows will state the stakes of faltering in that entry — if you slip after the clear proofs have come (2:209) — and the one after it the scene the slipper is waiting for. But the hinge-verse itself holds the whole demand in two clauses: the totality of the entry against the partiality of reservation, and the watchfulness of the path against the subtlety of the enemy. The community that left Minā knowing it would be gathered to God is now gathered to one command, and the command is the religion's own name turned into a verb: become what you are called — enter the silm, all of you, into all of it, with the enemy's footsteps visible on the ground at the door.
