## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:24

> But if you are unable to do so—and you will never be able to do so—then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conditional That Answers Itself**

The verse opens by completing the test of verse 23, and completes it before anyone has taken it: *fa-in lam tafʿalū* — but if you have not done it — *wa-lan tafʿalū* — and you will never do it. The second clause, set off in this translation by dashes, is an interruption of the conditional by certainty. The particle *lan* is Arabic's strongest future negation, and the commentators draw the grammatical-theological point precisely: a conditional from Allah is not an expression of uncertainty, just as the "perhaps" of verse 21 was not an expression of anxiety. Divine "ifs" state the logic of the situation from the human side while the divine side already holds the outcome. The effect on the reader is singular — the challenge is issued in full seriousness *and* its failure is announced in the same breath, so that the trial functions as revelation rather than information: the point was never to learn whether they could, but to let them discover, by trying or by flinching, what they are.

**An Aside Spoken Over Their Heads**

There is a shift of addressee inside the dash that deserves attention. "If you do not" addresses the doubters; "and you will never do it" is spoken about them — a confidence delivered past them, to the Prophet ﷺ and the believers, in the middle of a sentence directed at the objectors. Rhetorically the aside is devastating: the doubters are being examined and, simultaneously, their exam results are being read aloud to the proctor. The device also protects the challenge's sincerity. Had the verse said simply *you will fail*, it would be a taunt with no test; had it said simply *if you fail*, the confidence would be absent. Holding both in one sentence, the Qur'an performs perfect knowledge and perfect fairness at once — the door genuinely open, the outcome genuinely known, and the known outcome stated publicly so that the failure, when it arrives, arrives without excuse. The hypocrites of this sūrah's opening lived by managing appearances; this aside announces a courtroom where appearances are read before they are worn.

**A Fire With Fuel Named**

The consequence clause introduces the Fire with an engineering detail: *allatī waqūduhā al-nās wa-l-ḥijārah* — its fuel is people and stones. The commentators identify the stones as sulfur, the burning mineral of the classical imagination, and as the idols themselves, the objects of the worship that led their votaries in — and the Qur'an elsewhere states the idol reading outright: *"Certainly you ˹disbelievers˺ and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell"* (21:98). The fuel list is thus a moral equation: the worshipper and the worshipped burn together, and the false god becomes, in the only transformation it ever manages, an aggravator of its devotee's fire. The same phrase is turned on the believers elsewhere as pastoral warning — *"Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones"* (66:6) — which confirms the description is meant to be taken concretely by every party. A hell of abstraction frightens no one; a hell with a fuel specification is designed for the imagination, and the imagination is where choices are actually made.

**“Prepared”: A House Already Furnished**

One verb in the closing clause does quiet, heavy work: *uʿiddat* — it has been prepared, made ready in advance, for the disbelievers. The passive points to a preparation completed before any inhabitant arrives; the Fire is not improvised at the judgment but kept ready, stocked, waiting — the inverted mirror of verse 22. There the world was furnished for the addressee: a bed of earth, a canopy of sky, a supply chain of rain and fruit. Here the counter-house is furnished too: fuel laid in, appointment made, address written. The two houses stand in the same passage a verse apart, each with its provisions and its tenancy terms, and the reader of the sūrah is being shown that he is already a resident of one — by worship or by rivalry, by the direction his gratitude flows. The preparedness of the Fire also answers the preparedness of the Garden that follows in verse 25: neither destination is under construction; both are standing, and the only unfinished item in the cosmos is the choice.

**Ittiqū: The Command the Threat Actually Issues**

The main verb of the verse is easy to skim past: *fa-ttaqū* — then guard yourselves, then fear — the imperative of the very root that named the sūrah's opening people, the *muttaqīn*. The structure is worth stating plainly: the threat is not the verse's point; the command is. "Fear the Fire" is taqwā given an object, and the whole verse exists to move taqwā from disposition to decision. The God-consciousness of verse 2 was described as an achieved character; here its manufacture is shown — mindfulness made urgent by a named danger, made possible by an open test, made reasonable by a prepared alternative. The Qur'an's use of fear is instrumental and never terminal: fear of the Fire is prescribed the way a smoke alarm is installed, to produce motion toward exits, not paralysis. That is why the verse ends by naming the Fire's appointment — *for the disbelievers* — a class with membership rules and therefore with resignation procedures. The fear commanded here is, in its full function, hope wearing work boots.

**Threat Positioned After a Failed Test**

The verse's location in the argument completes its mercy. The sequence has been: command (worship, v. 21), evidence (the furnished world, v. 22), test (produce a sūrah, v. 23), verdict and consequence (v. 24). The threat arrives only after the intellect has been addressed and the experiment offered — it is not the opening move of revelation but its fourth, spoken to people whose doubt has been given every instrument to resolve itself and who are now shown the shape of the alternative. This ordering answers the commonest misreading of Quranic warning: the Fire is not a bludgeon wielded against the unexamined but the last honest datum given to the examined. And the paragraph does not end in the dark. The very next verse pivots from the prepared Fire to the Gardens prepared for belief and good works, so that the two houses stand side by side and the reader — the real addressee of both — is left where the Qur'an always leaves him: not informed about destinations but standing at the switch.
