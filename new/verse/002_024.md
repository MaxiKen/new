## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:24

> But if you are unable to do so—and you will never be able to do so—then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prediction Inserted Into a Threat**

The verse contains one of the most exposed sentences in the Quran: *you will never be able to do so* (2:24). That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is a statement about the future, made in public, to an audience with every reason to try — and a single successful imitation would have destroyed it.

The sūrah is therefore putting its own claim at risk, and the risk is worth naming. Threats can be dismissed; predictions cannot. What the verse offers is a test that anyone could have run, with a result that would have been visible to everyone, and the Quran's standing as scripture is, on its own account, staked on the outcome.

**A Conditional Used for Certainty**

The grammar is unusual: *if you do not — and you will not*. The first half is a conditional; the second half collapses it. Commentators noted the same feature in the previous passage, where a purpose clause seemed to imply anxiety in God, and resolved it the same way: the conditional form is the shape of a challenge, not the expression of a doubt.

The effect is to make the warning a certainty while preserving the form of an argument. The reader is not told to believe because he is commanded to; he is told to try, told in advance what the attempt will produce, and then told what follows from it. The sequence is deliberate, and it is the reason the sūrah's challenge carries the weight it does.

**The Fire's Fuel**

The Fire is described by what burns in it: *people and stones* (2:24). The same description appears in the sūrah's closing chapter: *O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones* (66:6). The pairing is deliberate and has been read two ways.

On the first reading the stones are the idols — the things that were worshipped alongside God, present in the Fire with their worshippers, having been useless to them and now useless in a different way. The sūrah supports this, since the verse before ends by forbidding the setting up of equals, and the stones of 2:24 answer the equals of 2:22. On the second reading the stones are fuel in the ordinary sense, the intensest combustible the audience knew. Both readings can stand together, and the first is the more pointed: what a person trusted is what he burns with.

The parallel passage adds a detail about the Fire's administration that belongs here: it is *overseen by formidable and severe angels, who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded* (66:6). The point is not the severity of the guards. It is that the Fire is described as something administered rather than abandoned — a place with a functioning order, which is a harder picture to dismiss than one of undifferentiated torment. A reader is being told that this consequence is maintained, not merely threatened.

**Prepared**

The Fire is *prepared* (2:24) — made ready, in advance, in the perfect tense. It is not described as something that will be constructed if the need arises. The sūrah speaks of it as an existing thing, finished and waiting, which is part of what gives the warning its plainness.

The word also settles a question that readers raise. If the Fire is already prepared, the threat is not an expression of divine frustration; it is a description of an arrangement. The sūrah's warnings throughout are of this character — statements about how things stand rather than declarations of what God will decide to do when provoked.

That framing matters for how the verse is meant to land. A threat issued in anger can be waited out; an arrangement cannot. The sūrah is not describing a God who will lose patience at some future point and act differently from how He acts now. It is describing consequences built into the structure of things, which is why the warning can be stated so calmly — and why the verse that follows can turn, without a change of tone, to the people who will not be in it.

**For the Disbelievers**

The Fire is *prepared for the disbelievers* (2:24), using the same word the sūrah used at 2:6 for those who persist in rejection. The description is not applied to everyone who has sinned; it is applied to a settled posture. Within eight verses the sūrah will describe the people on the other side of this division, and the two destinations are stated in the same register throughout — as outcomes of what a person has done rather than as verdicts handed down without reference to it.

**Then Fear**

The response demanded is *then fear* — the imperative of the same root the sūrah opened with at 2:2, where the Book was called guidance *for those mindful ˹of Allah˺*. The word has come full circle in twenty-two verses. The quality that qualified a person to benefit from the Book is the quality the Fire is meant to produce.

That is the argument the whole opening has been building. Reverence is not presented as a feeling to be worked up but as the appropriate response to two facts placed side by side: a provision that arrives every season, and a consequence that has already been made ready. The sūrah turns immediately to what that reverence earns.
