## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:24

> But if you are unable to do so—and you will never be able to do so—then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Conditional That Denies Its Own Contingency**

The previous verse had said *“if you are in doubt … then produce a sūrah”* (2:23) as a genuine hypothetical, opening a test whose outcome would decide the doubt. This verse closes the test before it is attempted: *“But if you do not — and you will not — then fear the Fire”* — *fa-in lam tafʿalū wa-lan tafʿalū* (2:24). The two verbs are not repetition. *In lam tafʿalū* — “if you do not” — preserves the form of a conditional; *wa-lan tafʿalū* — “and you will never do” — adds a categorical negation with *lan*, which in Arabic denies the future absolutely. The source rightly notes that the *if* here is not an expression of uncertainty on the Speaker’s part, any more than *laʿalla* (“so that you may,” 2:21) expresses anxiety. The *if* is the challenger’s perspective — *you* may still imagine you could — and the *lan* is the divine perspective — *you will not*. The conditional that seemed to open a competition is thus revealed to have been an invitation to discover an inability that was already known.

**“You Will Never Do So” — A Prophecy Inside a Warning**

*Wa-lan tafʿalū* is not merely a rhetorical flourish; it is a prophecy. It says that the inimitability of the Qur'an will not be overcome in the time remaining to the challengers, or in any time. The challenge of (2:23) was to bring a sūrah; the judgment of (2:24) is that the sūrah will not be brought, not because the challengers will choose not to try, but because the Book’s particular combination of brevity, resonance, and layered meaning in the language the challengers claim to master cannot be matched when attempted. That is the sense of *iʿjāz* — incapacitation — that the source describes as the Qur'an’s central miracle: it incapacitates the challenger’s argument against it, as miracles do, not by silencing the challenger’s tongue but by showing that the tongue cannot produce what it claims could be produced. The prophecy remains falsifiable in the most public way: a single sūrah would falsify it, and the sūrah has not been brought.

**Fear the Fire Whose Fuel Is People and Stones**

*Fa-ttaqū al-nāra allatī waqūduhā al-nāsu wa-l-ḥijārah* — “then fear the Fire whose fuel is people and stones” (2:24). The *fa-* — “then” — makes the fear the inference of the failed test: if you cannot produce a sūrah and you will not, then the doubt that motivated the test is not a neutral hesitation but a standing that is already answerable. The Fire is described by its fuel, not merely by its heat. Its fuel is *people and stones*. The stones are glossed as sulfur — the most combustible mineral — or as the very idols that were set up as equals (*andād*, 2:22) and that the challengers were told to call as witnesses (*shuhadāʾ*, 2:23) (IK; cf. 21:98, where the worshipped are thrown in). The image is precise. The equals that were set up alongside Allah and the witnesses that were summoned other than Allah become, in the Fire, part of the Fire’s own sustenance. What was worshipped as a rival and invoked as a helper is shown to be combustible.

**“Prepared for the Disbelievers” — A Fire Already Readied**

*Uʿiddat li-l-kāfirīn* — “prepared for the disbelievers.” The verb *uʿiddat* is perfect, already prepared, not “will be prepared.” The Fire is not a contingency that will be assembled if the challenge fails; it is a standing preparation whose existence the challenge presupposes. That is why the Qur'an elsewhere can describe Paradise as *prepared for the mindful* (*uʿiddat li-l-muttaqīn*, 3:133) in the same perfect: both abodes are already readied, and the human being, by his response to the Book that is *“no doubt”* (2:2) and that was *sent down upon the servant* (2:23), is moving toward one preparation or the other. The choice of *li-l-kāfirīn* — for the disbelievers — gathers those who persist in covering (2:6), those whose hearts are sealed (2:7), those who trade guidance for error (2:16), and those who are in doubt about the servant’s Book, under one destination that has been made ready.

**From “So That You May Become Mindful” to “Then Fear”**

The sequence from *laʿallakum tattaqūn* — “so that you may become mindful” (2:21) — to *fa-ttaqū al-nār* — “then fear the Fire” — uses the same root *w-q-y* that defined the mindful at the start. The telos of worship was mindfulness; the consequence of refusing the Book that would teach worship is the Fire that mindfulness would have guarded against. The sūrah therefore holds the same verb for both promise and warning. To become mindful is to be guarded (*waqāhum*); to fear the Fire is to guard against it (*ittaqū*). The Fire whose fuel is people and stones is the place where the guarding failed, and the guarding failed because the Book that is *“no doubt”* was met with *rayb*. The sūrah does not present the Fire to fascinate; it presents it to clarify what doubt costs when the doubt is about the very provision that was made to settle the doubter.

**Why the Fuel Is Named Before the Inhabitants**

The Fire is described as having fuel before it is described as having inhabitants. That order prevents the Fire from being heard as a mere container into which people are thrown. It is first a fire that burns people and stones, and then a fire that is prepared for disbelievers. The fuel precedes the clientele so that the clientele understands they are not guests in a place but sustenance for it. The stones, whether sulfur or idols, emphasize the indiscriminate combustibility of what is thrown in: the hardest mineral and the most revered idol are alike fuel. The people who are fuel are those who, having been made a place of settlement upon a floored earth under a roofed sky and given fruits as provision (2:22), set up equals while knowing (2:22), doubted the servant’s Book while being offered the fairest test (2:23), and then did not and will not produce its like.

**A Certainty That Preserves the Summons**

Finally, to hear *“you will never do so”* as a cancellation of the summons to humanity (2:21) is to mishear it. The certainty that the sūrah will not be matched does not cancel the invitation to worship; it secures it. The Book that cannot be imitated is the Book that can be trusted as guidance for the mindful, and the Fire that is prepared is the Fire that the mindful, by being mindful, are guarded against. The conditional that seemed to threaten — *if you do not, and you will not* — is, from the side of the mindful, a reassurance: the Book that calls you to worship your Lord is not a human composition that might be outwritten tomorrow. It is the same Book whose incapacity to be imitated is the public form of its claim to be *“no doubt”*, and the Fire whose fuel is people and stones is the private form of what doubt about such a Book entails.
