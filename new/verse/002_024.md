## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:24

> But if you are unable to do so—and you will never be able to do so—then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Condition Answered Before It Is Attempted**

The sentence opens where the challenge left off: if you have not managed it. Then it adds a clause that turns a possibility into a certainty, stating that the attempt will never succeed. Arabic marks the two halves differently — a plain conditional for the first, an emphatically negated future for the second — and the effect is a door closing while the invitation to knock remains printed on it.

The structure is the same one the sūrah used a few verses earlier when it described the hope attached to God's own purpose. Just as *perhaps* in a divine sentence amounts to a promise, here a conditional that sounds open ends in a flat denial. The Book is not being coy about its expectations. It has assessed the field and published the result before any contestant steps forward.

**Two Words for Fuel, Both Chosen With Care**

The fire is described by what feeds it: people and stones. The Qur'an uses the same pair in an exhortation to the believers: *“Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable and severe angels.”* (66:6). The classical commentators read the stones two ways, and the readings are not exclusive. One takes them as the black, sulfurous stones that burn hotter than wood, describing the fire's intensity. The other takes them as the idols themselves, the stone images that had been objects of worship, so that the worshipper and the thing he worshipped meet the same end.

The second reading fits the logic of the previous verse, where the audience was reminded that God made the sky a canopy and the earth a bed before being told not to set up equals to Him. The Book makes the point explicitly in another sūrah: *“Certainly you ˹disbelievers˺ and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell. You are ˹all˺ bound to enter it.”* (21:98). Nothing that was relied upon instead of God survives the fire, and the fuel list includes both the idol and the one who trusted it.

**The Command Turns From Argument to Warning**

Having finished the demonstration, the verse issues an instruction: fear the Fire. The Arabic verb comes from the same root as *taqwā*, the objective named in the call to worship two verses earlier. Reverence was presented there as the fruit of worship and the mark of the mindful; here the same vocabulary is turned toward a specific danger, as though the shield were being carried into a burning field.

That double usage shows how the Book handles its central virtue. Reverence in the Qur'an is not a general piety with no object; it is the protective awareness of someone who knows what is at stake and acts accordingly. The same sūrah that calls the Book a guide for the mindful now tells its audience what awareness is for, and it names the danger in the plainest available terms rather than leaving it to be inferred.

**Prepared, Which Means It Is Waiting**

The fire is described as prepared for the disbelievers, and the word indicates something made ready in advance rather than something improvised at the moment of judgement. The Book uses the same construction for the reward of the righteous: the sūrah's next sentence promises Gardens prepared for those who believe and do good, and elsewhere paradise is described as prepared in the same terms. The two destinies are presented as arrangements already in place, waiting for their occupants.

That presentation is uncomfortable and deliberate. A reader is not offered a period in which the outcome is undecided and being negotiated; he is told that the destination exists on the shelf, so to speak, and that the only open question is which one he is travelling toward. The Book uses this kind of language to remove the assumption that the reckoning can be deferred by inattention, and it does so in the middle of a lecture on evidence.

**The Wager Inside the Challenge**

Read as a whole, the passage from the previous verse to this one is an argument built like a stake. A claim is made about the revelation; a test is offered; the failure of the test is predicted in advance; and then the consequence of the failure is named. What the audience is asked to consider is not merely whether the Qur'an is beautiful, but what follows if they are wrong about it.

The Book takes this approach repeatedly, and it always places the cost on the side of the person being addressed rather than on the side of the claim. There is no attempt to prove the text's divinity with an argument that admits no counter-consideration. The proposal on the table is a comparison that anyone is free to attempt, and the sentence that closes the challenge states what the Book believes is at stake in refusing it.

**An Audience Still Being Addressed**

The most important detail in the verse is its grammar. The imperative addresses the doubters directly, which means the warning is being issued to the very people whose failure to match the revelation has just been predicted. If the passage were only a verdict, the imperative would be pointless; coming as it does, the verse tells its hearers that the prediction about the contest is not a statement about them.

That is the same pattern the sūrah has followed from its opening pages. It described people whose hearts were sealed, and then called upon all humanity to worship their Maker. It reported that some would never believe, and then invited everyone to consider the evidence. The warning in this verse belongs to the same arrangement: the outcome has been announced, and the door is still open while it is being announced.
