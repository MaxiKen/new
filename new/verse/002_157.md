## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:157

> They are the ones who will receive Allah’s blessings and mercy. And it is they who are ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Patient Receive**

The three verses form a single movement — the test announced, the words given, the payment stated — and this verse is the payment. Those who say the formula of return receive three things, named in sequence: blessings from their Lord, mercy, and the standing of the guided. The verse answers the question every command of endurance implicitly raises: patience costs, and cost demands compensation. The compensation is stated with a completeness that matches the catalogue of losses in the test — against fear and hunger and loss of wealth and lives and crops, the response is divine attention, divine mercy, and divine guidance. Nothing lost in the trial is replaced item by item; the replacement operates on a different plane entirely, which is the sūrah's consistent method: the world's ledger is settled in the world's currency only rarely, and the believer's ledger is settled in a currency the disaster cannot touch.

**Ṣalawāt: Prayer Running the Other Direction**

The word rendered "blessings" is *ṣalawāt* — the plural of *ṣalāh*, the same word that names the believer's prayer. The exegetes fixed its meaning here by its grammar: *ṣalāh* ordinarily denotes what goes from human beings toward God, but when it is used with the preposition *ʿalā* — upon, toward — it denotes what comes from God toward human beings. The controlling instance is the verse of the Confederates: *"Indeed, Allah showers His blessings upon the Prophet, and His angels pray for him"* (33:56), where the same construction carries God's blessing down and the angels' prayers around Him. Applied to the patient, the plural is generous — blessings, repeated and manifold, descending on the one whose prayer ascended in ten words. The exchange is visible in the mechanism: the mourner prayed *to* God; God answers with prayers *upon* the mourner. The same descending blessing appears in the sūrah of Repentance, where donations are described as a means of receiving the Messenger's prayers (9:99) and his prayer is called a source of comfort for the givers (9:103) — divine and prophetic ṣalāh functioning, in both cases, as mercy made audible over a person's life.

**Mercy After Blessing**

The second gift — *raḥmah* — is named in the singular after the plural blessings, and the pairing is deliberate. Blessing (*ṣalawāt*) is attention bestowed: the honored mention, the descending care, the good that is spoken and done toward the servant. Mercy is the quality behind the attention: the tenderness with which God regards the one who bore His test without breaking faith. The commentators note that God's blessings upon the patient are His forgiveness and His praise of them; the mercy that follows covers what the test cost — the fear borne, the hunger endured, the grave visited. A gift without mercy could be mechanical, a reward for performance; mercy without gifts could remain sentiment. The verse gives both, in order: the objective descent of blessings, then the personal quality that sends them. The order also answers the test's own sequence. The trial came first — fear, hunger, loss — and it was endured without any promise that the enduring would feel rewarded while it lasted. Mercy named after the fact is the assurance that nothing in the trial went unobserved: every hour of patience was witnessed by the One whose compassion now arrives as its stated counterpart. The bereaved who said the formula of return are not merely compensated; they are compassionated, held in the regard that the disaster tried to argue they had lost. The patient are thus not only paid but embraced, and the embrace is what converts the payment from wages into relationship.

**The Third Gift: Reclassification**

The verse's final clause is its quiet surprise: *and it is they who are the guided*. The patient have not merely been rewarded; they have been reclassified. Guidance in this sūrah is the standing description of those who are on the straight path — the quality of the successful at the sūrah's opening, the object of every prayer for direction, the thing the qiblah change sorted the community by (2:143). To declare the speakers of the grief-formula *the* guided ones — with the restrictive construction that excludes implication — is to say that patience under disaster is not adjacent to guidance but is guidance in act. The doctrinal point is sharp: a person's standing before God is revealed less by what he believes in comfort than by what he says in the first blow. The formula *to Allah we belong and to Him we return* is, in this verse's light, the shortest complete statement of faith under pressure — belonging, return, and trust spoken while the wound is open — and the one who says it has been guided to the exact words heaven intended for that moment.

**The Sequence Complete**

Read as the close of the three-verse unit, this verse retroactively colors what came before. The test was announced with certainty (2:155) — now it is seen as an examination whose passing grade is mercy and guidance. The words were given (2:156) — now they are seen as the performance itself, the thing that earns the blessings. And patience, defined at the first blow (2:155), is now defined again by its outcome: not a grim holding-on but the passage into the guided. The sūrah will return to patience repeatedly — in battle, in dispute, in waiting for provisions delayed — and each return carries this verse's guarantee with it. The community that heard these three verses in Medina had lost people at the wells and would lose more in the battles to come; what it was given was not the prevention of loss but its transfiguration. Fear and hunger and the deaths of loved ones were already on the syllabus; now they had their answer key — ten words to say, and blessings, mercy, and guidance on the other side of saying them.
