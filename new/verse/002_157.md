## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:157

> They are the ones who will receive Allah’s blessings and mercy. And it is they who are ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward Named**

The verse states what those who endure receive, and it names three things: blessings from their Lord, mercy, and guidance. The sentence is the answer to the instruction that opened the passage, and it completes the sequence that began with the command to seek help through patience and prayer.

The Book gives the reward in the present tense rather than describing a future payment, and the community's teachers noted that the blessings and the mercy are granted in this life as well as in the next. What the reader receives is not only an assurance about a distant accounting but a statement about the condition of the patient now.

**The Meaning of the Blessings**

The word rendered as blessings is the plural of a term that normally means prayer or worship, and the commentators explained that when it is used with the preposition that means upon, it describes what comes from God toward His servants rather than what is offered to Him. The same construction appears in another chapter where God and the angels are said to invoke blessings upon the Prophet, and in passages where the alms of the believers are described as a means of tranquillity for them.

The point of the wording is direction, and the community's scholars explained that what the patient receive is God's regard, His commendation, and His care. The Book describes the Prophets' prayers for blessings on others, and the sentence here describes the blessings of God arriving on those who have held firm. What the verse sets out is an exchange whose source is God and whose recipients are the people the passage has just described.

**Mercy as the Second Gift**

The second term describes mercy, and the Book uses it in a variety of contexts, including mercy between spouses, mercy toward the poor, and the mercy God shows to whom He wills. The word in this verse is the mercy that accompanies the blessings and that remains with the community after the loss.

The community's teachers connected the term with the relief that follows affliction, and they described it as the kindness that makes the difficulty bearable. The sentence gives the reader the assurance that the gift is not a reward reserved for the end of time but a condition into which the patient enter, and the pairing of the two terms shows that the community receives both regard and relief.

The term also appears in the community’s daily speech, since every unit of the prayer begins with the names that describe God as the compassionate and the merciful, and the sūrah’s opening verses use the same pair. The reader who recites the Book in his worship therefore meets the vocabulary of this verse before he meets the verse itself, and the teachers of the community pointed out that the reward granted to the patient is described with the same words the community uses when it addresses its Lord.

**Guidance as the Third Gift**

The third term is guidance, and the sūrah has used the word from its opening verses onward, where the Book is described as a guide for the reverent and the community asks for guidance in its prayer. The passage's placement of the term at the end of the list connects the endurance of trials with the direction of a person's life.

The connection is significant for the community's understanding of what testing is for. A person who holds firm under loss is not merely compensated for his suffering; he is confirmed in the path he is following, and the Book names the confirmation as one of the gifts. The reader learns that the trial, endured with patience, belongs to the process by which guidance is granted and maintained.

**Who Is Being Described**

The verse does not describe a small group within the community, and the Book attaches the promises to the conduct the previous verses have described. Those who say the words of the preceding verse when affliction strikes are the ones the sentence is about, and the community's praise in the Book is given to a behaviour rather than to a rank.

The passage therefore applies to every believer who suffers loss, and the sūrah's placement of the reward after the description of the words indicates that the door is open to all. What the Book establishes is that the community's honour is connected with its response to difficulty, and not with the absence of difficulty from its life.

**How the Verse Sits in the Sūrah**

The sentence closes the short passage on testing and prepares the reader for the subject that follows, which concerns the rituals of the pilgrimage and the signs of God in creation. The Book often places a statement about the reward of the faithful at the end of a passage about hardship, and the present verse follows that pattern.

The reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening pages has also met each of the three terms before, since guidance was the subject of the Book's first description of itself, and mercy was named among the attributes with which the sūrah began. The sentence therefore brings the reader back to the sūrah's opening themes at the close of a passage about loss, and the effect is to show that the gifts described here are the same ones the Book promised at the outset.

The Book’s habit of closing a passage with the reward of those it has praised is visible wherever the sūrah addresses the conduct of the believers, and the community’s teachers used the pattern when they divided the chapters for study. A reader who marks where the promises fall will find that they regularly follow a description of what the community is to do, so that the Book presents the reward as the answer to a specific behaviour. The present sentence follows that arrangement, and it leaves the reader with the three gifts attached to the response that the previous verses described.
