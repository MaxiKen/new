## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:157

> They are the ones who will receive Allah’s blessings and mercy. And it is they who are ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṣalawāt from the Lord**

The word *ṣalawāt* is the plural of *ṣalāh*, and the community's earliest teachers had to say what it means when the subject is God. Al-Bukhārī records the statement of Abū al-ʿĀliya: God's *ṣalāh* is His praise of the person before the angels, while the *ṣalāh* of the angels is their supplication for him. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have rendered it simply as blessings. The verse therefore describes a condition that the community cannot observe: the patient are being named in the highest assembly.

Al-Ṭabarī collects the readings together and concludes that the sense is the divine commendation and forgiveness that descends upon those who said the words of the previous verse. The plural has been read as intensification, so that the sentence gives them not one acknowledgment but a repeated one.

**Mercy Added to Commendation**

The verse does not stop at the announcement of praise; it adds *raḥma*. The commentators treat the two as different gifts: the first concerns the divine regard, and the second concerns what the person actually receives — protection from the punishment, leniency on the day of the reckoning, and the favour that accompanies him in both abodes. Some of the early teachers rendered the pair as forgiveness and grace.

Al-Rāzī makes a point of the order. Mercy is named after praise, because the verses describe a people who have just lost what they loved; the reward is presented first as honour and then as relief. The pairing recurs in the description of the Prophet's household in the Qur'an (33:43), where God and the angels are said to bless the believers, and the community's scholars read the two passages together.

**The Guided Ones**

The third element of the verse is a predicate of identity: *wa-ulāʾika humu l-muhtadūn* — "and these are the guided". Ibn Kathīr records the comment of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb on the sentence: what righteous things, and what a station — reading it as an expression of awe at what the verse has named. The community's teachers noted that the verse does not say the patient will be guided later, but that they are the guided already, at the moment they speak those words.

The choice of the word is significant in the passage. The sūrah has spent its length distinguishing guidance from its counterfeits, and it closes this episode by assigning the highest of its terms to the people who answered a calamity with a sentence of ownership and return.

**The Three Gifts in Order**

Praise before the community, mercy from the Lord, and guidance on the path: the sequence has been read as a description of what the patient gains at the moment of loss. The community's preachers divided the verse into those three and taught it as a promise in three parts, each of which answers a different fear — that the sufferer is forgotten, that he will not be compensated, and that he will not find his way afterwards.

The method belongs to the passage's structure. The announcement of the test came first, the sentence of the patient came second, and the accounting of their reward closes it, so that the reader who arrives at this verse has already been told what to say.

**The Recitation in the Community**

The passage from 2:153 to 2:157 became the community's text for bereavement. The sentence of the previous verse and the promise of this one are said together at the graveside and in the house of mourning, and the practice was established early enough that the phrase became part of ordinary speech rather than of formal rites. The teachers encouraged its recitation to the newly bereaved because the verse supplies what consolation cannot: a statement of what has actually happened.

The community's jurists added the practical matters that surround it — what is permitted in weeping, how the news is conveyed, what is said to the family — and the whole body of it was built with this passage in view.

The passage's use extended beyond funerals. The teachers encouraged its recitation when a person was struck by illness, by the loss of property, or by the failure of an undertaking, because the promise is attached to the sentence of the previous verse rather than to death alone, and the community's practice followed the general word that the verse uses for the calamity.

**The Angels and the Believers**

The blessing named in this verse belongs to a practice the Book describes in another register. God and His angels bless the Prophet ﷺ (33:56), and the same two verbs are used of the believers in 33:43, where the angels are said to seek forgiveness for them and to bring them out of darkness into light. The community's teachers gathered the three passages when they explained what ṣalāh means when its subject is God, and the conclusion they drew was that the praise of the patient is announced in the assembly in which the messengers are named. The gift is therefore a standing before the created order rather than a private sentiment, and the early teachers used the point when they consoled households that had lost one of their own.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse establishes the praise and mercy that belong to those who say the words of 2:156, and the guidance that is ascribed to them as a condition rather than a future reward. It closes the passage on patience that begins with the command to seek help through patience and prayer, so that the reader leaves the section with the complete arc: announcement, endurance, speech, and the divine response.
