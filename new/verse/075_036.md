## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:36

> Do people think they will be left without purpose?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Returns to Its Opening Question**

The movement away from threat and back to argument is marked by a repeated verb. Verse 3 asked whether a man supposes that God will not gather his bones, and this verse asks whether he supposes he will be left to no purpose. The verb is the same — *yaḥsabu*, to reckon, to suppose without grounds — and the two questions between them frame the entire sūrah. The first raised an objection about power: can scattered bones be reassembled? This one raises an objection about purpose: is a human being answerable for anything? The sūrah answered the first in v. 4 and will answer this one in the four verses that follow, using the same method in both cases — a premise the denier already accepts.

**What the Word Means**

The term rendered as without purpose is *sudan*, and it belongs to a root used of a camel left untended, wandering without a herdsman and without a destination. The image is not of something deliberately destroyed but of something carelessly abandoned, and that is exactly the claim being examined. Would God make a creature of this complexity and then leave it to wander? The choice of word places the burden where the sūrah wants it. The alternative to purpose is not hostility but negligence, and a negligent creator is not a credible description of the One whose power the sūrah has spent forty verses describing. The word also carries a social sense in its root usage: a thing left *sudan* is a thing without an owner to answer for it. The claim being tested is therefore not that God is cruel but that He is indifferent, and that is the more dangerous idea, since a cruel god could at least be appeased. An indifferent one offers nothing to do.

**What Being Left Would Entail**

If a human being were truly abandoned in that sense, several things would follow, and all of them are false on the Qur'an's account. There would be no command and no prohibition, since a creature with no purpose cannot be directed. There would be no messengers, since there would be nothing to say. There would be no record, no reckoning, and no return, since an aimless creature cannot be called to account for a journey it was never sent on. The Qur'an poses the same absurdity in a direct question: *“Did you then think that We had created you without purpose, and that you would never be returned to Us?”* (23:115). The two halves are inseparable: purpose and return stand or fall together. The purpose is named elsewhere in a single verse, and it is not a vague invitation to be good: *“˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life in order to test which of you is best in deeds.”* (67:2). A test requires an end at which the result is read, which is why the same Qur'an that insists on purpose insists with equal force on the reckoning. The sūrah of the Spider puts the consequence of purpose in the form of a question every believer is asked: *“Do people think once they say, ‘We believe,’ that they will be left without being put to the test?”* (29:2).

**A Different Argument From the One Before**

It is worth marking the shift in the kind of proof. Verses 3 and 4 argued from power — can the bones be gathered, can the fingertips be restored? — and the answer was that the Creator's power has no difficulty with either. This verse argues from purpose, which is a different claim altogether. Power answers the objection that resurrection is impossible; purpose answers the objection that it is pointless. A person can concede the first and still deny the second, and many have: the Makkan materialists did not doubt that a powerful god could raise the dead if he wished, and they argued only that nothing of the sort was going to happen. The sūrah closes that door separately.

**The Deniers Had Purposes of Their Own**

There is a subtlety in what is being denied. The people addressed here were not nihilists; they had projects, ambitions, trades, and family obligations, and they pursued them with real energy. What they denied was that any purpose had been *assigned* to them. The verse rejects that division. A creature made by a wise Maker has a purpose whether or not he acknowledges it, and the purpose is not up for negotiation: *“We only created them for a purpose, but most of these ˹pagans˺ do not know.”* (44:39). The ignorance is theirs, not the absence of the thing. The same movement appears in the Qur'an's account of what these people actually said: *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time.”* (45:24). That is not a claim that life has no purpose; it is a claim that the species continues. The individual, on their account, is expendable, and the Qur'an replies that the individual is exactly what the Creator is concerned with. A man is not a specimen of a continuing kind. He is the one who will be asked. A man who chooses his own aim instead of the one he was made for has not escaped purpose; he has substituted a smaller one for the real one.

**The Answer the Last Verses Supply**

The question is left hanging for exactly one verse, and the answer begins immediately afterwards. The sūrah will not argue about purpose in the abstract. It will point at the process by which each human being comes into existence — a drop, then a clot, then a formed and proportioned body, then the division into the two sexes — and it will ask whether a Maker who did all of that did it for nothing. The argument is from workmanship to intention: deliberate, staged, intricate work implies a worker with an end in view. That is why the sūrah ends with the question it ends on, and why the question is addressed to the same people who began by asking for a date.
