## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:181

> But whoever changes the will after hearing it, the blame will only be on those who made the change. Indeed, Allah is AllHearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Alteration Forbidden**

The verse states that a person who changes a will after hearing it bears the sin of the change, and it closes with the statement that God hears and knows. The sentence addresses the situation that arises after a bequest has been made and its terms have become known.

The Book's instruction protects the arrangement the previous verse required, and the community's scholars read the two sentences together. What the sentence establishes is that a will, once made and known, is not open to revision by those who would benefit from the change.

**Who Can Alter a Will**

The commentators identified those to whom the sentence applies, and they named the executor of the estate, the witnesses whose testimony is required, and anyone else with responsibility for carrying out the bequest. The prohibition applies to each of them, and the sentence states that the responsibility for the alteration falls on the one who makes it.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere requires the presence of witnesses when a bequest is made, and the sentence in this verse presupposes that the terms have been heard. What the reader receives is a rule about the handling of a document that another person has made, and the sentence places the duty of fidelity on those into whose hands the document comes.

**What May Be Changed**

The community's jurists noted an exception to the rule, and they explained that a bequest which is invalid in itself may be cancelled or modified. A bequest of more than the permitted share of the estate is reduced, and a bequest that would confer something unlawful is set aside, since such an arrangement is not a valid disposal at all.

The distinction the scholars drew is between an alteration that frustrates the testator's intention and the correction of an arrangement that the law does not recognise. What the reader is given is a rule with a boundary, and the community's practice has preserved the difference in the treatment of estates that come before a court.

**The Sin and Its Bearer**

The sentence states that the sin of a change rests on the one who makes it rather than on the person who made the will. The wording is precise, and the community's teachers read it as a statement about the assignment of responsibility in matters where several people are involved.

The Book's teaching about responsibility elsewhere is consistent with the sentence, since it states that no soul carries the burden of another. What the reader receives is the assurance that the person whose will has been altered is not charged with the act of the one who altered it, and the sentence leaves the offender with his own account.

**The Names at the End of the Sentence**

The sentence closes by describing God as hearing and knowing, and the community's scholars noted that the two names correspond to the act the sentence forbids. What is said about the will is heard, and what the executor intends is known, so that the alteration cannot be concealed.

The Book's practice of closing a legal instruction with a description of God appears throughout the sūrah, and the reader is shown that the legislation is given by the one whose knowledge covers the matters the law addresses. What the sentence establishes is that the disposal of a person's property is performed under the awareness of God, and the community's teachers read the ending as a warning to those who handle an estate.

**The Provision That Follows**

The next sentence treats the case in which a person fears that the testator has acted unjustly, and it permits a settlement among the parties. The verse in this place and the sentence that follows it form a pair, and the community's scholars read the two as the general rule and its exception.

What the passage establishes is that the fidelity required of those who carry out a will is not a fidelity to an unjust arrangement. The Book protects the testator's intention and it corrects his injustice, and the reader is shown how the legislation balances the two.

The next sentence permits a settlement when the testator’s arrangement is unjust, and the two verses form a pair in which the rule and its exception are stated. The community’s scholars read the passage as a whole and derived from it the rules that govern the correction of an improper bequest. What the reader receives is the account of a law that protects a person’s final instructions while refusing to enforce them when they work an injustice, and the community’s practice has preserved both sides of the arrangement.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes that a will is to be carried out as it was made, that the responsibility for altering it belongs to the one who alters it, and that the matter is heard and known. The reader is given a rule about a document that has become the property of those who survive the person who wrote it, and the passage continues with the case in which the document itself is unjust.
