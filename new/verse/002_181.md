## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:181

> But whoever changes the will after hearing it, the blame will only be on those who made the change. Indeed, Allah is AllHearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sin Transferred**

The verse legislates for the moment after the deathbed: the will has been made, heard, and recorded, and the testator is gone. If anyone now alters it, the sin of the alteration falls *only* on those who altered — the wording is exclusive and deliberate. The transfer answers a question the situation itself raises: when a bequest is changed, whose account carries the wrong? Not the dead man's — his act was completed in fairness or in its absence, and the verse of exception that follows (2:182) shows the law's concern for his possible fault separately. Not the heirs who suffer from the change — they are its objects, not its agents. The sin attaches to the changer and stays there, undistributed: the executors who adjusted a figure, the witnesses who "remembered" different terms, the relatives who intercepted a bequest before it could operate. Al-Qurṭubī's reading of the verse establishes the rule's shape: a bequest validly made cannot be altered — the dead man's word acquires, at his death, a legal inviolability, and the living hands that touch it take on the whole of the touching's guilt.

**Hearing as the Trigger of Responsibility**

The condition in the verse — *after hearing it* — is not incidental but constitutive. The alteration condemned is the knowing one: the changer heard the will, from the testator's mouth or the witnesses' attestation, and then changed what he had heard. Hearing creates custody; custody creates the duty of fidelity; the change breaches both. The same structure governs the bequest's attestation in the sūrah of the Table, where two just men are to be called as witnesses when death approaches (5:106) — the institution exists precisely so that the hearing is official, the will is not a private whisper, and any later alteration is provably against a heard text. The verse thereby protects the dying in the only way law can protect the dead: by deputizing the living. The witnesses, the executors, everyone within earshot of the bequest become its custodians, and the sin-clause tells them that custody is not honorary. What was heard must be delivered; the delivery is now their act, and its corruption is their sin alone.

**What the Verse Does Not Forbid**

The prohibition has a boundary the jurists drew carefully, and the boundary completes the law rather than weakening it. Al-Qurṭubī distinguished altering a valid bequest from correcting an invalid one: a bequest is not protected by this verse when it was itself unlawful, and such a bequest can be cancelled or modified without incurring the sin. The two examples he gives define the category — a bequest exceeding the third is reduced to the third; a bequest directing something forbidden, such as the gifting of wine, is nullified. The principle underneath is that the verse guards the testator's *lawful* word, not his wordlessness before the law: a will is inviolable inside the boundaries the previous verse set (the third, the non-heir, the fairness), and outside them it carries no protection, because the dead have no right to command sin. This reading is what connects 2:181 to its successor: the changer condemned here alters a bequest against the heirs' rights for his own ends, while the intervener excused there alters a bequest that was itself engineered against those rights. Same physical act — changing the will — opposite moral valence, distinguished entirely by which direction justice runs.

**All-Hearing of the Quiet Crime**

The verse closes with the two names, and their fit to the case is exact. The bequest is among the quietest of legal events: spoken on a deathbed, heard by two or three, committed to memory or a private page, and then tested at a moment when its author cannot testify. The alteration is quieter still — a number adjusted, a name omitted, a document lost, a witness persuaded — performed precisely where human oversight is thinnest, since the one person who could say *that is not what I said* is beyond saying anything. Against that structural invisibility the verse stations the two divine attributes: Allah is All-Hearing — He heard the will as it was spoken, in the room, in its exact words; All-Knowing — He knows the change, the changer, the motive, and the difference between the heard text and the delivered one. The names do the evidentiary work the case cannot otherwise have: the crime of altering a bequest is almost perfectly concealable from people and perfectly unconcealable from God, so the verse's deterrence is the same one the concealment-passages used (2:174) — the surveillance that survives every witness's death. The changer heard; God heard the changer. The sin is on those who alter, and the recording of it is on the One who does not die when the testator does.

**The Dead Man's Word as Continuing Act**

Underlying the verse is a legal-moral conception worth naming: the bequest is treated as the testator's act still in progress. Islamic law generally holds that a person's obligations survive him — debts are paid from the estate, wrongs are answered from what he left, the hajj owed may be performed on his behalf — and the will is the instrument by which the dying extend their agency past death. To alter the will is therefore not to adjust a document but to intercept a person: the dead man is still speaking through it, still giving, still discharging the duties of fairness the previous verse imposed, and the changer's hand falls on the speech itself. This is why the sin is charged so exclusively and so heavily — it is a wrong done to one who cannot complain, in the name of one who cannot consent, and it converts the community's most vulnerable legal moment, the interval between the deathbed and the distribution, into a test of exactly the virtue the surrounding verses keep naming: the mindfulness of God in transactions no one else can audit. The verse's economy of blame — *only on those who alter* — is finally a protection for everyone else in the room: the heirs need not fear the will's corruption, the witnesses know their attestation binds someone, and the dead keep the one power the law can still give them, a word that stands.
