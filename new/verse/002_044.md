## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:44

> Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Addressed to Teachers**

The verse is built as a question, and the question is aimed at people whose role was to instruct others. The Arabic verb describes ordering and commending something to another person, and the sentence is not about private devotion but about the public work of telling others how to live. The Book is asking how a person can recommend a good and then not perform it.

That situation is not unique to one generation. Any community that has a text, a school and an expectation that the learned will guide the unlearned will contain people whose instructions outrun their conduct. The sūrah places the question in the middle of its address to a community with a long tradition of scholarship, and it does so without softening the terms.

**Righteousness as a Word Wider Than Ritual**

The word being preached is rendered as righteousness, and the Book gives the term its fullest definition elsewhere in the same sūrah: *“Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets”* (2:177), after which the verse lists giving to relatives, orphans, travellers and beggars, establishing prayer, paying alms, keeping promises, and patience in hardship.

The definition is worth keeping in mind when reading this verse, because it means the standard being preached and neglected is not a small one. Righteousness in the Book covers belief, generosity, worship, honesty and endurance together. A person who urges that whole on others while practising part of it has not merely failed at a detail; he has separated a single word into a message for his listeners and an exemption for himself.

**The Charge Is Not Ignorance**

The verse adds a clause that closes the obvious excuse: they read the Scripture. Whatever the source of their conduct, it is not a lack of information. The Book is explicit about this pattern elsewhere, describing a community that was well versed in its teachings and still departed from them, and asking whether a covenant had not been taken from them in the Scripture (7:169).

The sūrah's accusation is therefore about knowledge that has failed to become conduct. That is a different matter from a person who does not know what is required, and the Book holds the two cases far apart. Its harshest language is reserved for those whose position came from knowing, since their influence multiplied whatever example they set.

**"Do You Not Understand?"**

The closing question appeals to the faculty the Book elsewhere treats as the seat of accountability. It is not asking whether the audience has mastered a text; it is asking whether they see the contradiction in what they are doing. The Book uses this kind of question repeatedly, and its function is always to place the listener inside the judgement rather than outside it.

The implication is that the absurdity is available to reason and does not require revelation to detect. Anyone can notice that a rule he presses on others also applies to him, and the Book treats the failure to notice as a failure of the understanding rather than of the memory. The question is therefore not rhetorical abuse; it assumes the audience is able to answer it correctly and is not doing so.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

Nothing in the sentence forbids instruction. The Book commands that knowledge be conveyed and warns against concealing it, and the preceding verses of this sūrah fault this same community for hiding what they were given. The verse rebukes a gap between teaching and conduct, not the act of teaching, and it leaves the duty to instruct in place.

That distinction matters for anyone who reads the verse as a reason to fall silent. The Book's position is that the message should continue to be delivered and that the messenger should be the first to comply with it. The question in this verse is addressed to a teacher mid-lecture; it does not stop the lecture, it corrects the situation of the person giving it.

**A Measure That Turns Back on the Reader**

The verse is written in the second person plural, and it is addressed to a specific community in a specific place. Nothing about it restricts the scope of the correction, and the sūrah's later treatment of the new community shows why: the Book warns the believers against the same pattern, and it does so in a passage that rebukes those who say what they do not do. The correction is a permanent feature of the Book's teaching about knowledge.

A reader who finishes the sentence and applies it only to the people being addressed has not read it. The Book's habit is to record a fault of one community and then hold every subsequent community to the same standard, and this verse is placed near the beginning of the longest such record in the Qur'an. The question remains open to everyone who has ever urged a good on someone else, and the Book's next sentence supplies the remedy rather than leaving the reader to find one.

**The Order of the Sūrah's Correction**

The correction is not delivered as an accusation followed by a threat. The sūrah gives the community three commands in the previous verse, then asks a question about the gap between teaching and conduct, and then immediately supplies the remedy in the next: seeking help through patience and prayer. The instruction, the diagnosis and the treatment arrive in that order.

That arrangement is worth noting for anyone reading the passage as a polemic. The Book does not use the community's failure as a reason to set them aside. It continues to speak to them as people whose speech about righteousness was right, and it treats the problem as one of practice. The question in this verse and the counsel that follows are addressed to the same audience in the same breath, and the sūrah will go on addressing them for another fifty verses.
