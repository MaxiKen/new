## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:44

> Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is a Verdict**

The verse arrives as interrogation: *a-taʾmurūna al-nāsa bi-l-birr wa-tansawna anfusakum* — do you command people to righteousness and forget yourselves? The Arabic's *a-tansawna* literally says *forget* — but the forgetting named is not memory's failure; it is the self-exemption of the preacher who writes himself out of his own sermon. The interrogative form is the Qur'an's idiom of rebuke: a question whose answer is already on the record, asked so the accused must hear himself pronounce it. And the addressees are identified with precision by the clause that follows — *and you read the Scripture* — the scholars of Madina, readers of Torah, commanders of *birr* to their congregations and to the Arabs around them, exempters of themselves from the very good they dispensed. The verse thus continues the audit begun at verse 41: there the charges were denial and selling; here the charge is inconsistency — the gap between the mouth and the hand, which is the scholar's characteristic sin.

**Birr: The Whole Good, Not a Part of It**

The object of the preaching is *birr* — one of the Qur'an's broadest moral terms, covering piety, righteousness, goodness, and virtue in a single word. Al-Baqarah itself defines its range later: righteousness as belief in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets, joined to charity from beloved wealth, the establishment of prayer, the payment of zakāh, the keeping of pledges, and patience in hardship (2:177). A people commanded to *birr* are commanded to the whole structure of the good life, not to a ritual minimum. That breadth sharpens the rebuke: these men were not failing one item on a list they taught but the entire thing they stood for professionally. Their office was the transmission of *birr*; their practice, the verse states flatly, left themselves out of the transmission. The word choice also connects the charge to the covenant of verses 40–43 — belief, prayer, zakāh, bowing with the bowers — which is *birr* itemized. The scholars who taught its items and skipped its practice are condemned by the syllabus they themselves handed out.

**The Double Damage of the Preacher's Fall**

The tradition took this verse with total seriousness because it identifies a damage beyond the personal. The sinning preacher does not merely leave one good undone; he discredits the good itself. His community watches righteousness recommended and not performed, and draws the only available conclusion — that the recommendation is professional talk, that the good is for audiences. Every subsequent sinner he addresses has his example to cite. The Prophet ﷺ supplied the image in a ḥadīth of Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim: a man is brought on the Day of Judgment and thrown into the Fire, his intestines pouring out, circling in it as a donkey circles a millstone; the Fire's residents gather and ask him — did you not use to enjoin good and forbid evil? He answers that he enjoined good and did not do it, forbade evil and did it (Muslim 2989a). The mill-donkey circuit is the verse's logic rendered visible: the preacher who turned the good outward while withholding it from himself ends turning inside the consequence. And the ḥadīth's own occasion refines the application: Usāma ibn Zayd quoted it when asked why he did not rebuke ʿUthmān openly — a reminder that the warning governs the *how* of speaking as much as the *whether*.

**Does the Sinning Scholar Still Speak? The Tradition's Answer**

Verse 44 raises the practical question every reader of it eventually asks: if preaching while failing is this dangerous, is silence safer? The tradition's answer is a firm no, and it rests on a distinction the verse itself implies. Enjoining good is one obligation; doing the good is another; failing the second does not dissolve the first — a man who abandons both has compounded two failures into one career. The scholars put it as arithmetic: the sinner-preacher sins twice and obeys once; the silent sinner sins twice and obeys zero times. The ḥadīth of the mill-donkey punishes the gap, not the speaking; nothing in it or in the verse licenses the hypocrite's comfort — *I will keep quiet until I am perfect*. The verse's real target is the direction of the exemption: the preacher whose sermon travels everywhere except home. Islamic moral instruction built its remedy accordingly — begin with yourself, then those nearest you — an order that keeps the tongue honest by making the hand its first audience.

**“You Read the Scripture”: Knowledge as the Aggravator**

The circumstantial clause *wa-antum tat-lūna al-kitāb* does the same work here that *knowingly* did in verse 42: it converts the charge from error to contempt. These are readers — reciters, students, transmitters — for whom the Scripture is not a rumor but a text handled daily. And the text they handle contains its own version of this rebuke: the Torah's prophets denounced precisely this priesthood, men who taught the law and did not keep it. The clause therefore accomplishes two things at once. It removes the plea of ignorance — no one can claim the standard was unstated to a professional reciter of the standard. And it indicts the reading itself: a scripture read daily and disobeyed knowingly is not being read but used, its recitation functioning as credential rather than command. The sūrah's pattern holds across its charges against this community — denial despite knowledge (41), mixing despite knowledge (42), self-exemption despite recitation (44) — until knowledge itself emerges as the verse's central asset and central liability: it multiplies the weight of every act performed or omitted under it.

**“Will You Not Reason?” and the Sūrah's Audit of ʿAql**

The closing question — *a-fa-lā taʿqilūn*, will you then not use your reason? — files this failure under irrationality. The Qur'an does not treat the preacher's inconsistency as weakness alone but as a collapse of basic inference: the man who commands a thing declares it good; declaring it good states that it benefits its doer; the one doer guaranteed present in every sermon's audience is the preacher. To exempt oneself from one's own conclusion is to fail the simplest consistency check the mind can run — which is why the sūrah keeps summoning *ʿaql* at exactly these junctures (2:44, 2:73, 2:75, 2:164, 2:171, 2:242). The interrogative particles stacked at the head — *a-fa-lā* — compress the rebuke: is it not, then, the case that you reason? The verse's portrait of sin is thus intellectual as well as moral: self-exemption is a bad argument the exempter would never accept from another, and the audit of reason demanded here is the audit every reader owes the gap between his own advice and his own conduct. Sūrah al-Ṣaff will level the same rebuke at the believers themselves — saying what one does not do is most hateful in Allah's sight (61:2–3) — universalizing what began here as a charge against scholars.
