## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:44

> Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Completes the Rebuke**

“Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?” (2:44). A-taʾmurūna al-nāsa bil-birr… The sūrah, after ordering “do not mix truth” (2:42) and “establish prayer… bow with those who bow” (2:43), turns the command back upon the commander. To enjoin birr — righteousness, piety, loyalty, kind obedience — while forgetting the self, while reciting the Book, is presented as incomprehension: a-fa-lā taʿqilūn — will you not understand?

The rebuke is not for enjoining. Enjoining piety is praised elsewhere (2:177; 2:189; 3:92; 3:193; 76:5; 82:13). It is for the gap between lip and limb: commanding what the commander abandons. The Book read becomes witness against the reader when the reader teaches what he does not do.

**What Birr Means Here**

Birr carries the source’s range: goodness, kindness, sincerity, obedience to God. In 2:177 the Qur’an will define birr not as turning faces east or west but as faith, charity to kin and needy, fidelity to covenant, and steadfastness — a whole life’s piety, not a slogan. In 2:44 the term may include prayer and alms just commanded (2:43), or acknowledgement of the Prophet’s mission whose foretelling they find written (7:157), or the earlier warning to be true to the covenant that predicted his coming. The breadth is intentional: whatever qualifies as birr that you commend to others, you are bound to first.

**“While You Recite the Book” — Knowledge as Aggravation**

“although you read the Scripture” — wa antum tatlūna al-kitāb (2:44). Tatlūna can mean recite, study, follow in recitation. The source notes that recite here may mean study (Ṭ). In either nuance, reading aggravates. To read and then forget is worse than not to have read, because reading claims access. The crime of mixing (2:42) and hiding while knowing (2:42) is now made personal: you hide from yourself what you expose to others.

The verse therefore identifies a specific deformation of the learned: to be able to phrase piety for others, to enjoy the standing that comes from phrasing it, while excusing the self under familiarity. The standing is precisely what 2:41 called “a paltry price” — the small gain that buys concealment.

**“Do You Not Understand?” — Intellect as Moral Sight**

A-fa-lā taʿqilūn (2:44) appeals to ʿaql not as cleverness but as tying, restraining, comprehension that holds together word and deed. To teach birr and abandon it is, in Qur’anic register, unintelligent — not because the teacher is dull, but because understanding would have bound mouth to foot. The question invites the teachers to think again about their own standing before the verses they carry.

**Why This Verse Follows Prayer and Alms**

The order 2:42 → 2:43 → 2:44 is deliberate: do not confound or conceal, then pray, give, and bow together, then — do you command birr and forget yourselves while reciting? The command to practice is placed between the diagnosis of the intellect’s failure (mix/hide) and the diagnosis of the will’s failure (say/do gap). Ritual without self-inclusion is exposed; exhortation without self-inclusion is condemned.

**A Prophetic Parallel, Not a Pretext for Silence**

A common misapplication of this verse is to silence enjoining unless the enjoinder is already perfect. The sūrah does not teach that. It teaches to enjoin and to include the self, not to cease enjoining until flawless. The remedy is not quietism but alignment: let the tongue that says “be righteous” be the first to hear itself.

**Living the Correction**

- Let recitation precede injunction. Read, then say, then do — in that order — so the Book judges the speaker before the audience does.
- Audit birr by 2:177: does your birr include faith, giving from love, promise-keeping, and steadfastness, or is it only exhortation?
- Keep the question as daily check: before posting, publishing, or preaching, ask the verse’s question verbatim to the self — “Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?” (2:44) — and let the answer change the next act, not only the next sentence.

**What Not to Extrapolate**

The verse addresses, in its first occasion, learned Jews of Madinah who enjoined others — including the Arabs — to follow a promised prophet or to live by Torah, yet delayed themselves. Its principle travels to every teacher, but it does not equate every shortfall with hypocrisy, nor does it warrant public shaming of teachers for private lapses where counsel is due. What is firm is the standard: enjoining birr binds the enjoinder first, especially when he recites the Book (2:44), and understanding is shown by closing that gap.
