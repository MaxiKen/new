## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:14

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "Will I take any guardian other than Allah, the Originator of the heavens and the earth, Who provides for all and is not in need of provision?" Say, "I have been commanded to be the first to submit and not be one of the polytheists."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Compromise That Was Being Proposed**

The verse answers a political offer rather than a philosophical question. The Makkans wanted the Prophet to acknowledge their deities, in exchange for their acknowledgement of his, and the answer comes back as a question that has already decided itself: will I take any guardian other than Allah? The Arabic word behind guardian is *walī*, the one who takes charge of a person's affairs and defends him, and the whole force of the sentence lies in asking whether such a protector is available anywhere else.

A guardian is measured by what he can do when his client is in danger. The sūrah's audience had spent generations treating stones as protectors, and the question quietly asks them what a stone does on a battlefield or in a famine. The Book records the same test elsewhere: how can you worship, besides Allah, those who can neither harm nor benefit you (5:76)?

**Fāṭir: The Name That Means "Splitter"**

The translation renders the title Originator, and the Arabic behind it is *fāṭir*, from a root meaning to split or cleave. In the Qur'an the word appears inside one standing phrase, Originator of the heavens and the earth, and it is applied to God in the prayers of Joseph (12:101), in the preaching of the earlier messengers (14:10), in the opening of the sūrah of the angels (35:1), in a supplication (39:46), and in a declaration of God's uniqueness (42:11). The verbal form of the same root is used for the forming of human beings, and its noun gives the word *fiṭrah*, the natural disposition God instilled in people and which the verse on upright faith says must not be altered (30:30).

The name therefore carries a picture rather than a title: the heavens and the earth were opened out from something closed, in the way a seed splits and a bud separates. Whoever did that does not need a partner for maintenance.

**The God Who Feeds and Is Not Fed**

The second clause is economic, and it is aimed straight at the cult of Makkah. God provides for all and needs no provision from anyone. The Book states it as a first-person claim: I seek no provision from them, nor do I need them to feed Me (51:57). That single sentence removes the entire commercial logic of sacrifice, in which the deity is sustained by what is brought to the shrine and the worshipper expects the arrangement to be honoured in return.

The same argument is turned against the divinisation of holy figures. The Messiah and his mother both ate food, the Book observes, as a sign that neither could have been a god (5:75). Appetite is the mark of a creature. A deity who must be fed has worshippers and rivals at the same time, since someone else can always bring a better offering.

**A God With a Kitchen Is Not a God**

Makkan worship was a farming and trading arrangement as much as a theology. Offerings of grain and livestock were diverted to the idols, and the sūrah later discusses the shares people assigned to God and to their deities (6:136). The verse's clause about provision attacks the practice at its base: the parties who needed the sacrifice were the ones receiving it, not the one being worshipped.

Set beside the earlier clause about splitting open the heavens and the earth, the contrast is severe. The Creator of everything is described as needing nothing, while the objects of Makkan devotion were represented by statues that had to be anointed and fed by the priests who kept them. That is the whole case, and the verse makes it in one line about dinner.

**"The First to Submit"**

Having refused the compromise, the Prophet is told to declare his own position: he has been commanded to be the first of those who submit. Interpreters read the word in three ways — first among the people of his own time to submit, first in rank among the submitters, or the one uniquely singled out to receive revelation. Two other passages carry the same claim in his mouth: he is commanded to be the first of those who submit (39:12), and he is the first to submit (6:163).

Each reading keeps the sense of priority in a sequence rather than superiority in a hierarchy. Abraham, raised among idolaters, is the model of the same submission (16:120), and the faith being claimed here is presented as the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not a polytheist (6:161).

**The Command Not to Be Among the Polytheists**

The verse closes with a warning addressed directly to the Prophet: and not be one of the polytheists. That the Book warns its own messenger is not a slight; it is the clearest possible statement that the prohibition is universal and that no station is exempt from it. The same phrasing recurs across the sūrah, attached to Abraham (6:79), to the Prophet's declaration of faith (6:161), and to his final profession (6:163).

Anyone who heard the demand for compromise should notice what the answer actually says. The Prophet is not described as too important to associate partners with God; he is described as commanded, like every other submitter, to stand outside that category entirely. The position he takes is not a privilege he owns but an instruction he carries. In a city that negotiated its alliances by exchanging acknowledgements, the refusal to trade a single word of testimony is the whole answer, and it is delivered as obedience rather than as pride.
