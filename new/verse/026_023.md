## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:23

> Pharaoh asked, “And what is ‘the Lord of all worlds’?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pharaoh Turns a Universal Claim Into a Challenge**

Pharaoh's question comes after Moses has invoked the Lord of all worlds. On the surface, it asks for clarification. In context, it also tests whether the messenger's Lord can be made to appear vague, distant, or irrelevant to Egyptian power. The title has already placed Pharaoh under a higher authority, so the question is not harmless curiosity. It is a challenge to the source of the command that he release the Israelites.

The expression Rabb al-ʿālamīn carries more than the idea of a creator who began the universe and then withdrew. Rabb includes the one who owns, sustains, orders, and brings a thing toward its proper state. The worlds are all created realms, not only one nation or one court. Pharaoh asks what this title means because accepting its scope would unsettle his own claim to rule.

**The Question May Be Sincere in Form but Hostile in Purpose**

Exegetes differed over Pharaoh's intention. Some treated the question as a request for the essence or exact nature of God. Others held that Pharaoh already understood enough to know that Moses was challenging his authority, and that he asked only to mock or provoke. The immediate setting supports the second reading more strongly. A ruler who presents himself as supreme is not approaching the title as a humble seeker; he is putting the messenger on trial before his court.

That judgment does not require claiming that Pharaoh had no knowledge of God at all. A person can recognize a claim's force and still resist its authority. Questions can seek understanding, or they can delay obedience by demanding a definition that the questioner has no intention of accepting. Moses' mission exposes the difference between the sound of inquiry and the purpose behind it.

**Rabb Is a Relational Title**

The question also reveals how Pharaoh wants God to be imagined. If the Lord is treated as an abstract object that must be captured in a formula before He can be obeyed, the ruler can keep control while the debate continues. Rabb points in another direction. It names the One whose relation to creation is shown through sustaining, ordering, and judging it. Knowledge of God begins with recognizing what He does and what His lordship requires, not with pretending that the human mind can contain His essence.

This is why the title is especially threatening to tyranny. A ruler can claim mastery over a limited territory, but he cannot be lord in the full sense. His power is received, bounded, and temporary. The title Lord of all worlds removes the possibility that Pharaoh's realm is a closed moral universe in which his word is final.

**A Demand for Definition Can Hide a Refusal to Submit**

There is a legitimate place for questions about divine names and attributes. Revelation itself teaches people how to speak about God and how to avoid comparing Him with creation. But a demand for exhaustive definition can become a tactic for postponing the obvious. Pharaoh does not ask what the title changes in his conduct. He asks what it is as if the messenger must satisfy a hostile court before the court will consider freeing the enslaved.

The difference between inquiry and evasion lies in what follows the answer. A sincere questioner is willing to revise his position when the answer is sound. An evasive questioner keeps moving the standard until no answer can count. The scene prepares us to watch Pharaoh test each description of God's lordship, not because none is meaningful, but because every meaningful description threatens his pretended supremacy.

**The Worlds Are Larger Than Pharaoh's Audience**

The plural worlds also prevents the question from being reduced to a dispute about Egyptian religion alone. God is Lord of all created orders, peoples, and places. His authority does not depend on whether one court recognizes it. Moses is not introducing a local deity who competes with Pharaoh's patron gods. He is announcing the One whose lordship includes every power that claims to be independent.

That universal scope gives the oppressed hope. If God were only the deity of a favored court, the Israelites would remain trapped inside Pharaoh's story. The title says that their lives are visible to a Lord beyond the court and that their enslavement can be judged by an authority Pharaoh cannot absorb into his system. Universal lordship becomes good news when the local ruler has made himself the measure of everything.

**The Question Reveals a Crisis of Authority**

Pharaoh asks what the title means because the title has already had a practical effect. Moses has not merely offered a theory of God; he has carried a command that threatens the structure of power. The ruler must either recognize a superior authority or treat the messenger as dangerous. The question is therefore part of a crisis: who has the right to command Pharaoh?

Moses' answer will describe divine lordship through acts of creation and governance rather than by claiming to map God's essence. That method respects the limit of human speech while giving Pharaoh enough to understand the challenge. The verse leaves the question suspended so that its tension can be felt. A ruler who asks about the Lord of all worlds is being asked, in turn, whether he knows that he is not the lord of even one world.

**The Question Is Also a Test of Pharaoh's Honesty**

A sincere question would place the ruler in the position of a learner. Pharaoh's style keeps him in the position of a judge. He asks Moses to define the authority that has commanded him, yet he does not first admit that his own authority might be answerable. The form of the question therefore tests whether Pharaoh will let an answer change him.

That test remains useful whenever a powerful person demands explanations from a weaker one. Questions can be a path to truth, or they can be a way to keep the weaker person speaking while the powerful person retains the right to dismiss every answer. Moses' mission will not be validated by Pharaoh's willingness to listen. The Lord's claim stands before the question, and the ruler's response will reveal what he intends to do with the answer he requests.
