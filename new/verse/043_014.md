## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:14

> **And surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return.” Allah’s Daughters?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Journey Ends Where It Began**

The rider's prayer, begun in the previous verse, closes with a clause about a different journey. Wa-innā ilā rabbinā la-munqalibūn: and truly to our Lord we are returning. The person who has just mounted an animal to travel across the earth ends his prayer by naming the destination of every traveller. The sequence is not accidental. The exegetes, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī, read the mention of return here as a deliberate turn from the small journey to the large one. A man setting out on a road is in a position to remember that his whole life is a road, and that its end is fixed. The Qurʾān makes a similar move in 2:197, where instructions about provisioning for the pilgrimage end with the reminder that the best provision is God-consciousness, so that the preparation of a saddlebag becomes an image of the preparation of a soul. Here the mounting of an animal becomes an image of setting out toward God. The prayer therefore asks the rider to hold two journeys in mind at once, and to let the one he can see remind him of the one he cannot.

**Two Senses of Munqalibūn**

The word munqalibūn is the active participle of inqalaba, to turn over, to turn back, to return. The exegetes offer two readings of what the turning means. On the first, favoured in the source tradition and paralleled in 7:125, the turning is a turning in prayer and devotion: to our Lord we turn, meaning we direct ourselves to Him. On the second, held by al-Ājurrī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī, the turning is the return to God after death: to our Lord we shall go back, in the Hereafter. The second is stronger, for two reasons. The particle ilā, "to", with a verb of motion naturally means arrival at a destination rather than orientation toward it, and the emphatic construction, innā with the lām on the predicate, is the form the Qurʾān uses for the certainty of resurrection, as in innā ilayhi rājiʿūn (2:156). The two readings are not far apart in effect, since one who knows he will return to God turns to Him now. But the verse is about the end of the road, and its grammar says so.

**Why the Return Follows the Ride**

Al-Rāzī gives a practical reason for the placement of this clause after the mention of riding. Every journey carries risk. A man who mounts a camel or boards a ship does not know that he will arrive, and the Arabs who crossed deserts and seas knew this better than most. To say "to our Lord we are returning" at the moment of departure is to acknowledge that the safe completion of the journey is not in one's own hands, and that if it is not completed, the traveller has still arrived somewhere. The clause is therefore both a confession and a comfort. It admits that the road may end in death, and it names death as a return rather than a loss. The Prophet ﷺ recited this clause as part of his travel prayer in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1342, and the same report records that on the way home he added: returning, repenting, worshipping, and praising our Lord. The return from a journey was made to echo the return to God, so that every homecoming rehearsed the last one.

**The Participle That States a Fact**

The verse does not use a verb, "we shall return", but a participle, "we are returners", munqalibūn. Arabic uses the active participle to describe a settled state or a fixed attribute rather than an action at a moment. The difference is small in translation and large in sense. A verb would say that at some future point we will go back. The participle says that being on the way back is what we are, now and always. From the Qurʾān's point of view, human beings are not waiting to begin their return to God; they are in the middle of it, whether or not they notice. Every hour moves them closer, and the choice available to them is not whether to travel but in what condition to arrive. The rider on the camel is a picture of this. He is moving whether he thinks about the destination or not. The prayer asks him to think about it. The emphatic la before the participle, la-munqalibūn, adds certainty to the fact: truly, without doubt, we are on our way back.

**Confession Against the Idolaters' Denial**

The clause is placed in the mouth of the rider as the natural end of gratitude, but it is also the sūrah's answer to a denial. The Quraysh admitted in verse 9 that God made the heavens and the earth, and the verses since have listed His works. What they denied was the return, the resurrection, the accounting. The prayer of verses 13 and 14 models the response the sūrah wants from them: to see the animal beneath them as subjected by God, to admit that they could not have subjected it themselves, and to conclude that the God who arranged their journeys will receive them at the end of the last one. The person who says this prayer honestly has already conceded the resurrection, because he has said that his Lord is his destination. The Quraysh said the first half of the prayer, in effect, every time they praised God for a safe arrival. The verse asks them to say the second half. The passage that follows shows what they said instead, and it begins with what they attributed to the God whose blessings they had just been reminded of.
