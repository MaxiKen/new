## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:42

> If they deny you ˹O Prophet˺, so did the people of Noah before them, as well as ˹the tribes of˺ ’Ȃd and Thamûd,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial Is Presented as the Pattern, Not the Emergency**

*Wa-in yukadhdhibūka fa-qad kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin* — and if they deny you, the people of Noah denied before them.

The conditional is doing something specific. It does not say "when they deny you" and it does not say "since they have denied you." It says *if*, and the apodosis is not a threat but a precedent. The verse's first move is to reclassify the Prophet's situation: this is not a crisis, it is the norm.

That is the Qur'an's most consistent consolation to its messenger, and it appears in the same form elsewhere: *“If they deny you, so did those before them. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures”* (35:25). The point is never that rejection is easy. It is that rejection is what happens to messengers, and that a messenger who is being rejected is not thereby failing.

Sūrah al-Anʿām adds what the precedent cost and what it produced: *“Indeed, messengers before you were rejected but patiently endured rejection and persecution until Our help came to them. And Allah's promise ˹to help˺ is never broken”* (6:34). Rejection, endurance, help — the three are presented as a sequence with the middle term supplied by the messenger. The consolation is therefore not that denial is unreal but that it is the first term of a completed pattern, and the help at the end of it is promised rather than merely hoped for.

**The List That Follows Is Arabian**

The three names given here — Noah, ʿĀd and Thamūd — are the three the Qur'an most often uses as the opening of this catalogue.

The choice is rhetorical rather than chronological. ʿĀd and Thamūd were the Arabian peoples whose destruction was part of the peninsula's inherited memory; their ruined settlements were places a traveller could point at. By putting them beside Noah, whose flood was known through the scriptural traditions of the region, the verse builds a list that its first audience could not dismiss as foreign folklore.

Sūrah Ibrāhīm uses the same opening for the same purpose: *“Have you not ˹already˺ received the stories of those who were before you: the people of Noah, ’Âd, Thamûd, and those after them? Only Allah knows how many they were”* (14:9).

Thamūd's dwellings were the most visible item on the route the audience travelled: *“Indeed, the residents of the Stone Valley also denied the messengers”* (15:80). The Stone Valley — al-Ḥijr, the carved facades the Syrian caravans passed — is named as a place rather than only a people, which is what makes the list unanswerable for its first hearers. Noah belongs to transmitted memory; ʿĀd and Thamūd belong to the road the merchants walked twice a year, with the houses of the destroyed still standing in the rock.

**A Catalogue With No Verbs of Its Own**

Grammatically, verses 42, 43 and 44 are one sentence. Verse 42 opens it with the denial of the people of Noah and the two Arabian tribes; verse 43 continues the list; verse 44 closes it with Midian and Moses, and only then does the sentence's main verb arrive: *I granted them respite, then I seized them.*

The effect of the suspended verb is suspense. Nine communities are named in three verses with nothing happening to any of them, and then the action arrives once, at the end, for all of them together. A reader hears the list accumulating and waits for what is going to be said about it.

The apodosis carries its own quiet emphasis: *fa-qad kadhdhabat* — so indeed they denied. The particle *qad* with the past tense presents the denial as an established fact rather than a mere report, the way a court recites what is no longer in dispute. The verse is not informing the Prophet that Noah's people denied him; it is entering the fact into the record as settled, so that the present denial can be measured against it rather than treated as unprecedented.

**What Was Denied Was a Messenger**

The verb throughout is *kadhdhaba*, to declare someone a liar — the verb of refusing a person rather than refusing a proposition.

This matters for how the catalogue functions. The parallel is not between the Makkans' theology and the theology of ʿĀd; the parallel is between the Makkans' treatment of their messenger and the treatment of Hūd, Ṣāliḥ and Shuʿayb. Sūrah Hūd records what each of them said, and the message is identical in every case: *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him’”* (11:50).

The offence, in other words, is not rejecting an unfamiliar doctrine. It is calling your brother a liar when he tells you something true.

Noah's sentence is recorded in the same words Hūd used: *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah — you have no other god except Him’”* (7:59). The first messenger on the list and the Arabian ones spoke one sentence with different names attached, which is what makes the catalogue a single charge rather than nine different ones. What was denied in every case was the same claim from a brother's mouth — and what the Makkans were denying was that same sentence again.

**Why the Prophet Is Told This Now**

The placement of the catalogue matters. It follows the permission to fight, and that is the moment at which a community might reasonably conclude that it has been vindicated and that the reckoning is about to begin.

Verses 42 to 48 are the correction to that expectation. The pattern in history is not that the truthful are vindicated quickly; it is that the deniers are given time and then seized. The Prophet is being told, before he fights his first battle, that the schedule is not his and that the delay is not a sign of anything.

The delay is then given its universal form, and the verse that states it removes the last comfort of the denier: *“If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term”* (16:61). The silence the Makkans were enjoying was not a verdict in their favour but the same deferral extended to everyone, including the believers. To be inside the interval is not to be acquitted; it is to be given an appointed term, which expires whether or not anyone is watching the clock.

**Why the Flood Is the Right Place to Start**

Noah's people are the one community on this list that left no ruins to look at. ʿĀd, Thamūd, Midian and Lot's towns are all places whose remains could be pointed at; the flood left a world drowned and a single boat on a mountain.

That makes the first item on the catalogue the one that requires the most from a reader. Nothing in the physical landscape testifies to it. The listener must accept it because the revelation says so, or because the story has been carried by the communities of the region for centuries.

The ordering is therefore an argument about the kind of evidence being offered. The catalogue does not rest on archaeology — it opens with an item that archaeology cannot supply — and it closes with the ruins, which are corroboration rather than foundation. What the sūrah is asking its audience to accept is a pattern, and the pattern does not depend on how many of its instances can still be visited.

**Noah's People First**

There is a reason Noah heads the list. His people were the ones who were given the longest period of warning in the Qur'an's account and the most complete destruction, and his story is the sūrahs' standing example of a messenger who kept calling while nobody listened.

Beginning with him sets the scale of the patience being asked for. The Prophet at Makkah had been preaching for a little over a decade. The catalogue opens with a man who preached for far longer, to a people who were removed from the earth entirely, and who kept preaching while the boat was being built in a place with no water in sight.

It also sets the scale of the outcome. Every community on this list comes to an end; the first one on it comes to the most complete end available in the record, and the sūrah wants its audience to measure the others against that rather than against their own impatience.
