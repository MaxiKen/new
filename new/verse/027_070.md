## Sūrah an-Naml 27:70

> Do not grieve for them, nor be distressed by their schemes.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Turns to the Messenger's Own State**

After verses of argument directed at the opposition, this one is addressed to the man delivering it, and it concerns how he feels. Do not grieve over them, and do not be constricted by what they plot.

The placement of it says something about what had been happening in Mecca. A command not to grieve is issued to someone who is grieving, and the Qur'an gives the Prophet this instruction repeatedly across several chapters. The pattern indicates a persistent condition rather than a passing mood. He was distressed by the refusal of his own people, and the Book addresses that distress directly rather than treating it as something unworthy of mention in scripture.

**Grief for Them, Not About Them**

The Arabic is *lā taḥzan ʿalayhim* — do not grieve *over* them. The preposition matters. He is not being told to stop being upset by their behaviour; he is being told to stop mourning for them.

That is a different emotion and a more specific one. The men mocking him were his kin, his neighbours, his clan. He had grown up among them and knew what was coming to people who persisted, having just recited the fate of Thamūd and Lūṭ's people. What he is described as feeling is the sorrow of someone watching people he cares about walk toward something. The Qur'an elsewhere puts this in stronger terms, telling him he might destroy himself with grief over their refusal to believe — an image of a man being consumed by anxiety about others. The instruction here is a limit on that, not a rebuke for having it.

**"Distressed" Means Constricted**

The second half uses *ḍayq*, from a root meaning narrowness and contraction. The image is physical: a space closing in, a chest tightening. Arabic uses the same root for a cramped room and for the feeling described here.

That is an accurate word for the effect of sustained hostility. Being plotted against does not primarily produce fear; it produces a narrowing, in which the range of what feels possible contracts and every interaction is examined for threat. The Qur'an chooses the word that names the sensation rather than a general term for worry, which is characteristic of how it handles interior states throughout. And what it identifies as the cause is precise — *mimmā yamkurūn*, from what they plot, the same verb used of Thamūd's nine conspirators twenty verses earlier. The Prophet was facing plots of the same kind, and the chapter has just shown the reader how one such plot ended.

**Consolation Grounded in What Preceded It**

This verse works because of its position. Immediately before it, the audience was told to travel and see how the guilty ended. Immediately after that instruction, the Prophet is told not to be crushed by the scheming of people currently in that category.

The connection is left implicit and it is the whole argument. He has just recited an account of nine men who swore an oath, made a plan, and were answered by a plan they could not perceive. Being told not to feel constricted by plots, one verse later, is not a request to suppress a reaction; it is a conclusion drawn from evidence just presented. The chapter does the consoling by means of the history it has spent sixty verses assembling, which is more durable than reassurance would be.

**The Qur'an Records a Prophet's Emotional Life**

Set this verse alongside the others in this chapter that report interior states. Mūsā ran from a staff in fear. Sulaymān smiled at an ant and worried that his own blessings were a test. Now Muḥammad ﷺ is grieving and constricted, and the Book says so.

None of these is presented as a failing, and none is edited out. The pattern is consistent enough to count as a position: the men God chose are shown feeling what people feel, and the feeling is addressed rather than denied. That has a practical consequence for how the text has been read. A community whose founder is recorded as needing to be told not to grieve has scriptural warrant for its own grief, which is a different inheritance from one whose exemplars are depicted as serene. What the mockers said next, and how the Prophet was instructed to answer, follows immediately.

**Two Burdens, Two Different Sources**

The verse names two distinct pressures and they do not come from the same place. Grief comes from caring about people who are refusing; constriction comes from being the target of their plotting. One is the cost of compassion, the other the cost of opposition.

Separating them is useful because they call for different consolations. The grief is answered by the fact that guidance is not the messenger's to grant — the Qur'an tells him elsewhere that he cannot guide whom he loves but God guides whom He wills. The constriction is answered by the chapter's account of what happens to plots, which the reader has just watched fail at Thamūd. Neither answer denies that the feeling is warranted. What the verse does is set a limit on how far each should be allowed to go, addressed to a man who by every indication was exceeding it. The instruction is brief because it is corrective rather than explanatory; the explanation has already been given at length. A man who has just recited the history of four nations does not need to be told again why plots fail. He needs to be told that the conclusion applies to his own situation, and that is what this single sentence does.
