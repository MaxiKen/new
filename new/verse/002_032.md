## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:32

> They replied, “Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Reply That Begins With Praise**

The angels answer the challenge without making an attempt at it. Their first words are an act of glorification, and the Arabic formula declares God free of any deficiency that could be imagined of Him. Beginning a reply to a divine command with praise is the pattern the Book gives to creatures throughout: the answer to a question from God is not an argument but a turning toward Him.

That opening also settles the tone of the whole sentence. The angels are not confessing a defect in themselves in a tone of complaint; they are naming the source of everything they know before they admit that their knowledge is limited. What they say in the second clause only makes sense because of the first.

**The Admission of Limit, Stated Without Excuses**

The middle of the sentence concedes the point of the test: they have no knowledge except what they have been taught. The phrasing is exact. It does not say they have little knowledge, or that the question was unfair, or that the objects were outside the scope of their expertise. It states that everything they possess came by way of instruction, which makes their entire competence a gift rather than a property.

The Book gives human beings the same assessment about their own capacities in passages that read like footnotes to this one: *“Its nature is known only to my Lord, and you ˹O humanity˺ have been given but little knowledge.”* (17:85). And it instructs the Prophet ﷺ to ask for more of it rather than to rely on what he already has: *“and pray, ‘My Lord! Increase me in knowledge.’”* (20:114). The angels in this verse are models of the attitude those commands describe.

**Two Names Chosen to Answer the Question**

The reply ends with two of the divine names: the All-Knowing and the All-Wise. They answer the two halves of the situation precisely. Knowledge answers the test that has just been set, since what the angels could not name is known to God before and after the naming. Wisdom answers the question they asked in the previous verses, since the objection about corruption and bloodshed was an objection about the fitness of the arrangement.

The Book pairs these two names repeatedly, and the pairing is always doing work of this kind. The sūrah declares that God knows what is ahead of His creatures and what lies behind them, and that none grasps any part of His knowledge except as He wills (2:255). Another passage closes a description of the whole creation with the same pair: *“˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and unseen—the Almighty, All-Wise.”* (64:18). Knowledge accounts for the facts, wisdom accounts for what is done with them.

**The Difference Between Conceding and Resigning**

What the angels do is not the abandonment of a question. They had asked something real, and their reply accepts the answer they were given earlier in the scene: there is knowledge the creature does not have. Their response preserves the question in a form that permits an answer, which is a different thing from withdrawing it or repeating it more loudly.

The Book treats this as the proper posture for any inquiry about God. The verse that classifies the Book's verses separates those that are precise from those that are elusive, notes that the deviant pursue the elusive ones looking for trouble, and then describes the stance of those who are well grounded in knowledge: *“We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.”* (3:7). The angels' sentence in this verse is the same stance applied to the appointment of the human being.

**The Test Served Its Purpose**

Nothing in the scene suggests that the angels were humiliated by the test. The Book does not describe a defeat; it records a concession and a name. The being whose appointment was questioned had been taught something the questioners had not, and the exchange ends with the questioners acknowledging the limit of their own knowledge rather than disputing the demonstration.

That outcome is what makes the passage a lesson about the difference between two kinds of ignorance. A person who does not know a thing and admits it stands inside a relationship where he can be taught. A person who does not know a thing and demands that the arrangement be justified to him stands outside it. The sūrah has already used the second posture for the critics of its parables and will use it again for the objectors to the very revelation in its hands.

**A Sentence the Book Gives to Creatures**

The words of this verse are reported as the speech of angels, and they are the words the Book teaches human beings to use in a range of situations. Praise in the face of something not understood, an admission that everything one knows was received, and the naming of God's knowledge and wisdom as the ground of confidence: that is the whole content of the reply, and it is available to any reader who finds himself in the position the angels were in.

The sūrah does not leave the scene at this point. The next verse returns to Adam and completes the demonstration, and in doing so it shows what the admission of the angels looks like from the outside when the missing knowledge is supplied. What they conceded is then displayed, and the display is described in one sentence that belongs to God rather than to the creature.

**The Model This Reply Sets for Inquiry**

The sentence has become the template for how Muslim scholarship describes the limits of reason in matters that revelation alone settles. The angels do not pretend to a knowledge they lack, do not speculate about the purpose behind the appointment, and do not treat their own excellence as evidence of a right to decide. They praise, they concede, and they name the two attributes that make the unanswered question livable: full knowledge and full wisdom.

That posture is what the sūrah's later verse on the Book's ambiguous passages asks of its readers, and it is what the Book praises when it describes people who accept what has come down to them without splitting into factions over what they cannot verify. For a tradition that has spent centuries debating the relationship between reason and revelation, this reply supplies the terms of the settlement: whatever is known was taught, and the teacher of it is both knowing and wise.
