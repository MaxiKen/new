## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:32

> They replied, “Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Angels’ Reply as Worship**

“They replied, ‘Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise’” (2:32). The angels, unable to name what was presented in 2:31, do not guess. They glorify. Their response models how a truthful creature meets limit: not by speculation but by subḥānaka — You are beyond flaw, deficiency, and partnership — and by confession of taught knowledge.

This is more than politeness. It is doctrine in a sentence. Knowledge is not generated ex nihilo by the knower; it is given. Even angels know only what they have been taught. The verb ʿallamtanā echoes ʿallama Ādam al-asmāʾ (2:31): the same teaching root makes both scenes intelligible — Adam was taught names; angels are taught what angels know. To be taught is not diminishment; it is creatureliness honored.

**“Glory Be to You” — Why Tasbīḥ Comes First**

The angels begin with tasbīḥ before they state limit. To declare God’s glory is to frame limitation as fitting worship rather than frustrated ambition. They do not say, “We do not know, therefore discontent.” They say, “Glory be to You, we do not know except what You taught.” Limit becomes praise, because a Lord worthy of limitless tasbīḥ is precisely the Lord who measures each creature’s share.

This sequence also answers the posture of 2:30 — “while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness” (2:30) — by returning to it with deeper awareness. The first glorification was offered as contrast; this one is offered as acknowledgment. The same words mean more after being taught the boundary.

**“We Have No Knowledge Except What You Have Taught Us” — A Universal Rule**

The phrasing is exclusive: lā ʿilma lanā illā mā ʿallamtanā. It states a law for every knower other than God. Human comprehensiveness in naming (2:31) does not contradict it; it exemplifies it. Adam’s breadth was taught. The breadth proves the giver, not self-sufficiency. For contemporary readers tempted to treat knowledge as conquest, the verse resets attribution: to discover is to be shown, even if by mediation of faculties and instruments that are themselves given.

**“You Are Truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise” — Two Names That Close the Confession**

The angels close by naming God as al-ʿAlīm al-Ḥakīm — the All-Knowing, the All-Wise (2:32). The pairing is not casual. ʿAlīm names breadth — He knows all things, as 2:29 already sealed (“perfect knowledge of all things”). Ḥakīm names placement — He places each thing with wisdom, putting knowledge in its site and measure.

In this scene, both names act. Because He is All-Knowing, He could teach Adam the names and lay them before angels. Because He is All-Wise, He asked the angels to show the reach of their knowledge and thus display why the khalīfah, with taught synthesis, is apt for earth’s trust. Wisdom is not only knowing; it is proportioning the showing.

**Why the Angels Could Not Name**

The source notes the view that 2:32 shows the limitation of angelic knowledge as regards comprehensiveness. Each angelic function is luminous within its charge — and veiled beyond it. This does not diminish angels; it delimits them. They are, in Ibn ʿArabī’s idiom rehearsed at 2:31, like specialized faculties: perfect at what they are, not gathering all at once. The “names” entrusted to Adam gather what the specialized do not gather alone. To be comprehensive is not to be more pious than an angel; it is to be differently constituted for a different trust.

**From Contrast to Confession — The Pedagogy of Limit**

Trace the movement: In 2:30 the angels set daring worship as contrast — “we glorify…while they will corrupt.” In 2:31 they are invited to name. In 2:32 they confess limit. The sūrah does not humiliate them to exalt Adam; it school them into a glorification that knows its measure. The same tongue that asked “Will You place…?” now says “Glory be to You!” The question is not retracted as fault so much as deepened into praise.

**Human Echo of Angelic Speech**

The human echo is plain: every seeker meets a frontier where naming fails and only tasbīḥ remains fitting. In study, in prayer, in perplexity before divine acts that permit corruption while intending hidden goods, the right sentence is often the angelic one: “Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us” (2:32). That sentence guards both humility and continuity: it does not cease seeking, but it refuses to claim what only wisdom can place.

**Living the Names al-ʿAlīm al-Ḥakīm**

- al-ʿAlīm steadies the student: you will not be asked beyond what you have been taught, and what you have been taught is gift enough to begin.
- al-Ḥakīm steadies the reformer: provision and vicegerency are measured; haste that ignores proportion is not wisdom, even if knowledge is present.

In household and governance alike, the pair corrects two errors: obscurantism that says “since only God knows, we need not learn,” and overreach that says “since we have learned something, we now know as God knows.” The angels model the middle: learn keenly, confess gladly, attribute truly.

**Bridge to “O Adam! Inform Them”**

The angels’ confession clears space for the next command without rivalry: “O Adam! Inform them of their names” (2:33). Teaching has been acknowledged as divine; now its bearer will show what teaching can do through a human mouth. The angels’ tasbīḥ becomes the frame in which Adam’s naming will be heard as praise of the Teacher, not boast of the student.
