## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:32

> They replied, “Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Begins by Exonerating the Questioned**

The angels' reply opens before it confesses: *subḥānaka* — glory be to You, Allah be declared beyond every defect. The word order is protocol, and the protocol is doctrine. Faced with a demand they cannot meet, the angels' first act is not self-defense and not despair but the vindication of the wisdom that framed the test — the same *tasbīḥ* they named as their office in verse 30, now performed at the exact moment it costs something. The exegetes note the pattern the Qur'an keeps: whenever divine action could be misread as defective — a decree, a judgment, an attribution of guidance and straying — *subḥānaka* or *subḥānahu* intervenes to sever the act from every imperfection. Here the glorification does specific work: it says, before the confession, that a test we will fail was still rightly designed. The sentence thus models the only posture the Qur'an recognizes for a creature at the limit of its knowledge — not the suspension of judgment but the prior acquittal of the Judge, held while the limit is admitted.

**One Negation, One Exception: The Epistemology in a Clause**

The confession itself is the Qur'an's most compressed statement of creaturely knowledge: *lā ʿilma lanā illā mā ʿallamtanā* — we have no knowledge except what You have taught us. The grammar is a total negation governed by a single exception, and the form is exact: no underived knowledge belongs to any creature — not to the angels, whose sinlessness and antiquity might have argued for self-sufficiency, and by the verse's silent inclusion, not to anyone else. Every cognition in existence is loaned. The clause also settles the scene's comparison without disparaging either party: the angels and Adam differ not in kind of knowing — both are taught — but in curriculum. Adam received the names, all of them; the angels received their functions. The exception-clause therefore does double duty. It absolves the angels of the failed demand (they cannot name exhibits outside their instruction), and it locates Adam's superiority inside the same principle (his instruction was comprehensive). One epistemology governs the whole room, and its first article is that ignorance, rightly confessed, is not a defect in the creature but a property of createdness.

**The Limitation the Verse Exists to Record**

The commentators draw the scene's formal conclusion from this reply: the verse records the limitation of angelic knowledge — not its weakness but its boundedness, specifically in universal comprehension. The angels know truly what they know; their knowledge simply does not extend to the whole, and verse 30's question was generated from inside that boundary. Read against the reply of verse 33 — *I know what you reveal and conceal* — and against the announcement of verse 29 — *He has knowledge of all things* — the scene arranges three epistemic ranks in one frame: the Knower of all things; the creature taught all the names; the creatures taught their functions. The ranking retroactively absolves the angels' question in verse 30 of every charge of insolence: it was the honest output of a bounded knower contemplating an unbounded decree. And it establishes the Qur'an's standing treatment of angelic error-adjacent speech — wonder, question, confession — as the behavior of knowers who are certain of their limit and therefore never pretend past it. The limitation is not their shame; it is their *tasbīḥ* in epistemic form.

**Two Names to Close: The Knower and the Wise**

The confession ends by naming the resolution the angels cannot articulate: *innaka anta al-ʿalīm al-ḥakīm* — You are truly the All-Knowing, the All-Wise. The two names answer the two difficulties of the scene with surgical fit. *Al-ʿalīm* answers the knowledge gap — what we cannot name, You know; the exhibits are inside Your science even when they are outside ours. *Al-ḥakīm* answers the wisdom gap of verse 30 — the placement of a blood-shedding vicegerent, the withholding of comprehensive naming from sinless worshippers, the design of a test its first-takers will fail: every item has a purpose the Wise holds even where the knowing cannot trace it. The pairing is one of the Qur'an's most frequent closings, and its logic here is that knowledge and wisdom are the two attributes a bounded creature must posit whenever he meets a boundary: that the Knower has the fact I lack, and that the Wise has the reason I lack. The angels model the complete act of confession — limit admitted, both perfections affirmed — in a single sentence, and the sentence becomes the pattern.

**The Sentence the Tradition Learned By**

This reply entered Islamic practice as the learner's standing prayer. The classical etiquette of study opens sessions with *subḥānaka* and this confession, and the great Qur'anic supplication for increase — *My Lord, increase me in knowledge* (20:114) — is recited in the spirit of a verse that makes all knowledge a taught gift to be requested rather than a possession to be displayed. The liturgical reuse is not incidental; it reads the scene as normative. Every student occupies the angels' position at some hour: confronted with material he cannot name, tempted either to bluff or to despair, and this verse supplies the third option the scene enacted — glorify, confess the exact limit, name the Teacher's two perfections, and wait for the instruction. The prayer of al-Fātiḥah, *Guide us along the Straight Path* (1:6), is the same posture in petition form; the angels' confession is its declarative twin. A tradition that begins every circle of learning with these words has made the failed test of verse 31 into its pedagogy: the demonstration every learner must first pass is that he knows what he does not know.

**Humility as the Highest Knowledge in the Scene**

There is an irony the verse completes, and it is the story's hinge. The angels pass the test by failing it. Asked for names they do not have, they produce the truest sentence available in the situation — a confession exact in its limit and complete in its theology — and the narrative treats that sentence as the right answer to the wrong question. The demonstration of verse 31 established the vicegerency's ground: comprehensive taught knowledge. This verse establishes the worshippers' ground: comprehensive taught *ignorance*, known as such and correctly related to its Teacher. Both grounds are sound, and the scene needs both — a deputy who can name the estate, and attendants who will not overreach it. The final effect on the reader is a redefinition of rank: in this narrative, knowing is honored, but knowing-one's-not-knowing is honored first, because it is the only knowledge no curriculum can confer and no exam can fake. The angels, senior in creation and sinless in function, speak the verse that every later knower — Adam's children included — must be able to say at the edge of his own instruction, and the saying of it is not defeat but the exact posture the Teacher designed the edge for.
