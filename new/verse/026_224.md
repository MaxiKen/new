## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:224

> As for poets, they are followed ˹merely˺ by deviants.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Rejects a False Description of the Quran**

The verse turns to poets because the opponents had described the Prophet and the Quran with that label. The Quran's concern is not that every patterned or beautiful line is evil. It rejects the claim that the revelation is the product of a poet drawing on the same source, social role, and moral world as pre-Islamic soothsayers and inspired performers.

Quran 36:69 states that the Prophet was not taught poetry and that the Book is a reminder and a clear Quran. The point is source and purpose. Poetry, as the deniers were using the term, could be attributed to human inspiration, imagination, rivalry, and praise-for-hire. Revelation comes from Allah through the trustworthy spirit and calls people to account before Him.

**The Historical Label Was Wider Than Modern Poetry**

The English word poet can sound neutral and artistic. In the Quran's first audience, the label carried wider associations with unusual inspiration, tribal praise, satire, and claims connected with unseen influence. That context matters. The opponents were not simply classifying the Quran by meter or imagery; they were assigning it a human and possibly occult source.

This is why the verse cannot be used as a blanket condemnation of all poetic language or every poem. It addresses a type of poet and a social role in which speech follows appetite, patronage, and faction. The Quran itself uses vivid images and powerful rhythms, but formal beauty does not decide whether a speech is revelation or human composition.

**Deviant Followers Reveal the Social Effect**

The verse says that poets are followed by deviants. The criticism includes the audience that gathers around speech without caring where it leads. A poet who flatters tribal pride, excites lust, or turns false accusations into entertainment can attract people whose desires are already seeking permission.

The Arabic term behind deviants can be understood more broadly than one narrow class. It points to people who wander from right guidance and follow the speaker's shifting impulses. The problem is not imagination itself. It is speech that gives wandering a beautiful form and then persuades others to call the wandering wisdom.

**The Quran's Beauty Has a Different Direction**

A person may notice the Quran's eloquence and still miss the difference between eloquence and poetry as an asserted source. The Quran's beauty serves remembrance, warning, and guidance. Its images do not ask the listener to admire the speaker's creative ego. They direct the listener toward Allah, judgment, repentance, and truth.

The distinction also protects the Prophet from a false kind of praise. Calling him a poet might sound like calling him gifted, but the compliment hides a denial of revelation. The Prophet is not being praised for inventing a beautiful message. He is being accused of producing human speech and then presenting it as divine.

**The Verse Leaves Room for Beneficial Speech**

Later passages distinguish between harmful poets and those who believe, do good, remember Allah, and defend the wronged. That exception shows how to read the present verse. The condemned pattern is not the mere use of rhythm, metaphor, or emotional force. It is the alliance of speech with deviation, falsehood, and conduct that contradicts truth.

Words are a trust whatever form they take. A poem can glorify injustice, or it can expose it. A speech can manipulate a crowd, or it can call people back to Allah. The test is not whether language is beautiful, but whether beauty serves truth and whether the person who follows the words is led toward righteousness or further wandering.

The verse should therefore be read against the specific accusation facing the Prophet. His opponents did not use poet as a neutral description. They used it to deny that the Quran was revelation and to place the Prophet among people whose words were attributed to inspiration, imagination, or unseen companions. Quran 52:29–30 records the same kind of pressure, contrasting the prophetic reminder with the labels of fortune-teller, possessed person, and poet.

The Quran answers by showing that its speech has a fixed purpose. It warns, clarifies, corrects, and calls people to Allah. A poet in the condemned pattern can change allegiance when the audience changes, because the performance is built around effect. Revelation does not change its truth to preserve a patron. It can praise a believer, condemn a tyrant, comfort the weak, and warn the messenger while remaining one message from one Lord.

The presence of imagery does not erase this difference. Human poetry can use metaphor for truth or falsehood, just as ordinary prose can deceive. The category is about source, purpose, and moral role rather than about whether a sentence is vivid. Quran 36:69 calls the Book a reminder and clear Quran, making its function explicit: it brings people to awareness and warning rather than to admiration of a human poet.

The verse also challenges the audience that follows speech uncritically. Deviants are not only people with a certain political label. They are those who let the speaker's excitement replace the question of where the words lead. A crowd can follow a talented voice into pride, lust, tribal hatred, or false accusation. The beauty of the performance then hides the ugliness of the destination.

A believer may appreciate eloquence while remaining morally alert. He can enjoy a poem, story, or speech without treating it as guidance in every matter. He asks whether the words are true, whether they honor people, and whether the speaker practices the values being praised. The Quran's exception in the final verse confirms that form alone is not the issue; the issue is whether language is governed by faith and good action.

Poetry is therefore not saved by a label or condemned by one. It is judged by the Lord who hears every word. The verse protects revelation from a false classification and teaches the community to treat verbal beauty as a trust rather than an authority independent of Allah.
