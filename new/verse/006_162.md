## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:162

> Say, “Surely my prayer, my worship, my life, and my death are all for Allah—Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Declaration of Dedication**

Say, Surely my prayer, my worship, my life, and my death are all for Allah, Lord of all worlds. The verse continues the declaration the Prophet is given, and it states the dedication that matches the guidance he has acknowledged. Four things are named, and they cover the acts of religion and the whole of a person's existence. The declaration offers them to the One who is described at the end of the sentence as the Lord of all worlds.

The Book's use of the phrase Lord of all worlds is one of its most familiar, and it opens the recitation of the Qur'an in the first sūrah, where praise is given to God under that name (1:2). The declaration in front of us attaches the Prophet's whole life to that lordship, and the pairing of the prayer and the sacrifice among the four items connects the verse to the practices the sūrah has been examining.

**The Four Terms of the Sentence**

The first term is the prayer, which the sūrah has emphasised throughout its instructions to the community (6:72; 6:92), and the second is the offering or act of worship, which the Book uses for the sacrifice performed in the name of God. The sūrah's dietary passages have dealt with the invocation of names over animals (6:118; 6:121), and the declaration places the practice of sacrifice under the same dedication as the prayer.

The third and fourth terms are life and death, and they extend the dedication beyond the acts of worship to the whole span of a person's existence. The Book's language elsewhere states the same totality: the sūrah of the Bee commands the believers to eat of what God has provided and to be grateful, and the sūrah of the Troops says that the whole matter belongs to God. The sentence in front of us states the dedication in the first person, and it leaves nothing outside it.

**Sacrifice and the Practice of the Audience**

The mention of the offering speaks directly to the audience's religious practice, which the sūrah has described in its passages about the shares assigned to God and to the idols (6:136) and about the animals that were not slaughtered in the Name of God (6:138). The declaration answers those practices by placing the sacrifice where it belongs, and the sūrah of the Abundance states the same requirement in its command to pray and sacrifice to the Lord alone (108:2).

The Book's treatment of the offering elsewhere states what the act is for. The sūrah of the Pilgrimage says that neither the meat nor the blood of the sacrifice reaches God, but that the piety of the person who offers it reaches Him (22:37), and the verse in front of us places the practice inside a declaration that also includes the worshipper's life and death. The four terms together show that the offering is not a transaction but a statement of the direction of a person's existence.

**Lord of All Worlds**

The declaration's closing phrase names the One to whom the four things are given. The Book uses the name throughout, and its placement here matches the scope of the sentence: the Lord of all worlds is the one whose claim covers a person's prayer, worship, life, and death. The sūrah of the Cattle has stated that God is the Lord of everything and that no one reaps except what he sows (6:164), and the declaration in front of us supplies the response of the person who accepts that lordship.

The Book's statements about the universality of God's lordship appear in its descriptions of the created order, and the sūrah has supplied them throughout its account of the signs. The stars, the rain, the harvest, the herds, and the human beings themselves are described as the provision of one Lord, and the declaration in front of us gathers the worshipper's response into the same framework.

**The Meaning of Sincere Devotion**

The Book's instructions to the Prophet elsewhere command the same sincerity in explicit terms. The sūrah of the Troops tells him to say that he is commanded to worship God, being sincerely devoted to Him alone, and to be the first of those who submit (39:11–12); the sūrah of the Clear Proof describes the command given to the people of the earlier books as the worship of God alone with sincere devotion in all uprightness (98:5). The declaration in front of us expresses the same intention, and it does so by naming the things that must be devoted.

The Book's teaching about sincerity belongs to its account of the difference between the acts that are performed for God and the acts that are performed for people. The declaration in this verse names the whole person, and it leaves no part of life outside the offering. That arrangement gives the community its model, and it states the standard against which acts of worship are measured.

**The Prophet's Example in the Closing Verses**

The three verses of the declaration belong to a single speech, and they present the Prophet as the person whose guidance, worship, and submission are complete. A comparable declaration from the Prophet stands in the sūrah of the Troops, with the command to worship God sincerely and to be the first of those who submit (39:11–12). The version in the sūrah of the Cattle adds the detail that the declaration is to be spoken aloud as a statement to the audience.

The Book's presentation of the Prophet's example in these passages serves the community that follows him. The declaration does not describe a privilege that separates him from the believers; it states the norm they are to adopt, and the verses that issue the same commands to the community elsewhere show that the norm is meant to be shared.

**The Place of the Verse in the Sūrah**

The verse stands in the middle of the three-part declaration that closes the sūrah's account of the Prophet's own religion, and it supplies the content of the dedication that follows from the guidance declared in the verse before it. The sūrah has completed its argument against the audience's practices in the passages about the dietary system and the covenant, and the closing declaration states what the religion of the messenger consists in.

The verse that follows adds the denial of any partner and the statement that the Prophet has been commanded and is the first to submit (6:163), and the sūrah then returns to the accountability of every person and to the ranks in which people have been placed (6:164–165). The declaration in front of us supplies the middle term of that closing arrangement, and the reader is left with the direction the revelation asks of him.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse completes the declaration with the statement that God has no partner, that the Prophet is commanded, and that he is the first to submit (6:163). The three elements answer the audience's association of partners with God, state the ground of the Prophet's conduct, and identify the religion by its character of submission.

The sūrah's closing verses then take up the question of accountability from the side of every person, stating that the Lord of everything is the one to whom people return and that differences will be settled before Him (6:164). The verse in front of us supplies the dedication that the message requires, and the verses that follow state the terms on which the dedication is judged.
