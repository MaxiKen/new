## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:184

> And fear the One Who created you and ˹all˺ earlier peoples.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fear of the Creator Grounds Every Other Duty**

Shuʿayb now gives the command behind the commands about measure, weight, and property: fear the One who created you. Taqwa is not panic that makes a person flee from God. It is alertness before the Creator that prevents desire, habit, and power from becoming false gods. A person who remembers where his life came from cannot treat another created person as material for his profit.

The warning returns the community from social rules to worship. Fair dealing is not a clever arrangement invented by traders. It is obedience to the One who made both parties and knows the right of each. The people may hide a reduction from a customer, but they cannot hide it from the One who gave them hands, goods, judgment, and time.

The verse does not invoke a local deity tied to one forest or one clan. The Creator made the present people and those who came before them. This wider claim removes the excuse that the community's customs are beyond correction. A practice inherited from ancestors remains answerable to the One who created ancestors and descendants alike.

Quran 7:85 places Shuʿayb's call to worship Allah beside his demand for just measure and weight. The order is significant: recognizing the Creator gives the community a reason to respect created persons. No customer is outside God's care, and no prosperous trader is outside God's judgment.

**Earlier Peoples Turn History Into a Warning**

The mention of earlier peoples gives the command a memory. The residents of the Forest are not the first community to receive guidance, and they will not be the last to decide whether prosperity licenses injustice. Earlier peoples show that a society can feel permanent while remaining answerable to a fixed moral law.

This is not a call to despise every inherited custom. It is a call to examine inheritance before God. Ancestors can transmit wisdom, but they can also transmit a habit of exploitation. The Creator's authority reaches behind the community's pride and asks whether its way of life honors the One who made all generations.

**The Arabic Term Allows a Wider Human Picture**

The Arabic term rendered here as earlier peoples has also been understood by exegetes as a multitude of created beings or a great body of creatures. Those readings arise from the breadth of the word rather than from a different moral direction. The translation emphasizes generations before the present audience, while the wider sense emphasizes the vastness of God's creation.

Both shades strengthen the verse. If earlier peoples are intended, history places the market under the judgment that reached former societies. If the phrase includes a multitude of creatures, human pride is reduced still further: the community is one small part of what the Creator knows and governs. Neither reading permits people to imagine that their customs set the limit of accountability.

**Taqwa Converts Economic Reform Into Worship**

Fear of the Creator changes the reason a person acts justly. He does not give full measure only because a guard may inspect his stall or a customer may complain. He gives it because every created thing belongs to Allah, and the rights of a created neighbor are not his to erase.

That inner reason protects justice when no one is watching. It also protects the reformer from hatred. Shuʿayb condemns corruption firmly, but the people he addresses remain creatures of the Lord who made them. Taqwa therefore produces both exactness toward rights and humility before the One who will judge every generation.

Remembering creation also prevents the wealthy from confusing provision with ownership without limits. Allah gives people ability, land, skill, and opportunity, then tests what they do with those gifts. A person may possess an object lawfully, but he cannot use possession as a license to injure the person who depends on him. The Creator's authority remains above every private claim.

The mention of earlier peoples adds another check on present pride. Every generation inherits the results of choices made before it, yet no generation can inherit immunity from judgment. A market may have operated unjustly for years, and a family may have profited from the arrangement for generations. Its age does not make the wrong respectable. Taqwa can require a descendant to end a practice that an ancestor began.

This fear also creates patience with honest reform. A person who remembers Allah's creation does not demand instant social praise before he gives a full measure or returns property. He can accept that changing a corrupt custom may be costly. The Creator knows the cost, the motive, and the benefit that others cannot yet see.

In this way the verse joins personal reverence to historical responsibility. The community is made of creatures who came from one Creator and will return to His judgment. It must not use ancestry, prosperity, or local identity to rank some people's rights below others. Fear of Allah is the inner force that makes the command about trade durable when no human authority is present.

The Creator is also the One who knows the difference between a mistake and a planned betrayal. Human law may need evidence and may sometimes reach the wrong conclusion. Divine knowledge does not depend on a witness who remembered every detail. This does not permit people to ignore legal process; it teaches them that escaping a court is not the same as escaping accountability.

When fear of Allah is sound, it produces repair rather than theatrical guilt. The person who discovers that he gave short measure does not merely feel ashamed; he corrects the account, informs the affected party, and changes the practice that caused the loss. Taqwa turns awareness of the Creator into a movement toward justice. It keeps the verse from becoming a slogan and makes it a discipline for the hand.
