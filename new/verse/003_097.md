## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:97

> In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham. Whoever enters it should be safe. Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people. And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Signs in the House**

The verse names signs in the House and specifies one of them as the standing-place of Abraham, then states that whoever enters it is safe, and then lays the pilgrimage on those who are able to make the journey. The sentences move from evidence to sanctuary to obligation.

The order matters. The signs establish the House's standing; the safety attached to it describes what it offers those who come; and the obligation follows from both. A reader who has just been shown the House's universal purpose in the previous verse now sees what stands in it and what is required because of it.

**What the Signs Are**

The commentators offered several identifications. Many took the signs to be the standing-place itself, and some connected it with the footprints said to be preserved there. Others read the signs as the evidences that this structure is the one Abraham built and that God has honoured it.

The syntax supports the second reading more easily, since the standing-place is named after the signs rather than being identified with them, which suggests that the signs are the marks of the House's origin and standing. Both readings point the reader in one direction, which is toward the connection between the building and the figure whose name the sūrah has been arguing about. The evidence in the House is evidence about Abraham, and it answers the dispute from the previous verses with something his opponents could see.

**The Sanctuary and Its Limits**

The statement that whoever enters the House is safe was read by some as describing the past, since the precinct around the Kaʿbah served in pre-Islamic Arabia as a place where a person fleeing pursuit could take refuge and not be seized.

The commentators then discussed whether the safety continued under the new community, and they reported a range of positions. One holds that the sanctuary no longer shields a person from prosecution and that offences committed in the sacred precinct are punishable like any other, though the punishment is not carried out within it. Another describes a strict regime under which a person taking refuge may not trade, speak, lodge, or be fed, which leaves him no option but to leave and face the case against him. A third holds that the verse is a command to the community to make the place secure, which people may fail to carry out. A further reading, reported from several authorities, takes the safety as deliverance from the Fire for the one who enters to perform the rites.

**The Inviolability of Makkah in the Prophet's Words**

The report of the conquest gives the city's standing in the Prophet's own words. Ibn ʿAbbās related that on the day Makkah was taken he said that God had made the town a sanctuary, so that its thorn bushes were not to be cut, its game was not to be chased, and anything dropped there was not to be picked up except by someone announcing it publicly (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1587).

The wording supplies what the verse's shorter sentence leaves open: the safety of the place is a status conferred by God, and it imposes duties on those who live there and visit it, not only a privilege for those who need protection. The two statements together keep the sanctuary from becoming a license. What God has made inviolable obliges the people who are inside it to treat it as such.

**The Obligation of Pilgrimage and Who Owes It**

The verse states that pilgrimage to the House is a duty owed to God by those among humanity who can find a way, and the qualification of ability is central to the passage. The commentators explained the phrase as covering physical capacity and the means to travel and to provide for dependents during the absence.

The Book attaches the obligation to the House rather than to the surrounding city, and it treats the journey as the duty of the individual rather than of a representative. The Book's other commands for the pilgrimage specify its months, its rites, and the conduct required within them, and this verse supplies the foundation on which those provisions rest: the House exists for the worship of God alone, and the able are required to come.

**The Closing Clause**

The verse ends by stating that God is beyond need of all creation, so that whoever disbelieves harms nothing but himself. The commentators took the disbelief in view either as the denial that the pilgrimage is obligatory or as a general rejection of God and the final accounting.

The clause protects the verse's meaning from a misunderstanding that the rest of the passage might otherwise invite. The House is a place of blessing and the duty is real, and neither fact creates a need in God that human beings satisfy by coming. The Book returns to this point whenever it lays down a costly obligation, which is why the sūrah can move from a house on a journey to a discussion of the people who withhold their testimony about it without altering its tone.

**A Duty Owed to God**

The verse's phrasing places the pilgrimage among the obligations owed to God rather than among the rights of people, and the distinction matters for how the duty is understood. No human being is wronged by its omission in the way a creditor is wronged by an unpaid debt; what is at stake is a person's standing before his Lord, which is why the verse can end with a statement that God needs nothing from anyone.

The commentators differed about whether this sentence or the passage in the sūrah of the Cow is the one that instituted the pilgrimage, and the difference concerns the order of the revelation rather than the obligation itself. The Book's treatment of the rite elsewhere supplies its months, its sites, and the conduct required within them, and it records the summons Abraham was given to call humanity to the journey. This verse stands at the head of all of it, stating the reason the journey exists: a House established for humanity, and an able worshipper who comes to it.
