## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:91

> They admitted, “By Allah! Allah has truly preferred you over us, and we have surely been sinful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Introduces an Admission**

The brothers begin with *tallāh*, by God — the formula they have used throughout this chapter. They swore by it when reporting the loss of Joseph with a bloodied shirt in hand (12:16), when denying the theft in Egypt (12:73), and when predicting that their father's grief would kill him (12:85). Here the same formula introduces the first true statement about themselves that the chapter records. The repetition is not accidental. A family that has sworn to a lie, sworn to a denial and sworn to a prediction now swears to a confession, and the chapter uses the identical opening word for all four so that the difference is located entirely in the content. The uniformity makes the four statements comparable, and the comparison is unflattering. Each of Joseph's disclosures has been sworn and each of the brothers' responses has been sworn too, while the content of the two sets could hardly be further apart. The chapter lets the formal parallel run so that the moral distance between the speakers is measured by the reader without narration.

**"God Has Preferred You Over Us"**

The verb denotes preference, choosing one thing over another. The brothers are not merely congratulating Joseph on his station; they are attributing the difference between them to a divine act. The commentators do not restrict the preference to any single respect, and the chapter supports several at once: Joseph received knowledge and wisdom at maturity (12:22), the interpretation of events (12:21), an office over the country's stores (12:55), and an establishment in the land (12:56). The brothers name the preference in the general form because that is the form in which they have just experienced it — a man they sold is feeding them, forgiving them and instructing them. The preference is stated as something observed rather than as doctrine accepted. They have watched Joseph govern a famine, spare a brother, refuse revenge and articulate a maxim about patience, and their comparison between him and themselves is drawn from that record. The confession is not a theological claim but a summary of the last thirteen verses, offered by the people who lived them.

**The Dream of Verse 4 Answered by the Dreamer's Brothers**

Joseph's first vision showed eleven stars, a sun and a moon prostrating to him (12:4), and his father warned him not to relate it to his brothers lest they plot against him (12:5). The plot happened anyway. Verse 91 is the beginning of the fulfilment: ten of the eleven stars are standing in front of Joseph, addressing him as the one God has preferred over them, and admitting a fault. The prostration itself is reserved for verse 100, when the parents arrive. The chapter divides the fulfilment across two scenes and places the brothers' submission in words here and in gesture later, which is why the recognition scene occupies ten verses rather than one. The length measures what the fulfilment costs. One verse could have reported the brothers bowing; ten are needed to carry a search, a detention, a private conference, a journey home, a father's blindness, a second journey, a disclosure, an admission and a forgiveness. The dream is fulfilled at the end of a moral process rather than at the end of a plot.

**"We Have Surely Been Sinful"**

The admission is emphatic and its scope is not limited. The brothers do not specify which act they are confessing — the well, the sale, the shirt, the report to their father, the slander about a theft in verse 77. They state a condition: we have been people who err. The chapter's own vocabulary supports the breadth of the claim. The word family used here concerns missing the mark and being at fault, and it is the same register in which verse 89 described their conduct as done in ignorance. What Joseph supplies as a mitigation, they accept as a description of themselves, and they accept it without qualification or exception. The absence of qualification is what makes it a confession. No circumstance is offered, no blame is distributed, no reference is made to youth, to their father's favour or to the pressure of the moment. The sentence has a subject, a verb and an adjective, and the brothers add no fourth element, which is the difference between an admission and an explanation.

**The Admission Follows the Maxim, Not the Disclosure**

Order matters here. Joseph identified himself and stated a rule — whoever is mindful and patient does not have his reward discounted (12:90). The brothers' confession comes after the rule and before the forgiveness. Nothing in the chapter suggests that the confession caused the forgiveness; verse 92 will grant it unconditionally, and Joseph has already named the injury and supplied the excuse in verse 89. The sequence therefore describes a confession that is not a plea. The brothers are not bargaining. They have been told who is speaking to them and what he thinks about patience, and they respond by agreeing with the second half of that assessment rather than by asking for anything. That ordering tells us what the chapter considers decisive. Learning who Joseph is would have produced shock; hearing what he believes about patience and the fear of God produces agreement. The brothers are moved not by the identity of the man in front of them but by the accuracy of his description of them, and that accuracy is something they can verify from their own history.

**The Same Words Are Said Again to Their Father**

Verse 97 records a second admission, addressed to Jacob after the family is reunited: the brothers ask him to pray for their forgiveness and add that they have certainly been sinful. The wording echoes this verse almost exactly, and the repetition is deliberate. The chapter has the confession made twice — once to the brother they injured and once to the father they deceived — because the crime had two victims and each requires a separate acknowledgment. The sīrah of the story is therefore complete only at verse 98, when Jacob agrees to intercede. Verse 91 is the first of the two admissions, and it is made to the person who has the least reason to accept it.

**What the Brothers Still Do Not Know**

The confession is comprehensive in tone but limited in knowledge. The brothers do not yet know that Joseph placed the cup in Benjamin's bag (12:70), that he searched their bags in a calculated order (12:76), that he disclosed himself privately to Benjamin before any of this (12:69), or that their detention of a second brother was arranged rather than accidental. Verse 100 will supply part of the explanation in Joseph's own terms, attributing the separation to Satan's stirring of rivalry between him and his brothers. Until then the brothers are confessing a crime they remember while standing inside a sequence they do not understand, and the chapter leaves them in that position for nine more verses.

**The Preference They Name Is the One They Tried to Prevent**

Verse 5 recorded the reason for the plot: Joseph and his brother were dearer to their father than the rest of them, and they said their father was clearly mistaken. The preference they resented was paternal and they acted to end it. The preference they now name is divine, and it has been confirmed by everything that followed — an office, a country's grain, a famine survived, a family fed. The chapter sets the two statements fifty verses apart and lets the reader compare them. What the brothers tried to reverse by disposing of a child was not reversed; it was enlarged, transferred from a father's affection to a ruler's authority, and returned to them in the form of the brother they removed. Their admission in verse 91 is therefore an acknowledgment that the plot achieved the opposite of its aim.
