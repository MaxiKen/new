## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:140

> Lost indeed are those who have murdered their own children foolishly out of ignorance and have forbidden what Allah has provided for them— falsely attributing lies to Allah. They have certainly strayed and are not ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict on the System Just Described**

Lost indeed are those who have murdered their own children foolishly out of ignorance and have forbidden what Allah has provided for them. The verse gathers the offences described in the preceding passage and states their result, and the Book's word for the lost describes a loss that cannot be recovered. The killing of children was described in the earlier verse as the work of the associates who made it appealing (6:137), and the prohibition of God's provision was described in the rules about the herds and the crops (6:138–139). The sentence in front of us states what the whole system produced.

The verse's order is worth noticing. The murder of children comes before the forbidding of provision, although the sūrah described the dietary rules first, and the arrangement places the gravest offence at the head of the list. The Book's scale of seriousness is stated throughout its treatments of the audience's practices, and the killing of the community's own children is presented here as the culmination of a religion built on invention.

**The Two Charges and Their Ground**

The clause foolishly out of ignorance names the cause of the killing, and the Book's word for the ignorance describes conduct undertaken without knowledge rather than a simple lack of information. The practice was carried out for reasons of poverty and honour, and the Book's later prohibition addresses the poverty directly, telling the audience not to kill their children for fear of want since God provides for them and for their parents (17:31).

The second charge, that they forbade what God provided, is attached to the same ignorance, and the sūrah's earlier verses have shown what the prohibition consisted of: the animals reserved from labour and from the invocation of the Name, the produce withheld from general use, and the offspring forbidden to the women (6:138–139). The verse's sentence treats the two practices as products of one condition.

**The False Attribution**

Falsely attributing lies to Allah. The clause names the act that made the system a religious offence rather than a local custom. The audience had attached the authority of God to regulations they had invented, and the sūrah has stated the same charge at each stage of its examination: the shares assigned to the idols (6:136), the killing of children (6:137), the reservation of the herds (6:138), and the rules about the offspring (6:139).

The Book's treatment of the charge of attributing lies to God runs through the chapter, and the sūrah has described the fabrication of revelation and the claim to have received it as the worst of offences (6:93). The verses in front of us apply the same charge to the community's regulation of its food, which shows how seriously the Book treats the claim of divine authority made on behalf of human invention.

**The Statement of Straying**

They have certainly strayed and are not rightly guided. The verse closes with the declaration of the outcome, and the two clauses state the condition in both directions. The people described have left the path, and they are not being guided back onto it, which is the condition the sūrah has described in its account of those whose hearts were turned away after their first refusal (6:110).

The Book's statements about guidance and straying appear throughout the sūrah, and the verse in front of us applies them to the community's practices. The people who built a religion out of inherited custom had removed themselves from the guidance that the message brought, and the sentence states the consequence as a settled fact.

**What the Verse Says About the Cost of the System**

The verse presents the audience's religious system as destructive to the community that maintained it. The practices described in the preceding passage divided families, restricted food by sex and status, and took the lives of children, and the sentence in front of us names the loss that resulted. The Book's treatment of the system is therefore not only a matter of doctrinal correctness but of the welfare of the people who lived under it.

The Book's account of the community that accepts the message presents the opposite condition. The sūrah of the Cattle's later passage about the gardens instructs the believers to eat of the produce and to pay what is due at the harvest without waste (6:141), and the sūrah of the Bee tells them to eat of what God has provided and to be grateful. The contrast between the two arrangements is one the sūrah's readers were expected to see.

**The Place of the Verse in the Sūrah**

The verse closes the passage that began with the shares assigned to God and the idols (6:136), and it states the judgement on the whole system the passage examined. The sūrah has been conducting its survey of the audience's practices for several verses, and the sentence in front of us supplies the conclusion that the survey was building toward.

The chapter then turns to the created order and to the food that God has actually provided, describing the gardens, the palms, the crops, the olives, and the pomegranates (6:141), and it follows that description with the instruction to eat of what God has given and not to follow the footsteps of Satan (6:142). The verse in front of us marks the turning point of the section, and what follows supplies the positive account of the provision the audience had been managing by custom.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse describes the gardens that God produces, both cultivated and wild, with their palms and their crops of different flavours, and it instructs the community to eat of the fruit and to pay the dues at harvest without waste (6:141). The shift from the audience's rules to the provision that God has made is the sūrah's way of answering the system it has just condemned.

The passage that follows continues with the pairs of livestock and the argument about which animals God has forbidden (6:143–144), and it closes with the declaration that those who fabricate lies against God to mislead others will not be guided. The verse in front of us therefore stands at the head of the sūrah's positive account of what God has given, and the account is set against the system the previous verses described.
