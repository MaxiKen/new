## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:27

> Say, “Show me those ˹idols˺ you have joined with Him as partners. No! In fact, He ˹alone˺ is Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Demand to See the Evidence**

*Arūniya alladhīna alḥaqtum bihi shurakāʾ* — show me the ones you have attached to Him as partners. The verb is *arā*, to show, to cause to see, and the demand is for visual production rather than explanation. This is the third challenge in the sūrah's series and the most confrontational. Earlier the opponents were told to call on their deities and see what answered (34:22); here they are told to bring them forward and point. The shift from calling to showing is not a repetition. A summons can fail for many reasons a believer might explain away — the god is busy, the petitioner unworthy, the request improper. Production has no such excuses available. The challenge asks for the simplest thing any claim can be asked for, which is the referent of its own noun.

**"Attached" — The Verb That Gives the Argument Away**

The most revealing word in the sentence is *alḥaqtum*, from *laḥiqa*, to join, to catch up with, to append. Something *mulḥaq* in Arabic is an annex or an appendix — a thing added to a structure that was complete without it. The verse does not say "the partners He has" or even "those you worship"; it says the ones **you attached**. Agency is placed squarely on the worshippers, and the grammar makes their gods a human addition to a finished arrangement rather than a discovered feature of reality. This is a considerable amount of argument compressed into one verb. It concedes nothing about the status of these beings while explaining exactly how they came to have any status at all: somebody appended them. The sūrah made the same move earlier with *tazʿumūn*, "those you claim" (34:22), where the divinity was called an allegation before it was refuted.

**Why Production Was Impossible on Either Account**

The challenge has two possible targets, and it defeats both. If what is meant is the carved figures in the sanctuary, they can certainly be produced — and producing them is the refutation, since a stone brought into the room and set down is visibly not a partner in the governance of the heavens and the earth. If what is meant is the invisible powers the figures were held to represent, then nothing can be brought at all, and the failure to produce is itself the answer. Either way the demand cannot be met in a way that helps the respondent. This is why the verse does not wait for a reply. It moves straight to *kallā*, no, and states the true position. An objection that cannot be answered either by compliance or by refusal is not really a question; it is a demonstration conducted in the form of a question.

**kallā: A Particle of Flat Refusal**

*Kallā* is one of the strongest negations in Arabic. It does not merely deny a proposition but rebukes the one who advanced it — closer to "certainly not" or "enough" than to "no". The Qur'an reserves it for moments where a claim is to be shut down rather than debated, and its placement here, between the challenge and the affirmation, gives the verse its shape: a demand, a refusal, a statement of fact. Commentators have read the refusal in two ways, and both are sound. It may mean there are no such partners to be shown, so the request cannot be fulfilled because its object does not exist. Or it may function as a rejection of the whole framework — not "you have no partners to produce" but "the matter is not as you have it at all." The second reading leads naturally into what follows, because the verse does not stop at denial.

**What Replaces the Denial**

*Bal huwa Allāhu al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — in fact, He is Allah, the Almighty, the All-Wise. The particle *bal* is corrective: not this, but that. Having cleared the ground, the verse puts something in its place, and the two names it chooses are the ones that answer what partners were wanted for in the first place. *ʿAzīz* means strong, unassailable, impossible to overcome — the root sense involves rarity and inaccessibility, the strength of a fortified height. A sovereign who is *ʿazīz* needs no allies, which removes the practical motive for imagining junior powers. *Ḥakīm* means wise, and from the same root comes the bridle that holds an animal to its course; wisdom here is the ordering of things to their proper ends. A ruler who is wise needs no advisers, which removes the second motive. Between them the two names explain why the partners could never be produced: there was no vacancy for them to fill.

**The Same Names, Deployed Against Different Objections**

The pairing *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* recurs throughout the Qur'an, and its use here is precise rather than formulaic. Al-Anbiyāʾ argues the unity of God from the consequence of its denial: *"Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted"* (21:22) — two authorities cannot govern one order without collision, which is an argument from wisdom. Al-Muʾminūn attacks the evidentiary side: whoever invokes another god *"for which they can have no proof"* will find his penalty with his Lord (23:117), which is the argument this verse makes. Read together, the Qur'an's case against partners runs on two tracks, one about coherence and one about evidence, and this verse takes the second and puts it in the imperative. Having refused the partners and named the sole authority, the sūrah turns from the object of worship to the scope of the messenger's mission.
