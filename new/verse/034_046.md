## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:46

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I advise you to do ˹only˺ one thing: stand up for ˹the sake of˺ Allah—individually or in pairs—then reflect. Your fellow man is not insane. He is only a warner to you before ˹the coming of˺ a severe punishment.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Instruction, Not a Programme**

*Innamā aʿiẓukum bi-wāḥidah* — I advise you of one thing only. The restrictive particle *innamā* narrows the demand to a single item, and the narrowing is itself persuasive. A man told to change his religion, abandon his ancestors, redistribute his wealth and accept a new authority has been given a list he can refuse as a whole. A man told to do one thing has nothing to push against. What that one thing turns out to be is smaller still: not belief, not submission, not even attendance at a recitation. It is an act of mental attention performed in private. The Qur'an here reduces its entire demand, for the purposes of this argument, to the lowest possible threshold — and the reduction is a challenge, since a man who will not do even this has revealed that the obstacle was never evidence.

**Standing Up for God**

*An taqūmū lillāh* — that you stand for God. The verb is the ordinary word for rising to one's feet, and it carries a second sense in Arabic of taking up a matter, standing for a cause, the way English speaks of standing for something. Both senses work here and the tradition has read it both ways. Taken as physical rising, it means to get up out of one's seat in the gathering — to leave the circle of men who are confirming each other. Taken as commitment, it means to take up the question sincerely, for God's sake rather than for advantage. The qualifier *lillāh*, for God, is what distinguishes this from ordinary thinking. It specifies the motive: not to defend a position, not to find an answer that protects your standing, but to find out what is true. Most reasoning about religion in any society fails at exactly this point, and the verse names the failure by naming its cure.

**Singly or in Pairs — and Why Not in Groups**

*Mathnā wa furādā*, in twos and one by one. The exclusion is as important as the prescription: no crowds. The sūrah has been describing crowd behaviour for forty-five verses — a staged mockery for an audience (34:7), the luxury class speaking with one voice (34:34), leaders and followers whose positions were social before they were intellectual (34:31–33). A man in a group does not evaluate a claim; he monitors the room. The prescription breaks the group into the two smallest units in which thought is possible. Alone, because that is where a man has no audience to perform for. In pairs, because a single honest interlocutor can catch errors that solitude protects, and two people talking can reach conclusions neither would reach separately. What no gathering of forty can do is let any member change his mind without cost.

**Then Reflect**

*Thumma tatafakkarū* — then think. The particle *thumma* marks sequence with a gap, so the thinking comes after the separation, not during it; first leave the room, then reason. The verb *tafakkara* is reflexive in form, which in Arabic gives the sense of working something over in oneself, turning it about. The Qur'an uses it often and always for the same operation: examining available evidence rather than receiving a conclusion. What is remarkable is that the verse specifies no subject for the reflection. It does not say think about the Qur'an, or about God, or about death. The object is left open because the argument only requires that they think at all, under conditions where the outcome is not predetermined by the company.

**The Verdict the Thinking Will Produce**

Having prescribed the procedure, the verse states its expected result: *mā bi-ṣāḥibikum min jinnah* — there is no madness in your companion. The word *ṣāḥib* is the key term and it is chosen with care. It means a companion, an associate, someone you keep company with — a man known to you. The Meccans had called him *a man* twice in this sūrah, stripping the name to make him a specimen (34:7, 34:43). The reply restores the relationship: he is not some man, he is your associate, whom you have known for forty years. That is the evidence the reflection will consult, and it is why no reasoning is supplied. Anyone who had actually watched him could answer the question about madness from memory. Al-Aʿrāf makes the identical argument in the same words, asking whether they have not reflected, and stating that there is no madness in their companion (7:184).

**A Warner Before a Severe Punishment**

The verse closes with the Prophet's own description of his office: *in huwa illā nadhīrun lakum bayna yaday ʿadhābin shadīd* — he is only a warner to you before a severe punishment. The idiom *bayna yaday*, between the hands of, means in advance of, ahead of — the position of a scout or a herald. The reduction is deliberate and consistent with this sūrah's insistence that he was sent only as a bringer of good news and a warner (34:28). It is reported that he climbed al-Ṣafā, called out until the clans gathered, and asked whether they would believe him if he told them a cavalry was about to attack from behind the hill; they answered that they had never known him to lie. He then said: I am a warner to you before a severe punishment (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4770, from Ibn ʿAbbās). Their own testimony about his honesty, given before the claim was made, is exactly what this verse asks them to go away and remember.
