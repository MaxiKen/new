## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:104

> O believers! Do not say, “Râ’ina.” [Herd us!] But say, “Unẓurna,” [Tend to us!] and listen ˹attentively˺. And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command About Words**

The sūrah changes addressee and turns to the believers with a rule about how they speak to the Prophet ﷺ. The instruction is specific to a single expression, and the change of subject is abrupt after the long account of the earlier community. The chapter's concern, however, is continuous: it has been describing how communities treat revelation and the men who bring it, and it now regulates the speech of the community that has accepted the revelation.

The placement of the command also gives it a practical purpose. The Muslims had been using a phrase that carried a mocking sense in the usage of the region, and the verse replaces it with an expression that conveys the same request without the insult.

**The Two Expressions**

The term that is prohibited was part of an idiom meaning to lend an ear, and the commentators explain that it had acquired a demeaning or mocking tone among the people of Makkah and Madinah. The Arabic construction allowed it to be turned into a joke at the expense of the person addressed, and the Book instructed the believers to stop using it.

The replacement carries the sense of attending to someone, of looking after him and giving him one's attention. The request made to the Prophet ﷺ in both cases was the same; what changed was the manner of addressing him, and the sūrah chose the expression that respected the one being asked. The change also shows that the request itself was not objectionable. The believers were not forbidden to seek the Prophet's attention; they were given the words in which to do it, and the substitute supplied by the verse carries the same meaning with the courtesy the earlier phrase lacked.

**Why Wording Mattered**

Al-Ṭabarī grouped this instruction with the Prophet's guidance about naming things and people with the most dignified available words, and he rejected the suggestion that the prohibited phrase had been borrowed from another language and misused unknowingly. His reasoning was that the command is addressed to the believers, who would not have adopted an expression they did not understand.

A parallel passage in the chapter of the women lists the same phrase among the verbal games of a party that distorted words from their places and said things calculated to injure the faith (4:46). The Book treats speech as conduct, and it regulates the vocabulary of its community accordingly. The Book elsewhere treats speech as conduct that is recorded and answered for, and it lists the guarding of the tongue among the marks of the faithful. A correction aimed at a form of address is consistent with the Book's habit of regulating the smallest expressions of a community's attitude.

**Respect for the Messenger**

The instruction belongs to a wider teaching about how the Prophet ﷺ was to be addressed. The Book commands the believers not to treat the calling of the Messenger as they treat their calling of one another, and it warns that doing so can nullify a person's deeds. The phrase that was prohibited fell into the category the Book is guarding against, since it allowed familiarity to shade into mockery.

The Prophet's companions, whose relation to him was the least formal in the community, are the ones the Book addresses here, and the instruction therefore sets the standard for every later generation. The sūrah's rule is that closeness to the Messenger does not relax the reverence owed to his office.

**"And Listen"**

The command to listen follows the instruction about wording, and the commentators noted that the verb carries the sense of obeying as well as hearing. The Book often pairs hearing with obedience when it describes the response of the faithful, and its rebukes to the earlier community were aimed at people who heard and did not obey.

The placement gives the sentence its balance. A corrected form of address without attention to what the Prophet says would be an empty courtesy. What the verse asks for is both the respect of the tongue and the acceptance of the message, and the Book's later verses describe the believers as those who say they hear and then act. The command completes the instruction, since a corrected greeting without attention to what is said would be an empty courtesy. The Book's later descriptions of the believers include hearing that leads to obedience, and it places the blame for the earlier community's failures on people who heard and did not act.

**The Warning at the End**

The sentence closes by stating that the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment, and the mention of the disbelievers at this point serves a purpose. The conduct being corrected among the believers was the conduct of people who had rejected the revelation, and the Book's method is to distinguish the community's discipline from the position of those outside it.

The warning is not addressed to the believers as a threat but as a statement of the contrast. What the sūrah has been describing for several verses is the response of people who declined what they recognised, and the instruction about speech stands in the middle of that account as a picture of how a believing community behaves instead.

**Adab as Part of the Argument**

The verse may look like a small matter of etiquette set inside a doctrinal passage, and the Book's treatment shows that it is not. A community's language is one of the ways it carries its reverence for revelation, and the sūrah's attention to a single phrase indicates how much is being guarded. The rest of the chapter continues with commands and warnings to the same community, and this instruction stands as the first of them. The instruction is placed at the start of a series of commands and warnings addressed to the believers, and the sūrah's decision to open that series with words rather than with worship is deliberate. A community's language is one of the ways it carries its reverence for revelation, and a rule about a single phrase marks how much is being guarded.

**Speech as a Form of Obedience**

The Book treats words as acts, and this verse applies that principle to a community whose members were in daily contact with the Prophet ﷺ. Their familiarity was a privilege, and the instruction ensures that it does not become a licence. The sūrah's concern runs further than one expression, since a community's habits of speech shape what it thinks it may do with sacred things. Later commands to the believers about lowering the voice in the Prophet's presence and about not treating his call as an ordinary call belong to the same teaching, and the discipline they require is one of the ways a community keeps its reverence alive. What the verse establishes is that the manner of a request can be as important as the request, and that the standard applies first to those closest to the message.
