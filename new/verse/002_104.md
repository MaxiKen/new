## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:104

> O believers! Do not say, “Râ’ina.” [Herd us!] But say, “Unẓurna,” [Tend to us!] and listen ˹attentively˺. And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word Banned for the Believers**

The recital turns, in a single verse, from the theology of selection to the vocabulary of the prayer-hall, and its subject is one word. *Rāʿinā* — in the believers' mouths a colloquial request for attention, something like *lend us your ear*, from an idiom current in the speech of the two cities: *rāʿinā samʿak*, attend to me with your hearing. The believers had been using it to the Prophet ﷺ in the ordinary way of people asking a teacher to slow down, to stay with them, to give them his regard. The verse legislates against it absolutely: *do not say rāʿinā — but say unẓurnā*. The command's scope deserves notice before its reasons: it addresses the believers themselves, the sincere users of the word, not its abusers. The legislation therefore is not about intent but about the word — a term the faithful meant innocently is withdrawn from the community's address to its Prophet ﷺ, and a substitute is installed in the same breath. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ records the other side of the word's history: some among the Jews twisted their tongues with it, saying *"We listen and we disobey"* and playing with *"Rāʿinā"* as an insult dressed in a request (4:46) — the same syllables carrying mockery in their mouths and innocence in the believers'. Between the two verses the word's career is complete: an ordinary idiom, a vehicle of abuse, and a revealed ban that removes the vehicle by removing the idiom.

**Why the Innocent Word Had to Go**

The exegetes give two grounds for the ban, and their weighing is instructive. The first is the idiom's own texture: in the usage of Makkah and Madina, *rāʿinā* carried a demeaning, familiar edge — the tone of one who herds cattle applied to one who is addressed as a shepherd of souls, impudence available inside an apparently neutral request. Al-Ṭabarī sets this verse with the Prophet's ﷺ standing practice of calling things and people by their noblest names, and connects it to the Qur'an's own etiquette-legislation in Sūrah al-Nūr: *do not treat the Messenger's summons to you as lightly as your summons to one another* (24:63) — the address of the Prophet ﷺ is not the address of an equal, and words whose idiom implies equality are barred from it regardless of the speaker's heart. The second proposed ground is the Hebrew wordplay — that the Jews exploited the word's resemblance to a term of abuse, and the ban removed their instrument. Al-Ṭabarī discounts this reading *for this verse* with a sharp argument: the command falls on the believers, who would not have adopted a phrase of insult they did not understand; the wordplay belongs to the 4:46 account of the abusers, not to the legislation addressed to the sincere. The weighing matters because it fixes the verse's principle: the ban protects the dignity of the address itself, not merely against one group's mischief. A word can be removed from sacred use because of what it sounds like in the culture's ear, even when every user means well — and the community's speech to its Prophet ﷺ is regulated at the level of idiom, not merely at the level of intention.

**Unẓurnā and the Command to Listen**

The substitute comes with its own instruction: *say unẓurnā, and listen attentively*. The verb *unẓurnā* — regard us, attend to us, and in other contexts wait for us, as in the Day-scene where the hypocrites beg the believers to *wait for us so that we may have some of your light* (57:13) — carries the same request as *rāʿinā* with the demeaning idiom stripped out: the believers may still ask the Prophet's ﷺ regard, still ask him to pace himself to them, still ask for the audience a learner needs. The content of the request survives; its cultural packaging is replaced. The verse's second imperative then completes the instruction: *and listen* — which the exegetes read as listening in its full sense, hearing that includes obeying, the same hearing-and-obedience the covenant demanded and the community of verse 93 refused with its inverted formula. The pairing of the two commands gives the verse's positive program: ask respectfully, then listen obediently. The speech-legislation is not only protection of the Prophet's ﷺ dignity but formation of the believers' learning posture — a community that addresses its teacher in the idiom of regard and receives his teaching in the idiom of obedience has structured its education as worship, and the verse's small lexical surgery is the recital's way of building that structure at the level where language lives.

**The Punishment Clause**

The verse ends with a sentence that appears to change subject entirely: *and the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment*. The shift is not as abrupt as it reads. The commentators locate the clause's referents in the word's abusers — the mockers who turned an idiom into an insult, whose wordplay Sūrah al-Nisāʾ files as playing with words and discrediting the faith — and on that reading the clause seals the legislation with its sanction: the same syllables that cost the believers a vocabulary change cost their abusers a sentence. But the clause's generality — *the disbelievers*, unnamed, unqualified — extends its coverage past any single occasion, and the extension is the verse's structural point. Speech that toys with the honor of revelation's bearer is not a misdemeanor of manners; in the recital's taxonomy it belongs to disbelief and draws disbelief's penalty. The verse has thus legislated in both directions at once: to the believers, whose innocent idiom is withdrawn lest dignity suffer by proximity; to the mockers, whose calculated idiom is answered with the punishment-clause. Between the two audiences stands the principle the whole verse enacts — that the words a community uses toward its Prophet ﷺ are themselves religious acts, weighted, audited, and legislated, and that the recital cares about the texture of address closely enough to ban a single word and install a better one in its place.

**The Discipline of Substituted Words**

The verse's transfer runs to every community's vocabulary. Religions accumulate idioms — forms of address, honorifics, casual phrases around sacred things — and the idioms drift: familiarity erodes the reverence they once carried, and outside tongues learn to hide abuse inside them. Verse 104 is the recital's precedent for managing the drift, and its method is precise. It does not lecture on reverence in the abstract; it names one word, bans it, and supplies its replacement — legislation at the granularity of vocabulary, where reverence actually lives. The method implies a standing audit for the community that receives it: which of our words for sacred things carry, in the culture's ear, a tone we would not choose if we heard it from outside? Which idioms of intimacy have become idioms of cheapness? The believers of Madina did not know their word had a demeaning register — the revelation knew it for them, which is the verse's quiet claim about the reach of revealed legislation: it regulates what sincere users cannot self-diagnose. And the installed substitute teaches the replacement's spirit: *unẓurnā* keeps the request and drops the contempt, which means the discipline is not silence but better speech — reverence is not the absence of familiar address but its purification. The listening-command attached to the substitute completes the verse's curriculum for every generation: ask in the words respect provides, and hear as those who intend to obey — while the punishment-clause stands over the whole economy of words, a reminder that in the recital's accounting, speech about the sacred is never merely speech.
