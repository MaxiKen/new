## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:108

> These are Allah’s revelations We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth. And Allah desires no injustice to ˹His˺ creation.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Revelations Described as Recited**

The verse states that what has been set out in the preceding passage consists of God's revelations recited in truth, and it adds that God desires no injustice for any of His creation. Both sentences are about the message itself and about the one who sent it.

The formula used here appears elsewhere in the Book in nearly identical words. One passage states that these are God's revelations recited in truth and then tells the Prophet that he is truly one of the messengers (2:252). The pairing of the two statements in that verse shows what the formula does: it identifies the material as revelation and locates the one reciting it within the sequence of those sent by God.

**Truth as a Property of the Recitation**

The truth in the verse is not offered as a claim to be tested later. The passage has presented commands, warnings, historical examples, and the descriptions of two outcomes, and the sentence declares the whole of it to be true.

The Book makes this kind of declaration at the close of passages that have answered objections at length, and the effect is to place the reader's response inside a settled frame. The matter has been explained, the objections have been addressed, and the revelation is stated to be what it claims. A reader who has followed the argument from the opening of the sūrah through the disputes about Abraham, the covenant, and the warning about division arrives at a sentence that tells him the account he has been reading is reliable.

**The Omission of the Addressee's Name**

The verse omits the name of the one being addressed, where the parallel passage adds that he is truly one of the messengers. The omission is the kind of variation the Book often uses in repeated formulas.

The reason lies in what the verse is doing at this point. The passage has just described the outcome for two groups, and the attention is on the message and its reliability rather than on the person receiving it. The next sentence states that God does not desire injustice for His creation, and the two sentences together form a statement about the revelation and about its giver, which is where the passage needs the reader to arrive.

**The Statement About Injustice**

The Arabic word translated as injustice describes the placing of something outside its proper place. The verse's sentence denies that God desires such a thing for any of the worlds, and the commentators offered two explanations that fit the wording.

Al-Rāzī read it as a denial that God desires human beings to commit wrong. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī connected the sentence with the verse that follows and read it as a statement that God has no need to wrong anyone, since everything belongs to Him and His power is complete. A third explanation, recorded among the commentators, takes the sentence as the plain statement that God does not in fact wrong anyone. Each reading denies injustice; they differ over whether the denial concerns God's will for human acts or the conduct of God himself.

**The Question the Verse Raises in Theology**

The sentence about injustice became one of the points at which the schools of theology worked out their positions on God's relation to good and evil. One school held that wrong is by definition a trespass on what belongs to another, and that since everything belongs to God, nothing He does can be described as wrong. Another held that justice and injustice are objective features of the world that human beings can recognise, and that God is always just by that standard.

The schools agreed about the conclusion the verse states and differed about how to explain it. What neither position loses is the practical point, which is that no person will be treated unjustly on the day the passage has described, and the passage leaves a statement about a person's own case rather than a puzzle about God's.

**The Distinction Between Two Kinds of Divine Command**

Some theologians drew a distinction between what God asks of human beings and what He brings into existence, calling the first the command of obligation and the second the command of creation.

On that distinction, a person can disobey what God has asked of him, and cannot prevent what God has decided to bring about. The verse's denial of injustice fits this account: God's requests to human beings can be refused, and what He wills to be cannot, so that the wrong that occurs in the world belongs to the creature's choice rather than to the creator's desire. The sūrah's passage has been built on exactly this understanding of human responsibility, and this verse states the divine side of it.

**How the Sentence Closes the Passage**

The verse brings the passage to a close by returning to the revelation and its source. The commands, the examples, the two outcomes, and the warning against division are all gathered under the statement that these are God's revelations, recited in truth, from a Lord who does not wish injustice for anyone.

That is the point at which the sūrah's argument reaches its conclusion for this stretch, and what follows returns to a statement about ownership and return. The reader has been shown what he must do and what awaits two kinds of people, and the verse assures him that the account of both is true and that the one who gave it is not unjust.

**The Two Sentences as a Single Answer**

The verse's two statements answer two different anxieties, and the passage needs both. The first answers the question of whether what has been recited can be trusted; the second answers the question of whether the Lord who sent it will deal justly with the people who receive it.

Taken together they close the argument the sūrah has been conducting through this stretch. The community is told that the message is true and that its Lord does not wish injustice to anyone, so that neither doubt about the revelation nor fear about the judge remains available as an excuse. The verse that follows adds the final statement about ownership and the return of all matters, and the three sentences bring the section to its end before the sūrah turns to the community's identity.

**The Ownership That Follows the Denial**

The sentence about injustice is followed immediately by the declaration that whatever is in the heavens and on the earth belongs to God and that all matters are returned to Him, and Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭūbī read the two together. What is stated in the second sentence answers the difficulty of the first. The one who judges owns everything he judges and holds everything that the judgment concerns, and the essay on the schools of theology traces how the community's thinkers worked this out: those who held that wrong means taking what belongs to another concluded that nothing God does can be described in that way, while those who held that justice and injustice are features of the world recognizable to human beings concluded that God is always just. The verse itself states the denial of injustice and leaves the argument to those who came after it, and the sentence that follows supplies the fact on which every version of the argument rests.
