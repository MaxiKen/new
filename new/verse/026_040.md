## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:40

> so that we may follow the magicians if they prevail?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Crowd Is Invited to Follow Victory Rather Than Truth**

The purpose of the gathering is stated plainly: the people may follow the magicians if they prevail. The condition makes victory the measure of allegiance. Instead of asking whether Moses is a true messenger or whether the Israelites deserve freedom, the public is asked to wait for a winner and attach itself to that winner. A religious and moral question is reduced to a contest of outcomes.

This framing favors Pharaoh before the contest begins. The magicians already represent the ruler's side, and their success will be presented as a public vindication. The people are not told that they might follow Moses if his sign prevails. The missing alternative reveals the limits of the invitation. It is not an open search for truth; it is a managed expectation of royal victory.

**The Omitted Alternative Exposes Propaganda**

Commentators noticed what the sentence leaves unsaid: no one proposes following Moses if the magicians fail. That omission may reflect the political risk of acknowledging Moses as a possible guide. If the crowd were invited to follow whichever claim proved true, Pharaoh's authority would already be placed below the evidence. The court cannot allow that possibility to be named.

The silence is therefore meaningful. Propaganda often controls not only what is said but what options are made imaginable. By presenting one acceptable winner, Pharaoh's organizers tell the people that loyalty to the throne is the normal conclusion. Moses' victory, if it occurs, will have to break through an option the public language has deliberately hidden.

**Prevailing Is Not the Same as Being Right**

The word prevail concerns what appears to win before an audience. A skilled performer can prevail in applause, political influence, or control of the story without possessing the truth. The verse exposes the danger of treating success as proof. If the magicians impress the crowd, the crowd may follow them even if their performance has not answered Moses' claim.

Truth can be outnumbered, mocked, or temporarily defeated in public. A community that follows victory alone will follow whoever controls the stage. Faith requires another measure: the source of the claim, the reality of the sign, and the moral direction it demands. The event will show why a person must not let a crowd's excitement decide the meaning of evidence.

**The Invitation Makes Belief Conditional on Spectacle**

The people are asked to suspend judgment until the contest's result. This can sound cautious, but it teaches them to seek spectacle rather than guidance. The message from Moses has already been stated, and the sign has already appeared. Yet the court offers the crowd a more entertaining standard: watch the performance and choose the side that looks stronger.

Such a standard is unstable. The crowd may follow one performer today and another tomorrow. It gives authority to whoever can control attention, not to the One who deserves worship. Pharaoh's system needs the people to believe that power is decided by display because display is the one arena it thinks it can manage.

**Following the Magicians Would Mean Following the Court**

The magicians are not independent candidates for religious authority. They have been gathered by Pharaoh's order and are being placed before the people as defenders of the existing order. To follow them if they prevail is therefore likely to mean accepting Pharaoh's interpretation of the event and preserving his claim over Israel.

The language hides that connection. It speaks of following magicians rather than obeying Pharaoh, making the choice appear about expertise. But the court has chosen the experts, set the appointment, and supplied the audience. The contest is designed to make royal policy look like the neutral result of technical skill. The people must see through that arrangement if they are to judge it justly.

**A Crowd's Faith Can Be Captured by Its Expectations**

Before the event begins, the people have been told whom they may follow and under what condition. Expectations guide perception. If the magicians produce a striking display, the crowd may interpret the sight through the promise already given. What it expected to see becomes what it believes it saw. This is why truthful judgment requires the courage to question the frame surrounding an event.

The verse does not accuse every person in the crowd of deliberate deception. Many may simply accept the options offered by authority. That makes the responsibility of the organizers greater, not smaller. Leaders shape public imagination, and they can make a false choice appear to be the only choice. The people are called to more than reaction; they are called to notice what the invitation leaves out.

**The Conditional Invitation Still Leaves a Door to Recognition**

Although the court intends the contest to support Pharaoh, the condition also means that the people are watching for a result. Their attention may be redirected when the result contradicts the official expectation. A managed event can escape its managers when the evidence is too strong to absorb.

The people therefore stand at a moral crossroads. They can follow the appearance of victory, or they can ask what the victory means and whose authority it reveals. The court has prepared a public contest, but God can turn the contest into a public exposure of falsehood. The invitation to follow the magicians will soon be tested by what the crowd actually sees.

The court has made a promise about how the people should respond, but a promise is not a proof. The crowd can still refuse the frame imposed upon it by asking whether the apparent victor has answered Moses or merely produced an appearance. A public result becomes guidance only when its meaning is judged truthfully.
