## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:145

> Even if you were to bring every proof to the People of the Book, they would not accept your direction ˹of prayer˺, nor would you accept theirs; nor would any of them accept the direction ˹of prayer˺ of another. And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, then you would certainly be one of the wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Evidence Has a Limit, and This Verse Names It**

The qiblah verses have so far argued: the directions belong to God, the change was a test, the new direction pleases the Prophet and the Scripture-people know it is true. This verse stops arguing and states a fact about the opponents that no further argument can alter: *even if you were to bring every proof*, they would not accept your direction. The conditional is maximal — every sign, every kind of evidence exhausted — and the refusal survives it. The sūrah of Jonah states the same law with its mechanism exposed: *"Indeed, those against whom Allah's decree ˹of torment˺ is justified will not believe—even if every sign were to come to them—until they see the painful punishment"* (10:96–97). The point is not that evidence is worthless; the qiblah passage is itself full of evidence. The point is that evidence persuades only where the will is open, and against a closed will the total argument and the partial argument produce the identical result. The verse liberates the community from the obligation of endless proof: there comes a term after which further demonstration changes nothing, and knowing that term saves the believer from mistaking every refusal for his own failure to argue well.

**The Qiblah as the Border of a Community**

The refusal is specifically about direction because direction is specifically about belonging. Jews traditionally face Jerusalem; Christian churches were historically oriented toward the east and the rising sun; and to face a community's qiblah is to be inside it, while to face another's is to be outside. The commentators draw the consequence bluntly: to say that someone follows a different qiblah is tantamount to excluding him from the religious community in question. The qiblah is thus not one ritual detail among many but the community's spatial signature — the bodily form of its creed. This is why the verse treats the three directions as mutually impossible rather than negotiable: *nor would you accept theirs; nor would any of them accept the direction of another*. There is no shared direction and no compromise bearing, because a direction adopted by negotiation would belong to no one's faith. The incompatibility stated here is not a lament but a clarification of what was never on the table.

**Facing Our Qiblah, Praying Our Prayer**

The identity function of the direction was fixed in the community's own law by a report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (393). Maymūn ibn Siyāh asked Anas ibn Mālik what makes a person's life and property sacred, and Anas answered with the Prophet's words: whoever says none has the right to be worshipped but Allah, faces our qiblah, prays our prayer, and eats our slaughtered animal — that person is a Muslim, and has the same rights and obligations as other Muslims. Three marks, and one of them is the direction of prayer: the confession of the tongue, the orientation of the body in worship, and the shared table of what is slaughtered in God's name. The ḥadīth shows what the verse's incompatibility means in practice — the qiblah is a constitutive marker of the community's boundary, so accepting another community's direction would not be a gesture of goodwill but an act of defection from the creed's visible form.

**Desires Against Knowledge**

The verse's second half shifts from the opponents to the Prophet — and through him, as always with such addresses, to the community: *if you were to follow their desires after all the knowledge that has come to you, you would certainly be one of the wrongdoers*. The two terms are chosen to be exact opposites. *Ahwāʾ* — desires, caprices, whims — is the plural of a word denoting what the soul inclines to without warrant; *knowledge* is what arrives from God with warrant. The religious life, on this verse's schema, is governed by one or the other, and there is no tertium: a direction of prayer is either received as knowledge or adopted as desire. The same warning brackets this section of the sūrah from its start — *if you were to follow their desires after all the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or help you against Allah* (2:120) — and recurs in the Medinan sūrahs with the same architecture: do not follow their desires over the truth that has come to you (5:48); if you followed their desires after the knowledge that has come to you, none would protect you from Allah (13:37). The repetition marks a standing danger rather than a historical one: the pull toward the opponents' ways does not cease when the argument is won.

**The Severity of the Address**

That the warning is addressed to the Prophet — who would never follow their desires — has a double function the exegetes recognized. It states the law at its maximum severity: if even the recipient of the knowledge would become one of the wrongdoers by trading it for desire, the calculation for anyone holding less knowledge is correspondingly bleaker. And it protects the community's future by putting the principle beyond personal exemption: no one's spiritual rank insulates him from the category. The wording refuses mitigation — *you would certainly be one of the wrongdoers*, not "you would be excused" or "you would err slightly." Knowledge, in this economy, is not credit but responsibility; the more clearly the direction has been shown, the graver the wrong of turning from it toward what merely feels agreeable. The verse thus completes the qiblah's moral: the direction was assigned by God, it is known by the Scripture-people to be true, it will not be accepted by those who refuse, and for those who have accepted it there is no legitimate road back — only knowledge followed, or desire obeyed and the name of wrongdoer earned.

**No Middle Direction Exists**

Read as a whole, the verse closes a door that the controversy might otherwise have left ajar: the door of the negotiated middle. Since each community's direction is its boundary, and since the boundaries are divinely assigned rather than humanly chosen, no ecumenical compromise-bearing is available — neither a physical direction between Jerusalem and Mecca nor a theological averaging of the creeds that face them. The community's task is not convergence toward a shared center but faithfulness to its own revealed orientation, coupled with the race in good deeds that the passage will soon command. This is the verse's practical legacy for interreligious life as the Qur'an frames it: honest incompatibility at the level of the boundary, and competition in virtue at the level of conduct — never a faked agreement about direction, because a faked direction would be desire wearing the clothes of knowledge.
