## Sūrah an-Naml 27:57

> So We delivered him and his family, except his wife. We had destined her to be one of the doomed.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception Inside the Rescue**

The deliverance is granted and immediately qualified. Him and his household — except his wife. The Qur'an states it without elaboration and moves on, and the flatness of the report is part of its force.

This is the counterpart to what happened at Thamūd, where the destruction was total and the believers were excepted from it. Here the rescue is the general case and one person is excepted from that. The two constructions together describe a single principle operating in both directions: the sorting is by the individual, and neither proximity to the condemned nor proximity to the saved transfers. A believer inside a doomed city came out; the wife of a prophet, inside the rescued household, did not.

**Marriage to a Prophet Saved Nobody**

The verse is the Qur'an's clearest statement that religious standing is not inherited or acquired by association. The woman lived in the house of a messenger of God, shared his table, and was part of the household the rescue covered.

The Book presses this point elsewhere with the same example. Sūrah at-Taḥrīm sets the wives of Nūḥ and Lūṭ side by side as a parable for those who disbelieve — both married to righteous servants, both of no avail to their husbands — and then places against them the wife of Pharaoh, who believed inside the house of the chapter's worst tyrant. The pairing is deliberate and it cuts both ways. Neither the best nor the worst household determines what the person inside it becomes. Lūṭ's wife appears in five other chapters of the Qur'an, and in every one of them the same exception is recorded. That consistency across passages of very different length and emphasis indicates how central the point was held to be.

**"Among Those Who Lagged Behind"**

The word describing her fate is *al-ghābirīn*, from a root meaning to remain behind after others have gone. It is not the ordinary word for the destroyed or the punished.

The choice is precise and it identifies the form her failure took. She stayed. When the household was brought out, she did not come, or did not come fully, and other accounts of the story add detail about a backward glance and a delay. What the term names is a relation to the departure rather than a crime committed in the city. The Qur'an's other accounts of her never specify what she believed; what they specify, consistently, is that she was of those who lagged. Her offence, in the way the Book chooses to record it, was an attachment to the place that was being left.

**"We Destined Her"**

The verb is *qaddarnāhā*, we decreed or measured it for her, and the phrasing raises the old question about foreknowledge and choice. If her outcome was decreed, in what sense was it hers?

The Qur'an's consistent practice is to state both the decree and the responsibility without reconciling them philosophically, and this verse follows it. Verse 4 of this chapter attributed the adornment of evil deeds to God, and the whole surrounding argument held the deniers accountable; the same structure appears here in miniature. What the classical positions generally maintain is that divine decree includes the choice rather than bypassing it — what was measured for her was the outcome of what she would do. Whether that satisfies as an answer is a question the Qur'an does not take up here. What the verse asserts is that her fate was known and fixed, and that she was one of a household given every advantage and did not use it.

**Nothing Is Said About Why**

This chapter gives no account of her conduct. No statement of hers is quoted, no act is described, and no motive is supplied. She appears in a subordinate clause and disappears.

The reticence matches everything else an-Naml has withheld — the name of Sheba's queen, the contents of her gift, the identity of the man with knowledge of the Book. What the chapter preserves in each case is the part that bears on its argument, and here the argument needs only the exception itself. A reader who wants to know what she did is reaching for a story the Qur'an is not telling. The story it is telling is about who comes out of a city and who does not, and the sorting principle is the point rather than the biography. What fell on those who remained is described next.

**The Household Is Rescued as a Household**

The rescue covers *him and his family*, a unit rather than a list of qualifying individuals. That framing is worth holding alongside the exception, because the two together describe how the Qur'an handles collective and individual fate.

Ordinarily the household travels as one. Nūḥ's family boards the ark, Ibrāhīm's leaves with him, Lūṭ's is brought out. The Book does not treat faith as a purely private matter with no social shape; a believing household is a real thing and its members are extracted together. Sharing a roof with people who believe exposes a person to the argument daily and makes the choice easier than it would otherwise be, and the Qur'an counts that as a genuine advantage. What the exception establishes is that the unit is not a container that protects whatever is inside it. Membership is the normal channel of rescue and it is not a guarantee, and the difference between those two statements is the whole content of this verse. Nūḥ's son, refused a place on the ark in another chapter, makes the same point from the other side.
