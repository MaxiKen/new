## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:180

> It is prescribed that when death approaches any of you—if they leave something of value—a will should be made in favour of parents and immediate family with fairness. ˹This is˺ an obligation on those who are mindful ˹of Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Deathbed Gets a Law**

The verse uses the sūrah's legislative verb — it is *prescribed*, the same word that set the retaliation (2:178) and will set the fast (2:183) — and applies it to the hour of dying. When death approaches and the dying person leaves *khayr*, something of value, a bequest is to be made for parents and near kin in fairness. The word *khayr* — good — standing alone for property is established Quranic usage: whatever good you spend is for your own sake (2:272); the sūrah of the Chargers describes the human being as fierce in his love of good things, meaning wealth (100:8). The law's trigger is thus twofold: the approach of death and the existence of something to leave. The exegetes read the second condition as limiting the first — the bequest-legislation addresses those who die holding property, and it does not disturb the prior claims on that property, since debts and financial obligations must be discharged from the estate even after death. The verse installs the will as the dying person's last lawful act: a final disposition, bounded by fairness, supervised by the community, and owed to the people closest in blood.

**Parents and Kin, in Fairness**

The beneficiaries are named in an order that carries moral weight: parents first, then the near relatives. The fairness qualifier — *bi-l-maʿrūf*, in what is honorable and fitting — governs both the amounts and the manner, and the early commentators pressed its social edge: bequests are to be made first to relatives and only secondarily to others, and those who left relatives in need while bequeathing property to strangers were severely condemned. The ruling answers a standing temptation of the deathbed — the wish to make a grand gesture to a friend, a teacher, an institution, while the family that will survive the testator inherits poverty. The Qur'an's economy of charity consistently orders the claimants by nearness: the same roster of kinsfolk, orphans, and the poor structures the giving of 2:177 and 2:215 and the kindness-command of 4:36. Fairness here is therefore not equality but proportion to claim — the parent's claim, the child's, the dependent relative's — assessed by the honorableness the word *maʿrūf* denotes: what the community's best judgment recognizes as right, without the need for a schedule.

**The Two Limits the Law Grew**

From this verse the jurists developed the bequest's two famous restrictions, and both have textual anchors. The first: a bequest may not exceed one-third of the estate. The proof-text is the report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (1295): Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, gravely ill in the year of the Farewell Pilgrimage and wealthy with only a daughter to inherit, asked the visiting Prophet whether he might give two-thirds of his property in charity — no; half — no; and the Prophet set the ceiling: a third, and even a third is much. Then the reason: it is better to leave your heirs wealthy than to leave them poor, begging from people. The second restriction: no bequest may be made in favor of a person who already inherits by the Quranic shares — the widely transmitted prophetic saying "there is no bequest for an heir." Together the two rules protect the fixed inheritance system from the deathbed's affections and grudges: the shares of 4:11–12 stand untouched by wills, and the will's own space — the third, the non-heir — is preserved for exactly the cases the shares cannot reach.

**Was the Verse Abrogated?**

The second restriction generated one of Islamic legal theory's most instructive debates, because it appears to collide with this verse's own command to bequeath to parents and kin — who are usually heirs. Three positions are recorded. Some held the verse wholly abrogated by the inheritance-verses of the sūrah of the Women, which assigned the shares directly. Others — al-Ṭabarī among them — held it partially abrogated: superseded for those entitled to a fixed share, still in force for relatives outside the quota system. A third group, including the Shīʿī jurists, denied abrogation entirely, harmonizing the texts and reading the verse as fully operative. Beneath the positions lies a constitutional question: the saying "no bequest for an heir" is Sunnah, and its acceptance as binding showed al-Qurṭubī that the Sunnah can abrogate the Qur'an — a position others rejected on epistemic grounds, since a report carrying less certainty than the Qur'an's transmitted text cannot repeal its ruling. Al-Qurṭubī himself acknowledged that this ḥadīth is the only text preventing full harmonization, and harmonizers proposed readings such as restricting the verse's parents and kin to those who would not inherit anyway — a disbelieving parent, for instance, outside the quota system. The debate is the verse's living afterlife: a single sentence about the deathbed became the test-case for how revelation's two sources relate.

**Before the Shares: The Arabian Background**

The verse also marks a transition in property history. Before the Quranic legislation, Arabian inheritance was agnatic — the male line took the estate, with custom excluding women and minors from succession entirely. The Quranic shares reorganized the field around fixed quotas for named relatives, including the women the custom had passed over: the male's share twice the female's, the daughter's half or two-thirds, the parents' sixths, the spouses' quarters and eighths (4:11–12) — all disbursed, as those verses repeatedly insist, *after the fulfilment of bequests and debts*, which is this verse's institution embedded in the arithmetic. The customary agnatic heirs were thereby demoted to a secondary position under Sunni law, inheriting the residue after the quota shares; Shīʿī law rejected agnatic priority altogether, distributing the remainder by proximity of blood-kinship through both male and female lines. Within that architecture the bequest of 2:180 holds its office: the testator's personal word, operating in the third, reaching the people and purposes the fixed shares do not name — the law's provision for the dying heart, fenced so it cannot trample the heirs.

**An Obligation on the Mindful**

The verse closes by naming its addressees' quality: this is an obligation upon the reverent — upon the people of taqwā. The formula does ceremonial work. A bequest is the one legal act performed at the moment when all other sanctions are failing: no court compels the dying, no reputation is being built, no force can reach a man on his bed. The law that governs that hour must therefore be carried by the one virtue that survives it — the fear and mindfulness of God — and the verse says so, assigning its execution to conscience. The same formula will return over the retaliation's rationale (so that you may be mindful, 2:179) and the fast's purpose (2:183), building the passage's pattern: each law is given, then grounded in taqwā. The dying testator who bequeaths in fairness, within the third, without cheating an heir, performs the definition of righteousness the previous verses supplied — the kept pledge extended to the pledge no one will be alive to enforce. The verse's placement between the murder-law and the fast is exact: blood regulated, property regulated, appetite next — and over all three, the same Presider, the mindful heart.
