## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:60

> That is so. And whoever retaliates in equivalence to the injury they have received, and then are wronged ˹again˺, Allah will certainly help them. Surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Seal**

*Dhālika* — that is how it is. The demonstrative appears here for the fourth time in the sūrah, closing the passage on the emigrants just as it closed the passages on the pilgrimage (22:30) and the rites (22:32).

Each time the word does the same work: it declares a matter settled and then turns to what follows from it. Here what has been settled is that God provides for those who emigrate and admits them to a place they are pleased with, and what follows is a ruling about retaliation.

The sūrah uses the pointer in two distinct ways, and the difference is instructive. At 22:30 and 22:32 it stands alone, sealing a block of instruction: what has been said is to be taken as stated. At 22:60 and 22:62 it is followed by *bi-anna*, because: a ground is about to be supplied. The retaliator's permission and the promise of help are therefore not asserted twice in a row. The first is asserted and sealed, and the four verses that follow give the reasons.

**"Whoever Retaliates With the Like of What He Suffered"**

*Wa-man ʿāqaba bi-mithli mā ʿūqiba bihi* — and whoever retaliates with the equivalent of what he was punished with.

The structure of the phrase is a built-in limit. The retaliation is measured against the original injury, and the measurement is the point: the verse authorises a response and fixes its size in the same clause.

Sūrah al-Naḥl states the rule and then the preference in one sentence: *“If you retaliate, then let it be equivalent to what you have suffered. But if you patiently endure, it is certainly best for those who are patient”* (16:126). Sūrah al-Shūrā does the same: *“The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah”* (42:40).

The question of the sacred month — which is where the reported occasion of this verse bites — is settled on the same principle of measure rather than on the calendar. *"˹There will be retaliation in˺ a sacred month for ˹an offence in˺ a sacred month, and all violations will bring about retaliation"* (2:194). The sanctity of the month is real, and so is the right of reply within it; what is prohibited is the excess, not the response.

**Equivalence Is What Separates a Response From Revenge**

The restriction to equivalence is what separates retaliation from revenge, and the distinction is not a matter of degree.

Revenge has no natural stopping point; it escalates, and the escalation is the point, since the gratification is in the excess. Equivalence has a stopping point built into it, and the stopping point is the original injury. A response that matches the injury can end; a response that exceeds it cannot, because the excess becomes a new injury requiring its own answer.

The Qur'an's law of retaliation in cases of homicide rests on exactly this logic, and it is stated with an explicit preference for waiving it: *“But if the offender is pardoned by the victim’s guardian, then bloodmoney should be decided fairly and payment should be made courteously. This is a concession and a mercy from your Lord”* (2:178).

**The Reported Occasion, and How Much Weight It Bears**

Several commentators relate this verse to a specific incident: a party of Muslims met idolaters during Muḥarram, one of the sacred months in which the Arabs themselves held fighting to be forbidden. The idolaters pressed for a fight despite the taboo; the Muslims responded in kind; and afterwards they were troubled by what they had done. [UNVERIFIED: this occasion is reported by al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī in the material available to me without a chain or grading I could check in this session.]

If the report is sound, the verse's ruling is sharper than it first appears. The Muslims' scruple was about fighting in a sacred month, and the revelation's response was not to rebuke them but to define the limit: responding in proportion is permitted even under those conditions, provided the response does not exceed the injury.

If the report is not sound, the general reading still holds. Al-Ṭabarī took the verse as a general description of the believers who had been persecuted in Makkah and driven to Madīnah, and that reading fits the sūrah's argument seamlessly, since the expulsion has been the sūrah's stated grievance since verse 40.

**"Then Was Oppressed Again"**

*Thumma bughiya ʿalayhi* — then, after that, he was wronged again.

The sequence matters. The person described has already responded proportionately and has then been subjected to further wrong. He has not escalated, and he has not been left alone either.

This is the case the verse addresses, and it is the hard case. A person who has been wronged once and responded in kind has done what the law permits. A person who has then been wronged *again* is in a different position, and the promise of help is attached to him specifically.

The implication is that the help is not promised because the retaliation was clever but because the injury was repeated. The second wrong establishes where the aggression actually lies.

The verb is passive, as it was in verse 58: he *was* transgressed against. Nothing in the clause credits him with an action, and the passive is what makes the promise coherent. He is the object of the second wrong, not its author, and what the verse promises him is help rather than permission — the help is God's to give, and it is not described as a second round of retaliation to be taken by his hand.

**"God Will Help Him"**

*La-yanṣurannahu Allāhu* — God will certainly help him. The emphatic form, with the doubled *n*, is the strongest available promise in Arabic.

The object of the promise is the person who stayed within the limit. That is the connection the verse is making, and it is the same connection the sūrah made in verse 39: the permission to fight was granted to people who had been wronged, and the help was declared to be God's to give rather than a consequence of taking up arms.

A community that has just been authorised to use force is now told that its help comes from God and that the condition attached to it is proportion.

**The Closing Names Are the Difficulty**

*Inna Allāha la-ʿafuwwun ghafūr* — God is certainly Pardoning, Forgiving.

The two names at the end of a verse about retaliation are the verse's most demanding element, and they are not decorative.

*ʿAfuww* is the intensive form of pardoning — the one who effaces, who wipes the offence out as though it had not been written. *Ghafūr* is the one who covers. Both describe what God does with offences against Himself, and the verse places them immediately after authorising a response to offences against a person.

The juxtaposition is the instruction. Retaliation is permitted within a limit, and the character of the One who permits it is pardon. A person who takes the permission and forgets the character has read half the verse.

The character is not left as a description of God alone; it is prescribed to the believers in the same terms. *"Let them pardon and forgive. Do you not love to be forgiven by Allah?"* (24:22). The question is a piece of reasoning rather than a piece of piety: the pardon a person wants for himself is the pardon he is being asked to extend, and the verse on retaliation closes by naming the attribute that makes the two ends meet.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Ethics of Force**

Read together, verses 39, 40, 41 and 60 form the sūrah's complete position on the use of force, and it is worth assembling because the parts are scattered.

Fighting is permitted, to those who were fought, because they were wronged (22:39). The grievance is specified: expulsion for a creed (22:40). Power, if granted, carries four duties, two of them public (22:41). Treachery is forbidden (22:38). Retaliation is permitted in exact measure, never in excess (22:60). And the God who authorises all of it is described as pardoning and forgiving.

Nothing in that set is a licence. Every element is a boundary, and the last one is a character description.

The set also has an outer boundary that the sūrah assumes rather than states, and it is worth stating because the objection is predictable. Force is directed at those who wage it: *"Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Surely Allah does not like transgressors"* (2:190). Read with verse 60, the shape of the ethic is a target, a measure and a prohibition on excess — a permission that is hedged on every side, addressed to a community that had been expelled from its homes and is being told to count before it strikes.
