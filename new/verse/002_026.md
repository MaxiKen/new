## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:26

> Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable Defended Before the Attack Lands**

The verse opens in the middle of an argument its hearers were having: *inna Allāha lā yastaḥyī an yaḍriba mathalan mā baʿūḍatan* — Allah is not ashamed to strike a parable of a mosquito, or of anything above it, which the commentators read as anything beyond it in smallness. The occasions reported behind the verse name the objectors and their objection: the hypocrites, stung by the parables of verses 17–20; or Jews scoffing that God would deign to compare idols to a fly (22:73) or the protectors taken beside Him to a spider's house (29:41); or idolaters pressing the same complaint. In every version the charge is one of dignity — such images are beneath divine speech. The verse answers not by raising the images but by lowering the objection: *lā yastaḥyī*, He does not shy away, does not hold back out of propriety. The answer implies a principle of revelation that runs through the whole Book: truth selects its own images, and the selection is made by fitness to the point, not by the social rank of the objects. A God who furnished the world with gnats can speak with gnats.

**The Mosquito and the Scale of Signs**

The choice of *baʿūḍah* — mosquito, gnat — is exact for the argument being answered. The objectors measured divine speech by the grandeur of its contents; the verse answers by naming one of the smallest creatures that nonetheless exists, is formed, feeds, reproduces, and does work in the world no elephant can do. If the objection is that small things cannot carry meaning, the mosquito refutes it by existing at all: the creature that is beneath dignity in the objectors' scale is not beneath creation in the Maker's. The classical scholars drew the corollary into the doctrine of signs — every existent, at every scale, is an *āyah*, and the sign-value of a thing lies in its craftedness, not its size. The clause "or what is even smaller" then pushes past the limit of the visible: whatever lies below the mosquito's scale — what the ancients could only posit, and later ages would actually see — belongs to the same field of signs and the same freedom of divine speech. The verse is, incidentally, one of the Qur'an's standing answers to every aesthetics of the sublime that excludes the small.

**Two Hearers, One Sentence**

The middle of the verse splits its audience with surgical symmetry: as for the believers, they know it is the truth from their Lord; as for the disbelievers, they say, *what does Allah mean by such a parable?* The same utterance produces knowledge in one group and objection in the other, and the verse's point is that the divergence locates itself entirely in the receivers. The believer's clause is worth parsing: he knows *that it is the truth*, and *from his Lord* — recognition of content and of source together, the parable received as both true and sent. The disbeliever's clause is a question that is not a question — *mādhā arāda Llāhu bi-hādhā mathalan* — phrased as inquiry, functioning as protest; the classical commentators note that had the objection been genuine seeking, the verse would have answered it with explanation, which it does not. The sorting happens in real time, in the ear. This is the verse's own claim demonstrated performatively: readers have just watched themselves receive it — some finding in it a principle, some finding in it a difficulty worth arguing with, and the difference between those two responses is the subject matter.

**A Message That Actively Divides**

The verse then states its most difficult doctrine without softening: *yuḍillu bihi kathīran wa-yahdī bihi kathīran* — by it He leaves many astray and guides many. The "it" is the parable — the same small utterance functioning as guidance and as straying simultaneously, and the commentators press exactly this: the effect described is stronger than being ignored. Revelation denied is not neutralized in the denier; it works on him, moving him further from what it says. The verse echoes the sealing of 2:7 and the sickness-increased of 2:10 as one continuous mechanism: divine speech is active substance, and the moral condition of the receiver determines the direction of its action — medicine or irritant, light or dazzle, the same sky that waters the valley deafening the men of the storm. Many scholars read here the Qur'an's fullest statement that guidance and misguidance both pass *through* revelation rather than around it: no one is misled by the Book who was not already positioned to be. The doubles — many astray, many guided — refuse the comfort of rarity in both directions.

**Al-Fāsiqūn: Straying With a Qualification**

The final clause restricts the damage: *wa-mā yuḍillu bihi illa al-fāsiqīn* — He leaves none astray by it except the rebellious, the *fāsiq*. The word comes from a root meaning to exit, to break out — used of the date that bursts its skin, of the animal that leaves the herd — and in Quranic usage names the person who has stepped outside a binding order: the covenant-breaker, the one who transgresses while knowing the line. The restriction answers the charge latent in the verse's own doctrine. If the same parable guides and misleads, is the outcome arbitrary — does God darken whomever He pleases? The clause says no: the straying has a qualification, and the qualification is prior conduct. The *fāsiq* is misled *by* the parable because he was already out of position relative to truth; the parable reveals and completes the exit he began. The exegetes identify the *fāsiqūn* of this passage with the hypocrites just described — men whose rebellion was not ignorance but breach — and the verse thereby hands its reader the test in advance: what this speech does to you will tell you where you are standing.

**An Open Verse, Deliberately Unclosed**

One formal feature of this verse is visible in every printed Qur'an and in this translation's final dash: it does not end its sentence. The description of the rebellious begun here is completed by the next verse, which lists their three breaches — and the sūrah thereby enacts at the level of syntax what the passage teaches at the level of meaning: the *fāsiq* is not defined in a word but in a continuation, his definition running on past the boundary of a single unit. The device also binds the two verses into one reading act; the listener cannot receive verse 26's restriction and stop — the sentence pulls him into the catalogue. Qur'anic verse-ends are famously flexible, sometimes closing a thought, sometimes leaving it mid-stride, and the classical sciences of recitation track these joints with care. Here the open ending serves the argument: straying was just described as a direction rather than an event, a going-out that continues; the grammar performs it by continuing. The dash is not a printer's convenience but the verse's last word — unfinished, on purpose.
