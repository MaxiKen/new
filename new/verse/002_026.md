## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:26

> Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Small Creature Carries a Large Question**

A mosquito weighs almost nothing. It lives a few days, hums, bites, and dies. Yet Allah says He is not shy to set it as a bearing for meaning (2:26). The verse answers an objection behind the previous parables of fire and rain: the complaint that a majestic God would not compare lofty matters to such lowly images. The reply reverses the complaint: smallness does not diminish truth. A gnat can carry theology, and a spider’s house can carry doctrine (29:41), and a fly can carry a proof. The dignity is in what is shown, not the size of the messenger.

Fawqahā — “what is even smaller” — is also read as “what is above it” in littleness or in subtlety. The phrase opens both directions: smaller still than a mosquito, and higher still in hidden precision. Either way, the point stands that nothing is too slight for God to teach through.

**“It Is the Truth from Their Lord” — Why Believers Recognize It**

Believers hear the same parable and say it is al-ḥaqq from their Lord (2:26). Recognition, not novelty, is their mark. The truth of a parable is not in its cleverness but in its coherence with what they already know of Allah: that He speaks by similitude, that He does not disdain reality as it is, and that guidance grows by likeness. Faith gives eyes that see likeness as instruction rather than as embarrassment.

Disbelievers hear the same line and ask, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” (2:26). The wording is dismissal disguised as inquiry. They do not ask to understand; they ask to expose. The same sentence that steadies one heart unsettles another, which leads to the verse’s next clause.

**One Speech That Guides and Lets Stray**

“Through this, He leaves many to stray, and guides many” (2:26). The pronoun points to the parable itself. Revelation does not land neutrally. Its very clarity tests receptivity. For the ready, the image clarifies; for the resistant, the same image hardens. This is not arbitrary misguidance. The verse closes the cause: “He leaves none to stray except the rebellious” (2:26) — al-fāsiqīn, those who have already stepped out of the pact.

The idea mirrors the seal discussed earlier: Allah is the agent of sealing and of leaving to stray, yet the desert is earned. The iniquitous turn the means of guidance into a means of distance, as one who uses medicine as poison by misusing the dose.

**Who Are “the Rebellious” Here**

Al-fāsiq is the one who exits obedience as a date slips from its skin. In this sūrah, the description fits the hypocrites in particular, but the rule is general. Fāsiq is not a label of lineage but of posture: to hear a sign and convert it into mockery, to break a bond and then blame the bond.

The classical reports cited in the source give three occasions for the verse: hypocrites who said God would not strike such parables as the fire and the rain; Jews who mocked parables of fly and spider; and idolaters who raised the same objection. The reports differ on the audience, yet agree on the attitude: disdain for small similitudes. The verse answers the attitude, not only the audience.

**Why Small Similitudes Shame Pride**

A mosquito exposes two prides at once. The proud think truth must arrive in grand forms to be worthy of God; the despisers think religion must avoid the lowly lest it appear weak. Both are corrected. If God creates the mosquito, He may teach by it. To be ashamed of the image is to be ashamed of the created world’s capacity to signify. Creation is not only scenery; it is script.

This also answers a modern echo that says parables are “unsophisticated.” The Qur’an treats sophistication differently: a precise likeness that helps a heart move is more sophisticated than abstraction that leaves it cold. A mosquito’s wing, timed and veined, is intricate enough to bear a point about guidance and misguidance without straining the point.

**Not Every Hardening Is Forced**

“Through this, He leaves many to stray” (2:26) is sometimes misread as coercion. The sūrah has already shown the balance: Allah seals as a sealing that follows persistent denial, and He leaves to stray as a leaving that follows rebellion. The Qur’an pairs this with “He guides many” in the same breath to show the same speech can do opposite work according to the hearer. The sun hardens clay and softens wax; the difference is not in the sun.

The phrase “except the rebellious” (2:26) therefore safeguards meaning: the straying counted here is not that of the confused seeker but of the exiters, those who have already violated a trust.

**What the Parable Teaches About Reading Parables**

Three counsels emerge for reading 2:26 onward:

- Read for truth, not for prestige. Ask not “is this image grand?” but “is this image true to what it portrays?”
- Expect division. A truthful parable will not flatter everyone equally; it will comfort the truthful and expose the cavilling.
- Watch the self as much as the sentence. Moral quality conditions understanding; the rebellious make truth look like error because they bring rebellion to the reading.

**Living the Verse Today**

The contemporary form of “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” (2:26) appears whenever a caller says religion must dress in the idiom of the academy to be respectable, or whenever a detractor says a Book that mentions gnats cannot be divine. The verse invites a steadier posture: let the mosquito remain a mosquito and still teach. In daʿwah, small, honest images often travel farther than elaborate theories; in self-reform, a small verse well understood can redirect a life that grand speeches have failed to move. The test is not over whether God can use small things, but over whether we can be guided by them.

**The Link Forward**

The next verse will define what rebellion looks like in deeds — breaking a pact, severing what God has joined, and corrupting the earth (2:27) — so that “the rebellious” does not remain an abstract tag but becomes recognizable conduct.
