## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:26

> Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable That Needed Defending**

The verse answers an objection rather than making an argument, which means the objection came first. The reports connect it with the two parables about the fire and the storm earlier in this sūrah, and with other passages where the Book compares its opponents to a fly or a spider: *“those ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah can never create ˹so much as˺ a fly”* (22:73), and the shelter of a spider (29:41). Critics found these comparisons undignified and said so, and the reply opens by naming the criticism.

The Arabic word rendered shy away carries the sense of being restrained by shame, and the sentence denies that God can be restrained in this way. What the Book refuses is the premise of the complaint — that some subjects are too small to be used for serious instruction. The reply does not apologise for the comparison, and it does not offer a grander one.

**A Gnat, or Something Smaller Still**

The creature named is the gnat or mosquito, and the phrase that follows it has two readings. It can mean something above it in size, which extends the range upward, or something beyond it in insignificance, which extends it downward. One scholar noted that the second reading makes the point sharper: if God can take the smallest creature in sight and build a lesson on it, then no part of creation is excluded from that use.

The Book's argument for this position is stated elsewhere in its own terms, when it calls attention to the creation of that sky and that earth as signs for people who reflect, and when it tells the reader that everything in the heavens and the earth serves a purpose given by its maker. Nothing in a world so constructed is too trivial to be evidence, and the objection to the gnat is finally an objection to the idea that God's teaching can reach that low.

**One Parable, Two Responses**

The verse then divides its audience by reaction. The believers know the parable is the truth from their Lord; the disbelievers ask what God meant by it. The believers are not described as understanding every detail of the comparison—they are described as accepting that it comes from the right source—while the critics are not described as failing to grasp a point but as turning a question into an accusation.

The Qur'an gives the same question to the same people in another passage, where the argument is about the number of angels guarding the fire: *“so that those ˹hypocrites˺ with sickness in their hearts and the disbelievers will argue, ‘What does Allah mean by such a number?’”* (74:31). Asking what a text means is ordinarily the beginning of study. In both verses it is described as the behaviour of people who have already decided against the text and are looking for a way to put the burden on the other side.

**Guidance and Misguidance From the Same Source**

The sentence that follows is one of the sūrah's most demanding statements: by this, God leaves many to stray and guides many. The same revelation that instructs one reader confirms another in his objection, and the Book states the outcome without apology. It states the same law when it describes the fate of the revelation itself: *“We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss.”* (17:82).

The key to the sentence is the clause that precedes it. The ones who stray are not strayed by the size of the creature in the comparison; they go astray by the same route they were already taking, and the parable supplies the occasion rather than the cause. The Book's description of the sealed heart earlier in the sūrah works on the same principle: what happens to a faculty that has been deliberately misused is a consequence rather than an ambush.

**None but the Rebellious, and the Meaning of the Word**

The verse ends with a restriction: none are left to stray except the rebellious, and the Arabic word for them, *fāsiqūn*, names people who have broken out of a boundary. It is the same word the sūrah has already used of the corruptors, and it recurs for the hypocrites elsewhere: *“They neglected Allah, so He neglected them. Surely the hypocrites are the rebellious.”* (9:67). The vocabulary is consistent: the rebellion comes first, and the misguidance follows.

That ordering is the verse's answer to a free-will objection it anticipates. If a parable can mislead, and God sends it, then the responsibility appears to move away from the reader; the last clause moves it back. What the Book never does is describe someone being confirmed in error because he had asked a question.

**Why Small Creatures Make Good Tests**

The objection that provoked this verse is itself the illustration of its claim. A reader's reaction to a gnat in a book he has already decided is divine reveals what he thinks about the author, and the Book uses small things for exactly that reason. The sūrah has just put three portraits and two parables in front of the same audience, and this verse steps back and explains what the reactions to them mean.

The answer it gives is not that the parables are unimportant. It is that the scale of the comparison has nothing to do with the scale of what is being taught, and that the people who cannot get past the creature have revealed something about their own attention. What follows this verse is a portrait of the people who are said to be left to stray, and the description begins with what they break.
