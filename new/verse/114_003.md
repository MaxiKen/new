## Sūrah an-Nās 114:3

> the God of humankind,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ilāh Names a Function, Not a Kind of Being**

*Ilāh* is ordinarily translated "god," and the translation conceals the word's grammar. An *ilāh* is not a member of a species; it is whatever a person worships. The root carries the sense of turning toward something in devotion, awe, and obedience, and the word denotes the object of that turning.

That is why the Qur'an's central sentence is a negation of the word and not of a being: *lā ilāha illā Allāh* — there is no god except God. The claim is not that other gods fail to exist as objects but that nothing deserves the position. Idols existed in Mecca and were called āliha; the testimony denies them the title, not the wood.

Used here, the word assigns a relation. God is *ilāh al-nās* — the object of humankind's worship — and the assignment is made over the same people named in the two previous verses. The one who provides for them and rules them is also the one they are to worship, and the three statements are one claim stated at increasing depth.

**Three Titles, Three Relations a Person Cannot Escape**

Every human being stands in three relations to power, and the chapter names them in order. He is provided for: something feeds him, grows him, keeps him alive. He is governed: something has authority over what he may do. He worships: something receives his ultimate devotion.

The three relations are not optional, and the only question is their object. A person who denies that he is provided for still eats; a person who denies being ruled still obeys; a person who says he worships nothing devotes himself to something. The chapter's titles are an instruction about where to place the three.

That is why al-Rāzī read the sequence as a formula of dependence: my Lord, my King, my God. The three addresses are the three admissions a human being has to make, and making them is itself the refuge. The illusion the chapter attacks is self-sufficiency, and self-sufficiency is contradicted not by an argument but by the act of saying these three words in order.

**Why Divinity Is Added to Lordship and Kingship**

A person can accept the first two titles without the third. Provision is observable and rule is enforced; neither requires devotion. The Qur'an's own account of the Meccan audience says exactly this: they acknowledged God as creator and provider while worshipping others alongside Him.

The argument appears as a question whose answer is never disputed: *“Who provides for you from heaven and earth? Who owns ˹your˺ hearing and sight? Who brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living? And who conducts every affair?” They will ˹surely˺ say, “Allah.””* (10:31). Acknowledging lordship was not the point of contention in Mecca; the contention was over who receives worship. That is why the third title comes last and why it is the decisive one.

Adding *ilāh al-nās* to the sequence closes the escape. It says that the one who grows you and rules you is also the one you are made for, and the Qur'an states the purpose in one sentence: *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”* (51:56). Creation and worship are tied together there as they are in this chapter's three verses.

**The Audience Already Had Gods**

The verse was first recited to people whose city contained a shrine with three hundred and sixty idols around it, each called an *ilāh*, each receiving sacrifice, petition, and loyalty. The claim made here was not abstract. It transferred the title, in the hearing of its holders, from the objects in the Kaʿbah to the Lord of humankind.

The transfer is total because the object is total. Not the god of the Arabs, not the god of Muḥammad's tribe, not a god among gods — *ilāh al-nās*, the God of humankind, with the same unqualified noun used in the two verses before it. Every nation's gods are excluded by the width of the phrase.

The Qur'an's polemic against the idols follows the same line. They are described as unable to create, to provide, or to hear; a thing that cannot do any of these cannot be an *ilāh*, since the word names a relation of total dependence. What the idols could receive was obedience, and obedience is what the next verse is about.

**Obedience Is Worship, and That Is What the Whisperer Wants**

The Qur'an calls following Satan worship, and it does so in God's own voice on the Day: *“Did I not command you, O Children of Adam, not to follow Satan, for he is truly your sworn enemy”* (36:60) — addressed to people who never prostrated to him. The charge is following.

That identification explains why this title is placed immediately before the description of the whisperer. The whisperer's method is suggestion, and the aim of suggestion is compliance. A person who acts on a whisper has, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, obeyed it; and obedience given to anything other than God is the substance of worship misdirected.

So the third title is not a doctrinal addition to a prayer for safety. It removes the whisperer's only route. He cannot provide for a person, cannot rule him, and — once *ilāh al-nās* has been said — cannot be obeyed as an object of devotion. What remains is noise in the heart, which is what the following verses describe and dismiss.

**The Order of the Three Titles Is an Argument**

Lord, then King, then God. The sequence moves from what God does for a person, to what God has authority over, to what God deserves. Each step requires the previous one and none follows from it automatically.

The tradition later organized its theology along these three lines: oneness in lordship, oneness in sovereignty and names, and oneness in worship. The categories are analytical, developed centuries after this chapter, but they map onto it because the chapter is already structured that way. The Meccan failure was located in the third category, not the first; the chapter puts the third last, where the failure was.

There is a pastoral reason for the order as well. A person in fear begins with what he knows — that he is being kept alive — and moves toward what he owes. The prayer takes him along that road in three verses. By the time he reaches the third title he is not merely protected; he is oriented, and orientation is the condition of the refuge he is about to ask for.

**The Three Titles Answer One Danger at Three Levels**

What follows in this chapter is a single enemy described in three phrases: the one who withdraws, who whispers into hearts, from among jinn and humankind. The three titles are matched to him, and he fails against each.

Against lordship he is not a provider: he offers nothing, and the appetites he inflames are already fed by God. Against kingship he has no jurisdiction: he commands nothing legitimately, and the person who obeys him has been deceived rather than enlisted. Against divinity he cannot be worshipped, since worship directed at him is what the Qur'an calls following Satan.

That is the whole chapter in outline. Three titles establish a governance; one enemy is described inside it; and the refuge requested is the enforcement of a rule that the enemy has already broken by being there. The final verses give the enemy's method and his species, and the method is why the titles were needed first.
