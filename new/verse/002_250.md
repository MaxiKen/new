## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:250

> When they advanced to face Goliath and his warriors, they prayed, "Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, make our steps firm, and give us victory over the disbelieving people."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Advance and Its First Act**

The remnant advances to meet the host, and the verse records what the advancing men do first: they pray. The narrative could have moved straight to the engagement — the disparity is established, the maxim is spoken, the companionship of God is claimed — but the revelation inserts the prayer between the advance and everything after it, making duʿāʾ the army's first deployed formation. The commentators note the sequence's instruction: the men who were certain of meeting God begin the meeting's approach by addressing Him, and the address is not a formality tacked onto the campaign but its opening move. The three petitions that follow are the shortest complete theology of struggle the Qur'an contains, and their order — endurance, firmness, victory — is itself the lesson: the army asks for the interior before the exterior, for what happens in its own hearts and feet before what happens to the enemy. The few who crossed the river now cross the last distance on their knees.

**Pour Down Patience Upon Us**

The first petition's verb is a pouring: *afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabrā* — empty out, pour in abundance, patience upon us. The metaphor is rain and vessel at once: the ṣabr asked for is not the patience the men already possess, deployed and rationed, but patience as a divine downpour filling them beyond their capacity — the word choice the commentators contrast with ordinary requests for patience, which ask for endurance within existing strength. The petition acknowledges what the river-test proved: the human stock of steadfastness is finite, drinkable in a handful or gulped past its limit, and the army facing a host of giants knows its own stock has already been spent down to a remnant. What remains must be supplied from above, and the supplying verb is generous — not give us patience but pour it, the abundance-form that matches the disparity. The Sufi commentators read the petition as the station of the certain: the men who know they will meet God ask, before the meeting's battle, for the quality that makes the meeting survivable in either direction.

**Make Our Steps Firm**

The second petition is military and spiritual in one phrase: *thabbit aqdāmanā* — make firm our feet. The foot is the soldier's point of contact with the ground he must not leave; the firming asked for is against the flight the story has now twice witnessed — the chiefs who fled when the order came, the thousands who drank and were dismissed from belonging. The verb thabbit, from the root of fixing and confirming, is the Qur'an's word for the steadying of hearts under shaking — the believers' feet made firm is the standing that replaces the running, in the line and in the heart. The commentators connect the petition to the maxim spoken on the far bank: the small force's advantage is not mobility but standing, and standing against a charging host is precisely what unaided feet cannot do. The army asks, in four words, to be made the kind of force its own doctrine describes — the few who do not break — and asks it of the only party who has ever made anyone such.

**The Third Petition and Its Parallel**

*And give us victory over the disbelieving people* — the exterior request arrives last, and its placement after the two interior petitions is the verse's ethics of prayer: victory is asked for a people already asking to be made worthy of it. The petition has a documented twin, and the Qur'an itself preserves the parallel: at the battle of Uḥud's crisis, the sūrah of ʿImrān records the believers' prayer — *Our Lord! Forgive our sins and excesses, make our steps firm, and grant us victory over the disbelieving people* (3:147). The two duʿāʾs share their second and third petitions almost word for word, and the shared middle — make firm our steps — is the link the tradition treasures: the community of Muhammad praying the same prayer, in the same order, as the remnant of Saul. The parallel does interpretive work in both directions: it identifies the Ṭālut-army's prayer as the believers' standing battle-duʿāʾ, and it reads Uḥud's shaken ranks into the story's remnant — a small force facing a large one, asking not for numbers but for feet.

**What the Prayer Does Not Ask**

The three petitions are bounded by everything they omit, and the omissions are as instructive as the requests. The army does not ask for reinforcements — no angels, no tribes, no belated arrival of the drinkers it left at the river. It does not ask for weapons, or terrain, or the enemy's confusion. It does not ask why the disparity exists or protest the smallness it was sifted into. The commentators note the prayer's economy: three requests, all of them qualities rather than quantities, two of them interior — and the single exterior request placed last and aimed at the enemy's unbelief rather than the enemy's numbers. The prayer thus performs the maxim it follows: a force that believes the small-over-large record asks, accordingly, not to be made large but to be made firm. The omission of every material request is the army's final sifting — the river separated the indulgent from the disciplined, the far bank separated the counters from the certain, and the prayer separates those who still expect God to change the arithmetic from those who expect Him to change the arithmeticians.

**The Duʿāʾ Preserved**

That this prayer is written down, in the revealed Book, is its own final lesson, and the commentators draw it: the Qur'an archives the words of God's servants at their moments of extremity, and the archive is an instruction. The duʿāʾ of the remnant joins the book's standing collection — the prayer of the patient (2:153's company), the prayer of those shaken at Uḥud (3:147), the prayers of Abraham, of Jonah, of Zachariah — each preserved as a form the community may inhabit. The preservation teaches that prayer under disparity is not a private resource but an inherited one: the believer facing his own Goliath does not need to compose a theology of the few from scratch, because the few have prayed before him and their words were kept. The verse's placement — between the maxim and the outcome the narrative now carries — makes the prayer the story's hinge: everything the sifting produced converges into these three petitions, and what the petitions ask for is exactly what a remnant needs to remain one. The army advanced, it prayed; the Qur'an, characteristically, wrote the prayer down and let the advancing speak for itself.
