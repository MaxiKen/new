## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:19

> **And Allah knows what you conceal and what you reveal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Knowledge That Encompasses Hidden And Open**

Wa-Allāhu yaʿlamu mā tusirrūna wa-mā tuʿlinūn — and Allah knows what you conceal and what you reveal. After describing uncountable blessings and Allah's forgiveness and mercy, the sūrah reminds that Allah knows inner and outer. Tusirrūna from s-r-r means to hide, conceal, keep secret, whether in heart or in private action. Tuʿlinūn from ʿ-l-n means to make public, reveal, show openly. The pair covers all human activity: secret thoughts, private deeds, public words, open actions. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Allah knows what hearts hide of intentions, beliefs, gratitude or ingratitude, and what limbs show of words and deeds. Nothing is hidden from Him. This knowledge is not like human knowledge that must be acquired; it is inherent, encompassing, as 16:23 will repeat: *Without doubt, Allah knows what they conceal and reveal* (16:23). The verse is short, but it establishes accountability: if blessings are uncountable and forgiveness is available, then response must be sincere, because Allah knows whether gratitude is genuine or pretended.

**Why This Verse Follows Forgiveness And Mercy**

Verse 18 ended with Ghafūr, Raḥīm, All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. Verse 19 follows with knowledge of concealment and revelation. The sequence teaches that forgiveness and mercy do not mean lack of awareness. Allah forgives, but He knows who deserves forgiveness and who is merely concealing ingratitude while showing outward thanks. Al-Rāzī notes that this pairing prevents two errors: despair that says my hidden faults are too many to be forgiven, and presumption that says I can hide ingratitude while appearing grateful. The first is answered by Ghafūr Raḥīm, the second by yaʿlamu mā tusirrūna wa-mā tuʿlinūn. Allah knows hidden faults and can forgive them if there is turning; He also knows hidden ingratitude that pretends gratitude. The verse therefore calls for sincerity, ikhlāṣ, in response to blessings. Gratitude must be both inward and outward, not performance.

**The Connection To The Argument Against Idols**

Verses 20-21 will say idols do not create, are dead, do not know when they will be resurrected. In contrast, Allah knows what you conceal and reveal. The contrast is between a Knower and non-knowers. Idols do not know, do not hear, do not see; Allah knows even what you hide from closest people. This knowledge is part of why He alone deserves worship. A deity that does not know your secret needs cannot help you; a Lord who knows both secret and open can. Al-Qurṭubī points out that knowledge of hidden and open is mentioned throughout the Qur'an as proof of divinity, as in 2:33 and 27:74. In al-Naḥl, it also serves as warning to those who plot, as verse 26 will mention those who plotted and were seized from where they did not expect. Their plotting was concealed, but Allah knew it. The verse thus bridges blessings to warnings: blessings show Allah's mercy, knowledge shows His awareness of response.

**Concealment And Revelation In Human Experience**

Every person experiences the difference between what is hidden and what is shown. You may smile while hiding resentment, you may give charity while concealing pride, you may appear grateful while feeling entitled. The verse says Allah knows both layers. This is not to induce paranoia but to invite integrity. If Allah knows what you conceal, then you can bring concealment into light before Him and seek forgiveness for it, rather than hiding it from yourself. The Qur'an often pairs sirr and ʿalan, secret and open, to show that Allah's knowledge is not partial. Human knowledge is partial: we know open, we guess hidden. Allah knows both equally. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that tusirrūna and tuʿlinūn are in present tense, indicating ongoing action: what you are concealing now and revealing now, continuously. Knowledge is therefore continuous, not occasional.

**A Verse That Prepares For The Next**

Verse 19 is brief and appears almost as a parenthesis between the uncountable blessings and the description of idols. Its brevity is functional: it inserts awareness of divine knowledge right before describing false gods who have no knowledge. It also prepares for verse 23 which repeats the same phrase but adds *He does not like the arrogant*. The repetition shows that knowledge leads to judgment: because Allah knows what you conceal and reveal, He knows who is arrogant and who is humble, who is grateful and who is not. The verse therefore does not stand alone; it is a hinge that turns the catalogue of blessings into an examination of hearts. The question is not only do you see blessings, but does your heart respond with sincerity that Allah knows?

**Linguistic Note On Sirr And ʿAlan**

Sirr from s-r-r means secret, hidden, from same root as surrah, navel, and sarīrah, inner self, indicating something inside, protected. ʿAlan from ʿ-l-n means open, public, from same root as iʿlān, announcement. The pair sirr and ʿalan appears in many Qur'anic verses to denote totality of knowledge. Yaʿlamu is present tense, He knows, indicating continuous knowledge, not knowledge acquired at a moment. The verse has no seal of divine names, unlike surrounding verses, which makes it stand out as a stark statement of fact. Its very shortness — five Arabic words — gives it weight, as if to say, this is enough to know: Allah knows what you hide and show. No further explanation needed.

**Knowledge That Invites Sincerity Rather Than Fear Alone**

Because Allah knows both hidden and open, the believer learns to align the two. The Qur'an praises those whose inner and outer match, and criticizes those who show one thing while hiding another. In practical terms, this verse encourages a person to make hidden gratitude match open thanks, to make private intention match public deed. Al-Rāzī notes that true gratitude includes heart, tongue, and limbs: heart recognizing blessing, tongue praising Giver, limbs using blessing in obedience. If any layer is missing, Allah knows. This knowledge therefore does not only threaten; it guides. It tells you that you cannot fool the One who gave you blessings, but you can be honest with Him about your shortcomings and find forgiveness, because the previous verse promised Ghafūr Raḥīm. The knowledge that He knows what you conceal becomes a door to repentance rather than a wall of exposure.
