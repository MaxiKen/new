## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:18

> They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Privations That Are One State**

The fire parable ended with *“leaving them in darkness, unseeing”* (2:17). The next verse condenses that darkness into three words: *ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun* — “deaf, dumb, and blind” (2:18). The trio is not a list of three separate afflictions but a single state described from three angles. Deafness names the inability to hear guidance when it is recited; muteness names the inability to speak guidance when it is needed; blindness names the inability to see signs when they are displayed. Together they describe a person who has lost the three principal channels by which the Qur'an reaches and then proceeds from the human being: hearing the Book, speaking the Book, and seeing the Book’s signs in the world. The verse then adds the consequence: *“so they will never return.”*

**Why the Sound of the Arabic Matters**

The source notes that when recited aloud — *ṣum-mun buk-mun ʿum-yun* — the phrase is startlingly evocative, its consonants themselves mimicking the blockage they name. The dense labials and nasals — *m, b, ʿ* — crowd the mouth, as if the organs of speech were being stopped. The effect is not decorative; it is mimetic. The listener hears, in the very texture of the recitation, the condition being described. That is why the same trio recurs in 2:171 for those who merely imitate, and in 17:97 for those raised deaf, dumb, and blind on the Day of Resurrection: the Qur'an uses the same sounds to name the earthly blockage and its eschatological image, so that the punishment is heard as the condition prolonged.

**Deaf, Dumb, Blind — Not Defective Organs but Withheld Functions**

The privations are not physiological defects but functional withdrawals. The hypocrite can hear thunder and press his fingers in his ears (2:19); he can speak fluently enough to say “We are only reformers” (2:11) and “We were only mocking” (2:14); he can see lightning enough to walk by its flash (2:20). What he cannot do is hear guidance as guidance, speak truth as truth, or see a sign as a sign. The Qur'an elsewhere distinguishes this functional deafness from the hearing of sounds — *“among them are those who listen to you, but can you make the deaf hear when they do not understand?”* (10:42-43) — and this functional blindness from the blindness of eyes — *“it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts within breasts that go blind”* (22:46). The trio therefore describes not the absence of capacity but the closure of capacity to its proper object, exactly as the sealing (2:7) closed the heart to understanding while leaving the organ intact.

**Why “They Return Not” Is Stronger Than “They Do Not Go”**

*Fa-hum lā yarjiʿūn* — “so they will never return.” The verb *yarjiʿūn* is not *yadhhabūn* (to go) but *yarjiʿūn* (to return, to come back). Return presumes a prior location that has been left. The mindful were described as *upon guidance* (2:5), as standing on an elevation; the hypocrites purchased error and stepped off that elevation (2:16), kindled a fire that was taken (2:17), and are now deaf, dumb, and blind. To return would be to come back to the guidance whose commerce they abandoned. The verse says that return is now blocked, not because guidance has moved but because the instruments of return — hearing that would hear the call to return, speech that would confess the need to return, sight that would see the path back — are the very instruments that have been rendered unable. That is why the commentators gloss *lā yarjiʿūn* as “they do not return to guidance (2:16) or to Islam, and they do not repent and take heed” (Ṭ). The un-return is both doctrinal and practical.

**The Wilful Insertion in Translation**

The translation adds *wilfully* — “wilfully deaf, dumb, and blind” — to prevent the misreading that the privation is an arbitrary infliction. The Arabic does not contain *ʿamdan* (wilfully), but the context has already established wilfulness: the commerce was chosen (2:16), the fire was kindled (2:17), the mockery was confessed as mockery (2:14). The addition therefore translates not a word but a history. The Qur'an’s own usage confirms that this deafness is culpable, not congenital: *“Deaf — dumb — blind, they do not understand”* (2:171) of those who merely follow the calls of shepherds, and *“We made them deaf and dumb and blind on the Day of Resurrection”* (17:97) as recompense. The privation is the form the chosen trade now takes as a constitution.

**A Trio That Has Been Prepared Since the Opening**

The three privations gather what has been detailed since the opening. Deafness recalls the sealed hearing (2:7) and the fingers pressed in ears against thunderclaps (2:19); dumbness recalls the tongue that said “We believe” without belief (2:8) and then said “We are with you; we were only mocking” (2:14); blindness recalls the covered sight (2:7), the light taken away (2:17), and the lightning that snatches sight (2:20). The trio is therefore not an isolated epithet but the summary of the opening’s sensory pedagogy: the hypocrite has been described as failing to perceive (*lā yashʿurūn*, 2:12) and as not knowing (*lā yaʿlamūn*, 2:13), and now he is described as unable to hear, speak, or see. The progression is from cognitive to sensory to communicative, until the whole person is described as closed.

**Why Three Words Suffice**

The verse is the shortest in the hypocrisy portrait — three adjectives and a consequence — yet it carries the weight of the preceding eight verses because it does not add information but condenses it. A heart that has been diseased and then increased in disease (2:10), that has called corruption reform, that has called believers fools, that has met one audience with “We believe” and another with “We were only mocking,” does not need a further narrative to explain why it cannot hear, speak, or see. The three words are enough because the narrative has already shown the organs being trained away from their proper objects. The verse therefore functions as a seal on the fire parable, just as (2:16) was a seal on the hypocrisy portrait. It says, in the fewest possible syllables, what that portrait has been laboring to show.

**So They Will Never Return — A Return That Would Have Been Possible**

The finality — *they will never return* — could be misheard as a denial of divine mercy. The sūrah blocks that hearing by the very next parable, which will show the same people walking when lightning flashes (2:20) — still moving, still granted flashes, still addressed by thunder. The un-return is therefore not a metaphysical impossibility but a descriptive present: as they are — deaf, dumb, blind — they do not return. The sūrah has already shown that hearts can be increased in disease and also increased in guidance; the same verb of increase governs both. To read this verse as a closure of the door to return is to ignore that the door is described as closed from the inside, by the privations the hypocrite has cultivated, and that the Qur'an elsewhere invites even the hypocrite to return if he will hear, speak, and see.

**A Collective Adjective That Is Singular in Experience**

Like the singular fire-kindler for a plural “their” (2:17), the trio *ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun* is plural in grammar but singular in experience. The hypocrites are *they* who are deaf, dumb, and blind, but each *they* is deaf, dumb, and blind alone, in the darkness that was left after his own fire was taken. The plural that has described the class contracts, in the sound of the recitation itself, to the individual mouth that cannot form the sentence that would be its return. That is why the verse ends where it does: the class that began by saying “We believe” has been reduced to a plural that cannot say, hear, or see, and therefore cannot return.
