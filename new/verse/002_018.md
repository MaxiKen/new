## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:18

> They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words in a Row**

The verse is three adjectives with no verb: *deaf, dumb, and blind* (2:18). In Arabic the three words fall in a rhythm that is almost percussive, and the sound of the phrase has been noticed since it was first recited — each word short, each ending in the same nasal, the three of them landing like three blows. The austerity is the point. There is no qualification, no exception, and no comfort anywhere in the line.

The three together cover the whole apparatus by which a person receives and responds to anything. Deafness closes the input. Blindness closes the second input, the one that confirms what was heard. Dumbness closes the output — the ability to answer, to ask, to say anything back. Nothing is left by which a message could get in or a response could get out.

**Deaf to What**

The Quran uses deafness constantly and never physically. Those who deny the signs are *˹wilfully˺ deaf and dumb—lost in darkness* (6:39). The Prophet is asked a question that answers itself: *Some of them listen to what you say, but can you make the deaf hear even though they do not understand?* (10:42). And the description recurs in the harshest form available: *The example of the disbelievers ˹not responding to the Messenger’s warning˺ is like a flock not comprehending the calls and cries of the shepherd. ˹They are wilfully˺ deaf, dumb and blind so they have no understanding* (2:171).

What is being described is not a defect of the ear. It is the condition of a person who hears perfectly well and to whom nothing arrives. The sound is registered; the meaning is not admitted. That is why the Quran's deafness is always paired with a kind of knowledge — the deaf person in these verses knows exactly what is being said and is not reached by it.

**Dumb: The Missing Answer**

Dumbness is the least discussed of the three and in some ways the most serious. The Quran's address runs on a call-and-response structure: God speaks, and what is wanted is an answer — repentance, prayer, a change of course. A person who cannot speak cannot answer. He cannot confess, or ask, or pray, or say the one sentence that would change his position.

The lack is not inability to make sounds. These people speak constantly; the sūrah has just quoted two of their speeches. What they cannot produce is the answer to the specific question being asked of them. Their speech is fluent and entirely directed elsewhere — to their associates, to their own defence, to the management of their position. Asked the one question that matters, they have nothing to say.

**Blind: The Evidence Not Taken**

Blindness completes the set, and the Quran locates it precisely: *it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind* (22:46). The distinction between the organ and the person is the whole claim. Eyes function; the one behind them does not take the evidence in.

That is why the Quran can describe the same people as seeing and not seeing within a single passage. They see the signs — the creation of the heavens and the earth, the provision that reaches them, the fate of the cities that refused — and the seeing produces nothing. A sign that is seen and not followed is, in this vocabulary, a sign that was not seen at all.

**Wilful, Not Afflicted**

The decisive word is the one the translator has had to insert: these people are *wilfully* deaf, dumb, and blind. The adjective is in the grammar, not in a footnote. The Quran never describes this condition as a misfortune that befell anyone, and it never asks for pity on that basis. It describes a capacity that was present, was exercised against its object, and is now described as gone.

The difference matters morally and pastorally. A man born blind is not blamed for not seeing. A man who has closed his eyes and kept them closed for years may, by the ordinary processes of the world, lose the ability to open them — and the loss is his, however natural its mechanism. That is the condition the verse names, and the sūrah has already explained how it is arrived at: the mocking, the lying, the trading, each of them chosen.

**They Do Not Return**

The final clause states the outcome: *so they will never return* (2:18) — to guidance, to the path, to the state they were in before they began spending themselves. The word for return presupposes a place they came from, which is the most economical statement of their history in the whole passage. They had it. They left. They do not come back.

For the sūrah's purposes, that is a description and not a revocation. The address continues, the arguments continue, and the next passage is a summons to all humanity. What the verse refuses is the expectation that a person in this state will correct himself — which is the same refusal the sūrah has been recording since 2:6.

There is a harder edge to the clause that should not be smoothed away. *They will never return* is a statement about a person's future, and the sūrah makes it without qualification. The tradition has generally taken it as a description of the state rather than a decree on the individual, on the ground that no reader can know whether he is the person described. That reading is right, and it is the only one the verse can bear: a verse that told a reader he was beyond return would be telling him to stop trying, which is the opposite of what the sūrah is doing. The sentence stands as a warning about where the road leads, addressed to people who are still on it.
