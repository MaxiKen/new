## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:18

> They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Faculties Named in One Beat**

The Arabic of this verse is a short, closed sequence of three adjectives with the same ending: *ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun*. Recited aloud, the repetition of the sound performs the condition it describes, and the commentators remarked on it. The Book is describing people who cannot take in a message, cannot answer one, and cannot see the road they are on, and it does so with three words that strike the ear like a locked door.

The triad is not unique to this sūrah. The Qur'an returns to it when it describes people dragged into judgement: *“And We will drag them on their faces on the Day of Judgment—deaf, dumb, and blind.”* (17:97). What in this verse is a condition of the mind becomes there a condition of the body, and the correspondence is deliberate. The Book's account of the afterlife keeps matching the shape of the life that preceded it.

**Deaf to What Was Actually Said**

Deafness in the Qur'an is not a physical handicap and the Book is careful about its object. People described this way have heard the recitation, understood the argument and watched the signs; what has closed is their responsiveness. Another verse gives both halves of the condition in one sentence: *“Those who deny Our signs are ˹wilfully˺ deaf and dumb—lost in darkness.”* (6:39). The translation adds the word wilfully, and the addition reflects a pattern in the Book, where this deafness is chosen rather than suffered.

The mechanism is described elsewhere: *“There are some of them who ˹pretend to˺ listen to your recitation ˹of the Quran˺, but We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears.”* (6:25). The verse in al-Baqarah states the resulting condition without the process. It does not say the hearing was blocked at some moment; it says these people are deaf, and the Book's earlier statement of the same fact explains how the state came about.

**Mute When the Truth Is at Stake**

Muteness is the strangest of the three, because the men described in this sūrah talk a great deal. They produce confessions in public, retractions in private, and accusations of foolishness against the believers. Their mouths are working; what has failed is their capacity to say something true about the matter that counts, and the contrast is the point.

The Book's portrait of honest speech elsewhere confirms what muteness here must mean. A believer asks God for forgiveness and mercy with the same organ the hypocrite uses to manage his position, and another verse records the speech of a servant who admits a wrong with no hedging: *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves.”* (7:23). Speech, on this reading, is the faculty by which a person aligns himself with reality, and it can be lost while the voice continues to work perfectly.

**Blind While the Eyes Function**

The third adjective completes the set, and the Book is explicit that the blindness in question is not of the eye. A verse in the pilgrimage passage makes the distinction a general principle: *“Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind.”* (22:46). The heart in the Qur'an is the organ of understanding and decision; when it is described as blind, the person is still seeing the world and no longer seeing what it means.

The same portrait appears with the consequences spelled out: *“They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with.”* (7:179). That verse ends by comparing its subjects to cattle and then placing them below that comparison. The sūrah here uses the shorter form, and by the time the reader reaches the final clause, the three deficits have been gathered into one fact about movement.

**A Verdict About Return, Not About Rescue**

The sentence closes with the result: they will not return. The verb describes going back to a place a person has left, and its object in the Book is nearly always guidance itself. The point being made is directional. These people are not described as unable to hear a fresh argument or as incapable of receiving a new sign; they are described as unable to come back to what they already had and abandoned.

That distinction matters for how the verse is read beside the sūrah's other verdicts. It does not say their condition cannot be changed by God; the Book elsewhere states plainly that *“the Most Compassionate will allow them plenty of time”* (19:75) and that forgiveness is available to those who turn. What it says is that the men being described are no longer travelling in that direction. The faculty that would have to do the returning is the one that has gone dark.

**The Portrait Completed by Subtraction**

The two parables in this passage work by taking things away. The fire is lit and the light removed; the faculties are named and each one is reported lost. Nothing in the sequence adds to the people described; everything subtracts from them, and the subtraction is what makes the portrait coherent. The men who carried two versions of their faith into two different rooms end up with no capacity to tell which version is true.

The Book leaves the sentence where it stands. No explanation follows, no appeal is made, and the next verse introduces a different scene with the same subjects caught in a storm. What the reader carries forward from this line is the arithmetic: a person can hear everything and end up deaf, say everything and end up mute, look directly at the road and end up blind.
