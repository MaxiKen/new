## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:20

> Indeed, Iblîs’ assumption about them has come true, so they ˹all˺ follow him, except a group of ˹true˺ believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Guess, Not a Plan**

The word governing this verse is *ẓann*, translated here as *assumption*. In Arabic it names the space between certainty and ignorance — an opinion held without proof, a supposition, a surmise. It is emphatically not knowledge, and the Qur'an is careful with the distinction elsewhere, dismissing those who *"follow nothing but assumptions"*. So the verse is not reporting that Iblīs knew what human beings would do. He guessed, and the guess turned out right. This matters because the alternative reading would grant him an insight into hidden things, and the sūrah has just spent a verse demonstrating that even the jinn who worked for Solomon could not detect a death in the room (34:14). The being described here is a strategist with a hypothesis, not a seer with foreknowledge. His accuracy is the accuracy of someone who has watched a great many people and formed an opinion about the type.

**What He Actually Said**

The Qur'an records the supposition in his own words at two places, and both are worth setting beside this verse. In al-Isrāʾ he asks for time and states his expectation: *"If you delay my end until the Day of Judgment, I will certainly take hold of his descendants, except for a few"* (17:62). In al-Aʿrāf he describes his method and predicts the result: *"I will approach them from their front, their back, their right, their left, and then You will find most of them ungrateful"* (7:17). Two elements recur in both statements — that most will be taken, and that a remainder will not. Al-Ḥijr has him make the exception explicit: he will mislead them all *"except Your chosen servants among them"* (15:40), and Ṣād repeats the same reservation (38:83). So his own prediction always contained a limit. This verse confirms the prediction and preserves the limit, which means even the enemy's estimate of his success was never total.

**Why the Verdict Is Delivered Here**

Placed immediately after the ruin of Sheba, the verse functions as a general statement drawn from a particular case. A nation with orchards and a safe road turned away and was scattered, and the question that naturally follows is whether something unusual was wrong with those people. The answer is that nothing was. What happened at Maʾrib is an instance of a pattern old enough to have been predicted before human history began. This widens the frame considerably. The preceding verses could be read as a local disaster explained by local ingratitude; this one says the local ingratitude is the standard outcome of a standing campaign. Notice too the word chosen for the confirmation: *ṣaddaqa*, he found it true, from the root of *ṣidq*, truthfulness. The verse grants Iblīs' assessment of humanity the status of an accurate statement, which is an uncomfortable concession and is made without hedging.

**Following Without Being Recruited**

*Fa-ttabaʿūhu* — so they followed him. The verb describes going after someone, walking behind, and the Qur'an is precise about what it does not say. It does not say he compelled them, seized them, or deceived them into a contract. The next verse will make the absence of compulsion explicit. What the sūrah is describing is a following that feels, from the inside, like independent movement. Nobody in Sheba experienced their prosperity as satanic influence; they experienced it as success, and their request for longer journeys felt like ambition. This is the most practically useful observation in the verse. A follower who knows he is following can reconsider. The condition being described is one in which the direction of travel is set by someone whose name never comes up. Arabic reinforces the point with the tense: the verb is in the past, reporting a completed record of behaviour rather than an ongoing compulsion, which is how a historian describes a choice and not how a physician describes a seizure.

**The Exception That Refuses to Be Rounded Down**

*Illā farīqan min al-muʾminīn* — except a group of the believers. The exception is stated without a number and without an apology, and its presence undoes any fatalism the verse might otherwise support. If the pattern were universal it would be a law of nature, and nobody is blamed for obeying gravity. A pattern with exceptions is a pattern that can be refused, and the existence of the group proves the refusal is available. The Qur'an's word here is *farīq*, a party or section — a portion of a larger body rather than a separate species. Iblīs himself predicted this remainder, which is the most striking thing about it: the enemy's own estimate included people he expected to fail with. The Book elsewhere calls them the chosen servants (15:40), which raises the question of whether their resistance is their achievement or a gift, and the Qur'an characteristically leaves both halves standing rather than resolving them.

**The Sūrah's Real Target: An Assumption About Assumptions**

There is a connection between this verse and the sūrah's opening dispute that is easy to miss. The men who said the Hour would never reach them (34:3) were also operating on *ẓann* — an opinion about the unseen held without evidence. Al-Jāthiyah quotes the type in their own words: *"We think it is no more than speculation, and we are not convinced"* (45:32). So the chapter now sets two suppositions against each other. Iblīs supposed that most people would follow him, and he was right. They supposed that no reckoning was coming, and the sūrah has been arguing at length that they are wrong. The difference between a guess that lands and a guess that fails is not the confidence of the guesser; it is whether the world happens to be built the way the guesser assumed. One of the two parties in this chapter has guessed correctly about human nature, and it is not the party that considers itself worldly-wise. Having named the follower and the followed, the passage turns immediately to the question this raises about responsibility.
