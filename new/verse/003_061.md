## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:61

> Now, whoever disputes with you ˹O Prophet˺ concerning Jesus after full knowledge has come to you, say, “Come! Let us gather our children and your children, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves— then let us sincerely invoke Allah’s curse upon the liars.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Challenge Issued After the Argument**

The verse begins by naming the situation: someone disputes with the Prophet about Jesus, and the dispute continues *after full knowledge has come*. The Book has set out its account, drawn its comparison with Adam, and declared the whole to be truth; what remains is the party that will not accept it.

The response is not another round of proofs. The Prophet is told to propose a procedure that would end the matter, and the procedure is an invocation of God's curse on whichever party is lying. The Book uses this kind of appeal in disputes where the facts are not in question and the disagreement has become a matter of who will abandon a position. What is at stake by this point is not information but the willingness to be wrong.

**The Summons: "Come, Let Us Gather"**

The words are an invitation, and they are addressed to the disputants directly, including their families. The Prophet is told to propose that the two sides bring their children, their women, and themselves, so that the appeal to God is made by whole households rather than by individuals.

The detail has been understood by the commentators as a method of testing sincerity. A person may argue a case with his intellectual commitment intact and his interests untouched, and the presence of his family removes that insulation. What the proposal asks the disputants to do is stake what they love most on the truth of their claim, which is a step that a person takes only if he believes it. The proposal is therefore a challenge to conviction rather than a rhetorical flourish.

**The Mutual Curse and Its Logic**

The procedure is that each side should invoke God's curse upon the liars. The formula does not name a party, and that is precisely what gives the verse its force in the Arabic: each side asks that the falsehood be identified and that its bearer be brought under the curse.

This is a recognised form of settlement in the region, where an oath or an imprecation could end a dispute that no court could decide. The Book's use of it is not a concession to superstition; it is a recognition that some disagreements cannot be resolved by argument and must be referred to the one who knows. The proposal also contains a threat: if a person enters such a proceeding in bad faith, he has called down on himself the verdict he invited. That is why the account of the event records the Christian delegation consulting among themselves before the appointed day and deciding not to appear.

**The Delegation From Najrān**

The commentaries connect this verse to the Christians of Najrān who came to Madinah and disputed with the Prophet about Jesus, arguing among other things that his having no human father made him the son of God. The account says that the parties agreed to invoke the curse the next day, that the Christians deliberated overnight, and that they concluded there was no advantage in proceeding.

Their reasoning as the commentaries record it took two forms: a fear of what would happen if the Prophet was in fact truthful, and a political calculation about the consequences of entering a contest with a rising power. The narrative does not require the reader to choose between the two motives, and it is honest enough to report both. What the account preserves is a decision made by men who had the opportunity to settle the matter and declined it.

**Who Was Brought to the Meeting**

The accounts differ in their details but agree on the essentials. The Prophet came out the next day for the appointment accompanied by his daughter Fāṭīmah and her two sons Ḥasan and Ḥusayn, and several accounts also include ʿAlī, her husband and the Prophet's cousin. Since the verse had named children, women, and selves, the party corresponds to the summons in each category.

The picture recorded in the tradition has become one of the most frequently cited scenes in later Islamic literature, and it is used to establish the standing of the household that was brought. The commentaries note that the Prophet brought no one else from among a large community, which the tradition reads as evidence of his confidence in the outcome. What the historical record shows without any interpretive layer is a small family group standing at an appointment that the other side did not keep.

**The Refusal and the Agreement**

The refusal of the challenge was followed by a settlement. According to the accounts, the Najrān delegation remained Christian, entered a treaty with the Muslim community, and undertook an annual payment, and the Prophet sent a trusted companion back with them at their request to serve among them as an arbiter.

The outcome is worth reading carefully, because it shows what the verses about challenge and curse were not. They were not an instrument of forced conversion. A community that declined the challenge and retained its religion kept its religion, and the terms that followed were political and financial rather than theological. The Book's argument about Jesus did not become an occasion for compulsion, which is consistent with the sūrah's own limitation of the Prophet's duty to delivering the message in the verses that came before this one.

**What the Verse Records Underneath the Drama**

The reader who removes the drama from the scene is left with a plain fact: an invitation was made, at the point where arguments were exhausted, to refer the entire disagreement to God. The Book preserves the proposal, the appointment, and the refusal, and it records them without commentary.

What the passage establishes is the point at which the sūrah considers the discussion closed. When a party has heard the account, seen the argument, and declined the only available means of settling the question, the Book's response is not to continue bidding. It states the truth once more in the next verse, and then turns to the community that will hear the same message.
