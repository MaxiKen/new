## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:3

> He is the Only True God in the heavens and the earth. He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal, and knows whatever you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Place God Is Named God of Both Realms**

This sentence has no exact twin in the Book: nowhere else is God described as the God in the heavens and the earth. The phrase "heavens and the earth" runs through the sūrah like a refrain (6:1, 3, 12, 13, 14, 73, 75, 79, 95, 101), but here the two halves of creation are named as the two halves of one jurisdiction. Some earlier commentators, uneasy that naming God "in the earth" might compromise His transcendence, explained the phrase as meaning that He is called God and worshipped as God in both realms. Others left the wording as it stands: God in the heavens and the God on the earth (43:84).

Either reading answers Makkan practice. Idols divided the world into districts — a god for the sanctuary, a god for the rain, a star for the season, a jinn for the valley. The verse collapses the map. A deity with a local office is a deity with neighbours, and the verse grants the neighbours nothing.

**Knowledge That Reaches the Hidden**

The second half of the verse tightens the sentence into a searchlight. Whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal are known; so is whatever you do. That is not a general remark about God's omniscience but a specific answer to the postures this sūrah is about to describe: arguments made in private, doubts kept behind a courteous face, mockery rehearsed among friends and withheld from the Prophet. Whether you hide what is in your hearts or disclose it, it is known to God (3:29); and God will call people to account for what they hid or revealed (2:284).

The hidden dimension is where the difficulty sits, because public behaviour can be managed for an audience while the inner position stays unchanged. The verse removes the audience and leaves the position exposed. A person can satisfy a neighbour, a clan, and a market and still be arguing with the One who hears the argument before it is spoken.

**Knowledge That Counts Deeds**

The Arabic word for what a person "does" throughout the Book carries the sense of earning, of acquiring a result. The verse therefore joins knowledge to a ledger rather than to a mood: God knows not only what was done but what it purchased. Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it (99:7), and the same rule runs the other way. There is no unnoticed act in the Book, and no act without a receipt.

That combination of a hidden realm and an exact record is the moral engine of the sūrah. Because the record is kept by the Maker, a person cannot settle his accounts by arranging appearances, and, just as important, he cannot lose his work by having it ignored. The slave who scrubs a floor at night and the man who prays where no one sees are both recorded as performers of the act, not as spectators.

**Why the Earth Is Named With the Heavens**

The pairing is not merely poetic. It is the widest thing language can name, and naming both halves claims the whole. To say God is God only in the heavens would leave the earth to the principalities the Makkans had already assigned; to say He is God only on the earth would make the stars a rival administration. The verse says the one God, in the one sentence, without a gap for a middle rank of beings.

Later in the sūrah the same claim is argued from the roof of the created order rather than asserted (6:75–79), where Abraham watches the stars, the moon, and the sun rise and set and refuses each of them on the grounds that a thing which disappears is not a lord. The argument works because the pairing in this verse is already in place: the heavens are inside the claim, not outside it.

**The Same Claim in Other Words**

Two other passages state the knowledge clause so closely that they read like annotations. In one sūrah the sentence is almost bare: "And Allah knows what you conceal and what you reveal" (16:19). In another the contrast is drawn between speech that is secret and speech that is public, with the heart placed underneath both: whether you speak secretly or openly, He knows best what is hidden in the heart (67:13). The wording varies, the extent does not.

Reading the three together shows what this verse adds to the family. It does not stop at what a person says or hides; it closes on what a person does, because conduct can be concealed as thoroughly as speech. A man who keeps his doubts to himself and a man who keeps his business dealings to himself are covered by the same clause. Nothing in the verse is left to an audience, a register, or a witness.

**A Sentence That Comforts and Frightens**

Two readers can take opposite things from this verse. For the one who is being watched by his clan and mocked for his belief, God's knowledge of what he conceals is protection: the line he cannot defend in public is already filed. For the one who hides his doubts behind a careful performance, the same sentence is a summons: the performance is known to be one. The Book is content to leave both readings standing, because both are true to the facts stated.

What the verse will not allow is a private zone exempt from the claim. The Makkans had such a zone: whatever a man privately thought of the idols, the public rites remained. The claim of verse 3 is laid over both, and it settles the question the whole sūrah exists to press — nothing in a life is off the record.
