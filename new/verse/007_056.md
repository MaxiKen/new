## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:56

> Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. And call upon Him with hope and fear. Indeed, Allah’s mercy is always close to the good-doers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Land Set in Order**

The verse forbids the spreading of corruption in the land after it has been set in order, and the phrase states that the world arrived in a condition of arrangement. The Book describes the created order as measured and balanced, and the sūrah of the Merciful states that God raised the sky and set up the balance so that people would not transgress it (55:7–8). The verse in front of us treats the order of the land as the gift that human conduct is not to undo.

The sūrah's audience lived in a region whose order it took for granted, and the verse states the obligation that the order carries. The sūrah of the Romans describes corruption appearing on land and at sea because of what people's hands have earned, and it states that God will let them taste some of what they have done so that they may return (30:41). The two passages describe the same relation between human conduct and the condition of the world.

**What Corruption in the Land Covers**

The commentary tradition gives the phrase a wide reach, and al-Rāzī treats it as a prohibition against everything that damages human life in its five dimensions: bodies through violence, wealth through fraud and theft, religion through denial and invented practices, lineage through adultery and slander, and reason through intoxication. The Book's prohibitions in other passages match the list, and the sūrah of the Table states that the one who kills a soul without just cause is as though he had killed all of humanity (5:32). The verse in front of us states the general rule of which those prohibitions are the detail.

The phrase is also read as a warning against damage to the physical world itself, and the modern reader of the verse finds the reading natural. The Book's descriptions of the created order include the balance, the measure, and the provisions placed in the earth, and a community that destroys the sources of its own provision has corrupted what it was given. The verse in front of us states the prohibition in the terms of the land and its condition.

**Calling Upon Him With Hope and Fear**

The verse returns to the command of the previous verse and adds the two attitudes that accompany the call, and the combination is one of the Book's standard descriptions of worship. The sūrah of the Prostration praises those who abandon their beds to call upon their Lord with hope and fear and to spend from what they have been given (32:16), and the sūrah of the Prophets describes the supplication of the prophets as a seeking of the reward of their Lord with hope and dread. This verse states the two dispositions in a single phrase.

The two attitudes answer the two errors that the verse is placed to correct. The person who calls with hope alone may neglect the obligations that accompany the call, and the person who calls with fear alone may despair of the mercy that the Book places at the centre of its teaching. The sūrah's command in front of us holds the two together, and the commentary tradition treats the balance as the mark of a sound religious condition.

**The Mercy That Is Near**

The verse closes with the statement that the mercy of God is near to those who do good, and the Book attaches the divine mercy to the practice of good conduct in several passages. The sūrah of the Cow states that the mercy of God is near to those who do right in its account of the signs, and the sūrah of the Heights' account of the people of the Garden includes their entry by the mercy of God. The verse here states the proximity of the mercy as the reason for the two attitudes it has commanded.

The description of mercy as close is a statement about availability rather than about place, and the Book's accounts of the answer to supplication use the same terms. The report transmitted from the Prophet in the collection of Muslim states that when God completed the creation He wrote in a book that lies with Him upon the Throne that His mercy prevails over His wrath (al-Bukhārī 3194; Muslim 2751), and the statement supplies the ground on which the verse's two attitudes can be held together.

**The Command and Its Audience**

The verse addresses the audience of the sūrah in the period before the migration, and the corruption it forbids includes the practices that the chapter has been opposing. The audience's religion involved the division of the harvests, the arbitrary prohibition of food, the killing of the animals it considered sacred, and the burial of infant daughters, and each of these is a corruption in the sense the verse uses. The Book's lists of the forbidden things earlier in the sūrah name the same conduct.

The Book's teaching about the land and its order appears in its instructions about the fair measure, the treatment of the orphan, and the fulfilment of agreements, and the sūrah's passage on the covenant collects those commands into a single list. The present verse states the general prohibition, and the sūrah's later verses supply the details of the conduct that keeps the land in order.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands fifty-sixth in the chapter, and it forms a pair with the command to call upon God in the previous verse. The two verses state the two duties of the audience toward their Lord and toward the world they inhabit, and the verse that follows presents the rain and the dead land as the sign of the resurrection (7:57). The verse under discussion supplies the ethical half of the passage.

The reader who has been told how to address his Lord is now told what to do with the world he has been given. The corruption is excluded, the call is made with hope and fear, and the mercy is named as near to those who do good; and the sūrah's next verses present the created order as the evidence for the raising of the dead.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that God sends the winds as bearers of good news before His mercy, that they carry the heavy clouds to a dead land, and that the rain brings forth every kind of fruit, and it states that the dead will be brought forth in the same way (7:57). The instruction about the land in front of us is followed by the account of the rain that revives it, and the passage turns the image of the earth into the proof of the resurrection.

The two verses together place the conduct of the audience and the work of God in the same frame. The land is not to be corrupted, and the rain that revives it is presented as the sign of the raising; and the verse that follows adds the difference between the land that receives the water and the land that does not.
