## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:65

> If they happen to be aboard a ship ˹caught in a storm˺, they cry out to Allah ˹alone˺ in sincere devotion. But as soon as He delivers them ˹safely˺ to shore, they associate ˹others with Him once again˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Honest Moment of a Person's Life**

The verse describes a scene that the Book returns to in several chapters, and its power lies in the psychology it records. A person on a boat in a gale, with waves coming from every side and the hull about to fail, abandons his usual arrangements entirely. The Arabic phrase rendered as sincere devotion means that the prayer is offered to God alone, with no partner added, and the Book notes that this purity appears precisely when every other recourse has been removed. The scene is presented without irony. What the drowning sailor feels and does is correct, and the Book lets the reader see that the capacity was there all along.

The phrase used for the prayer in this moment is one the Book applies to worship offered to God with nothing added, and its appearance on a sinking ship is the point of the verse. Monotheism, in this account, is not a doctrine that requires instruction before it can be practised; it is the position a person takes when he is stripped of alternatives and left with the truth. The passengers in the story were not reciting a creed they had been taught, and several of them had spent years arguing against the doctrine in the marketplace. Their conduct in the storm shows where their reasoning actually settled when their lives were at stake, and the Book records the moment because it settles the dispute in the opponents' own voice. What the verse examines is not the sincerity of the prayer but the agreement it constitutes.

**Why the Sea Produces This Response**

The Book chooses the sea for a reason, and it states the reason in another chapter: when the ships are overcome by a gale and the passengers assume they are doomed, they cry out to God alone, promising to be grateful if He saves them (10:22). A storm at sea strips a person of every intermediate defense. There is no tribe to appeal to, no patron to invoke, no market to flee to, and the promises of the sea are limited to the wood beneath his feet. The sūrah has been reporting the world's other supports throughout — idols, wealth, patrons, and towers — and the verse now presents a situation in which all of them are simply absent. What remains is the appeal to God, and the Book's point is that the appeal is made without difficulty.

**The Return to Shore and the Return to Shirk**

The second half of the sentence reports what happens on land, and the change of conduct is as abrupt as the change of weather. Once delivered, the same people associate others with God again. The Book states the same pattern with variations in several places, describing those who forget every deity they normally invoke when hardship strikes at sea and turn away once they are safe, with the comment that human beings are ever ungrateful (17:67). Sūrah Luqmān reports that when the waves cover them like mountains they call upon God with pure devotion, and that only some of them remain relatively grateful after being delivered (31:32). The variation and the constancy of the pattern across chapters indicate that the Book is describing a general condition rather than one incident.

**The Storm as an Image of Life**

Commentators read the sea voyage as a likeness of the afflictions that beset a person in the course of his life, and the comparison carries a further point about the reader himself. Every person passes through straits in which his usual protections fail, and every person has experienced the momentary clarity those straits produce. What the verse examines is what he does with that clarity after he has regained his footing. The Book's diagnosis, developed in the same passage, is that attachment to the present life is what returns a person to his old commitments once the danger has passed, and the illness it identifies is the one the previous verse described in two words.

The comparison is left implicit in the verse and spelled out by the sequence: a situation that overwhelms a person, a plea offered with total concentration, and a rescue that returns him to the ordinary conditions of dry land. Every human life passes through some version of that sequence, and the sūrah keeps the details of the voyage because the details are what make the pattern recognizable.

**Sincere Devotion as the Standard, Not the Exception**

The phrase describing the prayer at sea has become one of the Book's technical terms for undivided worship, and the sūrah uses it to make an uncomfortable comparison. People who had been arguing with a messenger about the association of partners with God were producing perfectly monotheistic prayer the moment their lives depended on it. Their own conduct therefore testified to the truth of the doctrine they resisted. The verse does not present this as a heroic act and does not praise them for it, since it was involuntary; it presents the moment as a court in which the question was settled by the defendant's own tongue.

Part of the argument's force lies in the admission. The Book is not asking its opponents to accept a claim about their inner lives; it reports what they themselves do in danger, when no audience is present to impress and no custom is being observed, and the sentence treats that moment as the true measure of what they already believe.

**The Gratitude That Did Not Follow**

The promise made in the storm is reported in another chapter, where the passengers pledge to be grateful if they are saved (10:22), and the sūrah under discussion notes what actually followed. The Book treats ingratitude as the characteristic human failing in these passages, and it names it in connection with precisely this scene. What the sentence describes is not a lapse of memory but a deliberate resumption of a practice that the person had abandoned under pressure and therefore knew to be false. The next verse takes up the conduct of the delivered passengers and states what their future holds, and the sūrah's final verses turn to the safety of the city they returned to.

Their conduct after the rescue is what the verse emphasizes, and it follows the same timeline as the confession: the appeal was made at the moment of greatest dependence, and the relapse came immediately after the relief. Nothing in the account suggests a period of reflection or a gradual drift, which makes the return to the old practices look like a decision rather than a lapse.

**Why the Book Tells This Story Against Them**

A reader might ask what the point of the verse is, since the conduct it describes is acknowledged to be wrong and the outcome is stated in a single clause. The Book's purpose in recording the scene is evidential. The people who disputed with the Prophet over the unity of God had demonstrated their own belief in it at the moment when belief mattered most to them, and the record of that demonstration could not be explained away by appeals to custom or ancestry. The verse therefore functions as a witness statement taken from the opponents themselves, and its inclusion among the sūrah's final arguments reflects the Book's confidence that the case was already closed before the discussion began. What remains is for the reader to decide which of the two moments in the story he intends to make his own.
