## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:10

> Awaiting them is Hell. Their ˹worldly˺ gains will not be of any benefit to them whatsoever, nor will those protectors they have taken besides Allah. And they will suffer a tremendous punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Behind Them or Ahead of Them**

The phrase rendered "awaiting them" is literally "beyond them" — min warāʾihim — and Arabic allows the expression to point in either direction, since a thing that is behind you is out of your sight and a thing ahead of you is not yet in your hand. Commentators take both readings. If Hell lies ahead, the image is of men walking toward a place they have not noticed, which fits a sūrah whose deniers are cheerful and unhurried. If Hell lies behind, the image is of men who have turned their backs on it and pretend it is not there, which fits the arrogance already described. The two readings are not rivals for the text so much as two views of one situation. The stronger emphasis runs in the direction of what comes next, because the sentence goes on to describe a fate that arrives rather than a fate that has been left. Either way, the phrase denies the denier any sense of safety in his present position.

**Wealth and Children Do Not Testify**

The verse names what they have gained, and the Qur'an's standard list of such gains is property and offspring — the visible marks of success in the society the Prophet addressed. A person may trust that what he has accumulated will speak for him, buy him protection, or at least earn him respect in the court that is coming. The Book will not have it: “Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire.” (3:10). The same sentence appears in another chapter with the emphasis sharpened: “Neither their wealth nor children will be of any help to them against Allah whatsoever. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.” (58:17). Two things cannot be bought with goods: the verdict of a judge who owns everything, and time that has already been spent.

**The Protectors Who Cannot Protect**

The second thing the deniers trust is a set of guardians taken besides God — idols in the first instance, and behind the idols the patrons, tribes, and powers a person leans on for safety. The Qur'an addresses the idols directly in one place, treating them as defendants rather than assets: “Certainly you ˹disbelievers˺ and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell. You are ˹all˺ bound to enter it.” (21:98). Another verse stretches the same warning to every form of reliance when it asks whether the disbelievers thought they could take God's own servants as patrons instead of him (18:102). The point is not that patronage is wrong in itself; the sūrah will tell believers plainly to be patrons of one another. The point is that at the one moment when a protector would be needed absolutely, the ones chosen cannot appear in the room.

**Why the Punishment Is Called Tremendous**

The word for the punishment is ʿaẓīm, which names size and weight, and the Qur'an uses it for what is beyond ordinary measure. Its placement here is a comparison the verse makes without stating it: against a great punishment stands a small profit. Everything the deniers collected, the property and the alliances and the standing, had a limited value in a limited world; what they receive instead has no limit attached to it. The sūrah has already called God the Almighty in its second verse, and the size of the penalty is a function of the one who issues it. If the judge is greater than anything the defendant ever gained, then the sentence he pronounces is greater than anything the defendant can offset against it. The word is doing arithmetic, not decoration.

**A Ledger With Only Two Columns**

The structure of the verse is a small balance sheet. On one side sit gains and protectors, both things a person gathers and keeps. On the other side sit Hell and a punishment without measure, both things a person receives. The Qur'an returns constantly to the language of profit and loss when it speaks about the Hereafter, and this chapter will use it again near its end, where those who made false claims are said to have lost. The commercial vocabulary is aimed at a trading people who understood a bad bargain when they saw one. What the verse shows them is a transaction they never agreed to enter and cannot refuse: the goods are handed over, and the price is exacted. No column of the ledger can be moved to the other side after the account is closed, and the sūrah's next verses show the account being read.

**What the Idols Were Made Of**

It helps to know what the "protectors" actually were, because the verse's confidence depends on a fact the first hearers knew about their own shrines. The gods of the Quraysh were stone, wood, and metal, worked by human hands, housed in buildings that men had raised, fed with offerings that men had brought. The Qur'an never treats them as rival powers; it treats them as manufactured objects, and it says so in verse after verse, including the passage that consigns them and their worshippers together to the Fire (21:98). A man who trusts an object he could lift has placed his safety in the least reliable thing in the room. The verse's list is therefore not a catalogue of temptations but an inventory of failures, and the failure is not that the protectors are weak in comparison with God — it is that they were never protectors at all, and their clients knew it whenever they were honest with themselves.
