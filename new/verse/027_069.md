## Sūrah an-Naml 27:69

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Travel throughout the land and see the fate of the wicked.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply Is an Instruction, Not an Argument**

They called the message ancient fables. The answer is: go and look. *Sīrū fī al-arḍ fa-anẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mujrimīn* — travel in the land and see how the guilty ended.

Nothing in the reply engages the philosophical objection about dust and bodies at all. The reply changes the mode of the dispute from assertion to inspection, which is the move the whole chapter has been preparing. An-Naml has twice already issued this command about a specific people: at Pharaoh's court in verse 14 and at Thamūd in verse 51. Here the same imperative is generalised and attached to a journey. What was a rhetorical instruction in those two places becomes a practical one here.

**Travel Was Available to This Audience**

The command is not abstract for the Quraysh. They were a merchant community whose wealth came from two annual caravans, north to Syria and south to Yemen, and the Qur'an names those journeys in another chapter as the defining feature of their livelihood.

Both routes passed ruins. The northern road went by the rock-cut façades of Thamūd's settlements and near the region of Lūṭ's destroyed cities; the southern road went toward Sheba, whose great dam and irrigated gardens the Qur'an describes as lost. The people being told to travel and look were already travelling, twice a year, past the evidence in question. That is what makes the instruction sharp rather than pious. It asks them to notice something they have been riding past for their entire commercial lives, and to draw from it the conclusion the chapter has already drawn twice.

**Looking With More Than the Eyes**

Al-Qurṭubī records the reading that *fa-anẓurū* here means to observe with the heart and with insight rather than merely with the eye. The gloss is not a spiritualising of a plain command; it identifies a real requirement.

Caravans had passed those ruins for centuries, and the passing produced no conclusions. Seeing a collapsed city and drawing an inference from it are separate operations, and the second requires a willingness to treat the ruin as a case rather than as scenery. This is the same restriction verse 52 attached to the empty houses of Thamūd, where the sign was said to be for a people who know. The evidence is public and the reading of it is not automatic, which is why the Qur'an issues an imperative about looking rather than simply stating what is there.

**"The Guilty" Rather Than "the Disbelievers"**

The word for those whose end is to be examined is *al-mujrimīn*, the criminals or the guilty, from a root that means to commit a serious offence. It is not the ordinary word for those who reject faith.

The choice keeps the argument on the ground the ruins can support. What a traveller sees at these sites is the outcome of conduct — nations that oppressed, cheated, plotted and refused, and then ended. The chapter's own catalogue supports the term: Pharaoh's court called corruptors, Thamūd's nine who worked corruption and never set anything right, the people of Lūṭ who tried to expel a household for being clean. Naming them by their crimes rather than their creed makes the lesson legible to anyone, and it also makes the warning harder to evade. A Meccan could dispute a theological category. He could not dispute that the men who lived in those houses are gone.

**A Different Kind of Evidence Than the Previous Section**

The six questions that preceded this passage drew on nature — rain, mountains, rivers, the guidance of stars. This verse turns to history, and the shift is a widening of the case rather than a change of subject.

Both kinds of evidence are called *āyāt* in the Qur'an, and both are public, checkable and unmiraculous. Nothing in either category requires the audience to accept a report about something they did not witness. What the two have in common is that they answer the charge of *ancient fables* in the only way that charge can be answered: by pointing at things that are not stories. A ruin on a trade road is not a tale told by old men. The Prophet's own position while all of this is being said is addressed in the next verse.

**A Command Repeated Across the Book**

This instruction is not unique to an-Naml. The Qur'an issues it in similar terms in Yūsuf, ar-Rūm, Fāṭir, Ghāfir and Muḥammad, each time inviting the audience to move through the land and examine how earlier peoples ended.

The frequency establishes it as a standing method rather than a local retort. What the Book is proposing is that history functions as a body of evidence with a legible pattern, and that the pattern is available to anyone willing to travel and observe. That is a strong claim and it accepts a risk: a reader who looks and finds no pattern has been handed a refutation. The Qur'an makes the offer anyway, repeatedly, which is not the behaviour of a text expecting the inspection to fail. It is also worth noting what the command does not ask for. Nobody is told to consult a scholar, accept a report, or believe a story. They are told to go. The evidence is placed outside the argument entirely, in terrain that belongs to nobody, and the audience is invited to reach it under their own power. That is an unusual way to conduct a religious dispute, and it is the way this chapter has conducted its case from the opening verse, where the Book called its own sentences signs rather than sentences.
