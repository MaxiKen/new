## Sūrah an-Naml 27:63

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who guides you in the darkness of the land and sea, and sends the winds ushering in His mercy? Is it another god besides Allah? Exalted is Allah above what they associate ˹with Him˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Finding the Way When Nothing Is Visible**

The question names a practical problem the audience knew intimately: moving through darkness on land or at sea without losing the road. Arabia's trade depended on it, and travel across open desert or open water at night is navigation without landmarks.

Al-Qurṭubī reads the darkness as suggesting a wilderness without guideposts or signs. That is exactly the condition. A caravan crossing sand at night and a ship out of sight of shore face the same difficulty — nothing in the immediate surroundings tells you where you are. What made both possible was reading the stars, which the Qur'an elsewhere names as their purpose: landmarks set in the sky for people to find their way by. The question asks who arranged that a world without paths should nonetheless contain a means of crossing it.

**Two Kinds of Darkness**

The commentators take the phrase both literally and as an image, and both readings are defensible. Physical darkness is the plain sense and the practical danger. Beyond it, the same words describe a person who cannot see how to proceed and has no marker to steer by.

The extension is not arbitrary, because the Qur'an itself uses light and darkness for guidance and its absence throughout, and it uses *hidāyah* — the verb in this verse — for both kinds of finding the way. What makes the double sense work here is that the two share a structure. In each case the traveller is competent, the destination is real, and the missing element is a reference point outside himself. That is precisely the argument the chapter has been making about religious knowledge since its second verse, where the Book was called guidance. A man who can read stars can cross a desert; a man with no stars cannot, however skilled.

**Winds That Arrive Before the Rain**

The second item is the wind sent *bushran bayna yaday raḥmatih* — as good news before His mercy. The mercy is rain, and the wind is what comes first.

Anyone who has watched a storm gather knows the sequence: the air changes, the wind picks up and turns cool, and then the water arrives. The Qur'an describes this several times and always in the same terms, with the wind cast as a herald. What the image asserts is that the world is arranged to give notice. Relief is preceded by a sign of relief, and the sign is legible to anyone paying attention. The word for that good news is *bushrā*, the same word verse 2 used when it called the Book good news for the believers. Scripture and weather are given the same description, and the parallel is the chapter's standing method.

**The Refrain Answered With Exaltation**

Each of these questions closes differently. Verse 60 answered with a charge of setting up equals; verse 61 with ignorance; verse 62 with failure to reflect. This one answers with *taʿālā Allāhu ʿammā yushrikūn* — exalted is God above what they associate.

The escalation is worth following. The first three answers describe the people; this one describes God, and it does so by distance. *Taʿālā* means to be high above, and the preposition sets Him above the comparison itself rather than at the top of it. This is the section's most direct refusal of the framing its own questions employ. Having invited a comparison five times, the refrain here states that the comparison was never available. That is not a contradiction. The questions were addressed to people who believed a comparison was possible, and they work by allowing the attempt until the attempt collapses.

**Mercy Named as the Thing Being Announced**

The verse calls rain *His mercy* directly, not a sign of it or a result of it. That identification runs through the Qur'an, which repeatedly uses the word *raḥmah* for water arriving on dry ground.

For an agricultural society the equivalence is not metaphorical. Rain is the difference between a year of food and a year of hunger, and it comes from nowhere the farmer can reach. Naming it mercy therefore attaches an abstract divine attribute to something with a date and a measurable quantity, which is the same operation this whole section performs — taking claims about God and cashing them out in the ordinary world. Note also what the sequence implies about the two halves of this verse. Guidance through darkness and rain after wind are both cases of something needed arriving in advance of the need becoming fatal. The chapter is describing a pattern rather than listing favours, and the next question moves from what sustains life to where life came from.

**Guidance Is the Chapter's Oldest Theme**

The verb in this question, *yahdī*, has been running through an-Naml from the beginning. Verse 2 called the Book guidance; verse 4 said the deniers wander blindly; verse 24 said Sheba's people were left unguided; verse 41 asked whether the queen would be guided.

Placing the same verb here, in a question about crossing a desert at night, is the chapter's most economical connection between its two halves. The narratives were about people finding or failing to find their way with evidence in front of them. This question asks about people finding their way with stars in front of them. Both are cases of a traveller who cannot generate direction from inside himself and must read something placed outside. The Qur'an does not argue the analogy; it uses one word for both operations and lets a reader who has come this far make the link.
