## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:233

> ˹Divorced˺ mothers will breastfeed their offspring for two whole years, for those who wish to complete the nursing ˹of their child˺. The child’s father will provide reasonable maintenance and clothing for the mother ˹during that period˺. No one will be charged with more than they can bear. No mother or father should be made to suffer for their child. The ˹father’s˺ heirs are under the same obligation. But if both sides decide—after mutual consultation and consent—to wean a child, then there is no blame on them. And if you decide to have your children nursed by a wet-nurse, it is permissible as long as you pay fairly. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Nursing of the Child**

The verse instructs the mothers to nurse their children for two full years for those who wish to complete the period, and it places the provision of the mother's maintenance and clothing on the father. The sentence follows the rules about divorce and address the care of the children of a marriage that has ended.

The Book's concern is the welfare of the child and the position of the mother who nurses it, and the community's teachers read the sentence as the instruction that governs the period after the separation. Readers are shown that the legislation provides for the consequences of the marriage beyond its conclusion.

**The Two Years**

The sentence names two full years as the period of nursing, and the community's scholars read the figure as the completion of the child's need for the mother's milk. The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere connects the period of nursing with the duty of the child toward his parents, and the community's teachers gathered the passages together.

The wording states the period for those who wish to complete it, and the community's jurists noted that the verse allows the parents to agree on a shorter time. Readers are given the account of a ruling that sets the full term while leaving room for the circumstances of the family, and the sentence supplies the framework for the arrangements that follow.

**The Father's Maintenance**

The sentence places the provision of the mother's food and clothing on the father during the period, and the community's teachers read the clause as the continuation of the husband's obligation after the divorce. The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere requires support according to the means of the one who provides, and the sentence here applies the standard to the nursing period.

The instruction gives the mother the means to devote herself to the child without the loss of her maintenance, and the community's scholars built the law on the combination of the passages. The passage shows that the Book's legislation on the household continues to bind the parties through the care of their children, and the sentence supplies the rule that governs the period.

**The Limit of Capacity**

The sentence states that no person is charged beyond his capacity, and the community's teachers read the clause as the measure of the obligation that the sentence has imposed. The Book's use of the same statement in other passages about the law shows that the principle is general.

The phrase accompanies the provision of maintenance and the instruction about the parents, and the reader is shown that the legislation takes account of what a person can provide. The account describes a rule whose demand is measured by the ability of the one who bears it, and the community's scholars used the clause when they discussed the obligations that accompany a divorce.

**The Protection of the Parents**

The sentence forbids that either parent be made to suffer on account of the child, and the community's scholars read the clause as the protection of both parties during the period of nursing. The commentators explained the harm that the mother might suffer through the pressure to nurse against her wish and the harm that the father might suffer through the withholding of the child from him.

The clause also extends the same obligation to the father's heir, and the community's jurists discussed the cases the extension covers. The passage shows that the Book's legislation on the subject addresses the position of each party, and the sentence supplies the principle by which the arrangements are to be conducted.

**The Agreement to Wean and the Wet Nurse**

The sentence permits the parents to wean the child by their mutual consent and consultation, and it permits the engagement of a wet nurse with the payment of what is due. The community's teachers read the provisions as the statement of the flexibility that the legislation grants to the family.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere includes the care of the child in the arrangements that follow a separation, and the community's scholars gathered the passages. The passage shows that the Book's concern is the child's welfare within the decisions made by the parents, and the sentence supplies the permission that allows the arrangements to be adapted to the circumstances of the family.

**The Child Between the Parents**

The two years that the sentence names have been read as the full term of the child's need for the milk of its mother, and the jurists measured the rules of nursing and of fosterage upon it. There is no pressure laid upon the mother, whose choice the verse describes as belonging to those who wish to complete the term, and there is no escape for the father from the duty of maintaining her while she performs it.

The provision of food and clothing according to the father's means supplies the practical arrangement, and the verse adds the rule that no one is charged beyond his capacity. The teachers drew from the clause the general principle of the law, that its obligations are measured by what the person is able to bear.

The child's welfare stands at the centre of the section. The parents may agree on the weaning before the term, and they may engage a nurse when the circumstances require it, so that the legislation leaves the arrangements to the family while holding them to the standard the sentence sets.

**The Closing Command**

The sentence closes with the command to be mindful of God and the statement that He sees what the community does, and the community's scholars read the pair as the ground of the instructions the passage has set out. The Book's practice of ending a passage with a description of God appears throughout the sūrah.

The passage closes with the rules about the care of children and with the statement that the conduct of the household is performed before the One who sees it, and the passage continues with the rules about the waiting period and the arrangements of divorce.

The community's jurists referred to the sentence in the rules of fosterage and of maintenance, and the teachers used it when they answered the questions that came to them about the care of children after a separation. The verse's closing command places the arrangements of the family under the awareness of God, since the One who sees what the community does is named at the end of the passage.
