## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:233

> ˹Divorced˺ mothers will breastfeed their offspring for two whole years, for those who wish to complete the nursing ˹of their child˺. The child’s father will provide reasonable maintenance and clothing for the mother ˹during that period˺. No one will be charged with more than they can bear. No mother or father should be made to suffer for their child. The ˹father’s˺ heirs are under the same obligation. But if both sides decide—after mutual consultation and consent—to wean a child, then there is no blame on them. And if you decide to have your children nursed by a wet-nurse, it is permissible as long as you pay fairly. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Nursing Legislated**

The verse extends the divorce-code past the marriage's end to its longest-lasting consequence: the child. The mothers shall nurse their children the breast — two full years — to whoever wishes the nursing completed. The addressees are the divorced mothers the preceding verses legislated for: the marriage is dissolved, the waiting complete, the couple separated — and the nursing obligation survives the dissolution, because the child's need outlives the marriage's life. The two years are the completion's measure, not an absolute command on every mother: the verse's own qualifier — for those who wish to complete — and its later consultation-clause allow shorter periods by agreement, and the jurists draw the full ruling from the wording: the two years cap the father's support-duty toward the nursing mother; she may stop earlier, and no power of his can force her to continue — while the support runs, when the nursing does, to the full term (al-Qurṭubī). The period's coherence with the rest of scripture is exact: the sūrah of Luqmān states the weaning at two years (31:14), and the sūrah of the Dunes gives the arithmetic — bearing and weaning together, thirty months (46:15) — from which the tradition derives the six-month minimum gestation and the twenty-four-month nursing as the two terms that sum to the whole. The child's first two years are thereby legislated across three sūrahs as one continuous provision: carried, born, nursed, and supported under a calendar God set.

**The Father's Provision**

The verse's second clause assigns the nursing's economics: upon the father is their provision and their clothing, in what is honorable — bi'l-maʿrūf. The provision covers the nursing mother's maintenance through the nursing period, and the honorable-qualifier carries the standard the domestic legislation has used throughout: what is fitting, recognized as right, proportionate to the case. The verse immediately bounds the standard by capacity: no soul is charged beyond its scope — the same principle the sūrah will close with as its final verse's comfort (2:286), and which the scripture repeats as a rule of all obligation (6:152, 7:42, 23:62, 65:7). The two clauses together produce the provision's actual measure: the father gives according to his means, honorably — the wealthy father's maʿrūf and the poor father's maʿrūf are different amounts under one standard, and the verse acknowledges the difference explicitly: fathers differ in capacity, and the obligation is defined by the honorable within the possible. The child's support is thus secured without crushing its source: the law takes the child's need seriously enough to bind the father for two full years, and takes the father's capacity seriously enough to bind him only within it.

**No Parent Harmed by the Child**

The verse's third clause legislates the child against weaponization: neither parent may be wounded through the child — not the mother through hers, nor the father through his. The commentators read the mutual prohibition in both directions with concrete cases: the mother may not wound the father by withholding her nursing or by using the child as a wall between them; nor may the father wound the mother by holding the child back from a breast that wishes to give (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī). The clause's scope covers nursing and custody together — the two domains where the divorced parents' conflict has its instruments — and its principle is total: the child is not a lever. The divorced couple, whose marriage the preceding verses dissolved with counts and honors and prohibitions of harm, are here forbidden the last remaining harm available to them: the use of the child they share as an instrument against each other. The clause also protects the child from itself, so to speak — from becoming, in the parents' war, a thing rather than a person: the mother's nursing is her right as well as her duty, the father's provision is his obligation as well as his burden, and the child's interest — fed, clothed, held by both — is the fixed point the clause exists to keep fixed while everything around it negotiates.

**The Heir's Obligation**

The verse extends the provision past the father's life: and upon the heir is the like. The identity of the heir generated a range of readings the commentators preserve, and the range extends the child's support through the whole structure of the family. The general understanding: the heirs of the father — those who inherit the estate carrying the mother-and-child's support-claim — bear the duty if the father dies inside the two years (al-Qurṭubī). Some specify the agnates, the male-line relatives; others any relative too close in blood to marry the child; another opinion names the child himself as the heir — the support coming from the child's own inheritance from his father, and if there is none, falling to the state, and failing that, to the community. The obligations' content was also debated: some limit the heir's duty to refraining from injury through the child; others extend it to the whole support the father owed. Every reading shares the verse's purpose: the child's provision is not extinguished by the father's death but transfers to the structures that inherit him — kin, estate, state, community — in an order of responsibility that leaves no orphan of the divorce unfed. The clause completes the code's arc: the marriage may end, the father may die, and the child's two years continue, carried by whoever carries the father's place.

**Weaning by Consultation, Nursing by Wage**

The verse's final rulings legislate the nursing's alternatives. If the two wish to wean — through joint agreement and deliberate counsel — no blame attaches: the two years may be shortened, but only by the joint decision of both parents, reached through tashāwur, deliberate counsel together. The consultation-clause is the verse's quiet legislation of co-parenting: the divorced couple, who could not keep a marriage, are required to keep a deliberative partnership over the child — the weaning decided not by the stronger parent, not by custom, not by spite, but by the two in counsel, and their agreement's honor is the ruling's only condition. And if you prefer a hired nurse for the child, no blame attaches — provided the payment given is honorable: the substitute nurse is lawful, her wage assigned (by the father, the jurists specify), and the child's feeding secured whether at the mother's breast or the hired one. The two permissions together complete the nursing-law's flexibility within its frame: the completion preferred and supported for two years; the shorter course lawful by joint counsel; the substitute lawful by fair wage — every path ordered toward the same end, the child fed and the parents unharmed, under rulings that adapt to the household's actual condition as the provision adapts to the father's actual means.

**The Seeing, and the Custody's Afterlife**

The verse closes with the domestic legislation's final name-pair movement: hold God in awe, since His seeing misses no act. The seeing is the clause's enforcement and its comfort in one: the nursing's details — the milk given or withheld, the provision rendered or shorted, the counsel sought or bypassed, the wage paid fairly or meanly — are the invisible transactions of a household under strain, audited by no court in most of their moments, and the verse stations the all-Seeing over every one. The jurists drew the verse's remaining question — custody beyond the nursing — from its logic rather than its letter: most agree that the mother has the stronger custody-claim while she remains unmarried — the reasoning being that a new husband would not willingly raise another man's child; positions differ, and some grant the child a choice upon reaching years of discretion (al-Qurṭubī). The custody-debate inherits the verse's two constants — the child's interest as the fixed point, and the harm-prohibition as the governing rule — and extends them past the two years the verse legislates. The whole domestic series now stands complete: from the marriage's gate of faith (2:221) through the body's cycles, the tilth's harvest, the oaths, the estrangement's four months, the divorces' counts and honors, the remarriage's consent — to this verse, where the legislation ends at a nursing child and the All-Seeing watches the milk. The household's law began with belief as its gate and ends with a fed child as its final subject: the code's last word is not about the spouses at all, but about the small person their union produced and their separation must not harm.
