## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:141

> The people of Thamûd rejected the messengers

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thamûd Is Introduced by Its Response**

The account of Thamûd begins with a verdict about how the people received revelation: they rejected the messengers. The verse does not first praise their settlements, skill, or resources. It puts their moral response before their material description. A people may be remembered for what it builds, but the Qur'an asks first whether it accepted the truth sent to it.

This opening also connects Thamûd to the communities already mentioned. Noah's people and ʿĀd had heard warning and refused it. Thamûd now enters the same sequence, not because every historical detail is identical, but because human pride keeps returning to the same decision. The names change; the test of submission remains.

**The Plural Includes the Shared Prophetic Call**

Ṣāliḥ is the messenger immediately associated with Thamûd, yet the verse says messengers in the plural. Rejecting one messenger who brings the true call is treated as rejection of the prophetic mission as a whole. The messengers do not compete over separate gods or incompatible foundations. They call people back to the Lord who created them and judges them.

The plural therefore prevents Thamûd from reducing the matter to a disagreement with one local man. Ṣāliḥ stands within a line of warning that began before him and continues beyond his people. The community's refusal becomes a refusal of the authority represented by every faithful messenger, not merely a personal dislike of Ṣāliḥ.

**The Community Has a History, Not an Excuse**

Thamûd is a named people with its own memory and way of life. That specificity matters. Revelation does not condemn an imaginary type called “the arrogant”; it addresses a real community whose habits can be examined. Naming the people makes the warning concrete and prevents the reader from treating rejection as a vague flaw found only in legends.

At the same time, a historical name does not place the story safely in the past. Every community develops reasons for trusting its own customs and achievements. Thamûd becomes a mirror when a later society recognizes the same instinct in itself: defend inherited confidence first, and ask about God's command only afterward.

**Rejection Comes Before the Famous Sign**

The verse records the response before the narrative describes the she-camel. This order matters. The people do not begin with an innocent search for evidence and then reluctantly reject an unmistakable sign. Their basic stance toward revelation is already resistant. The later sign exposes that stance; it does not create it from nothing.

Quran 7:73 likewise presents Ṣāliḥ as Thamûd's brother and describes the she-camel as a sign from their Lord. That parallel shows that the miracle was placed within a prior call to worship Allah. The community's central problem was not a shortage of marvels but a refusal to let evidence govern its allegiance.

**Material Skill Does Not Settle Spiritual Truth**

Thamûd is associated with skilled settlement and mountain dwellings in the verses that follow. Such ability can create a powerful sense of self-sufficiency. A society that can shape difficult terrain may begin to think that every limit is merely an engineering problem. The prophetic message asks a different question: who has given the ability, and for what will it be used?

Achievement deserves gratitude and careful use, but it cannot decide whether worship belongs to God. A strong economy cannot prove that its gods are real. A beautiful settlement cannot prove that its rulers are just. Thamûd's rejection shows the danger of allowing visible competence to become a substitute for submission.

**The Messenger Comes From Among Them**

The next verse identifies Ṣāliḥ as their brother, so the opening verdict should be read with that closeness in mind. Thamûd was not confronted by a stranger who knew nothing of its language or circumstances. The messenger belonged to the people and understood the world whose assumptions he challenged.

That familiarity removes one common excuse but creates another temptation. People may dismiss a truthful neighbor because they have known him as an ordinary person. They can accept his honesty in daily dealings yet reject the authority of the message he conveys. Thamûd's response must therefore be judged as resistance to guidance, not as a justified complaint about distance.

**The First Sentence Sets the Moral Scale**

The verse is short, but its placement sets the scale for everything that follows. Watch the community's prosperity, its buildings, its demand for proof, and its treatment of the sign through the opening verdict. None of these details can be evaluated apart from the fact that Thamûd chose rejection.

For the reader, the sentence is a warning against admiring a civilization without asking what it worshipped and how it responded to correction. A society can be capable, established, and culturally rich while remaining answerable for denial. The real measure of Thamûd is not what it could carve, but whether it would bow to the Lord behind every gift.

The opening verdict also separates collective reputation from individual responsibility. A people can be called by a single name, yet each person still chooses whether to support, question, or resist the community's direction. The verse warns against hiding behind a group identity. A society's refusal is built out of many personal refusals, and a person's faith is not invalidated merely because the crowd rejects it.

Thamûd's story therefore begins with a decision that cannot be buried under later detail. The reader is invited to hear the word rejected as a moral act, not as a label for an unfortunate outcome. Revelation was placed before the people, and they chose a posture toward it. The coming account will show what that posture did to their understanding of their own gifts.
