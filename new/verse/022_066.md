## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:66

> And He is the One Who gave you life, then will cause you to die, and then will bring you back to life. ˹But˺ surely humankind is ever ungrateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Verbs, and the Sūrah's Whole Argument in One Line**

*Wa-huwa alladhī aḥyākum thumma yumītukum thumma yuḥyīkum* — and He is the One who gave you life, then will cause you to die, then will give you life again.

Three verbs, in sequence, with the same subject. The sūrah opened with the Resurrection as something to be argued for and ended with it as something to be asserted; here, near the close, it is stated as a bare sequence of acts by a single Agent.

The compression is the point. The argument of verses 5 to 7 took three verses to establish what this verse states in eleven Arabic words. A reader who has followed the sūrah from its first line is now being given the conclusion without the scaffolding, which is how a conclusion is supposed to arrive.

**The Sequence Matches the Sūrah's Own Structure**

The three stages — life, death, life — are the sūrah's architecture as much as its doctrine.

The sūrah began by addressing people who are alive and in doubt about what follows (22:5). It described the death of the earth and its revival under rain. It described communities that were given life, then taken. It will close by commanding a striving from people who will be brought back. Every movement of the chapter has been inside this sequence.

That is why the verse can say *thumma* twice without explanation. The intervals are the whole subject of the sūrah; the three verbs are the shape of the timeline.

**"Then He Will Give You Life" Is the Difficult One**

The first two verbs are uncontested. Everyone grants that he was given life and that he will die.

The third is the one the sūrah has spent its length on, and what the verse does with it deserves attention: it states it in exactly the same form as the other two, with no additional emphasis, no oath and no qualifier. The Resurrection is presented as an event of the same order as birth and death — which is precisely the claim. A person who accepts the first two and denies the third is not being asked to accept a stranger event; he is being asked to accept the same Agent doing the same kind of thing a third time.

**"Man Is Ungrateful"**

*Inna al-insāna la-kafūr* — truly the human being is ungrateful.

The word is *kafūr*, the intensive form of *kafara*: to cover over, to deny, to be ungrateful. It is the same root that gives *kāfir*, the disbeliever, and the Qur'an uses the ambiguity deliberately. Covering over a benefit and covering over the truth are the same act in different registers.

The subject is *al-insān*, the human being as such, not a particular community. The verse is not naming the Quraysh; it is naming the species, and the sūrah has already given the evidence — the man on the brink who was content with the gift and forgot the giver (22:11), the man who wanted the punishment hurried (22:47), and the people who saw the ruins and drew no conclusion (22:46).

**Why the Charge Follows the Sequence**

The placement is exact. The verse has just listed three acts, two of which are universally acknowledged and one of which is disputed, and then it says that the human being denies.

What is denied is not only the third act. The charge is that a person can receive life — which he did not produce, did not request and cannot sustain for a moment by himself — and treat it as though it were his own. Gratitude in this verse is the recognition of having been given; its absence is the assumption of ownership.

That is why the word *kafūr* is the right one. The ungrateful person and the disbelieving person are doing the same thing: treating a gift as a possession and the giver as unnecessary.

**The Verse Is Addressed to Believers**

One more feature of the placement deserves attention. This verse is not addressed to the sūrah's opponents. It sits in the closing movement, in a passage of instruction and signs, and its subject is *al-insān* — the human being.

The believers are therefore included in the charge. They were given life, they will die, they will be given life again, and they are members of a species that forgets its giver by default.

That inclusion is what makes the sūrah's closing practical rather than merely declarative. If forgetfulness were the failing of the opponents only, the remedy would be argument. Because it is the failing of the species, the remedy has to be repeated: prayer, remembrance, a pilgrimage performed every year, a scripture recited daily. The apparatus of the religion is the answer to the condition this verse names.

**The Verse's Honesty About the Species**

There is no exception stated here, and the sūrah does not soften the claim. *Al-insān* is *kafūr*.

The sūrah has been merciless about this all the way through — the man on the brink, the disputants, the idolaters, the doubters who will not stop doubting. And yet the verse sits in a passage addressed to believers, in a sūrah that will close by telling them to hold fast to God.

The purpose of the statement is diagnostic rather than condemnatory. A species that forgets its giver by default needs reminders, which is what a scripture is, and needsrites, which is what the pilgrimage is, and needs a warning, which is what the Hour is. The whole apparatus of this sūrah is the answer to the condition named in this verse.
