## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:196

> Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage for Allah. But if prevented ˹from proceeding˺, then ˹offer˺ whatever sacrificial animals you can afford. And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination. But if any of you is ill or has a scalp ailment ˹requiring shaving˺, then compensate either by fasting, charity, or a sacrificial offering. In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage then make the sacrificial offering you can afford. Whoever cannot afford that ˹offering˺, let them fast three days during pilgrimage and seven after returning ˹home˺—completing ten. These offerings are for those who do not live near the Sacred House. And be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is severe in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Complete Them, for God**

The verse opens the sūrah's hajj-legislation with the command to complete the ḥajj and the ʿumrah *for God*. The first word raised a legal question the exegetes pursued at length: is the ʿumrah obligatory like the ḥajj? The pilgrimage proper is one of the five pillars, universally held binding on every able Muslim who can afford the way — an obligation established by other texts, above all the verse in Āl ʿImrān making pilgrimage to the House a duty upon people for whoever can find a way thereto (3:97). The ʿumrah, the lesser visit, is generally counted voluntary in Islamic law, though some scholars require it as the ḥajj is required. Al-Ṭabarī records a reading of *complete* that harmonizes the positions: the command may mean that once a person enters either rite, he must finish it without interruption — parallel to the supererogatory fast, which becomes obligatory to complete once begun — so the ʿumrah can be commanded to be completed without being obligatory to start. Others take *complete* as "perform them according to their rules." What all readings share is the phrase *for God*: the rites are to be done for worship alone, not for trade, not for meeting people, not for display — an intention concretely embodied the moment the pilgrim enters iḥrām, the ritual state in which many ordinarily permitted things become forbidden.

**If You Are Hindered**

The second clause legislates the blocked pilgrim: if you are prevented from proceeding, send whatever offering is within reach. The hindrance covers anything that stops a pilgrim already committed — illness or injury on the road, a hostile party barring the way — and since the sick are exempt from setting out at all, al-Ṭabarī reasons the clause addresses obstacles arising *after* entry into iḥrām. The offering is specified almost unanimously as a sacrifice, a sheep at the minimum, with cattle and camels also named. The clause's historical ground is Ḥudaybiyyah itself: when the Prophet and his Companions were barred from the ʿumrah in the sixth year, they shaved their heads and slaughtered their animals right there on the road, not in Mecca — and that precedent generated the jurists' question about the offering's "place": some hold it means the place where it will be eaten, since most ritual sacrifices are consumed after slaughter; others require the animal to reach Mecca before the head is shaved. The Ḥanafī school's practical ruling extends the clause to every road-casualty — the lost, the stung, the bitten, the sick — who sends the offering or its value ahead and is thereby released. The range of positions on what counts as hindrance, where the offering goes, and whether a missed attempt must be repeated is the verse's living jurisprudence.

**The Head Not Shaved Until the Offering Arrives**

Between hindrance and illness the verse sets a sequencing rule: do not shave your heads until the offering reaches its place. The shaving — or hair-cutting — is the rite that exits iḥrām, the final act of the ʿumrah and the pilgrim's return to ordinary life; the verse forbids taking that exit before the sacrifice has arrived at its destination. The rule ties the pilgrim's release to the offering's completion: the body stays consecrated while the animal is still on its way, so that no one leaves the sacred state with the sacred duty undone. The Ḥudaybiyyah precedent again supplies the working example — the Companions shaved and sacrificed in one place because one place was all the rite had — and the jurists' debate over whether the destination is Mecca or the eating-place determines how far ahead of the pilgrim the animal must travel. The clause is small legislation with a large principle inside it: the rites are a sequence, not a menu, and each step's validity depends on the step the law placed before it. The pilgrim's impatience — the shaved head that ends the discomfort of iḥrām early — is exactly what the sequencing rules out.

**The Ransom of Fasting, Charity, or Rite**

The fourth clause provides for the pilgrim whose body will not keep the rule: whoever is ill or suffers a complaint of the head requiring the shave compensates with fasting, charity, or a rite. The word *nusuk* here means sacrifice specifically, though its literal sense is any act of worship. The compensation amounts were fixed by the jurists al-Qurṭubī records: the fasting is ten days; the charity is the feeding of ten poor people; the sacrifice is a sheep at minimum. The clause's architecture is the sūrah's familiar pattern — a duty, then its concession, then the concession's price — the same three-step legislation the fast received (2:184–185): the rule stands for the able, the unable are not simply excused but transferred to an equivalent, and the equivalence keeps the rite's gravity intact. The scalp-ailment case is not hypothetical decoration; it is the practical instance the road always produced — weeks of iḥrām in desert heat, with its prohibitions on grooming, made the head's affliction a common trial, and the verse legislates relief without relaxing the consecration: the head may be shaved, but the fast, the feeding, or the sacrifice answers for it.

**The Enjoyment: Three Ways to Combine the Rites**

The fifth clause — when you are secure, whoever enjoys the ʿumrah ahead of the ḥajj owes an offering within his means — legislates one of the three classical combinations of the two rites. The exegetes define all three. In *ifrād*, the pilgrim enters iḥrām and performs the ḥajj alone, exits, and may then perform an ʿumrah separately. In the mode this verse addresses — *tamattuʿ*, "enjoyment" — he enters iḥrām, performs the ʿumrah first, exits the sacred state, and enjoys the interval of ordinary lawful life before re-entering iḥrām for the ḥajj; for that enjoyed interval he owes the offering. In *qirān*, "joining," he performs both within a single unbroken iḥrām. The schools differ over which mode is most meritorious, and the verse's own historical setting explains the offering: the Arabs had frowned severely on performing the ʿumrah during the hajj-season and were reluctant to do so until the Prophet himself performed an ʿumrah in Dhū'l-Ḥijjah, the month of the ḥajj. The tamattuʿ concession — doing the lesser rite first, resting from iḥrām, then taking up the greater — is thus a mercy legislated against an old scruple, and the offering or its substitute fast is its acknowledged price: even the permission to perform the ʿumrah in the hajj-months is itself a gift.

**Three Days and Seven, and the Local Exemption**

The verse's arithmetic clause serves the pilgrim without means: whoever cannot afford the offering fasts three days during the hajj and seven on returning — ten in all. The jurists differed on the placement of the three days — some allowing the days preceding the stay at ʿArafah, others the days after it when the pilgrims are at Minā — and on the seven: best performed, for some, once the pilgrim is home in his own land, while for others the return simply means the exit from iḥrām. The clause then draws the legislation's geographic boundary: this is for those whose family does not dwell near the Sacred Mosque. The residents of Mecca and its vicinity are not subject to the tamattuʿ offering-and-fasting regime — one opinion defining the near-dwellers as those who would not shorten their prayers if traveling to Mecca, another placing the limit at the mīqāt boundary-points where pilgrims enter iḥrām. The distinction is equitable in its logic: the combination-rites legislation addresses the traveler who has come from afar and carries the road's burdens; the local, for whom the sanctuary is home, keeps the simpler rites. The whole verse reads as one economy of worship — duty, hindrance, illness, means, distance — each variable priced, each hardship answered, nothing left to the pilgrim's improvisation.

**Mindful of God, Severe in Retribution**

The verse closes with the pairing the sūrah reserves for its weightiest legislation: be mindful of God, and know that God is severe in retribution. The severity-clause inside a passage so full of concessions is deliberate and does specific work. The hajj is the one pillar performed at a distance from the community's oversight — weeks of journey, the iḥrām's invisible prohibitions, the intention that no one can audit — and the verse's concessions (hindrance, illness, poverty, residence) could be mistaken for looseness. The closing names hold the balance: the concessions are mercy, not license; the rite is for God alone, and God's severity stands over its corruption — the pilgrim who exploits the exemptions, who performs for show, who shaves before the offering arrives out of impatience, who treats the sanctuary's seasons as convenience. The same severe-in-retribution formula will return at the end of the sūrah's spending-legislation, marking the domains where the inner life is the whole of the law. Between the completed rites and the severe retribution stands the taqwā the verse commands first — the mindfulness that makes the pilgrimage what its opening phrase requires: not a journey merely performed but a journey completed, for God.
