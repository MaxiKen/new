## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:15

> And so, when they took him away and decided to throw him into the bottom of the well, We inspired him: “˹One day˺ you will remind them of this deed of theirs while they are unaware ˹of who you are˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Everything Between the Departure and the Well Is Skipped**

Two clauses stand side by side — they took him away, and they decided to throw him into the well — and between them the narrative places nothing. No journey, no argument, no moment of seizure. The elision is one of the sūrah's most calculated effects. A reader arriving from verse 14 expects the outing, the games, the pasture, and instead receives the pit. The gap is where the transmitted reports insert their material: the brothers carrying the boy in turn while Jacob watched them leave, the throwing down once out of sight, the beating, the elder brother's intervention to honour a promise not to kill him, the rope, the stripping of the shirt, and the hands struck as he clung to the well's edge. None of that is in the text, and the text's silence is more severe than any of it. The Qur'an declines to dramatise the violence and moves directly to its consequence.

**"We Inspired Him": Revelation Outside Prophethood**

The verb *awḥaynā* describes a communication from God that the Qur'an applies well beyond prophets. It is used of the mother of Moses, instructed to nurse him and then to put him into the river with a promise of return (28:7); of the bees, directed to build in mountains and trees (16:68); and of the earth itself on the last day, which speaks *“having been inspired by your Lord”* (99:5). The term therefore covers a range, from an inward casting of meaning into a heart to a genuine commissioning. The commentators divide accordingly. Some hold that Joseph received this through an angel and that this moment is the beginning of his prophethood, which would place it after the childhood vision of verse 4. Others take it as *ilhām*, inspiration proper — a certainty placed in the heart without an office attached. The first reading has the stronger textual support, since verse 21 later describes God teaching him the interpretation of dreams as part of establishing him in the land, a formation that fits a prophetic career.

**A Promise Delivered at the Worst Possible Moment**

The content of the inspiration is not rescue, comfort or explanation. It is a prediction about a conversation decades away: you will inform them of this affair of theirs. No deliverance from the well is announced, no guarantee of survival, no address to the fear a boy in a pit must feel. What is given is the certainty that this deed will one day be named to its perpetrators by their victim. That choice of content is the point. Consolation in extremity takes the form of a future in which the wrong is acknowledged, and acknowledgement is what the injured party most lacks in the moment of injury. The commentators read the words as solace and as strengthening: he would be freed from this trial, and his brothers would one day stand under his authority. Both are true in the story, but only the second is promised here.

**"While They Are Unaware" Has Two Readings**

The final clause is grammatically ambiguous and both readings have been defended. On the first, it belongs to the predicted future: you will tell them of this deed at a time when they do not realise who you are. That is precisely what happens — Joseph recognises them in Egypt and they do not recognise him (12:58) — and al-Bayḍāwī treats the clause as a foreshadowing of that scene. On the second reading the words describe the present: God is revealing to Joseph something of which his brothers, standing above him, are unaware. Al-Rāzī supplies a reason for the concealment on that reading: had they known he was a prophet, their envy would have grown and they would certainly have killed him. The two readings are not rivals in substance. Both assert the same structural fact, that the meaning of this moment is invisible to everyone in it except the One arranging it.

**The Fulfilment Arrives in a Single Question**

The promise is paid out in verse 89, in one sentence and without preamble: *“Do you remember what you did to Joseph and his brother in your ignorance?”* (12:89). Decades have passed; the speakers are ministers of a famine-stricken province addressing supplicants for grain; the addressees have no idea who is speaking. The wording matches this verse almost exactly — this affair of theirs, they unaware — which is how the reader identifies the fulfilment. The verse that follows records the collapse of the concealment: *“Are you really Joseph?”* and the answer, *“I am Joseph, and here is my brother ˹Benjamin˺!”* (12:90). Nothing about the scene is triumphal. The revelation produces confession rather than revenge, and the confession uses the word the brothers had used about themselves in verse 14 in a different sense: we have been sinners (12:91).

**What the Reports Add, and What They Cannot Establish**

The commentaries preserve a body of detail around the well: that Joseph was three days in it, that the water was bitter and became sweet, that the darkness was lit, that he stood on a rock in the middle, that he wore only an amulet containing a shirt of paradisal silk brought once to Abraham and handed down through Isaac and Jacob. These accounts are transmitted without chains that would support them as reports of fact [UNVERIFIED: the details of the beating, the rope, the rock, the three days and the paradisal shirt are exegetical and folkloric material with no graded chain; cited as commentary, not as evidence], and they belong to the exegetical literature's habit of completing an unfinished scene. The text itself supplies only the well, the decision and the words spoken into it. A reader who wants the chapter's own teaching should stay with that: a boy at the bottom of a pit receives a promise about a conversation he cannot imagine having, and the promise keeps.

The brothers now go home to deliver their version of the day.
