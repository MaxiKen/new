## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:20

> If you desire to replace a wife with another and you have given the former ˹even˺ a stack of gold ˹as a dowry˺, do not take any of it back. Would you ˹still˺ take it unjustly and very sinfully?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Stack of Gold Does Not Change the Rule**

The condition *wa in aradtum istibdāla zawjin makāna zawj* — if you desire to replace a wife with another — names a common human turn: the wish to end one marriage and begin another. The verse attaches to that wish a stark test: *wa ātaytum iḥdāhunna qinṭāran fa lā taʾkhudhū minhu shayʾan* — even if you have given the former a stack of gold as a dowry, do not take any of it back. *Qinṭār* was explained as a large amount, a heap, a measure that conveys abundance without a fixed figure. By choosing the largest image, the Qur’an makes the rule amount-proof. It is easy to respect a modest gift; the test is whether a person respects a great one when desire shifts. The sūrah has already said to give dowries graciously as a free gift (4:4) and to permit only what is waived with a contented soul, and to forbid pressuring a wife to ransom herself (4:19). This verse removes the last rationalization for reclaiming: that the size of the gift makes it unreasonable to leave. Size does not make what was given freely into something that can be retrieved by pressure. The gift’s owner remains the woman, even when the husband’s preference has moved to another.

**Would You Take It Unjustly and Sinfully**

The rhetorical question *a taʾkhudhūnahu buhtānan wa ithman mubīnan* — would you take it unjustly and very sinfully — does more than forbid; it names the act. *Buhtān* is slander, false imputation, the act of covering one’s own desire with an invented fault in the other. A husband who wishes to replace his wife may be tempted to cast blame to justify retrieval, to suggest she was at fault so that taking back the gift seems like compensation. The Qur’an calls that covering what it is: a lie told to make taking look lawful. *Ithm mubīn* — clear, manifest sin — adds the moral weight. The sin is not hidden or ambiguous; it is open once the gift’s status as *niḥlah* — a free, sincere gift — is remembered. The pairing *buhtān* and *ithm* appears elsewhere for grave violations of trust, showing that to take back the dowry under pretext is not a minor lapse in generosity but a named combination of falsehood and sin. The question form invites the listener to answer no, and by answering no to commit to the rule that follows even when the amount tempts the opposite.

**Why the Qur’an Uses the Wish to Replace**

The phrase *istibdāla zawjin makāna zawj* was read not as a recommendation to replace but as a description of a case that will occur. The Qur’an does not encourage divorce in order to marry another; elsewhere it describes divorce as a hated permitted matter and it sets careful procedure for it. What the verse does is attach a protection to that case when it happens. The man who has decided to replace speaks from a position of power: he is the one initiating change, and he holds the leverage of maintenance, housing, and social expectation. The sūrah, which began with *“be mindful of your Lord who created you from a single soul”* (4:1) and which has guarded the orphan’s wealth with witnesses (4:6) and the heir’s share with limits (4:13–14), now guards the wife’s wealth at the precise moment when the husband’s power is greatest. That timing matters. Laws that protect only where power is balanced are not protections at all. A protection that speaks at the point of replacement shows that the dowry is not a deposit held by the wife while she remains pleasing and reclaimed when she does not; it is a settled transfer whose permanence does not depend on continuation of the marriage.

**Dowry as Permanent Gift**

The thought that a large gift might be reclaimed rests on treating the dowry as a price for continued marriage rather than as a gift that seals a covenant. The Qur’an rejects that treatment with language that will follow in the next verse: *“how could you take it after you have enjoyed each other intimately and she has taken from you a firm commitment?”* (4:21). The word *ifḍāʾ* — intimate going through — conveys the privacy and completeness of married life, while *mīthāq ghalīẓ* — firm, weighty covenant — recalls the covenant language used for prophets and for God’s own bond with humanity. Between those two facts — shared intimacy and a weighty pledge — there is no room to treat the bridal gift as a conditional loan. The sūrah has consistently moved wealth toward its owner and then kept it there: the orphan’s property to the orphan (4:2, 4:6), the bystander’s provision from the estate with kind speech (4:8), the heirs’ obligatory shares (4:7, 4:11–12). Here it moves the dowry to the wife and keeps it there, even when the marriage ends and even when the amount is a heap. The consistency is the point. What God has made obligatory is not to be undone by a change of desire.

**Unjust and Very Sinful — Two Names for One Taking**

The verse’s double accusation — *buhtānan wa ithman mubīnan* — was explained as covering both the method and the substance. *Buhtān* covers the false justification: to take the gift, the taker must invent a fault, magnify a dislike, or claim a right that does not exist, especially after being told to live with a wife fairly even when dislike occurs and to trust that God may place abundant good in what is disliked (4:19). *Ithm* covers the taking itself: it violates the law that gave the dowry as *ṣaduqah* with *niḥlah* and that permitted return only with *ṭīb al-nafs* — a contented soul (4:4). Together they show that reclaiming the *qinṭār* is not an isolated act of stinginess but a chain: desire to replace leads to justification by blame, which leads to seizure of what was not his. By naming both links, the verse makes the whole chain visible and therefore harder to walk. A person who sees that his justification would be called slander and his seizure called clear sin is more likely to leave the heap where it is. The sūrah’s earlier warning that consuming orphans’ wealth is eating fire (4:10) and that exceeding limits brings humiliating punishment (4:14) belongs to the same pedagogy: the true name of the act deters where the desire invites.

**The Ethic That Holds Across Replaceability**

Read with the previous verse’s *“live with them fairly”* (4:19) and the next verse’s *“a firm commitment”* (4:21), this verse forms a short sequence that teaches how to act when attachment changes. The first says be fair while you live together, even when you dislike. The second says do not reclaim a heap when you decide to replace. The third asks how could you take it after intimacy and covenant. Together they form an ethic that does not depend on continuity of feeling. The gift was given sincerely — *ṣaduqah* — as a sign of truthfulness at the start; it must be left sincerely at the end, even when the start feels distant. That ethic extends beyond dowry to any entrusted wealth. The sūrah has been training the reader to handle property as a trust watched by God (4:1), reckoned by Him (4:6), and bounded by Him (4:13). A stack of gold tests that training more purely than a small sum, because it offers the strongest reason to make an exception. By forbidding the exception here, the verse teaches that no amount is large enough to turn a gift back into a claim, and that the one who leaves it has preferred truthfulness to advantage and has left the matter to the One who sees both the heap given and the heap left behind.
