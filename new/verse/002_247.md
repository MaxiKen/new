## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:247

> Their prophet told them, "Allah has appointed Saul to be your king." They protested, "How can he be our king when some of us are more deserving of kingship than he, and he has not been blessed with vast riches?" He replied, "Allah has chosen him over you and blessed him with knowledge and stature. Allah grants kingship to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Appointment and the Protest**

The request is granted, and the grant detonates the story's second crisis: the king named is Saul — Ṭālūt, his Qur'anic name carrying the length that the verse's answer will explain — and the chiefs' first response is not allegiance but objection. *How can he be our king when some of us are more deserving of kingship than he, and he has not been blessed with vast riches?* The protest has two grounds, and the exegetes note that both are claims about entitlement: we are more deserving — a claim of comparative right residing in the claimants — and he lacks wealth — a claim that the office requires an endowment he does not have. The men who pledged to fight under any king now contest the king given, and the contest reveals what the first verse of the story suspected: their pledge had been to an idea of command — a command like theirs, from their rank, resourced like their houses — and the appointment's actuality tests the pledge again before any order to fight has arrived.

**Saul in the Biblical Record**

The objection's historical basis is documented, and the commentators cite the parallel record. When Samuel first speaks to him, Saul himself argues his own improbability: *Am I not a Benjaminite, of the least tribe and the humblest family?* (1 Samuel 9:21). And after his anointing, the scripture records the same objection the Qur'an preserves: certain base men asked, *How can this man save us?* — they despised him and brought him no gift (1 Samuel 10:27). The Qur'anic account adds the genealogical detail the exegetes draw from al-Rāzī: Saul came from neither the kingly line — Judah — nor the priestly line — Levi — but from Benjamin, outside both pedigrees of power. The objection was thus, by the lights of its makers, entirely reasonable: the tribes had a constitutional expectation of where kings come from, and the appointment violated it. The narrative's whole design is to show that the expectation was the error.

**The Mirror of the Prophet's Own Rejection**

The commentators do not let the story stand alone, and the connection they draw is the verse's sharpest edge for its first hearers: the ground of the objection to Saul was the ground of the objection to Muhammad. The Quraysh's notables asked why the revelation had not descended on a great man of the two cities — wealth and station were their stated criteria; the Jewish tribes objected on lineage, the prophet from outside the prophetic houses. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both note the parallel with the sūrah of ʿImrān's sovereignty-verse (3:26), where kingship is likewise God's disposal. The Israelite chiefs, in this reading, are not an ancient curiosity but a mirror held up to Mecca and Medina: every generation re-enacts the protest that divine election ignores human resumes, and every generation's protest is answered with the same two words the prophet answers with here — God has chosen. The story's placement in a sūrah addressed to a prophet whose own credentials were disputed on exactly these grounds is the placement's purpose.

**Knowledge and Stature**

The prophet's answer does not argue about wealth; it changes the criteria. *Allah has chosen him over you and blessed him with knowledge and stature* — the election named first, then its visible content: ʿilm, knowledge, and a widening of the body — the breadth and stature that made Saul, the biblical record adds, stand head and shoulders above the people. Al-Rāzī draws the philosophical distinction the pairing performs: knowledge and bodily capacity are intrinsic qualities — they inhere in the man, they are exercised in command, they cannot be borrowed — while wealth and lineage are extrinsic, accidental to the person, attached to him by circumstance rather than nature. The chiefs' objection had rested entirely on the extrinsic (he lacks wealth; we are more deserving by house), and the answer rests entirely on the intrinsic (he knows; he stands; and behind both, he was chosen). The verse thus legislates a theory of leadership in one sentence: the qualifications of command are the qualities of the commander, and their source is the election of God.

**Kingship Is Given**

*Allah grants kingship to whoever He wills.* The sentence is the objection's formal refutation: the chiefs had spoken of deserving as though kingship were a wage earned by pedigree, and the verse names it a gift conferred by will. The Qur'an's parallel statements gather around this one — the sovereignty-verse of ʿImrān, which gives dominion to whom God wills and takes it from whom He wills (3:26); the sūrah of Women's question — *do they envy people for what Allah has given them of His bounty* (4:54); and Joseph's prayer at the end of his own rise, *My Lord! You have surely granted me authority* (12:101) — the same vocabulary of grant, the same recognition that the office was received. The theological stake is the objection's root: if kingship is earned, the chiefs' resumes are valid grievances and God's choice is appealable; if kingship is given, the objection is not against Saul but against the Giver — which is why the verse's final clause names the Giver's two relevant attributes rather than answering the chiefs further.

**All-Bountiful, All-Knowing**

The closing pair answers the two grounds of the protest in order. Wāsiʿ — All-Bountiful, All-Encompassing, the Wide — answers the wealth-objection: the God who grants kingship is the God whose giving is not narrow, whose bounty exceeds the chiefs' arithmetic of endowments, and who can attach to an unrich man everything the office requires. ʿAlīm — All-Knowing — answers the deserving-objection: the God who chose knows the interior the chiefs' comparison could not see, the knowledge and the capacity and the fitness that no lineage-roll records. The exegetes note the pair's fitness to the story's coming trial: the wide bounty will be displayed in what the unwealthy king is given — the returning token of the next verse, the tested army, the victory — and the perfect knowledge will be displayed in what He knows of the men who objected, and of the few who did not. The verse leaves the protest formally answered and practically unresolved: the chiefs have been told who their king is and why; whether they will follow him is the question the narrative now walks toward.
