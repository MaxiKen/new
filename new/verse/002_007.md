## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:7

> Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qalb: The Organ That Thinks**

The Qur'an's heart is not primarily the seat of feeling. *Qalb* — a word whose root means to turn and flip — names the organ of consciousness, knowledge, and decision, the place where evidence becomes conviction. It is where revelation itself landed: Jibrīl *"revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah's Will"* (2:97). Because it thinks, it can malfunction in cognitive modes: it can be veiled from comprehension — *"We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears"* (6:25); it can be stained by accumulated acts — *"their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit"* (83:14); it can harden past feeling — *"your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder"* (2:74). Sealing stands at the far end of this pathology. The choice of the heart as first object tells us what kind of loss the verse describes: not missing information, but a disabled faculty — the organ that converts seeing and hearing into believing has been closed at the source.

**One Verb, Three Locks**

The Arabic syntax divides the verse's action, and the division is deliberate. One verb, *khatama* — He has sealed — governs two organs, the hearts and the hearing, as if a single act closed both. Sight is handled separately: upon it a *ghishāwah*, a covering, is placed, arriving in the sentence as its own accusative object. The translation renders the second clause passively — "their sight is covered" — and the effect in recitation is a slight widening of the picture: two organs sealed shut, a third draped over. The three channels named are the three by which guidance enters a human being. The heart decides; the ear receives the word, and revelation is above all a heard thing; the eye receives the sign, and the Qur'an constantly points at horizons and bodies and ruins as āyāt. With decision, hearing, and sight all disabled, no input remains — which is why the previous verse could declare warning useless. The anatomy and the prognosis fit each other exactly.

**A Seal Set by Allah — and Earned**

Two readings of the verse have run through the tradition since the first century. The predestinarian reading takes the seal at face value: faith and its absence issue from God's prior command, and the human will is not the author of either. The other reading makes the seal a consequence — spiritual punishment, following choices already made. The Qur'an itself arbitrates, and it arbitrates for the second reading by showing the order of operations: *"Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and hearts, and placed a cover on their sight"* (45:23). Desires enthroned first; the seal follows, marked by "and so." The same sequence appears in the description of the constricted chest (6:125). Allah's action is real and sovereign in both readings — He is the one who seals — but the sealing ratifies a self the person built. The verse punishes by confirming: what they chose becomes what they are.

**Sealing as a Family of Images**

The Qur'an does not rely on one metaphor for this closure but on a family, and the variety is diagnostic. Rock-hardness after repeated refusal (2:74). A veil laid over comprehension, paired with manufactured deafness (6:25). Rust — the word *rān*, a staining that accrues deed by deed until the surface no longer reflects (83:14). Tightness, as of a chest forced to climb into the sky (6:125). Each image carries the same two claims: the closure is real, and it was built incrementally. No one wakes sealed. The heart hardens by refusals the way metal oxidizes by exposure, and the seal of this verse names the finished state of a long process. That is why revelation, which heals one constitution, worsens another: *"it only increases the wrongdoers in loss"* (17:82). The same downpour that fills the guarded valley of verse 2 runs off the sealed surface, and the running-off is itself recorded as increase.

**A Punishment Matching the Closure**

The verse ends on its second sentence: theirs is a *tremendous* punishment — *ʿadhāb ʿaẓīm*. The adjective is the largest the passage has used, and its scale answers the scale of the loss. These are not people who missed a favor but people who closed the organ by which any favor is received; the penalty-word matches the crime-word in magnitude. The same pairing recurs wherever deliberate rejection is judged: those who open their chests to disbelief after belief are answered with condemnation and *"a tremendous punishment"* (16:106). There is also a harder reading available in the verse's own structure — the closure itself already functions as penalty. A sealed heart is not merely scheduled for punishment; it is living inside one, cut off from the only thing that could rescue it. The final sentence then adds what the soul has lost the equipment to fear. The Qur'an's economy is exact: the tremendous punishment belongs to those who made tremendous the shutting of their own doors.

**A Diagnosis Facing Inward**

Readers have always known the verse's danger lies in its direction. Nothing in it invites the reciter to inventory other people's hearts and pronounce them sealed; its function in the sūrah — and in the prayer where al-Baqarah is recited — is to make the hearer examine the processes that end here. Every image in the family hardening, veiling, staining, tightening — describes something that begins small and ordinary: one refused truth, one desire enthroned, one sign looked past. The seal is the terminus of a road whose early miles feel weightless. That is the pedagogical terror of the verse: it exists so that the reciter never arrives at it. And the door the verse leaves open is the one it never mentions — while life lasts, repentance pries at what refusal has closed, since the confirmation described here operates on the living, not the dead. The next verses turn to a third company, whose hearts are sick rather than sealed.
