## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:7

> Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sealing Described After a Refusal, Not Before It**

The previous verse stated a human fact — warning and its absence are the same to those who persist in disbelief (2:6). This verse supplies the divine correlate: *“Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered”* (2:7). The ordering matters. The sealing is not narrated as a prior decree that causes refusal; it is narrated as the divine confirmation of a refusal that has already been described. That is why classical debates over predestination (*qadar*) and acquisition (*kasb*) gather around this verse without dissolving it. The text can be heard predestinarianly — faith is not finally a product of human will alone — and it can be heard judicially — the seal is the consequence and wages of persistent covering. The sūrah, by placing the human sameness before the divine sealing, preserves both hearings without choosing between them, and the reader is asked to hold them together rather than to collapse one into the other.

**Heart, Hearing, Sight — An Order That Teaches Hierarchy**

The verse names three organs but seals only two. Hearts and hearing are sealed (*khatama*); eyes are covered (*ghishāwah*). The distinction is not stylistic. In Qur'anic anthropology the heart (*qalb*) is not the seat of sentiment alone but of understanding, remembrance, and assent. It is the locus to which revelation descends — *“the Trustworthy Spirit brought it down upon your heart”* (2:97) — and it is the organ that can be rusted (*“rather, rust has covered their hearts for what they earned”* (83:14)), hardened (*“their hearts became hard”* (2:74)), veiled (*“We have placed coverings upon their hearts lest they understand”* (6:25; 17:46)), or made to fail to understand (7:179; 22:46). Hearing follows the heart because, in the Qur'anic order of acquisition, hearing is the gate: knowledge enters by hearing before it is weighed by the heart. To seal hearing after sealing the heart is to seal both the processing center and its inlet. Sight is then “covered” rather than sealed, because sight in the Qur'an is often even more outward than hearing — the eyes can see signs while the heart remains unmoved. The covering upon sight leaves the form of vision intact while blocking the lesson that vision should confer.

**Sealed Hearts Elsewhere — A Pattern of Moral Hardening**

The image of sealing recurs across the muṣḥaf, and the pattern clarifies that sealing is not an arbitrary stamp but a hardening that follows a history. *“Allah has sealed their hearts so they do not understand”* (6:46), *“We sealed their hearts for their disbelief”* (7:100-101), *“a seal upon their hearts”* in the portraits of those who stayed back (9:87, 93), *“so We seal the hearts of the transgressors”* (10:74), *“Allah has sealed their hearts and hearing”* as a summary of those who chose the world (16:108), and the veil placed on hearts lest they comprehend (18:57) each attach the sealing to a human antecedent — conceit, preference, denial — and then describe the divine act as ratification. The sūrah of al-Baqarah participates in that pattern. It does not say “We will seal whomever We will without cause”; it says, as a summary after the sameness of (2:6), *“Allah has sealed”* as the completed fact about those who have already rendered warning inert.

**Reward and Punishment as Spiritual Substance**

The source correctly pairs this verse with its later counterpart: *“In their hearts is a disease, and Allah has increased them in disease”* (2:10). The increase in disease is not an additional external penalty but the disease’s own momentum, now divinely authorized to grow. Likewise, wisdom is described as a good granted — *“whoever is granted wisdom has been granted much good”* (2:269) — and understanding therefore as a spiritual gift that can be given or withheld. That is why the Qur'an couples the assurance of sealing with the prayer for increase: *“My Lord, increase me in knowledge”* (20:114). Knowledge is not merely information; it is a standing that can be expanded. Sealing is the withdrawal of that standing, and the withdrawal is itself a recompense. The moral world in al-Baqarah is one in which acts create capacities and capacities create further acts, so that mercy and judgment take the form of increase.

**Hearing Sealed, Sight Covered — Two Different Privations**

To seal hearing is to remove the ability to be addressed; to cover sight is to leave the address visible but unreadable. The Qur'an plays on that distinction when it pairs the confounded heart with confounded sight — *“We confound their hearts and their eyes”* (6:110) — and when it contrasts the heart that goes blind with the eyes that go blind — *“it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts within breasts that go blind”* (22:46). The verse in al-Baqarah does both at once: the inner organ and its primary inlet are sealed, so that even what is heard cannot be weighed; the outer organ is veiled, so that even what is seen cannot instruct. The disbeliever thus inhabits a world full of signs — the heavens, the earth, the alternation of night and day — with the organs of reception rendered unable to carry those signs inward. The punishment that closes the verse — *“a tremendous punishment”* (*ʿadhābun ʿaẓīm*) — is therefore not merely a future sanction; it begins as a present sensory privation.

**Great Punishment as Present and Future**

*ʿAẓīm* — tremendous — is the same adjective used for the throne, the covenant, and the reward. To call the punishment tremendous is to say it corresponds in magnitude to the faculty it has closed. A sealed heart is a tremendous loss because the heart was meant for tremendous receptivity. The verse places that punishment at the end, after the sealing, so that the sealing itself can be heard as the first installment of the punishment, not merely its prelude. That is why later verses can speak of *“a painful punishment”* specifically for lying (2:10) and of mockery being returned (2:15) as the hypocrites’ own currency: the punishment fits the closure that produced it, and the closure that produced it is already punishment.

**Who Seals — Grammar That Assigns Agency Without Removing Responsibility**

The verb *khatama* — He has sealed — is active, with Allah as subject. The grammar refuses to disperse agency into impersonal forces. Yet the Qur'an elsewhere can attribute a similar result to the human subject — *“they sealed their own hearts by their earning”* in paraphrase of 83:14 — so that the same reality is described from two angles. The sūrah’s earlier insistence that guidance and prosperity belong to those who cultivate mindfulness (2:5) had already tied flourishing to human walking; the present verse ties closure to divine sealing after walking has been refused. Neither pole is allowed to explain away the other. That double attribution is not a contradiction to be solved by choosing one verb; it is the Qur'anic grammar of responsibility itself, where human persistence and divine confirmation are said together and neither is retracted.

**The Seal That Still Invites the Reader**

Finally, to hear this verse as a verdict upon others and not as a warning to oneself is to repeat the error it describes. The heart that is sealed cannot be observed from outside; the reader cannot know that another’s heart is sealed, and the verse does not invite him to try. It invites him to ask whether his own heart, hearing, and sight remain open — whether warning still moves him, whether hearing still reaches conviction, whether sight still yields reminder. The sūrah will soon describe hearts that are diseased and then increased in disease (2:10), ears that hear mockery as if it were guidance, eyes that wander blindly. The seal announced here is therefore also a mirror: the tremendous punishment is described so that the mindful will maintain the trembling that keeps the heart unsealed.
