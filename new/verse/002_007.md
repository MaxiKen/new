## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:7

> Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Seal, and the Three Faculties It Closes**

The verse names three organs in a fixed order. The heart comes first because the Qur'an treats it as the organ of understanding rather than merely of feeling; hearing follows because it is the channel by which revelation arrives; sight comes last, and it receives the weakest treatment of the three, a covering rather than a seal. The Arabic verb for sealing, *khatama*, is the verb used for closing a container and stamping a document.

The Qur'an issues the same challenge elsewhere as a question of power: *“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, ‘Imagine if Allah were to take away your hearing or sight, or seal your hearts—who else other than Allah could restore it?’”* (6:46). Both verses treat the faculties as possessions that can be withdrawn. Hearing, sight and understanding are not the private property of the person who has them in this Book; they are grants, and the verse describes a grant that has been taken back.

**Cause or Consequence? The Question the Verse Raises**

If God seals the hearts of those who disbelieve, the objection writes itself: what freedom is left to them? The commentators answered along two lines. One reading takes the seal as a cause, God's prior decree governing who believes. The other takes it as a consequence, the natural end of a choice already made, so that the closing of the heart is a sentence passed on a settled direction rather than a trap set in advance.

The second reading gathers more support from the sūrah's sequence, since the previous verse described people whose rejection is already complete, and it is supported by the way the Qur'an states the rule elsewhere: *“This is how Allah seals the hearts of the disbelievers”* (7:101), and after the rejection of earlier messengers, *“This is how We seal the hearts of the transgressors”* (10:74). The same sentence pattern appears at 16:108 and 18:57, where the sealing follows a refusal that is already narrated. The verse does not describe an arbitrary verdict. It describes what happens to the faculty of understanding when a person uses it against what he knows.

**The Heart in the Book's Vocabulary**

The Qur'an's heart is not the sentimental organ of modern speech. It is where a person knows, decides and believes, and it is capable of being changed in either direction. The revelation reaches it directly: *“he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah's Will”* (2:97). It can be hardened: *“Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder”* (2:74). It can be stained: *“In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!”* (83:14).

A report recorded in Sunan al-Tirmidhī 3334 describes the process in physical language: a black mark appears on the heart when a servant sins, and if he abandons the sin and repents, the heart is polished, while returning to the sin increases the blackness until it covers the heart. The Prophet ﷺ in that report identified the covering with the verse *“their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit”* (83:14). Sinning, on this account, is not neutral in its effects on the organ that understands.

**Three Verbs for One Condition**

The Arabic does not use the same verb three times. A seal is placed on the heart and on the hearing, while over the sight a covering is stretched. The change of image is not stylistic variation; it describes two types of closure. A sealed container is closed to its own contents, while a covered eye is separated from what it is built to perceive. The faculties that receive warnings are shut, and the faculty that could notice a warning is veiled.

The verse then states the outcome: a tremendous punishment. The word is the same one the Book uses for rewards of the largest scale, and it places the sentence on the same footing as the promise given to the mindful in the previous verse. The sūrah's opening portraits are balanced deliberately, and the punishment is stated as a fact without an interval of argument.

**What the Verse Refuses to Say**

The passage does not say that these people never had the opportunity to believe, and it does not say that the seal was placed on a blank page. The previous verse described warning offered and refused; this one describes the state that follows the refusal, which is why the Book can hold a person responsible for a condition he did not choose at a single moment. Responsibility in the Qur'an attaches to the direction of a life rather than to the individual decision that began it.

Nor does the verse cancel the preaching that has already failed. The messenger is told in 2:6 that his warning will not change these people, and his task remains what it was. The Qur'an keeps the two claims side by side: the hearts are sealed, and the reminder continues to be delivered, because the outcome is God's and the delivery is the messenger's.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does**

The three portraits at the head of al-Baqarah follow a pattern: the mindful with their marks, the settled rejecters with their seal, and then a third group whose membership is hidden. Placing the closed heart between the believer's portrait and the hypocrite's is instructive, because the sūrah's next passage will describe people whose condition is the opposite of a sealed heart — a public confession attached to a private denial.

The verse therefore functions as the far end of a road. Its punishment is stated without description, and the sentence is short; the rejection it describes required no elaborations. What the Book withholds from a sealed heart is precisely what it gives to the mindful earlier: the faculties of hearing and sight are named in both passages, and in one they receive guidance while in the other they are closed.
