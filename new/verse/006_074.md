## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:74

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said to his father, Ȃzar, “Do you take idols as gods? It is clear to me that you and your people are entirely misguided.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Beginning of a Narrative Section**

After seventy verses of argument, the sūrah turns to a story, and the story is the one the Book treats as the model of the case it has been making. Abraham is introduced in the middle of a declaration about the One God, and what follows is the account of a man who arrived at that declaration through the created world. The sūrah of Maryam calls him a man of truth and a prophet (19:41), and the Book elsewhere credits him with sound judgement given to him early (21:51).

The placement matters. The sūrah has just stated that God created the heavens and the earth in truth, that His command brings things into being, that authority belongs to Him alone, and that His knowledge covers the seen and the unseen (6:73). Abraham's story is the demonstration of what those statements mean for a human being who looks at the world honestly, and the sūrah will end the section by calling his argument the one God gave him against his people (6:83).

**The Address to a Father**

And remember when Abraham said to his father, Āzar. The Book names the father only here, and the commentators spent considerable effort on the name. In the Biblical account Abraham's father is Terah, and some held that Āzar was another name for the same man; others suggested that Āzar was the name of an idol and that Abraham was addressing his father by it as a rebuke; and a third group held that Āzar was an uncle who was called a father in the wider sense in which the Book uses the word for older relatives, citing the verse where Jacob names Ishmael among his fathers though Ishmael was his uncle (2:133).

The discussion behind that third view is worth noting, because some Muslims, particularly among the Shia, held that the father of a prophet could not have been an idolater, and they cited a report in which the Prophet says that God transferred him from pure loins to pure wombs and that he was never touched by the filth of the age of ignorance. The most literal reading of this verse, as al-Rāzī acknowledged, is that Āzar was Abraham's father and that he worshipped idols, and the Book's own account of Abraham's later dissociation from him gives the story its pathos.

**A Question Rather Than a Denunciation**

Do you take idols as gods? The question is the same device the sūrah has used throughout, and it works the same way here: the practice is named in a single clause and the hearer is left to see it. The Book records a harsher version of the same question in the sūrah of the Prophets, where Abraham asks his father and his people what the statues are to which they are so devoted (21:52).

What follows in the verse is not a personal insult. Abraham states that he sees his father and his people in clear error, and the sentence is about the error rather than about the man. The Book's prophets address their closest relatives in this way because the message begins at home, and the command given to the Prophet in the sūrah of the Poets to warn his nearest kin has a precedent in the first of the prophets to argue with his own household.

**The Tension Between Kinship and Conviction**

The verse introduces a theme that returns at several points in the Abraham story and that the community reading the sūrah would have felt immediately. The Book elsewhere praises those who honour their parents and forbids the believer to obey them if they press him toward idolatry, while requiring that he keep their company courteously in this world (31:14–15). Abraham's case shows how far the requirement of courtesy could be stretched and where it ended.

The Book records the end of that story in the sūrah of Repentance, where it says that Abraham's prayer for his father's forgiveness was only in fulfilment of a promise he had made, and that when it became clear to him that his father was an enemy of God he broke ties with him, while noting that Abraham was tender-hearted and forbearing (9:114). The Book's sympathy for the difficulty of that decision is visible in the wording, and the sūrah of Maryam preserves the words Abraham used when he left: peace be upon you, I will pray to my Lord for your forgiveness (19:47).

The same sūrah records how he spoke to the man. He called him dear father and asked him why he worshipped what could neither hear nor see nor benefit him at all (19:42); he told him that he had received knowledge his father had not received, and asked him to follow so that he could guide him to a straight path (19:43); he warned him against worshipping Satan, who is ever rebellious against the Most Compassionate (19:44); and he said that he feared his father would be touched by a punishment and become a companion of Satan in the Fire (19:45). Every sentence is an address of respect, and every sentence states the danger without softening it.

**What the Verse Assumes About the Audience**

The Makkans were the descendants of Abraham and knew it. The Book had already told them that Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian but a man of pure faith, a Muslim, and not one of those who associate partners with God (3:67), and it uses his story here because the claim of descent gave the argument its edge. The ancestor whose name they invoked had begun his mission by rejecting idols of the same kind.

That is the force of the opening of this section. The sūrah is not introducing a distant figure from a foreign tradition; it is introducing the man the audience claimed as their founder, at the moment when he turned his face away from the objects their own city was built around. The verses that follow describe what he saw and what he argued, and the account is presented as the argument God gave him.
