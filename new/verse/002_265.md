## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:265

> And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah's pleasure and believing the reward is certain, is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce. If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Garden on the Height**

The diptych's second panel reverses the first in every element: where the rock was flat, hard, and shallow, the garden sits on a rabwah — an elevation, a fertile height. The commentators work the topography carefully, because the hill is not decoration but argument. Al-Qurṭubī records the agronomy behind it: certain slightly raised grounds collect rich soil and flourish, and — crucially — a garden on a height has no stream running through it, so it depends entirely on the sky: nothing waters it but rain. Al-Rāzī presses the point into a difficulty and a resolution: a hilltop garden is exposed to wind and beyond the reach of irrigation, while a garden in a hollow drowns; he prefers to read rabwah from its root — to rise, to grow — as the fertile ground whose crops rise, and argues the best garden stands on level earth. The readings converge on the image's function: the sincere giver's heart is ground that cannot be watered by the world — no stream of audience-approval reaches it — and depends, for every drop of its fruitfulness, on what falls from above.

**The Two Motives**

The verse names what the garden's owners spend for, in two clauses: *seeking Allah's pleasure and believing the reward is certain*. The commentators read the second clause — the translation's *believing the reward is certain*, literally a confirmation in their souls — in three directions, each recorded: the givers act from confirmed faith, the certainty of īmān itself (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī); they act from confirmation of God's truth, the ṣidq that answers the show-off's lie (al-Zamakhsharī); or they confirm their charity's placement — taking care where they put it, checking that it lands and is used properly (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī). The three readings describe one disposition from three sides: the heart settled about God, settled about the promise, and settled enough to be careful with the deed. Pleasure-seeking — ibtilāgh riḍwān Allāh — is the first clause's content, and it is the show-off's motive inverted: the riyyāʾ-giver's pleasure-sought is human and immediate; this giver's is divine and deferred, which is why the second clause matters — only certainty about the unseen reward makes an unseen audience sufficient. The verse's psychology is exact: nobody spends against a merely probable return; the garden grows on the soil of yaqīn.

**The Rain's Double Arithmetic**

The parable's weather completes the diptych: *when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce. If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient.* The same downpour that stripped the rock to bare stone doubles the garden — and the verse adds the economy's second clause: even the light rain, the ṭall, the drizzle and dew, does the garden's work when the heavy water is withheld. The commentators draw the full accounting. The garden's yield does not depend on the rain's amount but on the ground's quality: abundant grace produces abundance, and scarce grace still produces — the sincere deed multiplies in seasons of trial and survives in seasons of drought. The image answers the giver's two fears at once: that his small gift will not matter (the drizzle suffices) and that his circumstances will not permit the great one (the doubling waits on the soil, not the season). Read against the rock, the arithmetic becomes the section's whole theology of intention: identical weather, opposite outcomes, because the difference between dust-on-stone and garden-on-height was settled before the first drop fell — at the moment the giver chose his audience and confirmed his certainty.

**The Date That Becomes a Mountain**

The tradition's most transmitted comment on this verse's multiplication is the Prophet's own image. He said, in the report carried by both Ṣaḥīḥs: whoever gives in charity the equivalent of a date, from lawfully earned wealth — and God accepts only the lawful — God accepts it with His right hand and nurtures it for its giver as one of you nurtures a foal, until it becomes like a mountain. The ḥadīth is the grain-parable (2:261) and the garden-parable in one scene: the smallest possible gift, a single date, and the largest possible increase, the mountain — with the mechanism named as tending. God's handling of the sincere deed is the breeder's handling of the young animal: patient, daily, growth-oriented, the increase invisible at any single observation and undeniable across the season. The verse's twofold yield and drizzle-sufficiency are this nurture's agricultural form; the ḥadīth is its zoological form; and both fix the same two conditions — the date must be lawfully earned (the coming verse's good-things legislation), and the nurturing is God's own act, which is why no season's drought and no audience's indifference can interrupt it.

**Seeking the Pleasure**

The first motive-clause deserves its own weight, because riḍwān — God's pleasure — is the Qur'an's name for the destination beyond reward. The sūrah of Man's portrait of the righteous gives the motive its purest formulation: *We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you* (76:9) — the givers who refuse both currencies the show-off collects. This verse's formulation is deliberately below that summit and above the rock: seeking God's pleasure *and* believing the reward is certain — the pleasure sought and the reward expected, neither excluded. The commentators do not force the two apart: the reward-certainty is what keeps the pleasure-seeking honest, since a giver who doubts the promise will inevitably start collecting visible returns. The Face-of-God idiom the coming verses will use (2:272) is the same orientation — the giving whose direction is God, whose confirmation is internal, and whose weather comes entirely from the sky. Between the mountain-nurtured date and the 76:9 summit, the verse locates the sincere giver's ordinary state: not the purity that refuses reward but the certainty that makes reward's deferral bearable.

**All-Seeing of What You Do**

The closing name is the section's recurring seal — the same All-Seeing that ended the nursing legislation (2:233) and the half-dowry ruling (2:237) — and its placement here is the diptych's final stroke. The rock and the garden are distinguished by what rain reveals; the givers they depict are distinguished by what only the Seer witnesses: the pleasure sought or the audience courted, the certainty confirmed or the return calculated. The verse's parable runs entirely on surfaces — soil, dust, water, fruit — and its closing name withdraws every surface from the record: the seeing that governs the yield is the seeing of interiors, and the doubling or the stripping is its verdict made agricultural. For the giver the name is the section's comfort and its demand at once: the drizzle-season gift nobody noticed is seen, and the mountain it is becoming is seen too; the dust-film on the stone that fooled the neighbors was never deceiving the Seer. The garden on the height, watered only by sky, watched only by God — the verse leaves its reader standing in it, holding a date, deciding what the ground is.
