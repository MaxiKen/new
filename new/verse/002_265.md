## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:265

> And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah’s pleasure and believing the reward is certain, is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce. If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Parable**

The sentence presents the second of the two comparisons and describes those who spend their wealth seeking the pleasure of God and out of a confirmation within their own souls. The community's teachers read the figure as the counterpart of the rock in the verse before, so that the same rain is shown falling on two kinds of ground.

The wording names two conditions in the giver, the purpose of the act and the certainty that accompanies it. The commentators explained the second as the assurance of the heart that the promise of God is true, so that the person gives without the fear that the matter is uncertain.

The verse therefore describes the inner state of the giver before it describes the harvest. The Book's concern in the passage runs through the intention as well as the act, and the sentence supplies the statement of the intention that the earlier verses have commended.

**The Garden on the Height**

The image the sentence uses is that of a garden situated on elevated ground, and the commentators discussed the placement of the garden at length. Some explained that such a spot is reached only by the rain and not by the streams of the valley, so that the garden depends on the bounty that comes from above.

Others related the term to the growth that the ground produces, so that the phrase describes a fertile rise from which the crops come forth. The teachers set out the readings and noted that both of them serve the sentence's purpose, since the garden in either case yields its fruit by the gift that the sky supplies.

The community's scholars also observed the contrast with the bare rock of the previous verse. The one surface keeps nothing, and the other holds what it receives and returns it many times over.

**The Double Harvest**

The sentence states that when a heavy rain falls the garden yields twice its ordinary produce, and the community's teachers read the phrase as the description of the reward that accompanies the act of the giver. The Book's earlier parable has promised the harvest of seven hundred grains, and the two figures were read together by the community's scholars.

The word rendered as twice has been explained as the statement of a full and repeated return, and the commentators noted that the figure describes the reward of the life to come rather than the property of this one. The teachers used the sentence when they spoke about the abundance that the Book promises to those who spend.

The clause also places the harvest within the order that God has established in the earth, since the rain and the ground work together. The community's preachers drew from the image the lesson that the act of the giver is the seed and the reward is the fruit that follows it.

**The Soft Rain**

The sentence adds that if no heavy rain falls, a light rain is sufficient, and the commentators explained the clause as the statement that the garden is never left without its yield. The teachers read the detail as the account of the giver whose opportunities are modest, since his act also produces its fruit although the measure of the rain differs.

The wording gives the community the assurance that the scale of the gift does not determine whether it is accepted. What the sentence requires is the character of the giver, and the rain that reaches the garden, whether heavy or light, is enough for its fruit.

The community's scholars also noted the consolation that the clause supplies to the believer who owns little. The Book's promises in the passage are addressed to all who give in the manner the verses describe, and the sentence here includes the small measure within the promise.

**The Certainty Named**

The sentence names the confirmation within the souls of the givers, and the commentators explained the phrase as the certainty that their gift is received and their reward is kept. The Book's description of the believers elsewhere includes the assurance that accompanies their deeds, and the teachers gathered the passages on the subject.

The phrase also supplies the answer to the fear that the previous passage addressed, since a person who knows that his gift is not lost gives without the anxiety that spoils the act. The community's scholars read the sentence as the account of the disposition with which the believers spend.

The wording places the confirmation within the person rather than in the circumstances, so that the state of the giver does not depend on the response of those who receive. The teachers used the sentence when they answered the community's questions about the acceptance of the gifts that were given to people who did not acknowledge them.

**The Attribute at the Close**

The verse closes with the statement that God sees what the community does, and the community's scholars read the clause as the ground of the whole passage. The attribute places the acts of the givers and the hidden conditions of their hearts before the One who observes them.

The teachers drew from the closing the lesson that the manner of a gift is as visible to God as the gift itself. The person who gives in the way the sentence describes is seen, and the person whose act is spoiled by the conduct that follows it is seen as well, so that the two parables rest on the same knowledge.

The sentence therefore ends with the assurance that accompanies the reward. The community's preachers used the clause when they spoke about sincerity, and the passage's treatment of charity reaches its conclusion with the statement about the divine sight.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the description of the givers who seek the pleasure of God with certainty in their hearts, the image of the garden on the height, the double harvest that the rain brings, the sufficiency of the light rain, and the attribute of sight that closes the passage. The community's teachers used the verse when they encouraged the believers whose means were small, and the sūrah's next verses turn to the parable of the garden that is destroyed at the end of a life, so that the whole section presents the harvest and its loss together.
