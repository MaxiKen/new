## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:75

> Joseph’s brothers responded, “The price will be ˹the enslavement of˺ the one in whose bag the cup is found. That is how we punish the wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Penalty Is the Person**

The brothers' answer is unusual in form. They do not name a fine, a beating or a period of servitude; they say that the recompense is the man in whose bag the object is found — he himself is the penalty. The wording identifies the thief with the compensation owed for the theft, so that the person is handed over rather than punished and then released. The commentators explain the custom behind the formula: in their tradition a thief was given over to the person he had stolen from, just as the thief had taken that person's property. The logic is one of equivalence rather than retribution, and it produces a transfer of the offender into the victim's custody.

**A Family Law, Not an Egyptian One**

The clause *that is how we punish wrongdoers* specifies whose law is being stated. Not the king's, not the land's — ours. The brothers are describing the practice of their own household, and they offer it as the rule to be applied if their denial proves false. The distinction is what makes verse 76 necessary. The narrator explains that under the king's law Joseph could not have taken his brother, and one account of the Egyptian rule describes beating plus double restitution, after which the offender went free. The brothers have therefore supplied, unprompted, the only legal basis available for the outcome Joseph wants. The specification is what makes it operative. Egyptian practice would have imposed a fine or forced labour; the law of Jacob's household attaches the thief's own person to the offence, which is the only formulation under which a man may be lawfully detained for a theft found upon him. Joseph needed that formulation and he received it from the men he was prosecuting.

**They Answer Without Hesitation**

The verse records no deliberation. Ten men, one of whom is about to be searched along with the rest, state a penalty that would put the offender into the custody of the official accusing them, and they state it immediately and confidently. The confidence is explicable: they believe the cup is not in any of their bags. A person certain of his innocence does not calculate the cost of a hypothetical penalty. The chapter is careful here. The brothers are not foolish; they are misinformed, and their misinformation is the result of a disclosure made privately to one of them and withheld from the rest (12:69). Hesitation would have been natural, and its absence is the point. Nine men who believe their brother innocent have no reason to qualify a statement of law, and none of them suspects that the law being recited is about to be applied to one of their own. Their confidence is not stupidity; it is the ordinary confidence of people with no reason to think the question concerns them.

**The Trap Is Theirs**

The whole device reaches its completion in this verse, and the completion is spoken by the trapped party. Joseph placed the cup, the herald announced the loss, the reward was offered, the denial was sworn and the penalty question was asked — but the rule that will actually be applied comes from the brothers' mouths. Nothing is imposed on them from outside their own legal tradition, and nothing is done to them that their own stated custom does not authorise. The chapter's interest in this arrangement is evident in verse 76, which attributes the plan to divine inspiration precisely because it required the brothers to supply the sanction voluntarily. The voluntariness is why the attribution is needed at all. A trap set by one man's cleverness would leave the detained party a grievance and the reader a question about fairness; a sanction supplied by that party's own mouth, under a law his own father taught him, leaves neither. The chapter settles the ethics of the scene before the scene has ended.

**Why the King's Law Could Not Be Used**

Verse 76 states the constraint plainly: Joseph could not have taken his brother under the king's law, unless God willed. The commentators explain the point in practical terms. Egyptian procedure for theft would have punished Benjamin and released him, so the objective — keeping him in Egypt — could not be reached by applying the local rule. Joseph was the ruler's minister and was bound by the ruler's law; he could not invent a penalty for his own purposes. What he could do was ask a party of foreign traders what penalty their own tradition prescribed, and then apply that penalty to a case in which they had accepted its application in advance. The verse is a study in jurisdiction.

**Benjamin Is Included in the Statement**

The brothers say *we* punish wrongdoers this way. Benjamin is one of them, has been travelling with them, has sworn nothing separately and is present when the family's law is announced. He knows the cup is in his bag. He hears his brothers state the consequence of its being found, and he does not interrupt. The tradition reports that Joseph asked him to keep the disclosure secret, and the verse is consistent with that instruction: silence here is obedience to a brother rather than complicity in a theft. The chapter does not explain Benjamin's silence at this point and does not need to, since verse 69 has already supplied the reason. His inclusion completes the bind. The law he has just heard his brothers state applies to him as the person in whose bag the object was found, and he cannot contest it without revealing what he was told in confidence. Benjamin stands caught between a rule he accepts and a secret he has promised to keep, and the chapter leaves him inside both.

**The Verse Establishes the Ground of Verse 79**

When the brothers plead in verse 78 for one of themselves to be taken in Benjamin's place, Joseph's refusal in verse 79 rests on the rule stated here: God forbid that we take anyone other than the one with whose property we found our goods — to do so would make us wrongdoers. The refusal is not arbitrary. It appeals to the standard the brothers supplied in verse 75, under which the offender and only the offender is handed over. Joseph holds them to their own law at the precise moment when relaxing it would serve them, and the chapter presents that as justice rather than as severity. That ground is supplied entirely by the accused. Joseph never states the penalty himself, never argues for it, and never has to defend it when he applies it; he enforces a rule his prisoners announced and then repeated. The chapter is careful to make the detention lawful in the eyes of everyone present, including the nine men who leave Egypt without their brother.

**A Rule Stated in Confidence Becomes a Rule Applied in Grief**

The distance between verse 75 and verse 78 is the chapter's sharpest irony. The brothers state a penalty they believe cannot reach them, and three verses later they are begging to be substituted under it. What was offered as proof of innocence becomes the instrument that detains their youngest brother and keeps their eldest in Egypt (12:80). The sūrah does not comment on the reversal. It reports the statement, reports the search, reports the discovery and reports the plea, and leaves the reader to observe that the law these men invoked was invoked by them, in their own words, about a brother they had once sold and were now about to lose.
