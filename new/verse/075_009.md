## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:9

> and the sun and the moon are brought together,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Orbits, One Ending**

The moon was darkened in the previous verse; here the sun is joined to it. *Jumiʿa* is a passive of gathering, and the two bodies that had been kept apart for the whole of created time are brought into one place and one condition. The sūrah does not say they collide, and the commentators did not read it that way — they read it, with the parallel passage in 81:1–2, as an enfolding in which both lights are extinguished and rolled up together. The joining is therefore not a crash but an assembly: two separate things are made to share a single fate. What had been the most reliable pair of objects in the sky becomes, in one clause, a pair of undone things.

**What the Gathering Is Not**

Care is needed about what the verse is claiming, because the imagination runs ahead of the text. Nothing here describes the sun falling on the earth, or the two bodies striking one another, or a physical catastrophe of a kind that could be modelled. The verb is the ordinary verb of collection, the same verb used elsewhere for assembling people and for assembling scattered parts. Its force is relational: two things which were defined by their separation are now defined by their conjunction. The created order was built out of distinctions — light and dark, sun and moon, day and night — and the undoing of that order is described as the collapse of the distinctions rather than as the destruction of the material. The reader is not being given physics. He is being told that the arrangement is over.

**The Order Was Built on Separation**

The Qur'an is emphatic that the two lights are kept apart by command, and it says so in the same terms in several sūrahs. *“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own.”* (36:40). *“And He is the One Who created the day and the night, the sun and the moon—each travelling in an orbit.”* (21:33). *“The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision.”* (55:5). The pattern in these verses is always the same: distinctness, measure, and a term. Two bodies on separate tracks, each with its appointed course, never interfering. That arrangement is what the present verse terminates. Gathering the two is the exact negation of the description that has been repeated throughout the Book, which is why the image lands as hard as it does.

**The Same Verb That Was Used for the Bones**

The most striking feature of the wording is one that translation hides. The verb here — *jumiʿa*, they are gathered — is from the same root as the verb in v. 3, where the denier asks whether God can *gather* his bones. The sūrah has thus answered the objection by using its own word against a harder object. The man who thought that gathering scattered bones was beyond the Creator is now told that the sun and the moon are gathered, and the sentence is not presented as difficult. If the greatest bodies in the sky can be collected into one place, the collection of a skeleton is not the difficult case it was made out to be. The sūrah's argument has come full circle in six verses, and it closes by making the denier's own vocabulary describe the undoing of his world.

**Both Lights Are Servants, Not Powers**

Nothing in the Qur'an permits the sun or the moon to be treated as an independent power. They are described repeatedly as *musakhkharāt*, subjected, made to serve by command, and always for a stated purpose: *“He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (6:96). The regularity that impresses an observer is the regularity of something obeying, not of something ruling. This is also why their dissolution requires no contest. Nothing is being defeated here, and no rival power is being overthrown; a servant's commission has simply reached its end. The universe does not end in a battle but in a discharge. The sun's own term is stated in this vocabulary elsewhere: *“The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (36:38), and the same note is struck in the creation accounts, where the sun, moon and stars are created *“all subjected by His command.”* (7:54). A fixed term is the opposite of a necessity. Whatever has been given a term is a thing that stops, and the regularity that looked like independence was only the reliability of obedience. The Qur'an even anticipates the religious use people would make of these bodies: *“Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah, Who created them ˹all˺, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺.”* (41:37). Servants cannot shelter anyone.

**The World That Made Postponement Possible**

Taken with the previous verse, the couplet removes both of the instruments that made delay feel safe. The moon was the measure of intervals; the sun was the marker of days. With the measure darkened and the marker gathered in, the world in which a person could say *later* has closed. That is the thread running from the mockery of v. 6 to this point, and it explains why the sūrah spends two of its shortest verses on the sky rather than on the earth. The denier's objection depended on the stability of the order above him. The sūrah answers by turning out its lights. It is a small detail that the two lights are named in the order of their usefulness to a person postponing something: the measure first, then the day. The sequence reads like the dismantling of a schedule, and the sūrah's hearers, who had asked for the date in mockery, are being shown the date's precondition being removed.
