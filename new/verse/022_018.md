## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:18

> Do you not see that to Allah bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and all those on the earth, as well as the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, and ˹all˺ living beings, as well as many humans, while many are deserving of punishment. And whoever Allah disgraces, none can honour. Surely Allah does what He wills.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Catalogue Runs Downwards, and Then Stops at You**

The verse opens with the widest possible claim — everything in the heavens and everything on the earth prostrates to God — and then particularizes: the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, the moving creatures, and many of mankind.

The order is a descending one, and it is worth following. It moves from the greatest luminous bodies to the fixed features of the landscape to growing things to animals, and then it arrives at human beings, where the grammar changes. For everything before, prostration is asserted outright. For humanity, the verse splits: many prostrate, and many have the punishment become due against them.

The structure is the whole point. Human beings are the only item on the list that can do otherwise, and the verse marks that difference by breaking its own pattern at exactly the place where the reader is standing.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat draws the conclusion the catalogue implies, as a direct prohibition: *“Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah, Who created them ˹all˺”* (41:37). The luminaries named, the prostration redirected, the reason given in the relative clause — *who created them*. Verse 18's list does the same work by enlistment rather than by command: the sun that is itself bowing cannot be bowed to, and the reader who sees the catalogue aright has already obeyed the prohibition.

**How a Mountain Prostrates**

The obvious question is what prostration means when the subject is a star or a stone, and the commentators answered it in two directions that are not mutually exclusive.

One reading takes *sujūd* in its literal sense of bowing down and holds that every creature performs an act proper to its own mode of being, which we do not perceive. Sūrah al-Raʿd is the controlling text: *“To Allah ˹alone˺ bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and the earth—willingly or unwillingly—as do their shadows, morning and evening”* (13:15). Sūrah al-Naḥl adds the angels to the same list (16:49), and Sūrah al-Raḥmān includes the stars and the trees in a single line: *“The stars and the trees bow down ˹in submission˺”* (55:6).

The other reading takes the prostration as the creature's total submission to the laws it cannot break — a star's obedience is its orbit. Sūrah al-Isrāʾ holds the two together and explains why the language is not ordinary description: *“There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification”* (17:44).

The stronger reading keeps both: the submission is real, and it is not the kind of thing a human observer could confirm. The verse is making a claim about what creation is, not about what can be measured.

**Why the Sun Is Listed and the Angels Are Not**

A detail that has drawn comment: the great bodies mentioned are precisely the ones human beings have worshipped. The sun, the moon and the stars were the objects of the oldest cults known to the Arabs and to their neighbours.

The verse's move is to enlist them on the other side. The things people bowed to are themselves bowing, and they have been doing it the whole time. This is the same argument Ibrāhīm makes in Sūrah al-Anʿām when he watches a star, then the moon, then the sun and rejects each in turn because it sets — the argument being that a thing which is itself subject is not a thing to be subject to.

That is why the list is not decoration. Every item on it is a witness for the prosecution.

Abraham's argument is preserved at the moment of the star's setting: *“When the night grew dark upon him, he saw a star and said, ‘This is my Lord!’ But when it set, he said, ‘I do not love things that set’”* (6:76). Setting as the disqualification — what disappears cannot be depended on. Verse 18 applies the same test to the whole catalogue at once: everything listed is shown in the act of submission, and submission is the more general form of setting. What bows is not to be bowed to.

**The Reader Is Asked to Prostrate Too**

This is one of two verses in this sūrah at which the Qur'an's reciters bow, and the practice is attested in the ḥadīth literature. ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir reported that he asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ whether Sūrat al-Ḥajj contains two prostrations, and was told that it does, and that whoever does not perform them should not recite them — recorded in *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* (1402), where al-Albānī graded the chain weak, though others have accepted it. A related report from ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, also in Abū Dāwūd (1401), counts fifteen prostrations in the Qur'an including two in this sūrah.

The fit between verse and practice is exact. A reader who has just recited a list of everything in creation prostrating is invited to join it. The alternative is to be the exception the verse names — the one who hears the catalogue and remains upright.

Sūrah al-Najm closes on the command verse 18 implies: *“Instead, prostrate to Allah and worship ˹Him alone˺!”* (53:62). The reciter who has heard the catalogue and reaches its end faces the same two options the verse lays out — join the prostrating many, or stand with the many against whom punishment is due. The bowed recitation is the choice made with the body: the list read, the invitation accepted, the reader no longer upright among the upright.

**The Split Inside Humanity**

*Wa-kathīrun mina al-nāsi wa-kathīrun ḥaqqa ʿalayhi al-ʿadhāb* — and many of mankind, and many against whom the punishment has come due. The repetition of *kathīr*, many, sets the two groups in balance, and the word for the second group's condition is *ḥaqqa*, became due — a claim that has matured, the way a debt does.

Both readings of that clause deserve their weight. On one, the division is between those who prostrate and those who refuse. On the other, the second *kathīr* is a continuation of the first: many of mankind prostrate, and many of mankind have punishment owed against them — meaning the same broad category described from two angles, since a person can be both.

What is clear is that humanity is the only part of creation described as internally divided. The stars are not split; the trees are not split. The creature made to bow freely is the creature that can be counted on both sides of the line.

**"Whoever God Disgraces, None Can Honour"**

The closing sentence is the sūrah's third use of this form, and its logic is the same each time: if God abases someone, no one can confer honour on him instead.

The point is sovereignty, and it is stated without qualification for the same reason it is stated in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān: *“If Allah helps you, none can defeat you. But if He denies you help, then who else can help you?”* (3:160). Honour in this life is a commodity that human beings trade in and believe they control. The verse places its source somewhere no one can touch, which is simultaneously a warning to the proud and a consolation to the humiliated.

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān places both verbs in the same hand: *“You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please — all good is in Your Hands”* (3:26). Honouring and disgracing as two motions of one hand, with the good named as what the hand holds. Verse 18's closing is that sentence with its consequence drawn: disgrace from that hand has no appeal, because there is no second source of honour to apply to. The proud man's creditors cannot help him, and the humiliated believer's debtors cannot harm him.
