## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:117

> Indeed, your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Formula and Where It Appears**

Indeed, your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is rightly guided. The sentence closes the warning about the majority with a statement about knowledge, and the Book repeats it almost word for word in other places. The sūrah of the Pen uses it to answer those who accused the Prophet of being a madman, saying that his Lord knows best who has strayed from His way and who is rightly guided (68:7). The sūrah of the Star uses the same words at the end of its discussion of the people who followed assumption (53:30). And the sūrah of the Bee attaches the sentence to the command to invite people to the way of the Lord with wisdom and good instruction (16:125).

The repetition tells the reader how the Book wants the division between belief and disbelief treated. Both passages in which the formula appears most fully are answers to disputes in which the audience was claiming to know who was on the right road, and the Book's reply moves the question to the knowledge of God.

**The Name of the Way**

The verse speaks of God's Way, and the sūrah has used the same expression in the sentence before it, warning the Prophet that the majority would lead him away from the Way of God (6:116). The Book's term describes the road rather than the destination, and its usage throughout this chapter has been developed through the imagery of paths and travellers. The straight path is described at the end of the sūrah's long passage of prohibitions (6:153), and the sūrah of the Bee's version of this formula follows a command about inviting people to the Way of the Lord.

The same noun serves both verse halves here: there is one Way, and people either follow it or wander from it. The verse does not divide humanity into categories of temperament or standing; it describes them in relation to a road, and the road is the one the revelation marks out.

**The Two Groups Named**

The verse names two conditions rather than two classes of people, and the Book's descriptions of the two are stated throughout the sūrah. The people who strayed are the ones whose conduct the chapter has described in detail; the people who are guided are those who hear the message and follow the best of it, as the sūrah of the Troops describes them. The sentence states that the membership of each group is known with certainty, and the knowledge is God's.

That arrangement removes the judgement from human hands without removing the distinction itself. The Book tells the Prophet not to be a keeper over the people and not to claim purity for himself, and it tells the community that the final sorting belongs to the day when every person's deeds are presented. The formula in this verse is the positive statement of that arrangement: the distinction is real, and the knowledge of it is complete in one place.

**What the Verse Meant for the Prophet**

The Prophet lived among people who had committed themselves to denying his message, and the formula was given to him as a form of relief. He was not required to determine the standing of each person he met, and he was not required to secure the outcome of the dispute. The sūrah had already told him that he was neither keeper nor maintainer over the people who denied his message (6:107) and that the matter would be settled on the day when God informs them of what they did.

The Book gives the same relief in the sūrah of the Bee's version of the formula, which stands at the end of a passage on how the message is to be delivered: the invitation is made with wisdom and the outcome belongs to God. What the verse in front of us adds is the statement that the outcome is already known to God, which means the uncertainty belongs only to human observers.

**The Knowledge Claim in the Sūrah's Argument**

The claim that God knows the two groups is continuous with the sūrah's account of His knowledge in other passages. The sūrah stated that God knows the secret and the open, that not a leaf falls without His knowledge, and that He is the one who has the keys of the unseen (6:59; 6:60). The formula in this verse applies that knowledge to the question of guidance, and it does so in the context of the warning that the majority would lead the Prophet away from the Way.

The Book's arguments against the audience's religion have rested on the audience's ignorance of the unseen, and the contrast between human assumption and divine knowledge has run through the passage since the demand for signs. The verse states the contrast in a single sentence: the people are following assumptions, and the Lord knows.

**The Transition to the Passage That Follows**

The next verse returns to the dietary practices of the audience, commanding the believers to eat of what the name of God has been mentioned over and forbidding what has been dedicated to other than Him, and then describing the people who argue about it. The subject changes, but the argument continues along the same lines, since the dietary rules concerned claims about what God had permitted and forbidden.

The verse in front of us supplies the framework for that passage. The audience's practices rested on inherited assumption; the guidance rested on revelation; and the difference between the two roads is known to the One who made both claims possible. The Book states the framework once here and proceeds to another instance of the same dispute.
