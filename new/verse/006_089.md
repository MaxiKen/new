## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:89

> Those were the ones to whom We gave the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood. But if these ˹pagans˺ disbelieve in this ˹message˺, then We have already entrusted it to a people who will never disbelieve in it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Three Gifts Named Together**

Those were the ones to whom We gave the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood. The sentence summarizes what the catalogue of names had in common, and it names three gifts where a reader might have expected one. The Scripture is the revelation given to a messenger; wisdom is the understanding that allows it to be applied; prophethood is the office of carrying it to a people. The Book uses the same triple formula for the children of Israel generally, saying that God gave them the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood, granted them good and lawful provisions, and favoured them above the others (45:16).

The combination is important for the argument the sūrah has been making. The men listed in the previous verses were not honoured because of their lineage but because they received a message, understood it, and delivered it. The three gifts describe an office rather than a status, and the sentence that follows this one states what happened to the office when the audience refused it.

**Wisdom as a Distinct Gift**

The Book treats wisdom as a distinct gift from revelation, and it describes its own message as containing both. The sūrah of the Family of Imran says that it is not appropriate for a man blessed with the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to tell people to worship him instead of God, but that he would instruct them to be devoted to the worship of their Lord (3:79). The sūrah of the Women describes the descendants of Abraham receiving the Book and wisdom along with great authority (4:54).

Those passages show what the sūrah is claiming for the men in its catalogue. They had the text, the understanding of the text, and the authority to teach it, and the combination is what the Book means when it calls a man a prophet. The audience in Makkah was hearing a man who claimed the same three things, and the sūrah places him in the line by naming the gifts he shares with them.

**The Transfer of the Trust**

But if these pagans disbelieve in this message, then We have already entrusted it to a people who will never disbelieve in it. The sentence announces a transfer, and it does so without naming the people who will receive it. Commentators took the community in question to be the believers of Madinah and the generations that carried the message afterward, and the Book states the same principle in several places: if the audience turns away, God will replace them with another people (47:38; 9:39).

The sūrah of the Table gives the promise in a form that describes the replacement community, saying that if any among the believers abandon their faith, God will bring a people whom He loves and who love Him, humble with the believers and firm toward the disbelievers, striving in His way and fearing no blame from anyone (5:54). The verse in front of us states the same fact in the past tense, as though the transfer had already been decided, which is the Book's way of expressing certainty about what God will do.

**Why the Warning Is Addressed to the Audience's Claim**

The sentence is aimed at the claim of inheritance that the sūrah has been dismantling. The Makkans assumed that the tradition of Abraham guaranteed their standing, and the verse answers that the tradition is carried by whoever receives it, not by whoever can claim descent from it. The previous verse made the same point by stating that even the prophets would have lost their deeds by association with God (6:88).

The Book's treatment of the Children of Israel in other sūrahs follows the same pattern. They received the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood, and the Book describes their failure to keep the covenant and the consequences that followed, including the transfer of favour to a community that would receive the final message. The sūrah's audience is being shown that the pattern they knew from the earlier communities was now applying to them.

**The Identity of the People Who Will Not Disbelieve**

The verse leaves the recipients of the trust unnamed, and the commentators offered several identifications: the emigrants and the helpers of Madinah, the people of the Book who accepted the message, the angels, or a community whose identity would be revealed by events. The Book uses the same formula when it says that God will replace a people with others whom He loves and who love Him (5:54), and in another place when it says that He has entrusted the message to a people who will never disbelieve in it.

What the verse establishes is that the message does not depend on any one audience. The sūrah's audience had been told in the previous verse that association with God would waste the deeds of the greatest men in their tradition; this verse tells them that the message would survive their rejection and find other carriers. The warning and the assurance are stated in one breath, and the assurance is about the message rather than about the audience.

**The Comfort the Sentence Gives the Prophet**

The sentence also addresses the Prophet's situation directly. He had been told that he was not a keeper over his people (6:66), that every matter has a fixed time (6:67), and that he would face mockery (6:68). The announcement of a people who would never disbelieve gave him something to look toward while the city he lived in refused the message.

The Book states the same comfort in other forms, including the promise that God is sufficient for the Prophet against those who mock him and the statement that the message is a reminder to the whole world (6:90, in the sentence that follows). The sūrah is moving from the argument with Makkah toward a declaration of the message's scope, and the next verse completes that movement by commanding the Prophet to follow the guidance of the men just catalogued.
