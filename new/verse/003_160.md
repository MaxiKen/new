## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:160

> If Allah helps you, none can defeat you. But if He denies you help, then who else can help you? So in Allah let the believers put their trust.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Conditions Stated Together**

The verse sets the help of God against His withholding of help, and it states the consequence of each. If God helps the community, nothing can overcome it, and if He abandons it, no one else can take His place.

The second clause of the sentence is the one that carries the weight. The community at that moment was calculating its strength in terms of numbers, allies, and the quality of its position, and the verse reduces the calculation to a single term. The help it needed was not the kind that could be secured by arrangement.

**The Truth of the First Clause**

The first clause is stated as an absolute: when God helps, none can overcome. The Book's promises of this kind are always connected in the surrounding passages to the community's own conduct, and this verse does not suspend the connection.

The community had been helped at Badr with everything the two clauses describe, and it had been shown at Uḥud what happens when the connection is broken. The sentence does not promise that every engagement will end in victory; it states who controls the outcome, and it leaves the community's responsibilities where the earlier verses placed them.

**The Reading That Sees the Two Battles**

The commentators frequently read the two clauses of this verse as a description of the community's recent history: God helped the believers at Badr, and He withheld His help at Uḥud. The verse would then be a statement of the difference between the two days rather than a conditional promise.

The reading gives the sentence a particular force for its first listeners, who had lived through both days within a year. The sūrah had already explained the change in terms of the community's own conduct, and this verse states the result in terms of help received or withheld, without adding any qualification that would soften the contrast.

**The Second Reading of the Closing Phrase**

The phrase rendered as after Him, or thereafter, can be read in two ways. Some take it as referring to time, so that the question is who could help after God has withheld His help. Others take the pronoun as referring to God Himself, so that the sense is who could help apart from Him.

Al-Rāzī records the second reading, which harmonises the verse with the sentence in the sūrah that declares God to be the master of the believers and the best of helpers. On either reading the question in the verse is unanswerable, and the function of the sentence is to leave the community with no alternative to reliance on God.

**Who Else Could Help**

The Book states the same thought in several places. One passage asks whether the idols that people invoke besides God could undo a harm if God willed it, or withhold a mercy if He willed that, and concludes that God is sufficient and that the faithful place their trust in Him.

Another passage reports the declaration of the believers that nothing will befall them except what God has destined, that He is their protector, and that in Him the believers put their trust. The verse before the reader states the same principle in the form of questions, and the community is left to answer them.

**The Command to Trust**

The verse closes by instructing the believers to place their trust in God, and the instruction is the practical conclusion of the two questions. Trust in the Book's usage is not the abandonment of effort; the sūrah has just commanded consultation, preparation, and firmness.

Trust, in the passages that explain it, is what accompanies a person's work once he has done what he can. The Book names it among the marks of the believers in a passage that describes hearts trembling at the remembrance of God and faith increasing when His revelations are recited. The community's task, on this understanding, is to act and then to leave the outcome with the one who holds it.

**A Verse Recited Throughout Life**

The sentence has been carried by Muslims in every generation as a statement of where reliance belongs, and it is used in circumstances far removed from a battlefield. Its usefulness in ordinary life comes from its structure: it disposes of the question of who controls outcomes before any particular difficulty is described.

The Book states the same teaching in other passages with different images, and the community's teachers have placed this verse at the head of their instructions about trust. The reason is that it gives the doctrine in a form that can be recited in a single breath, which is what a person needs in a moment of fear.

**The Close of the Passage's Movement**

The verse brings the passage that began with the rumour of the Prophet's death to a close. The community had been told that the messenger would die, that death comes at an appointed time, that the failures of the day had been pardoned, and that its dead had been gathered to God. It is now told where help comes from.

The sequence is one of the sūrah's characteristic movements, from a crisis to its lessons and then to a statement about God that reorders the community's understanding of its position. The verses that follow move on to new material about the fate of those who deny and the signs God has given, and the sentence about help and trust stands as the ground on which the community is expected to read everything that comes next.

**The Chain of Statements About Help in This Sūrah**

This sentence is the last in a series of statements about divine assistance that runs through the chapter, and reading them together shows how the argument has been built. The passage about Badr promises reinforcement from the angels and attaches it to the community's patience (3:124–25). The statement that God helped at Badr when the community was weak follows shortly after (3:123), and the reminder that victory comes from God alone closes the same section (3:126). Later in the account of Uḥud, the community is told that God had fulfilled His promise to them and that some of them wavered (3:152).

By the time the reader arrives at this verse, help has been promised, granted, conditioned, and apparently withdrawn. The two clauses here stand apart from all of that as a statement about agency itself: assistance is not a resource that appears and disappears but an act of a single will, and the second clause names the consequence for anyone who looks elsewhere. The sentence therefore anchors the community's reading of its own history, whether that history consists of a victory or of a defeat.
