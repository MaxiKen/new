## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:92

> Joseph said, “There is no blame on you today. May Allah forgive you! He is the Most Merciful of the merciful!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word Used Twice in the Qur'an**

The noun Joseph chooses for what he is withholding denotes reproach, upbraiding, the rubbing of a fault into a person's face. The commentators describe the phrase as a beautiful form of forgiveness, and note that the word can also carry the sense of physical punishment. Both senses are relevant. Joseph is refusing to blame these men and refusing to punish them, and he is doing both in a single clause that names the thing he will not do rather than the thing he has done. The chapter's use of the term is economical: one word covers the reproach they expect, the penalty they might fear, and the accounting they have just offered in verse 91.

**"Today" Fixes the Amnesty in Time**

The temporal particle is significant. The brothers stand before Joseph on the day of their lowest position: a famine has stripped them of goods, their youngest brother is detained under a penalty they proposed, their eldest is stranded by a vow, their father's sight has failed from weeping, and they have just confessed. The amnesty is dated to that day. Joseph does not wait for evidence of reform, for a period of probation, or for his father's judgement. The forgiveness is immediate and it is announced at the moment when the offenders are least able to offer anything in exchange for it, which is the condition under which forgiveness is distinguishable from negotiation. The date also forecloses the future. A forgiveness without a time reference could be withdrawn the next time the offender disappointed; a forgiveness dated today closes the matter at the moment it is spoken. Joseph is not promising patience with his brothers going forward, he is declaring that the case is finished, and the chapter records the declaration as the end of the accusation.

**The Prophet ﷺ Used These Words at the Conquest of Makkah**

The tradition records that Joseph's sentence became the model for the Prophet's own amnesty. During the conquest of Makkah, when the city had been overtaken and the Quraysh had no hope of escape, he asked them what they expected him to do with them. They answered that they expected good from a noble brother, the son of a noble brother. He then said that he spoke to them as Joseph had spoken to his brothers: there is no reproach against you — go, for you are free. The report is transmitted in the sīrah and tafsīr literature, and its significance is that the Qur'anic verse was treated by the Prophet ﷺ as a precedent for a political amnesty granted to people who had persecuted him for two decades.

**He Does Not Forgive in His Own Name**

The second clause is a prayer: may God forgive you. Joseph has removed the reproach that belonged to him, and the sin that does not belong to him he refers to God. The distinction is exact and it matters. A person can waive an injury done to himself; he cannot absolve an offence against God. The brothers' crime involved both — a brother betrayed and a father deceived, a trust broken before God and an oath sworn in His name (12:66). Joseph's sentence handles the first by renouncing it and the second by interceding for it, and the chapter will show the intercession being taken up by the father in verses 97 and 98. The referral is both humble and practical. Forgiveness given in God's name cannot be measured against the forgiver's feelings, which is what makes it reliable; it also opens a route for the brothers' remaining obligation, since an offence against God requires an approach to God. Joseph supplies the first half here and the chapter supplies the second in verses 97 and 98.

**"The Most Merciful of the Merciful"**

The superlative closes the verse and it belongs to a family of usage the chapter has already established. Jacob used the same formula when refusing to trust his sons with Benjamin, pairing it with the statement that God is the best of keepers (12:64). Moses used it after the golden calf, asking forgiveness for himself and his brother (7:151). In each instance the formula is spoken by someone who has been wronged or bereaved and has no remedy left. Joseph's use of it here places him inside that pattern and answers his father's earlier words from the same vocabulary — the son forgives in the terms the father trusted. The shared vocabulary binds the two halves of the family. Jacob used the same superlative when he consented to let Benjamin go, transferring the risk to God rather than minimising it; Joseph uses it when he releases his brothers from blame, transferring the debt rather than collecting it. Father and son are described in identical terms at the two decisive moments of the sūrah.

**The Qur'an's General Ground for This Act**

Nothing in the verse argues for forgiveness, but the Qur'an supplies the argument elsewhere in explicit terms. Believers are told: *“Let them pardon and forgive. Do you not love to be forgiven by Allah?”* (24:22). The reward for pardoning is stated as exceeding the equivalence that justice would require: *“whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah”* (42:40), and *“whoever endures patiently and forgives — surely this is a resolve to aspire to”* (42:43). Verse 92 is the narrative instance of those rulings, and verse 90 has already supplied the other half of the pairing — patience — in Joseph's own mouth moments before. The pairing is completed across two verses by the same speaker. Joseph describes himself as one who fears God and is patient, and then forgives in the name of the Most Merciful; the qualities he claims and the quality he invokes belong to one account of why the amnesty is possible. The chapter refuses to separate the ethics from the theology behind them.

**The Forgiveness Precedes the Full Account**

Verse 100 will contain Joseph's own reading of the last thirty years: God was kind to him when He freed him from prison and brought his family from the desert after Satan had stirred up rivalry between him and his brothers. The amnesty in verse 92 is given before that explanation, and before the brothers have any idea of the arrangement they were inside. They are forgiven without being shown the mechanism, and the mechanism is disclosed to a family already reconciled rather than as a condition of reconciliation. The chapter's order is deliberate: forgiveness first, understanding afterwards, and the understanding attributed to a third party rather than to the offenders. Both choices protect the offenders. Forgiveness offered before the account means the brothers are not required to justify themselves in order to be released; understanding attributed to God means their eventual comprehension is treated as a gift rather than as an achievement. Joseph removes from the transaction every element that would have let him hold something over the men standing in front of him.

**Nothing Is Required of the Brothers**

The verse attaches no condition to the amnesty. Joseph does not ask for an explanation of the well, does not request the return of the price he was sold for, does not require a public acknowledgment in Canaan, and does not make the forgiveness contingent on the brothers' future conduct. Verse 91 has already supplied a confession, and verse 92 accepts it without building on it. What follows is practical rather than penitential: a shirt to carry, a father to heal, a family to bring. The chapter's account of reconciliation contains no rite, no restitution and no probation, and the absence is the point — the injured party simply stops holding the matter, in the same sentence in which he asks God to forgive it.
