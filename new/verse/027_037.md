## Sūrah an-Naml 27:37

> Go back to them, for we will certainly mobilize against them forces which they can never resist, and we will drive them out from there in disgrace, fully humbled.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Threat Is Stated Plainly**

Having refused the gift, Sulaymān sends the envoy home with a message that contains no diplomacy at all. Forces they cannot face, expulsion from their own land, humiliation. Al-Ṭabarī identifies the addressee as the envoy from Sheba, which is the plain reading — the verb is singular and the delegation is being dismissed.

There is no point softening this. The letter had been restrained, the demand brief, the tone measured. This is not. What changed between the letter and this speech is that the demand was answered with treasure, and treating a claim about God as a matter for purchase is what draws the sharpness. The escalation is a response to the form of the reply rather than to its content. She had not refused; she had tried to convert the question into a different kind of question, and he refuses the conversion in the strongest terms available.

**Her Own Words Turned Around**

The most striking feature of this verse is that Sulaymān uses her vocabulary. She had said that kings entering a land debase its nobles — *jaʿalū aʿizzata ahlihā adhillah*, making the honoured into the abased. He now says he will drive them out *adhillah*, abased, and they will be *ṣāghirūn*, humbled.

Whether or not the envoy reported her council speech to him, the Qur'an places the same root in both mouths within four verses, and the effect on the reader is deliberate. Her general law about what kings do is quoted back at her as a specific forecast. She had used the observation as a reason to avoid war; he uses it as a description of what war would bring. Neither of them disagrees about the facts. The difference is that she thought the prospect was an argument for delay, and he is telling her it is an argument for decision. The echo is the most efficient possible reply to her reasoning, because it concedes her premise entirely.

**A Threat That Was Never Carried Out**

No army marches. The Qur'an reports no campaign against Sheba, no siege, no expulsion. What follows this speech is the queen deciding to travel to Jerusalem herself, and the next time the two are in the same narrative she is walking into his palace as a guest.

That outcome is what the threat was for. A warning that produces the change it warns about has succeeded and becomes unnecessary in the same moment, and the Qur'an's presentation of the episode assumes the reader will notice. There is a general principle here that the Book applies to its own warnings of punishment: they are issued to be acted on, not to be enjoyed. The chapter will shortly tell the stories of Thamūd and the people of Lūṭ, where the warnings were not acted on and the destruction came. Sheba is the case where the mechanism worked. Reading this verse as evidence of Sulaymān's harshness misses that its function in the narrative is to make harshness unnecessary.

**Why Force Was Legitimate to Threaten and Not to Use**

There is a real question here about consistency. If submission to God cannot be compelled, what is an army doing in the argument at all? The answer lies in what the threatened action actually addresses.

Sulaymān's stated aim is expulsion from a land, not conversion of a population. That is a political consequence, applied to a state that had received a clear demand and answered it with an attempt at purchase. Nothing in the threat says the people of Sheba would be made to believe. It says their court would be removed. The distinction holds throughout the Qur'an's treatment of the matter: belief is never described as coercible, while the political arrangements that protect a false order are treated as removable. Whether that distinction fully satisfies a modern reader is a fair question, but the text is careful about it, and the care shows in the verbs chosen here — mobilise, drive out, humble. None of them is a verb of conversion.

**The Envoy Carries Back Two Findings**

Consider what the delegation actually has to report. First, the king refused a treasure of the highest order without hesitation, which answers the queen's question about his motive. Second, he is willing and able to use force, which answers a question she had not asked but needed settled.

The two findings together are what make her next move rational rather than fearful. If he had accepted the gift and threatened anyway, she would face a greedy aggressor and the correct response would be resistance. If he had refused and made no threat, she could have ignored him. What she receives is the combination that forecloses both easy paths: a man who cannot be bought and who is not bluffing. Her own analysis of what kings do to lands had already told her the cost of testing the second part. She had built her decision procedure to produce exactly this kind of clarity, and it has produced it. Sulaymān, meanwhile, begins preparing for a visitor he has not yet been told is coming.

**"We" Rather Than "I"**

Every verb in the threat is first-person plural: we shall come upon them, we shall drive them out. In the previous verse Sulaymān spoke of himself in the singular — what God has given *me*. The shift is not stylistic grandeur.

Arabic royal speech did use the plural for a single sovereign, but the reading available here is more concrete, since Sulaymān genuinely commands a plurality. The forces described in verse 17 were jinn, humans and birds marshalled in ranks, and the threat concerns exactly those forces. When he speaks of what God gave him he uses the singular, because the gift was personal; when he speaks of what will arrive at Sheba's border he uses the plural, because an army is a collective. The grammar tracks the difference between a man and a state. The envoy hearing it would have drawn the intended conclusion: what is being promised is not a royal temper but a mobilisation, and the speaker has the apparatus to mean it.
