## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:1

> **The command of Allah is at hand, so do not hasten it. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him in worship˺!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Past Tense Used For A Future That Will Not Fail**

The sūrah opens with a verb in the past: atā — it has come. The subject is amr Allāh, the command or the decisive matter of Allah. Grammatically the event still lies ahead, yet it is spoken of as already accomplished. Arabic does this when certainty is absolute. Al-Ṭabarī reads the past here as taḥqīq, a way of saying the arrival is fixed and nothing will turn it back. Al-Rāzī notes the same device in other passages where the Hour is described as having drawn near. The effect on a Makkan listener was direct. They had heard warnings before and measured them by the calendar of their own impatience. By putting the verb in the past, the Qur'an removes the calendar from human hands. What Allah has decreed moves on its own schedule. The verse therefore does not argue about dates. It announces a reality and then forbids the response that turns warning into sport. Do not seek to hurry it. The prohibition touches both mockery and anxiety, both the scoffer who says bring it and the believer who wishes it would come quickly to settle accounts.

**Why The Command Covers More Than One Event**

Commentators differ on what amr means here, and the difference is instructive because both senses live in the word. Amr can mean a spoken command, as in kun fa-yakūn, and it can mean a matter, an affair that Allah brings about. Ibn Kathīr gathers reports that point in two directions. Some early authorities understood the command as the Hour itself, the Resurrection when every soul stands for reckoning. Others understood it as the worldly punishment that was already moving toward Quraysh, the defeat and breaking of their power that would become visible at Badr. The two readings support each other rather than compete. In Qur'anic usage the coming of Allah's amr often marks a turning point where denial can no longer be maintained, whether that turning point is the death of an individual, the collapse of a town, or the final gathering. Verses like 40:78, where judgment is passed when Allah's command comes, and 11:40, where the flood begins when the command arrives, show the pattern. Al-Qurṭubī observes that al-Naḥl will return to both horizons again and again: verses about the Hour, verses about destroyed peoples, verses about the moment angels take the soul. All are instances of one law: when Allah's decisive matter arrives, excuses end.

**The Mockery That Prompted The Prohibition**

Reports preserved by al-Wāḥidī and others link this opening to a specific Makkan habit. When the Prophet ﷺ recited that the Hour has drawn near, some listeners paused and waited, half expecting the sky to split that afternoon. When nothing visible happened, they turned waiting into mockery and said, if you are truthful, hasten it for us. The Qur'an records this demand in several forms: *They say, when will this promise be, if you are truthful* appears in 10:48, 21:38, 27:71, 34:29, 36:48, and the bolder challenge *If this is the truth from You, rain down stones upon us* appears in 8:32. The answer in this verse does not accept the premise. It says the command is at hand and forbids seeking haste. Al-Zamakhsharī explains the wisdom: to demand haste is to confuse delay with cancellation. A creditor who grants time has not forgiven the debt. Moreover, haste would harm the very people who ask for it. If Allah hastened evil for people as they hasten good, their term would already be ended, as 10:11 says. Every additional day is a space where a heart may turn, where a wrong may be repaired, where a witness may become a believer. The prohibition therefore carries mercy inside severity.

**Human Haste As A Described Weakness**

The Qur'an names haste as part of human makeup. *Man was created of haste* (21:37) and *man is ever hasty* in prayer for good and ill (17:11). This is not a passing remark. It explains why warnings are mocked and why relief is demanded before its time. The impatient person wants the future now, whether that future is punishment for his enemies or reward for himself. The Qur'an corrects both. To the scoffer it says, you will not hurry Allah. To the distressed believer it says, Allah's timing contains knowledge you do not have. In al-Naḥl this theme will return in verses about those who were wronged and then emigrated, who were told to be patient, and in the closing counsel to respond to harm with patience rather than excess. The opening thus sets a moral posture for the whole sūrah: do not live by the clock of impatience. Live by the clock of trust. The command is coming. Your task is not to pull it forward but to be ready when it arrives, and readiness in the Qur'an always means acknowledgment of Allah alone and deeds that match that acknowledgment.

**Glorified Above What They Associate**

The second half of the verse shifts from threat to transcendence: subḥānahu wa-taʿālā ʿammā yushrikūn. Subḥān declares Allah free of every defect and need. The root suggests swift movement away, as if the mind swims away from any unworthy thought. Taʿālā adds height: exalted far above. The phrase ʿammā yushrikūn carries two meanings that both apply. Allah is exalted above the partners themselves — the stones, idols, and imagined intercessors that cannot create, cannot hear, cannot know when they will be raised — and He is exalted above the act of association, which does not touch Him but only harms those who do it. This closing is not decoration. It explains why the demand for haste is foolish at its root. The Makkans imagined Allah as one power among others, whose threats could be tested like a rival's. If He is subḥān, beyond likeness and need, then His command does not compete for space. Nothing beside Him can delay it, prevent it, or shield anyone from it. The same seal appears again in 16:3 and elsewhere in the Qur'an, for example 10:18 and 30:40, always after a mention of creation or judgment, to remind the listener that tawḥīd is not one doctrine among many but the frame that makes sense of all the rest.

**The Name Al-Niʿam And The Place Of This Verse In The Sūrah**

Al-Qurṭubī reports that some early scholars called this chapter Sūrat al-Niʿam, the chapter of blessings, because no other Makkan sūrah counts Allah's gifts so patiently. That name helps place verse 1. Before counting blessings, the sūrah must clear the ground of mockery. A heart that treats Allah's promise as a joke will not see milk, honey, wool, and stars as signs; it will see them as mere things. So the sūrah begins with awe, then moves to gratitude. The sequence matters. The command that is at hand gives weight to every blessing that follows: cattle, horses, rain, crops, night and day, sea, mountains, landmarks, stars. Each is presented as coming from the One who is glorified above partners and whose command cannot be hurried. Linguistically, the insertion markers ˹with Him in worship˺ in the translation reflect the translators' effort to make clear that association here is in worship, not in essence. The Arabic yushrikūn already implies association in worship and lordship. Classical Arabic lexicons list sh-r-k as sharing or assigning a share, and in Qur'anic usage it almost always denotes giving to another what belongs to Allah alone in worship or obedience.
