## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:40

> Surely those who receive our revelations with denial and arrogance, the gates of heaven will not be opened for them, nor will they enter Paradise until a camel passes through the eye of a needle. This is how We reward the wicked.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Those Who Deny and Disdain**

The verse returns to the condition that the passage has described, naming those who deny the signs of their Lord and are too proud to accept them. The Book mentions the same combination in several passages, and the sūrah of the Heights itself, the sūrah of the Troops, and the sūrah of Fussilat describe the proud among those who reject the message (7:36; 39:59; 41:15). The verse in front of us places the condition at the head of the sentence that excludes them from the Garden.

The denial and the disdain are joined because the sūrah's narratives have shown them operating together. The communities that refused their messengers did not simply lack information; they regarded the message as beneath them, whether because the messenger was a man like themselves or because the message came to the poor before it came to the great. The verse in front of us states the condition in the terms that the sūrah has used throughout.

**The Gates of Heaven Closed**

The verse states that the gates of heaven will not be opened for those who are proud, and the commentary tradition gives two readings of the phrase. One reading refers to the Hereafter and to the entry into the Garden, which the verse states directly in its following clause. The other reading refers to the ascent of the deeds and prayers of such people, on the basis of the sūrah of Fāṭir's statement that good words ascend to God and that He lifts up the righteous deed (35:10). The two readings are joined in the tradition, since the refusal of the deed's ascent and the refusal of the person's entry belong to the same exclusion.

The image of the gates appears in several of the Book's descriptions of the Hereafter, and the sūrah of the Troops describes the crowds driven to Hell until its gates are opened, and the gardens whose gates are opened for the mindful as they arrive. The verse in front of us states the exclusion of the proud from the opening itself, and the sūrah's later descriptions of the two abodes supply the rest of the picture.

**The Camel and the Needle**

The verse states that the deniers will not enter the Garden until a camel passes through the eye of a needle, and the image is one of the Book's most memorable for an impossibility. The commentary tradition notes that the Arabic word may be read in two ways, one of which denotes the camel and the other a thick cable of the kind used to moor ships, and al-Zamakhsharī preferred the second reading because a cable through a needle's eye gives a more natural image. The two readings leave the force of the comparison intact.

The image appears in the Gospels as well, where the difficulty of a rich man's entry into the kingdom of God is compared to a camel's passage through the eye of a needle. The Book's use of the image in front of us concerns the proud and the deniers rather than the wealthy as such, and the point of the comparison is the same in both cases: the outcome is impossible while the condition remains.

**The Recompense of the Wicked**

The verse closes with the statement that this is how the wicked are recompensed, and the term identifies those who have earned the exclusion. The Book's descriptions of the reward of the wicked appear throughout, and the sūrah of the Heights will state in its following verses that the Fire is their bed and that the flames are their covering. This verse supplies the verdict, and the verses that follow describe the condition.

The Book's practice of closing a description of punishment with the statement that it is the recompense of a particular class appears throughout its accounts of the Hereafter, and the sūrah of the Clatterer and the sūrah of the Inevitable use the same form. The passage in front of us applies the form to the deniers and the proud, and the reader is left with the contrast between their condition and the condition of those who entered the Garden with their Lord's permission.

**The Pride That Bars the Garden**

The exclusion of the proud in front of us recalls the sūrah's account of the first refusal, when the adversary was told that arrogance had no place in the Garden (7:13). The two passages are connected by the same diagnosis, and the sūrah's narrative of the first human pair and its descriptions of the peoples who refused their messengers present the same fault in its individual and communal forms. The verse under discussion states the outcome for those who carry the fault to the end.

The Book's teaching about humility appears in its instructions about prayer, its warnings against boasting, and its statements that the servants of the Most Merciful walk upon the earth with humility. The text in front of us states the other side of the matter, since the pride that the Book condemns is the condition that bars the gate.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands fortieth in the chapter, and it follows the dialogue of the condemned and precedes the description of their bed in the Fire and of the gardens prepared for the believers. The passage as a whole closes the sūrah's opening section, which began with the letters and the Book and has moved through the commands, the account of the first pair, and the reckoning.

The reader who began the sūrah with its four letters has now been given the warning that the letters introduced. The Book has been sent down, the command has been stated, the term has been fixed, the messengers have been sent, and the two outcomes have been described. The verse before us states the last of them for the proud, and the chapter's following verses state the reward of the believers.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that the Fire will be the bed of the wicked and that flames will be their covering, and it repeats the statement that this is how the wrongdoers are recompensed (7:41). The exclusion from the Garden in front of us is followed by the description of the punishment in which the excluded remain.

The two verses together complete the picture of the end of the deniers. The gates are closed, the impossibility is stated, and the condition that awaits them is named. The sūrah then turns to the believers and their gardens, and the contrast that the passage has been building reaches its statement.
