## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:11

> Allah commands you regarding your children: the share of the male will be twice that of the female. If you leave only two ˹or more˺ females, their share is two-thirds of the estate. But if there is only one female, her share will be one-half. Each parent is entitled to one-sixth if you leave offspring. But if you are childless and your parents are the only heirs, then your mother will receive one-third. But if you leave siblings, then your mother will receive one-sixth—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. ˹Be fair to˺ your parents and children, as you do not ˹fully˺ know who is more beneficial to you. ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

### Expanded Commentary

**A revealed alternative to family power**

This is the first detailed inheritance ordinance in the sūrah, and it begins by placing children under divine protection. The pre-Islamic rule that inheritance belonged to adult male relatives is displaced by a schedule that recognizes daughters, sons, and parents. The verse does not leave the distribution to the preferences of the deceased or the strength of the surviving clan. It assigns portions through revelation because the people who benefit from a person’s affection are not always the people whom his power would protect.

**The shares of children**

When sons and daughters inherit together, the son’s portion is twice the daughter’s. This ratio is not a general statement that a son is twice a daughter’s worth. It is part of a larger financial structure in which the male heir bears obligatory maintenance for wives, children, and needy relatives, while a daughter’s inheritance remains her own property and is not a fund from which she must maintain a household. The distinction is a distribution of financial duties, not a ranking of human dignity.

If there are only daughters, the verse gives two or more a collective two-thirds and one daughter one-half. The remaining questions—such as residuary heirs and particular combinations—are treated through Prophetic practice and juristic reasoning. The apparent simplicity of the language should not encourage a person to calculate a complicated estate without qualified advice; debts, valid bequests, parents, spouses, and other heirs interact in ways that require the science of *farāʾiḍ*.

**The parents’ claims**

Each parent receives a sixth when the deceased leaves children. If there are no children and the parents are the only heirs, the mother receives a third, with the father taking the remainder under the established rules. When siblings are present, the mother’s share is reduced to a sixth. These provisions recognize that the deceased’s relationship to parents is not erased by marriage or adulthood, while also making room for the needs of a surviving household.

The statement that people do not know which parents or children are more beneficial to them corrects human attempts to measure affection. A child who seems dependent may become a source of good; a powerful heir may become a source of harm. God assigns because He knows the future and the hidden obligations better than the heirs do. The final words—All-Knowing, All-Wise—turn arithmetic into worship: the believer accepts a rule whose complete wisdom may exceed his immediate preference.

**Why the law is detailed**

The precision of the shares should itself be read as mercy toward families. Vague exhortations to be generous leave the weaker heir dependent on the stronger heir’s character. Fixed portions do not remove all complexity, but they set a floor below which affection, anger, or custom may not push a person. Learning the law is therefore a form of protecting kinship.
