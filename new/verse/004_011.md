## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:11

> Allah commands you regarding your children: the share of the male will be twice that of the female. If you leave only two ˹or more˺ females, their share is two-thirds of the estate. But if there is only one female, her share will be one-half. Each parent is entitled to one-sixth if you leave offspring. But if you are childless and your parents are the only heirs, then your mother will receive one-third. But if you leave siblings, then your mother will receive one-sixth—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. ˹Be fair to˺ your parents and children, as you do not ˹fully˺ know who is more beneficial to you. ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Divine Command, Not a Family Suggestion**

The verse opens with *yūṣīkum Allāhu fī awlādikum* — Allah commands you regarding your children. The verb *yūṣī* carries the sense of a binding directive, a bequest that the speaker expects to be carried out. By framing shares as something God enjoins, the Qur’an removes them from the realm of preference, negotiation, or clan custom. The sūrah has already moved from creation to guardianship to kinship, and now it turns that moral preparation into arithmetic. The community had just heard that men and women have obligatory shares whether little or much (4:7), and that the division should be witnessed and watched by God (4:6) and softened by provision for bystanders (4:8). Here the fractions arrive: a son’s share equal to two daughters when they inherit together, two-thirds for two or more daughters alone, one-half for a single daughter, one-sixth for each parent when there are offspring, one-third for the mother when there are no offspring and no siblings, and one-sixth for the mother when siblings exist. The verse closes with *farīḍatan min Allāh* — an obligation from Allah — matching its opening and making clear that these portions are not advice about fairness; they are the fair measure set by the One who knows.

**The Share of the Male Twice the Female Among Children**

The first rule — *li al-dhakari mithlu ḥaẓẓ al-unthayayn* — the male receives the equivalent of two females among children — has drawn the most discussion, and the sūrah itself supplies part of its context. The next verse will set the spouse shares where the husband receives half or a quarter and the wife receives a quarter or an eighth (4:12), showing a consistent pattern where the party bearing the duty of maintenance receives the larger portion. The Qur’an elsewhere explains that men are tasked with supporting women financially (4:34), and the law of inheritance follows that assignment of duty rather than a judgment of worth. When children inherit together, the son who will later be expected to maintain his own wife, children, and often his sisters and parents receives twice the daughter’s share, while the daughter’s share remains hers to keep, not to spend on others, including her husband’s family. Early commentators noted that when parents are the only heirs alongside a daughter, the equal sixth for each parent shows that the two-to-one ratio is not a universal male-female rule but a specific rule for siblings sharing as children. Shiite jurisprudence offers a further variation where remaining portions after fixed shares differ, but all schools agree that the daughter’s half, two-thirds, and the parents’ sixths are fixed by text.

**Daughters, Parents, and the Role of Siblings**

The verse details cases that had remained unsettled in custom. If there are only daughters — two or more — they receive two-thirds; if one, one-half. That single-daughter half leaves room for other fixed heirs and for the residue that goes to the nearest male relative, often the father, who in practice receives his sixth plus the remainder when he is that nearest kinsman. When offspring exist, each parent receives one-sixth, equally, which abrogated the earlier practice where parents received only through a discretionary bequest. When there is no offspring and parents are the sole heirs, the mother receives one-third, with the father taking the rest as residue, effectively two-thirds, which preserves the two-to-one pattern at the parental tier as well. When siblings exist alongside parents, the mother’s share drops from one-third to one-sixth. Commentators debated whether that reduction requires two siblings or one, and whether siblings inherit at all when the father is alive, with the majority holding that siblings are excluded by a living father and that the mother’s sixth in that case leaves five-sixths for the father. All shares are *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin yūṣī bihā aw dayn* — after bequest and debt — and Prophetic practice clarified that debts are paid first, then bequests up to one-third, then the fixed shares, a sequence that protects creditors and prevents a testator from disinheriting heirs by will.

**After Bequests and Debts — The Standing Order**

The repeated clause *after the fulfilment of bequests and debts* appears three times across verses 11 and 12, anchoring the whole inheritance system in a clear order. A person’s debts travel with his estate before any heir is considered; the Qur’an elsewhere warns against harming heirs through a will, and the addition *ghayra muḍār* — without harm — in the next verse (4:12) makes that explicit. A bequest that favours one heir over others, or that exceeds one-third when it reduces fixed shares, is counted as harm and therefore invalid. That limit surprised those who assumed a dying person’s word could reshape divine portions, but the sūrah treats the estate at death as already belonging to God, who then redistributes. The phrase *“you know not which of them are nearer to you in benefit”* (4:11) explains why God fixed the shares rather than leaving them to affection. A parent may love one child more, or expect more help from a son than a daughter, yet none knows who will be nearer in benefit in this life or the next, or who will serve the parent in old age or pray for him after. By assigning the math, the verse frees the testator from guessing and frees heirs from the bias of being favoured or forgotten.

**You Do Not Know Who Is More Beneficial**

The clause *ābāʾukum wa abnāʾukum lā tadrūna ayyuhum aqrabu lakum nafʿan* — your parents or your children, you do not know which are nearer to you in benefit — was read as both theological and practical. Theologically, benefit includes intercession, supplication, and the good deeds that continue after death; a child’s prayer or a parent’s loyalty may outweigh present utility. Practically, fortunes change, and the child who seemed least able may become the most supportive, or the parent thought independent may later need care. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse therefore asks the testator to surrender the illusion of foresight and to trust the division of the One described as *ʿAlīman Ḥakīmā* — All-Knowing, All-Wise. Those two Names close the verse for a reason. Knowledge covers every family configuration the law anticipates, and wisdom covers why the portions differ across tiers. The sūrah will repeat the same pair of Names after the spouse shares (4:12, 4:17, 4:26), creating a refrain that what seems intricate to people is known and wise with God. That refrain is not decoration; it is reassurance that the law is not arbitrary even where it is not immediately intuitive.

**An Obligation That Generates Knowledge**

The specificity of these verses produced a discipline. Because the fractions must be applied without error — halves, thirds, sixths, eighths, two-thirds — Muslim jurists developed the science of the shares, *ʿilm al-farāʾiḍ*, whose calculations require finding common denominators and handling residuary portions, a practice that contributed to the growth of algebra. The historical point matters because it shows how a verse read for family settlement generated tools for mathematics itself. More immediately, the verse taught the community that piety includes precision. Worship is not only prayer but also measuring correctly when emotion tempts to round up or round down. The sūrah will follow these shares with a promise — Gardens for those who keep God’s limits — and a warning — a humiliating punishment for those who transgress them (4:13–14) — showing that the arithmetic is also an act of obedience. A household that divides by these portions after debts and bequests, with witnesses and with *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6) still echoing, has turned a potential quarrel into an act of submission, and has honoured both the parents and the children without claiming to know better than the One who assigned each a share.
