## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:137

> Likewise, the pagans’ evil associates have made it appealing to them to kill their own children—only leading to their destruction as well as confusion in their faith. Had it been Allah’s Will, they would not have done such a thing. So leave them and their falsehood.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Killing of Children**

Likewise, the pagans' evil associates have made it appealing to them to kill their own children. The verse names the practice that the sūrah condemns in several later passages, and it attributes the impulse to the associates the audience had taken. The Book's word for the associates is the one it uses for the partners associated with God, and the same verse's opening connects the practice to the influence those partners had on the community.

The practice was the burial of infant daughters, which the audience carried out for reasons of poverty and honour, and the Book names the reason in its later prohibition: do not kill your children for fear of poverty, since God provides for them and for their parents (17:31). The sūrah of the Cattle's closing list of prohibitions repeats the command against killing children, and the sūrah of the Folding Up describes the day when the girl buried alive is asked for what crime she was put to death (81:8–9).

**The Leading to Destruction and Confusion**

Only leading to their destruction as well as confusion in their faith. The sentence states the two consequences the practice produced, and the Book's word for the second describes a division or splitting in the community's religion. The killing of children divided a community against itself, since the practice was applied to one sex and not the other, and it separated the people who carried it out from the guidance that the messengers brought.

The sūrah of the Cattle's later passage states the same consequence in another form, saying that the people who killed their children out of ignorance and forbade what God had provided have certainly strayed and are not guided (6:140). The verse in front of us supplies the description of the practice as a work of the associates, and the later verse states the loss that follows from it.

**The Standard the Book Exposes**

The Book returns to the practice and to the attitude behind it in several passages. The sūrah of the Bee describes the man who is given the news of a baby girl, whose face grows gloomy as he suppresses his rage, and who hides from his people wondering whether to keep her in disgrace or bury her alive, and the verse ends by calling the judgement of such people evil (16:58–59). The sūrah of the Ornament gives the same description of the man given news of what he attributes to the Most Compassionate (43:17), and the sūrah of the Star asks the audience whether they prefer sons for themselves while attributing daughters to God (53:21).

The Book's answer to the attitude is stated in the sūrah of Consultation, where God is described as creating whatever He wills, blessing whom He wills with daughters, blessing whom He wills with sons, granting both to whom He wills, and leaving whom He wills infertile, for He is All-Knowing and Most Capable (42:49–50). The verse in front of us describes the practice that the Book's statements about daughters answer, and the passage places the killing of children inside the larger account of the audience's false attributions.

**The Statement About God's Will**

Had it been Allah's Will, they would not have done such a thing. The sentence repeats the formula that the sūrah has used before in answering the audience's conduct, and its placement here is important. The same statement was made about the idolaters' association of partners with God (6:107), and the sūrah will later record the audience using the same plea as an excuse for their practices and rejecting it (6:148).

The formula states the dependence of everything on God's will, and the Book uses it in the passages that describe the resistance of the community to the message. What the sentence does not do is to excuse the practice. The Book elsewhere makes the same statement and then states the punishment, and the two belong together in its account of human responsibility.

**The Instruction to Leave Them**

So leave them and their falsehood. The verse closes with the instruction the Prophet has received twice already in this sūrah, when he was told to leave those who took their religion as play and amusement (6:70) and to leave the enemies with their deceit (6:112). The instruction in each case concerns the Prophet's own conduct rather than the abandonment of the mission, since the sūrah continues to address the audience's practices in the verses that follow.

The Book's use of this instruction is a feature of the sūrahs revealed in Makkah, where the community was small and the opposition was strong. The Prophet is told that the conduct of the people he addresses is not his to correct by force, and the sūrah's statement of the practices continues so that the reader knows what the message has come to replace.

**What the Verse Says About Inherited Practice**

The practice described in this verse was inherited, and the Book presents it as the product of the community's own invention and of the influence of the beings they associated with God. The sūrah's treatment of the audience's system has followed the same pattern throughout: the practices are stated in the community's own words, their origins are identified, and their results are described.

The verse's mention of the associates shows how the sūrah connects the different parts of the audience's religion. The same beings whose partnership with God was refuted in the earlier verses are described here as the source of a practice that destroyed families, and the Book treats the theology and the social consequences as parts of one system.

**The Passage That Follows**

The next verses continue the examination with the audience's rules about the cattle and the crops, the animals reserved for certain people, and the offspring of the herds (6:138–139), and the passage ends with the declaration that those who have murdered their children and forbidden what God provided have strayed (6:140). The verse in front of us introduces the theme of the section, which is what the community's false attributions produced in practice.

The sūrah has been laying out the audience's religion in pieces since its account of the jinn, and the passage that follows completes the survey. The reader who has followed the argument now sees the full shape of the system the message was addressing, and the verses state both what the system claimed and what it cost.
