## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:191

> ˹They are˺ those who remember Allah while standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth ˹and pray˺, “Our Lord! You have not created ˹all of˺ this without purpose. Glory be to You! Protect us from the torment of the Fire.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Three Postures of Remembrance**

The verse describes the people of understanding as those who remember God while standing, while sitting, and while lying on their sides, and the three positions cover the ordinary conditions of a human life. The sūrah about Women uses the same three modes in its instruction about remembrance after the prayer.

Al-Rāzī observed that no human being is ever outside one of those three positions, so the instruction covers the whole of a person's waking and sleeping life. The verse's description is therefore of a life rather than of a ritual, and the community that reads it is being shown what the constant awareness of God looks like in practice.

**The Forms the Remembrance Takes**

The commentators explained the remembrance in several ways: as the movement of the heart, as the words of the tongue, and as the positions of the canonical prayer, including those adopted by people unable to stand. Ibn Kathīr included the positions of the prayer among the meanings, and others extended the phrase to every form that remembrance can take, the recitation of the Book among them.

The verse has consequently been treated in the tradition as one of the foundations of the practice of remembering God at all times, and the Sufi tradition in particular drew on it. The wording supports the practice: the three postures leave no part of a person's day outside the activity the verse describes, and the sequence traces a person from activity through rest.

**Reflection on the Creation**

The second act attributed to the people of understanding is reflection on the heavens and the earth as made things, and the Book invites this activity in many passages. It directs attention to the rain, the growth of plants, the differences among creatures, and the order of the sky.

The Book's argument in such passages is that the created world displays the qualities of the one who made it. The people described in this verse move from the observation of the world to a conclusion about its maker, and the conclusion they reach is stated in the quotation that follows.

**The Creation Is Not in Vain**

The words the verse places in the mouths of these people are a declaration that God has not created the world in vain. The Book states the same in other passages: one declares that heaven and earth and all that lies between them were not made in play, and another states that they were not created without purpose, contrary to the thought of those who deny.

A third passage asks whether people imagined that they had been created without purpose and would never return to God. The sentence in this verse belongs to that group, and the community's reflection on the creation leads it to the conclusion that the arrangement of the world is neither accidental nor pointless.

**The Distinction Between Creation and the World's Allure**

The commentators drew a careful line between the created order, which the Book presents as a sign, and the present world, which other passages describe as play and diversion. The two are not the same thing.

The distinction matters for the verse's instruction. What the people of understanding reflect upon is the act of creation and the qualities it displays, and what the Book warns against is the absorption in worldly desire that turns the same world into an end in itself. The same objects can be a sign or a distraction, and the difference lies in what a person does with them.

**Meditation and Its Limits**

Al-Rāzī drew from the verse the principle that a person may meditate on God through His creation and through the signs of His qualities within it; the essence itself, however, is not a subject for such meditation, since no limited consciousness can grasp it.

The Book supports the distinction in a passage that promises to show people the signs on the horizons and within themselves until it is made plain to them that the message is true. The instruction is to look at what God has made rather than to attempt what the verse's own limitation describes.

**The Tradition of Reflection**

The tradition preserved statements about reflection that the commentators attached to this verse. One report states that an hour of reflection is better than a night spent in vigil, and the practice appears in the reports about the companions: Abū Dharr's wife said that he passed his days thinking in a corner of his home, and the account from Abū Bakr's household says the same of his nights.

Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is quoted as saying that speech which carries no wisdom is empty, that silence which carries no reflection is heedlessness, and that thought which carries no contemplation is amusement. The community's teachers have used these sayings to explain what the verse asks of its readers.

**The Petition That Ends the Verse**

The verse closes with a request to be shielded from the Fire's punishment, and the prayer places the reflection and the request side by side. The Book uses the same petition in other passages, including the prayer of the believers in the earlier part of this sūrah and the request in the sūrah of the Forgiver.

The order of the sentence is instructive: the people of understanding observe the world, conclude that it has a purpose, and then ask to be shielded from the destination of those who fail to draw that conclusion. The request is short, and its brevity matches the confidence of those who make it. The next verse continues their prayer and states what they understand about the fate they are asking to avoid.

**How the Passage Defines the People of Understanding**

The phrase describing those for whom the signs exist is one the Book uses elsewhere to set the thoughtful beside the heedless. One passage asks whether the one who knows that the revelation is the truth can be like the one who is blind, and answers that only people of reason will take the reminder (13:19). The same term appears in the sūrah of the Companies, where the one who devotes his hours to worship, standing and prostrating, is described as hoping for the mercy of his Lord before the question is put: are those who know equal to those who do not (39:9)?

The passage in this sūrah answers that question by showing what the people of understanding do. They remember God in every posture, reflect on the world He made, and turn their reflection into a petition that includes protection from the fire. Elsewhere the Book records the same kind of prayer in a shorter form, asking for good in this world and the next and deliverance from the punishment (2:201). The verse before the reader supplies the conduct that gives the term its content, and the verses that follow complete the portrait.
