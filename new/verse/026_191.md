## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:191

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Power and Mercy Belong Together**

The account closes with two names that must be held together. Allah is the Almighty, so the people cannot escape the consequence of defiance or protect a corrupt order by force. He is the Most Merciful, so the destruction is not an arbitrary display of power. Warning, time, and the opportunity to change belong to mercy before judgment arrives.

Separating the names produces two distortions. If power is remembered without mercy, fear becomes despair or harshness. If mercy is remembered without power, delay is mistaken for approval and forgiveness is treated as permission to continue harming people. The verse refuses both errors.

The people may have thought their land, wealth, and social influence could define the limits of what was possible. The name Almighty breaks that illusion. Allah is not one authority among others, and no local custom can make a command impossible to enforce.

The name Lord also makes the judgment personal without making it arbitrary. The One who governs the world knows what His servants do and judges them with complete authority. Shuʿayb's people were not destroyed because the land had become unimportant. They were judged while standing within the dominion of the Lord who had given them life and provision.

**Mercy Appeared Before the Punishment**

Mercy in this account is not only the possibility of pardon after the event. It appears in Shuʿayb's patient warning, the clarity of his commands, and the time given to the people before the cloud. They were told what harmed others and were invited to return to Allah without needing to invent a new moral system.

Quran 26:9 repeats these two divine names at the close of the earlier signs in the sūrah. The refrain teaches that every account of judgment carries a prior history of care. The Lord is powerful enough to end corruption and merciful enough to send guidance before doing so.

**Mercy Does Not Cancel the Rights of Victims**

A false idea of mercy might ask victims to accept continued fraud so that wrongdoers are not confronted. This verse gives no support to that confusion. The Most Merciful protects the moral order in which people have rights and the oppressed are not required to call theft harmless.

Real mercy seeks repentance from the wrongdoer and safety for those harmed. It speaks plainly before the consequence becomes final. Shuʿayb's message is compassionate precisely because it does not flatter the community's corruption. It offers a path back that includes truth, repair, and submission to Allah.

**The Refrain Directs the Living**

The final names are not an invitation to study punishment from a safe distance. They direct the living to fear the Almighty without losing hope in the Most Merciful. A person who has cheated can return what he took. A community that normalized corruption can change its standards. Delay remains an opportunity only while it is used for correction.

The balance of the verse is a discipline for action. Trust God's power enough to stop excusing injustice, and trust His mercy enough to repent without despair. Shuʿayb's account ends by making both truths clear: no corruption outruns the Almighty, and no sincere return is outside the mercy of the Lord.

The two names also correct the way a believer evaluates worldly success. A prosperous corrupt society may appear powerful, but prosperity cannot resist the Almighty when its strength is used against His limits. A poor or isolated believer may appear insignificant, but the Most Merciful can preserve faith and reward a truth that receives no public applause. Surface conditions are not the final measure of safety.

Mercy likewise does not mean that every desired outcome will be granted immediately. The people were given time, but time became evidence against them when they used it to deepen refusal. A believer should welcome delay as space for repair, not treat it as a guarantee that the present arrangement is approved. The Almighty can close a path, and the Most Merciful can open repentance before it is closed.

The names offer a sound posture toward people who are caught in wrongdoing. Power requires the believer to oppose harm and protect rights. Mercy requires him to leave room for repentance and to avoid turning correction into humiliation. Shuʿayb did not flatter the Forest people, but he warned them before the punishment. That combination is stronger than either softness that abandons victims or anger that abandons hope.

The conclusion of the account therefore returns responsibility to the living. Fear the Almighty enough to change the measure, restore property, and reject the corruption that a crowd calls normal. Hope in the Most Merciful enough to begin that change without waiting for perfect conditions. Judgment belongs to Allah, while faithful conduct belongs to the servant.

The final pairing also keeps the account from becoming an argument for despair. The Almighty's judgment is certain, but the Most Merciful sent the warning before judgment. A person who has participated in a corrupt system can still step away, repair what he can, and ask for forgiveness. Mercy does not erase the past by pretending it never happened; it opens the door to truthful return and responsible repair.

For the community, these names set the tone of reform. Do not tolerate harm because you fear powerful people, and do not crush the repentant because you enjoy moral superiority. Resist corruption with the certainty that Allah is Almighty, and invite change with the hope that He is Most Merciful. Shuʿayb's account leaves both duties joined. A sound community fears the force of divine judgment and hopes in the breadth of divine mercy, so it neither excuses the oppressor nor abandons the person who can still return.
