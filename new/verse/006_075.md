## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:75

> We also showed Abraham the wonders of the heavens and the earth, so he would be sure in faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Showing Attributed to God**

The verse states that God showed Abraham the wonders of the heavens and the earth, and the attribution is the point of the sentence. The case for the oneness of God can be made from observation, and the sūrah has been making it that way, but this verse says that Abraham's insight was given rather than merely achieved. The Book's invitation to reflect on the created order is addressed to everyone, and it states that signs are of no benefit to people who refuse to believe (10:101), so the difference between Abraham and his people was not access to the world but the use made of it.

The Book places the same kind of statement at the end of the sūrah of Fussilat, where God says that He will show people His signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that the message is the truth (41:53). The sūrah of the Scatterers states the same in two verses that begin with the signs on the earth for those with sure faith and end with the signs within the readers themselves (51:20–21). What Abraham received in this verse is what the Book offers to every reader of it.

**What the Wonders Were**

Commentators differed on what exactly Abraham was shown. One reading takes the phrase at face value: the wonders of the heavens and the earth are the phenomena everyone can see, the sun, the moon, the stars, the turning of the day and night, and the ordering of the world. On that reading the verse describes Abraham applying his reason to what was in front of him, and the Book credits the sound judgement he had been given early for that use of it (21:51).

The other reading, which appears in most of the commentaries, takes the showing to be an unveiling in which the heavens were opened so that Abraham saw their levels up to the Throne and the earth was laid bare so that he saw its depths. Al-Rāzī held that the manifestation of God's power in natural phenomena is always objectively available but is veiled from most people by error and by preoccupation with worldly affairs, and that Abraham received it with the inner eye of his understanding.

**The Sufi Reading of the Sequence**

The Sufi commentators read the star, the moon, and the sun of the following verses as stages in the growth of spiritual knowledge, and their interpretation of this verse sets the context for it. Al-Qushayrī took the star to represent the intellect that first witnesses the truth through demonstration, the moon to represent knowledge that grows clearer through reflection, and the sun to represent the light of direct knowledge of God. Abraham's movement from one to the next was, on this reading, the progress of a seeker who discovers the limits of each station and is led beyond it.

That reading has the merit of explaining the pattern of the narrative that follows, in which each celestial body is abandoned at the moment of its setting. The Book records the conclusion Abraham drew on each occasion, and the Sufi interpretation treats each setting as the collapse of a partial understanding rather than as an ordinary astronomical event.

**Certainty as the Purpose**

The verse gives the purpose of the showing: so that Abraham would be sure in faith. The Book's word for that certainty describes knowledge that has passed beyond doubt, and it is used for the assurance the believers seek about the meeting with their Lord and about the reality of the accounting. The same word appears in the sūrah of the Cow, where Abraham asks to be shown how God gives life to the dead and explains that he already believes but wants his heart to be reassured (2:260).

The comparison between the two passages is instructive. Abraham asked for the reassurance, and in this verse the showing is given without a request. The Book presents certainty as a state that can be sought, asked for, and granted, and it presents the created world as the material through which it is reached.

**The Argument and the Certainty**

The sūrah closes the Abraham section by naming the discourse with his people as the argument God gave him against them, and by stating that God elevates in rank whoever He pleases (6:83). Read with this verse, the two statements describe the same gift from two angles. The certainty given to Abraham in this verse is what produced the argument in the following verses, and the argument is credited to God rather than to Abraham's ingenuity.

That arrangement is the sūrah's answer to a question the reader may not have asked but which the commentators raised: if Abraham reasoned his way to the truth, where does revelation fit? The sūrah's answer is that the showing preceded the reasoning and the argument was supplied. The Book does not treat natural reflection and divine guidance as competing explanations; it treats the first as the field in which the second operates.

**What the Verse Teaches the Audience**

For the Makkan audience, the verse offered Abraham as the man their own tradition claimed, whose reasoning had led him out of the very practices they were defending. For a reader in general, it offers a description of how conviction is formed: an honest look at the world, a refusal to treat the familiar as exempt from examination, and the understanding that the ability to see clearly is itself a gift.

The sūrah now moves into the account of that reasoning, and it records Abraham's words as he watched the objects the audience revered appear and disappear across the sky. The demonstration in the next verses is built entirely from things the Makkans themselves could observe, and its conclusion is the declaration that Abraham made when the last of them set.
