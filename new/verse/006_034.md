## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:34

> Indeed, messengers before you were rejected but patiently endured rejection and persecution until Our help came to them. And Allah’s promise ˹to help˺ is never broken. And you have already received some of the narratives of these messengers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rejection as the Standing Condition of the Work**

The verse hands the Prophet a fact about his own office: the messengers before him were rejected. The Book states it flatly in several places, naming the messengers generally rather than complaining about any single people. If you are rejected by them, so too were messengers before you who came with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures (3:184); if you are rejected by them, so too were messengers before you, and to Allah all matters will be returned (35:4).

Those earlier messengers are named by community in other passages, where the people of Noah, ʿĀd, and Pharaoh of the mighty structures are listed among those who denied the truth (38:12), and where every community is described as having plotted against its prophet to seize him and argued in falsehood to discredit the truth (40:5). The pattern is not a series of unfortunate incidents. It is the ordinary reception of a message by the society it challenges.

**Patience Until the Help Arrived**

The second clause defines the shape of that patience with a time limit: they endured until Our help came to them. The Book insists on that order in every account. Our help came to the messengers at the moment when they and their followers had reached the end of their resources (12:110); We certainly help Our messengers and the believers both in this worldly life and on the Day the witnesses will stand forth (40:51); and the believers are told that they will not enter Paradise without being tested as those before them were, until they ask when Allah's help will come, and are told that it is always near (2:214).

The wording keeps the Prophet from measuring his position by the middle of the story. A messenger standing in the middle of his mission can see only the rejection, and the verse tells him where the account is going, in the same way the Book tells the whole community that its help is guaranteed in the end.

**The Promise That Cannot Be Broken**

Then the verse states the guarantee without any conditionality: Allah's promise to help is never broken. The Book records the promise itself in the sūrah of the Ranged Ranks, where God's word has gone forth to His servants, the messengers, that they would surely be helped and that His forces would prevail (37:171–173), and again in the sūrah of the Pleading Woman, where God has decreed that He and His messengers will certainly prevail (58:21).

The same guarantee is given about God's word in general, where the Word of the Lord is perfected in truth and justice and none can change His words (6:115), and where the Book tells its readers that there is no change in the promise of Allah and that this is the ultimate triumph (10:64). What the verse offers the Prophet is therefore not encouragement but information. The outcome was announced before the dispute began.

**What the Help Looked Like**

Help in these accounts was not always a military victory or an immediate reversal. For the messengers it came as rescue: We then saved whoever We willed, and Our punishment is never averted from the wicked people (12:110). For the Prophet's own community the deliverance took the form of the eventual establishment of the religion, after years in which the believers were fewer than their opponents and could not protect themselves. The Book also counts the endurance itself as part of what was being perfected, since it tells the believers that they will be tested as those before them were tested, and that the help comes after the shaking rather than instead of it (2:214).

That range matters for anyone reading the verse in a period of weakness. The promise does not specify the date or the method, and the Book deliberately leaves both open, while binding itself on the fact. What the Prophet was entitled to conclude from the histories was that no messenger was abandoned, not that every messenger was vindicated on the timetable he would have chosen.

**Why He Had Already Received the Narratives**

The verse closes by pointing to what the Prophet already possessed: some of the narratives of these messengers. In the Makkan period a substantial body of those accounts had been revealed, and the Book states their function plainly when it says that the stories of the messengers are related to reassure the heart, and that in these stories there is the truth, a warning for the disbelievers, and a reminder for the believers (11:120; 12:111).

So the consolation in this verse is not an abstraction. It is a library the Prophet could open in his own recitation, listing men who were mocked, imprisoned, hunted, and dismissed, and reporting in each case what happened next. The Book treats those reports as equipment for the mission rather than as history for its own sake.

**The Use of a Pattern Rather Than a Promise of Ease**

Nothing in the verse tells the Prophet that his difficulties are about to end. What it tells him is that his difficulties are a repetition, and that the repetition has a known ending. That is a different kind of comfort: it does not remove the hardship, and it removes the uncertainty about what the hardship means.

For the Makkan audience the sentence quietly makes the same point from the other side. A city that had decided the Prophet's struggle was evidence against him was being told to look at how the previous rejections turned out. The verse is addressed to the messenger, and it is heard over his shoulder by the men who were making his life difficult.
