## Sūrah an-Naml 27:93

> And say, “All praise is for Allah! He will show you His signs, and you will recognize them. And your Lord is never unaware of what you do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Instruction Is Praise**

After ninety-two verses of argument, narrative, warning and judgment, the chapter's final command to its Prophet is to say *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh*. Praise, not a summary and not an appeal.

The placement is deliberate and it completes a frame. Praise has appeared at the pivot of this chapter's greatest scenes: Dāwūd and Sulaymān said it when given knowledge, and Sulaymān said it again in a different form when the throne arrived. Both times it followed a gift. Here it follows something else — a long refusal by an audience that has not accepted the message. The chapter ends by putting the same words in the mouth of a man who has been given no visible success, which says something about what praise is anchored to.

**"He Will Show You His Signs"**

The promise is future and it is addressed to the audience rather than to the Prophet: *sayurīkum āyātih*, He will show you His signs. The chapter has been pointing at signs from its first verse, and now it says more are coming.

Al-Ṭabarī reads this as signs both within the soul and in the created order, and the reading connects to well-known verses elsewhere — the promise that God will show them His signs on the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear that it is the truth, and the statement that there are signs on the earth for those with certainty, and within your own selves. Taken this way the closing line is an invitation with no deadline. The evidence is not finished; the audience has not seen the last of it.

**The Other Reading: Signs Too Late**

Al-Rāzī records a harder interpretation. The sentence may be a warning rather than an invitation: they will indeed see God's signs, but only after it is too late, when the signs are the ones that accompany the end of the world.

Both readings fit the grammar, and the chapter supports each. It has just described a trumpet, mountains moving like clouds, and a creature brought out of the earth to announce that people had no certainty — signs of exactly the kind al-Rāzī means. But it has also, in the preceding verse, spoken of whoever chooses to be guided, which assumes the option is open. The ambiguity may be the point. A sentence that reads as promise to one listener and as threat to another is well suited to close a chapter whose argument has been that the same evidence lands differently depending on who receives it.

**"And You Will Recognize Them"**

The second clause is the one that decides the tone, and it is not softened on either reading: *fa-taʿrifūnahā*, and you will know them. Whatever the signs turn out to be, they will not be ambiguous when they come.

This is the chapter's answer to the charge it levelled at the judgment. Verse 84 asked the deniers whether they rejected the signs without encompassing them in knowledge. This verse promises knowledge — recognition, the thing they lacked. What it does not promise is that the recognition will arrive in time to be useful, and the chapter has already described the case where it does not: a creature announcing the failure of certainty, and a contingent standing silent before a question they cannot answer.

**Not Unaware**

The final clause is *wa mā rabbuka bi-ghāfilin ʿammā taʿmalūn* — your Lord is not heedless of what you do. The chapter has said versions of this four times, and it ends on it.

Verse 74 said God knows what breasts conceal and what they disclose. Verse 75 said nothing hidden anywhere is outside a clear record. Verse 88 closed with the statement that He is aware of what you do. Now the last sentence of the sūrah says it once more, and in the negative form, which is the strongest of the four. A positive claim about knowledge can be heard as general; denying heedlessness answers the specific hope that something has gone unnoticed. The chapter's opponents included nine men who swore a night oath believing themselves unobserved, and its close forecloses on the assumption they were operating under.

**A Chapter That Ends Without Resolution**

Nothing is concluded here. No one converts, no punishment falls, the Meccans are not answered, and the judgment stays in the future. The chapter simply stops, with a man told to praise God and an audience told they will see more.

That is unusual and it is consistent with everything the sūrah has done. It refused to name the Hour, refused a date for the reckoning, placed the adjudication of religious disputes in the Hereafter, and told the Prophet his responsibility ended at the recitation. A chapter organised around deferral could hardly finish with a resolution. What it offers instead is the posture to hold while waiting, and the posture is the one the verse names first.

**The Ant and the Last Verse**

It is worth returning, at the end, to the incident the chapter is named for. An ant saw an army approaching and warned her colony to get inside. Sulaymān heard her, smiled, and asked to be marshalled into gratitude for the favour.

Almost nothing else in an-Naml is as small as that scene, and the chapter takes its title from it rather than from the queen, the throne, or the judgment. What the ant did was notice something coming and speak about it in time. What Sulaymān did was hear a warning not addressed to him and respond with thanks. Between them they perform the two acts this final verse asks for — recognising signs and saying *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh* — and the chapter that closes with both commands was named, from the beginning, after the creature who managed them first.
