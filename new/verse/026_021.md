## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:21

> So I fled from you when I feared you. Then my Lord granted me wisdom and made me one of the messengers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses Answers the Threat With a Plain History**

Moses does not deny that he fled. He names fear of Pharaoh as the reason. The admission is important because Pharaoh has tried to turn the past into a weapon. Moses refuses both concealment and exaggeration. He left the reach of the court because he feared that the ruler would kill him, and that flight became the setting in which God prepared him for a different return.

The flight was not a celebration of fear or a permanent rejection of responsibility. It was a human response to an immediate threat. The fuller account in 28:21–22 describes Moses leaving Egypt in fear and then turning toward Midian while asking God for right direction. That detail keeps the event within a moral journey. Moses leaves danger, but he does not leave God. His movement away from Pharaoh becomes movement toward dependence.

**Flight Can Be Protection Before It Becomes Preparation**

A person may need to leave a place where a powerful wrongdoer can harm him. Such departure is not automatically cowardice or betrayal. Moses' flight preserves his life at a time when he is not yet equipped to confront the court. The same God who later commands him to return first allows a period outside Egypt. Protection and mission are not opposites; protection can be part of how a mission is made possible.

This point corrects a harsh idea of courage. Courage does not require a person to remain in every danger simply to prove sincerity. Wisdom may require withdrawal, patience, and a change of setting. The question is what the departure serves. Moses does not flee to build a private life free of obligation. He flees, receives formation, and is eventually sent back with a clearer commission.

**Ḥukm Names a Gift Beyond Mere Information**

Moses says that his Lord granted him ḥukm. The word can mean judgment, sound decision, authority to judge, wisdom, or understanding. Commentators differed because the term has a wide field of meaning. Some connected it with prophethood or authority, while others understood it as intelligence and the ability to judge rightly. Since the verse separately says that Moses was placed among the messengers, the narrower sense of practical wisdom and sound judgment is stronger here.

That gift answers the weakness exposed by the earlier deed. Moses had acted without proper guidance; now God gives him a capacity to distinguish, weigh, and decide. Wisdom is not merely an increase in facts. It is the ability to put facts, motives, timing, and consequences in their right relation. A messenger needs revelation, but he also needs judgment in delivering it to frightened people and confronting a ruler who turns every word into a weapon.

**The Years Away Are Not Empty Years**

The verse compresses the period between flight and return into one divine act: my Lord granted me wisdom. Exile can look like lost time when it is measured only by public achievement. Moses is not standing before Pharaoh during these years, but he is not outside God's care. The hidden period changes the man who will later stand in the court.

Waiting can expose habits that public success conceals. Away from the palace, Moses must live without the authority of his childhood environment and without the certainty that he will ever return. Such a life can teach humility, patience, and dependence. The verse does not romanticize exile or say that every hardship automatically produces wisdom. It asserts that God used this interval to give Moses what the mission required.

**Messengerhood Is a New Placement, Not Self-Appointment**

The final clause distinguishes wisdom from messengership. Moses was granted an inward gift and then placed among the messengers by God. He did not infer that personal intelligence entitled him to speak for the Lord. Authority came through divine selection. This protects the message from becoming a philosophy invented by a gifted man with a painful past.

The wording also gives Moses a new identity without erasing the old facts. He remains the man who fled, the man who acted without guidance, and the man who lived away from Egypt. Yet God now places him in a company whose task is to warn and deliver. Repentance and formation do not create a false past; they create a faithful present. Pharaoh wants to keep Moses in the category of fugitive and ingrate. God names him among the messengers.

**The Return Carries Both Memory and Guidance**

Moses' answer is strong because it neither boasts of transformation nor uses transformation to deny responsibility. He remembers the fear that drove him away, the wisdom God gave him, and the commission that now brings him back. Each part matters. Memory prevents pride, wisdom prevents reckless action, and messengership prevents the past from becoming a permanent sentence.

Anyone who has left a harmful situation can recognize the shape of this answer. Departure may protect life; a period of learning may repair judgment; a later return may serve a purpose larger than personal vindication. The return is not powered by confidence in Moses' own history. It is powered by the Lord who turned a flight into preparation and a prepared servant into a messenger.

**Wisdom Gives the Return Its Proper Measure**

Moses does not return to settle a personal score with Pharaoh. The gift of ḥukm teaches him to distinguish vindication from obedience. A person shaped only by anger might return to punish the ruler who frightened him. A person shaped by wisdom returns because God has commanded a message that concerns more than his own injury. The same past that could feed resentment becomes material for a carefully judged mission.

This is why the verse places wisdom between flight and messengership. Moses' courage is not the raw courage of a man who has forgotten danger. It is the settled courage of one who has learned what danger means and where authority lies. He remembers Pharaoh's threat without allowing Pharaoh to define the next act. God has changed the meaning of the return by giving the servant judgment before giving him the public confrontation.
