## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:51

> But those who strive to discredit Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Hellfire.”
**Expanded Commentary**

**Not Disbelief but Campaigning**

*Wa-alladhīna saʿaw fī āyātinā muʿājizīna* — and those who strove against Our signs, seeking to frustrate.

The verb *saʿā* means to strive, to exert oneself, to work at something with effort. It is the same verb used for a person running to the prayer or working for a livelihood. What is described here is not a state of unbelief but an expenditure of energy.

That is the verse's target, and it is narrower and more serious than simple denial. A person can disbelieve passively, and the Qur'an treats that as one thing. This verse describes people who work at it: who organize, argue, mock, and spend their days trying to make the signs ineffective.

The verb's other use in the Qur'an shows what is being graded. The same imperative is addressed to the believers about the Friday prayer: *"O believers! When the call to prayer is made on Friday, then proceed ˹diligently˺ to the remembrance of Allah and leave off ˹your˺ business"* (62:9). Striving is not the offence; it is the raw material of every human life. What distinguishes the two groups is not that one expends itself and the other does not, but where the expenditure is pointed. Both verses use the same verb for the same human capacity, and the sūrah is interested in the direction of the effort rather than in its quantity.

**What "Muʿājizīn" Means**

The participle carries two readings, and the commentators recorded both.

On the first, it means *trying to escape* — striving to outrun the judgment and put themselves beyond God's reach. On the second, it means *trying to frustrate* — working to render the signs powerless, to defeat them, to nullify what they were sent to do. The root contains the idea of making someone unable to act.

Both readings fit the people the sūrah has been describing since verse 3: those who dispute about God without knowledge, who turn their shoulders to lead others astray, who call the revelation ancient fables, and who demand the punishment as a way of showing that it will not come. What all of it has in common is effort directed at making the message not work.

The word appears elsewhere in exactly this construction, and the parallel confirms the second reading: *"As for those who strive to discredit Our revelations, it is they who will suffer the ˹worst˺ torment of agonizing pain"* (34:5). There the striving is met with the one thing it was trying to escape, and the punishment is described as a place from which there is no exit — the frustration the strivers wanted to inflict on the signs is the frustration they arrive at.

**What the Campaign Looked Like**

The Qur'an records the specific tactics, and they are worth listing because they are not what modern readers expect. The opponents did not typically argue that God does not exist. They disputed *about the signs* (40:35), they plotted against them (10:21), and they deviated with regard to them (41:40).

The most common tactic was reclassification: calling the revelation sorcery, poetry, or tales of the ancients. Each of those moves takes the message out of the category where it demands a response and puts it in a category where it can be dismissed. That is a genuinely clever strategy, and the Qur'an treats it as the serious offence it is.

Two further tactics are recorded from Makkah, and both are attempts to change the terms of the exchange rather than to answer it. One was to ask for the message itself to be revised: *"Bring us a different Quran or make some changes in it"* (10:15) — a request framed as a negotiation, to which the Prophet was told to answer that he does not alter what is revealed to him. Another was to claim the ability to match it and then decline to bother: *"We have already heard ˹the recitation˺. If we wanted, we could have easily produced something similar. This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!"* (8:31). The claim of ability is unfalsifiable by design, and the demand for a spring to gush from the earth (17:90–93) belongs to the same family: conditions set so that the sign, if given, would be reclassified as well.

**"The Companions of the Fire"**

*Ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-jaḥīmi* — those are the companions of the Fire.

The word *aṣḥāb* means companions, associates, those who belong together. It is the same word used for the companions of the Garden, and the symmetry is deliberate: each group ends up with what it kept company with.

The noun also implies permanence in a way that "inhabitants" does not. A person can pass through a place; a companion belongs to it. The verse is not describing people who visit the Fire but people whose association with it is the settled fact about them — which is what the campaign of the first half of the verse has been building, act by act.

The Qur'an uses the same noun for both destinations in a single comparison, and the inequality it draws is between the two companies rather than between two locations: *"The residents of the Fire cannot be equal to the residents of Paradise. ˹Only˺ the residents of Paradise will be successful"* (59:20). Elsewhere the companionship is stated with the adverb that settles the question: *"But those who disbelieve and deny Our signs will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever"* (2:39). Belonging is permanent on both sides, and the sūrah's point is that the permanence is what the striving was building all along.

**Why Effort Is the Aggravating Factor**

The sūrah has already graded offences by whether they stay private, and it does so again here. Verse 9 described people who disputed, and then described people who disputed *in order to lead others astray* — the purpose clause was what doubled the sentence.

Verse 51 works the same way. Disbelief is one thing; striving against the signs is another, and the difference is the energy. A person who simply does not believe has declined something. A person who works to make the signs ineffective has joined a campaign against them, and his sentence is described in terms of what he has joined.

The principle behind the grading is stated in a single line of Sūrah al-Najm, using the same root as this verse: *"and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards"* (53:39). What a person gets is what he worked for, and the work is counted as his own. That is why the verse can describe the punishment as the appropriate outcome rather than an arbitrary one: the strivers spent their effort making the signs ineffective, and what they receive is the one condition in which the signs are no longer available to be acted on.

**"Our Signs" — What Is Being Attacked**

*Āyātunā*, Our signs. The word *āyah* covers the verses of the revelation and the phenomena of the natural world, and the Qur'an uses both senses deliberately in the same word.

That breadth matters for reading the offence. The campaign described here is not only against a text. It is against the evidence as well — against reading the created world as evidence, which is precisely what verses 5, 18 and 46 of this sūrah demanded. Striving against the signs is the attempt to make the world unreadable.

It is worth being concrete about what that looks like. The sūrah's own signs are the embryo in the womb, the field that greens under rain, the ships held on the sea, the submission of everything in creation. Each of these is an ordinary occurrence with an interpretation attached. The campaign of this verse is the effort to detach the occurrence from the interpretation — to leave the world in place and empty it of meaning.

That is a more ambitious project than atheism, and the Qur'an treats it as the more serious offence. A person who denies God outright has taken a position that can be argued with. A person who works to make the world illegible has removed the ground on which any argument could stand.

**The Verse's Pairing With Verse 50**

The two verses are one sentence in argument if not in grammar: believers get forgiveness and a noble provision, and the strivers against the signs get the Fire.

The sūrah has used this pairing repeatedly — verses 19 and 23, verse 14 against verses 11 to 13, and now here — and each time the two halves are described in matching terms. The believers receive; the strivers belong. Neither is described as a surprise to the person it happens to.

What is new in this pairing is the emphasis on effort. Verse 50's people believed and did sound deeds, which is also an expenditure. Verse 51's people strove, and the striving is what the verse names them by. Two groups, both working, arriving at two destinations — and the difference is not the energy but its direction.
