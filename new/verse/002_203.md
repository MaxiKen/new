## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:203

> And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺, so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺. And be mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Days Numbered**

The verse legislates the hajj's final station: remember God in the days numbered — the eleventh through the thirteenth of Dhū'l-Ḥijjah, which the pilgrims spend at Minā, the valley east of Mecca, and which the tradition calls the days of tashrīq, a word whose derivation is uncertain and whose sense the rites preserve: the three days after the Day of Sacrifice, not counting it. The station's central act is the stoning — the casting of pebbles at the columns (jimār) that represent Satan's three ambushes of Abraham on the road of the sacrifice — and the verse stations remembrance over the whole of it. The numbered days complete the pilgrimage's arithmetic: months appointed (2:197), a night at the sacred monument (2:198), a surge with the people (2:199), rites completed (2:200), and now days counted — three, named, and bounded, like the fast's numbered days before them. The numbering is the legislation's characteristic mercy: the rite has a defined end, the pilgrim knows his remaining obligation to the hour, and the remembrance that fills the days has a frame that keeps it from dissolving into the journey's fatigue.

**Remember God in Them**

The remembrance commanded in these days has a precise form in the tradition's practice: the takbīr — Allāhu akbar, God is greatest — recited while casting the pebbles at the columns and recited at the times of the prayers throughout the days. And the recitation is not the pilgrims' private liturgy: the takbīr of these days is performed by all Muslims, in every city, from the Day of Sacrifice until the last of the tashrīq days, so that the whole ummah's tongue moves with the valley's. The litanies are the same ones the fasting-legislation produced — the verse of Ramadan's completion commanded magnifying God for having guided (2:185), and the formula the tradition recites there is the formula recited here: Allāhu akbar, Allāhu akbar, lā ilāha illa'Llāh, wa'Llāhu akbar wa li'Llāhi'l-ḥamd — God is greatest, God is greatest, there is no god but God, and God is greatest, and to God belongs the praise — with other litanies added in some practice. The two festivals thus share one doxology: Ramadan ends in takbīr for the guidance, and the hajj's last days run on takbīr at the columns — the community praising at every summit its rites reach, the remembrance itself the rite's stated substance.

**Who Hastens and Who Tarries**

The verse's famous flexibility governs the days' length: whoever hastens on after two days — departing Minā on the twelfth — bears no sin, and whoever delays — staying through the thirteenth — bears no sin. The ruling created the hajj's one open option at its close: the pilgrim may complete the stoning in two of the three days and proceed to the farewell circumambulation at the Kaʿbah, by which the hajj is complete, or may remain for the third day's casting. Both courses are declared sinless, and the declaration's double negative is emphatic — no sin on him, no sin on him — legislating freedom where religious scruple would otherwise manufacture anxiety. The option answers real conditions: the caravans' departure schedules, the travelers' distances, the weak and the hurried; a rite drawing the whole inhabited world to one valley cannot require of every pilgrim the valley's maximum. And the option preserves the maximum's honor: the delay is not corrected or discouraged — it stands equally sinless, which in the tradition's reading means equally meritorious for the one whose circumstances allow it. The verse thus closes the hajj as it closed the fast: a fixed obligation with legislated margins, the strictness at the center and the mercy at the edges.

**For the One Who Fears**

The flexibility carries a condition, and the condition is the verse's hinge: the no-sin ruling is *for the reverent* — for the one who keeps his taqwā. The exegetes read the qualifier in complementary directions. It attaches to both options: the hastener is sinless only if his hastening is not a flight from the rite's discipline but a traveler's necessity kept inside piety, and the delayer is sinless only if his staying is not self-righteous display. It also attaches, in the stricter reading, to the departing specifically: the one who hastens must have kept the fear of God in his whole pilgrimage — the rafath, fusūq, and jidāl avoided (2:197), the rites completed for God (2:196) — for the exemption to cover his early exit. The condition thereby prevents the flexibility from becoming laxity: the option is not a discount on piety but a discount *for* the pious, the margin granted to the heart the rite has already formed. And the verse reinforces the point by repeating its command — be mindful of God — immediately after the options, so that both departures, the two-day and the three-day, are dispatched under the same taqwā, the only luggage whose inspection matters at the valley's exit.

**To Him You Will Be Gathered**

The verse ends by projecting the gathering at Minā into the gathering to God: know that to Him you will be gathered. The clause is the days' eschatological frame, and its fit to the setting is exact. The pilgrim at Minā stands inside the largest rehearsal the faith performs of the Day of Gathering — the nations assembled in one valley, ranked in tents like the ranks of the resurrection, performing the stoning of the devil who will be judged, awaiting the departure that resembles the final departure. The knowledge commanded is the rehearsal's lesson: this gathering disperses, that gathering does not; these days are numbered, that Day is not; this valley's exit is by the farewell circuit, that assembly's exit is by the portion earned. The clause also seals the passage's accounting-thread — the sacred month's ledger (2:194), the portion from what was earned and the swift reckoning (2:202) — with the destination of all accounts: the gathering to Him. The pilgrim who hastens after two days and the pilgrim who tarries to the third walk out of the valley under the same knowledge, and the verse leaves them both with it: the option was about days; the gathering is about the One who numbered them.

**The Hajj's Closing Verse**

Within the legislation's architecture this verse is the coda. After it the passage turns — abruptly, in the sūrah's manner — from the pilgrim's duties to the hypocrite's portrait (2:204–207), as though the completed hajj demanded the contrast: the community that has just been legislated through remembrance, flexibility, and taqwā is shown the man whose speech impresses and whose hand corrupts. The turn confirms the coda's function: the days numbered have produced a people defined by the takbīr on their tongues, the option in their law, the mindfulness in their hearts, and the gathering in their knowledge — and the verses that follow measure every claimant to that people against the portrait. The hajj-legislation's own arc closes in the verse's three movements: the remembrance commanded (the tongue's rite), the freedom granted (the law's mercy), the gathering announced (the heart's horizon). A pilgrim who has kept all three has carried the valley home: the takbīr that outlasts the columns, the ease that outlasts the days, and the knowledge that outlasts the dispersal — to Him you will be gathered, and the numbered days were the counting-down to it.
