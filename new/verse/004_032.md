## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:32

> And do not crave what Allah has given some of you over others. Men will be rewarded according to their deeds and women ˹equally˺ according to theirs. Rather, ask Allah for His bounties. Surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Do Not Crave What Was Given to Another**

The prohibition *wa lā tatamannaw mā faḍḍala Allāhu bihi baʿḍakum ʿalā baʿḍ* — and do not crave what Allah has given some of you over others — addresses the envy that the sūrah’s distribution laws could stir. After fixing shares for men and women whether little or much (4:7), detailing sons and daughters, parents and siblings after debts and bequests (4:11–12), and describing trade by mutual consent (4:29), a listener might look at another’s portion — larger inheritance, larger dowry, larger earning — and wish it were his. The Qur’an names that wish as *tamannī* — craving, wishing — not as planning to earn but as longing to possess what another was given. The verse was revealed in a context where some men wished they had the double share and some women wished they had the same share as men, and where people coveted the martial rewards of others. The answer gathers both sexes and says the division of favour — *faḍl* — belongs to God’s apportioning, not to envy. The sūrah elsewhere says *“We have allocated among them their livelihood in this world”* (43:32), and here it asks that allocation to be met with asking, not craving.

**Men According to Their Deeds, Women According to Theirs**

The corrective *lil-rijāli naṣībun mimmā iktasabū wa lil-nisāʾi naṣībun mimmā iktasabna* — men will be rewarded according to their deeds and women equally according to theirs — provides the alternative accounting. *Iktasaba* — to earn, to acquire through striving — places the measure on what each does, not on what each wishes another had not been given. The echo of *naṣīb* — share — from the inheritance verses (4:7) is deliberate. Just as men and women have obligatory shares in estates, they have shares in earnings before God. A man’s deeds are his share, and a woman’s deeds are hers; neither is credited to the other by gender. The addition *equally* in the translation renders the same structure *mimmā iktasabna* for women as *mimmā iktasabū* for men, showing parallel agency. The sūrah elsewhere promises that *“whoever does good, whether male or female, while faithful, We will give a good life”* (16:97) and that God never wrongs even an atom’s weight but multiplies good many times over (4:40). This verse’s pairing makes that promise concrete at the point where comparison is most tempting. The antidote to craving another’s favour is to turn to one’s own field and work it, knowing the harvest is kept without diminishment.

**Rather Ask Allah for His Bounties**

The positive command *wa asʾalū Allāha min faḍlihi* — rather, ask Allah for His bounties — redirects the energy of envy into supplication. *Faḍl* — bounty, surplus, favour — is the same word used for the apportioning among people; to ask Him for His bounty is to ask the Source rather than to resent the recipient. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“If you are grateful, I will give you more”* (14:7) and encourages asking from the same bounty that sustains trade and family. The sūrah has already taught that God is ever Watchful (4:1), sufficient as Reckoner (4:6), a Witness over all things (4:33), and ever Merciful to you (4:29); asking Him is therefore not a vague hope but a request to the One who sees, counts, and shows mercy. It also respects the division of roles the sūrah has described — men as caretakers tasked with support (4:34) and women as protected bearers of entrusted wealth — without freezing either. Asking for bounty can mean asking for lawful earnings, for knowledge, for patience to avoid major sins (4:31), and for the ability to trade by mutual consent (4:29). Each is a form of *faḍl* that can be requested without craving what another was given.

**Perfect Knowledge Over Every Deed and Apportioning**

The closing *inna Allāha kāna bi kulli shayʾin ʿalīmā* — surely Allah has perfect knowledge of all things — was read as the guarantee behind both sides of the verse. He knows what favour was given to each — wealth, strength, lineage, opportunity — and why it was given in that measure. He also knows what each earned — *mā iktasabū* and *mā iktasabna* — with nuance that people miss, including the hidden effort, intention, and constraint behind an outward deed. That twofold knowledge removes the ground for envy. If the apportioning was done with perfect knowledge, it was not arbitrary; if earnings are recorded with perfect knowledge, no deed done faithfully is lost, even if its worldly return seems smaller than another’s. The sūrah has closed similar verses with All-Knowing, All-Wise (4:11, 4:26) and All-Knowing, Most Forbearing (4:12); here the knowledge is said to cover *all things* — *bi kulli shayʾ* — because the temptation to compare covers all things as well. The one who remembers that God knows both gifts and earnings can leave craving aside and turn to asking.

**A Cure for Comparison After Law**

Placed between the promise that avoiding major sins brings an honourable entry (4:31) and the reminder that heirs are appointed and pledges must be honoured (4:33), this verse cures the comparison that law can breed. Law that divides — obligatory shares, dowries, trade — invites the eye to compare. The sūrah answers that invite with a different economy: shares in the next life are by deeds, equally open to men and women, and bounty in this life is to be asked from God, not extracted by envying people. The counsel is practical. A woman who sees a brother’s larger inheritance is told her deeds are counted as hers; a man who sees another’s profitable trade is told to ask God for his own bounty rather than to devour by falsehood (4:29). The sūrah has already said humankind was created weak (4:28) and that God wills to lighten and turn toward you (4:26–28). Here it lightens the specific weight of envy by giving the weak a prayer that is always available. To ask rather than crave is itself a deed that earns a share, and the One who knows all things will not forget it. In that exchange — asking instead of craving, earning instead of envying — the sūrah’s earlier promise holds: the lesser faults are covered, and the major sin of devouring another’s portion, whether in thought or in act, is avoided.
