## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:123

> And guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. No ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Warning in the Same Words**

The sentence repeats the warning that appeared earlier in the sūrah, where the community was told to guard against a Day on which no soul would help another, no intercession would be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help given. The repetition frames the passage: the reader meets the same alert before and after the account of the community's history.

The Book's habit of repeating a warning at the boundaries of a section is a teaching device, since the material enclosed by the two statements is read in the light of them. What lies between is the record of a community that had been given much and had produced the conduct the sūrah has been describing.

**The Four Negations**

The sentence sets out four things that will not happen on that Day: no soul helps another, no intercession is accepted, no ransom is taken, and no assistance is given. The list is arranged from the general to the specific, and each item removes a different hope.

The first negation rules out solidarity, the second rules out the mediation of the influential, the third rules out payment, and the fourth rules out rescue by any other means. Taken together, they exhaust the resources that a person might rely on, in this world or in the next, and the sentence leaves the reader with nothing except his own record. The list also removes the categories on which the community's confidence had been built: kinship, patronage, wealth, and rescue. Each item answers a specific hope, and the sentence's completeness is the point, since nothing outside the four remains to be relied on.

**Ransom in the Book's Usage**

The term rendered as ransom describes compensation offered in place of a person, and the Book uses it for the substitutions people attempted in earlier accounts. The community's ancestors are reported to have proposed offering wealth, or their own children, to secure release, and the Book states that no such payment will be accepted.

The point of the clause is not that God needs compensation but that the transaction the community had in mind is impossible. The Book has already told the reader that the earth belongs to God and that nothing can be offered which He does not already own, and the warning here applies that fact to the Day in question.

**Intercession and Its Conditions**

The Book does not deny intercession universally; it states elsewhere that it occurs by God's permission and for those He approves. What the sentence excludes is the assumption that intercession is available as a right, or that a person can secure a patron in advance and rely on him.

The Prophet's own community was warned against exactly this kind of reliance, and the Book's descriptions of the Day of Judgment repeatedly mention permission as the condition of any pleading. The clause in this verse therefore corrects a misunderstanding that the community had inherited, and it does so without denying the concept altogether. The passage also distinguishes between the intercession the Book affirms by permission and the reliance on patronage that it rejects. What the sentence rules out is not the concept but the assumption of a right, and the Book's descriptions of the Day of Judgment supply the conditions.

**"No Help Will Be Given"**

The last clause states that no assistance will arrive from anywhere, and the word chosen suggests victory and support in the sense of aid granted to a party under attack. The Book uses the same term for divine assistance given to the believers in this world, so that the warning consists precisely in the absence of what the community assumed would be provided.

The sūrah has already told its readers that God alone is their protector and helper and that no one besides Him can guard them. The sentence here applies that statement to a particular Day, and the reader is left with the comparison the passage has built: the same community that had been honoured above others is warned that none of its distinctions will operate on that Day.

**What the Warning Requires of the Reader**

The command to guard oneself appears in the Book where action is still possible, and it is addressed to people who have time to prepare. The warning is therefore not a description of an unalterable fate but an instruction to act while the opportunity exists, which is how the Book presents every account of the Hereafter.

The clause about no soul helping another also speaks to the reader's present relations. A person who is depending on the standing of a community, the merit of an ancestor, or the intercession of a figure he has never obeyed is being told that these supports will not operate. What remains is the conduct the Book has been describing throughout the passage, and the following verses turn to the man whose obedience the sūrah offers as the model for it.

**The Transition to Abraham**

The warning and the transition that follows work together in the sūrah's arrangement. Having removed every substitute for personal obedience, the passage introduces Abraham, whose greatness consisted in the fulfilment of what he was commanded. The narrative that follows answers the question the warning leaves open, which is what a person should rely on when nothing else will be of use. The connection between the warning and the narrative also gives the reader a model of what will be weighed. The person described in the verses that follow is one who fulfilled what he was commanded, and the warning in this sentence prepares the reader to appreciate what that fulfilment involved.

**Why the Warning Is Repeated Here**

The warning appears twice in the sūrah because the material between the two occurrences needs to be read under it. The community's history has been set out in detail, and the second statement of the warning keeps the reader from treating that history as an interesting record rather than as a lesson about accountability. The Book uses this kind of bracket in other chapters as well, placing a statement of the final reckoning before and after a narrative so that the narrative is understood as evidence for it. What the reader is meant to carry away is the connection between what a community does with its provision and what it will be able to rely on when nothing else remains. The verses that follow turn to Abraham, and the reader meets him already knowing that conduct rather than lineage will be the measure on that day.
