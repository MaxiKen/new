## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:100

> Yet they associate the jinn with Allah ˹in worship˺, even though He created them, and they falsely attribute to Him sons and daughters out of ignorance. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they claim!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Jinn Associated With God**

Yet they associate the jinn with Allah in worship, even though He created them. The verse names a specific form of the audience's belief, and the Book records the same practice elsewhere. The sūrah of the Ranged says that the idolaters established a relationship between God and the jinn, and it notes that the jinn themselves know that those who say such things will be brought for punishment (37:158). The sūrah of the Jinn records the jinn's own declaration that their Lord has taken neither a mate nor offspring (72:3).

The claim being refuted took several forms in the region's religions: that the jinn were divine beings with a share in the rule of the world, that they were God's offspring, and that certain objects of worship were their representatives. The verse's answer to all of it is contained in a single clause: He created them. Whatever is created cannot be a partner of its creator, and the sūrah has been applying that reasoning to idols, celestial bodies, and prophets throughout the chapter.

**The Argument From Creation**

The phrase He created them is the same argument the sūrah used against the idols, where it said that the beings invoked besides God are created like their worshippers (7:194). What changes from one passage to the next is only the category of being: in each case the Book removes the possibility of divinity by stating the fact of creation.

The audience's error in this verse is worth stating plainly because it is not a crude mistake. They believed in unseen beings and in their influence, and the sūrah does not deny that the jinn exist. What it denies is the elevation of a created order to a share in the divine, which is the same error it has been correcting in every form of idolatry it has described.

**The Attribution of Sons and Daughters**

And they falsely attribute to Him sons and daughters out of ignorance. The Book records several versions of this claim. The sūrah of the Night Journey asks the pagans whether their Lord has favoured them with sons while taking angels as daughters, and it calls the claim outrageous (17:40); the sūrah of the Star records the same objection, asking whether they prefer sons for themselves while attributing daughters to God (53:21); and the sūrah of the Ornament says that they labelled the angels, who are servants of the Most Compassionate, as female, and asks whether they witnessed their creation (43:19).

The Book's response to the claim about the angels and the claim about Jesus follows the same line, and the sūrah of the Table and the sūrah of the Women address the Christian formulation directly by saying that the Messiah was a messenger of God and that God is far above having a son (4:171). To attribute offspring to God is, in the Book's account, to import the conditions of created life into the divine, and the verse names the source of that error as ignorance.

**The Phrase Out of Ignorance**

The word the translation renders as ignorance describes the absence of knowledge rather than a defect of intellect, and the sūrah has used the same charge repeatedly. It said that those who argued about the signs were following nothing but conjecture, and it said that the idolaters spoke about God what they did not know. The attribution of children to God is presented here as a claim made without evidence, and the Book's objection is to the presumption of speaking about God on the basis of inherited custom.

That charge is the sūrah's consistent method with the audience's theology. The Book never presents the Makkan beliefs as a rival system that has been examined and found wanting; it presents them as assertions inherited from ancestors and defended for reasons that had nothing to do with their truth.

**The Formula of Transcendence**

Glorified and Exalted is He above what they claim. The verse ends with the Book's standard declaration of God's transcendence, and the declaration is used throughout the Book at exactly these points, where a false claim about God has been described. The sūrah of the Cow records the claim that God has offspring and answers it with the same words before stating that everything in the heavens and the earth is subject to Him (2:116).

The formula is not a substitute for argument but the conclusion of it. Having stated what the audience claimed and why the claim conflicts with God's relation to His creation, the verse separates the Creator from the description entirely, and it does so with the vocabulary of exaltation that the Book uses in its descriptions of God's majesty. The next verse supplies the positive account of what God is, in a sentence built from the same argument.

**What the Verse Establishes for the Sūrah**

The passage that follows this verse states that God is the Originator of the heavens and the earth, that He has no mate, and that He created everything and knows everything (6:101). The two verses belong together, since the denial of offspring in this verse gives way to the explanation of why the claim is impossible in the next.

The sūrah is also closing the section of signs that ran from the seed to the harvest, and it does so by naming the beliefs that the signs should have corrected. The audience had watched the creation, the rain, the growth, and the harvest, and the verse states what they made of it: an association of created beings with the Creator and a set of claims about His family. The contrast between the two accounts is the argument, and the sūrah states it without further comment.
