## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:43

> When Our clear revelations are recited to them, they say, “This is only a man who wishes to hinder you from what your forefathers used to worship.” They also say, “This ˹Quran˺ is no more than a fabricated lie.” And the disbelievers say of the truth when it has come to them, “This is nothing but pure magic.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Reactions to One Recitation**

The verse records three separate statements, each introduced by its own verb of speaking, and the accumulation is the point. First an attack on the man, then on the text, then on its effect. What connects them is that none is an argument. Each is a classification — he is a *mere* man with a motive, the book is a *mere* fabrication, the experience is *mere* magic — and each uses the Arabic restrictive construction *mā hādhā illā*, this is nothing but. The formula appears three times in one verse, which no Arabic listener would miss. A man reaching for the same dismissive frame three times in succession is not weighing a case; he is closing it repeatedly, and the repetition suggests the closing did not hold the first time. The verse also specifies the trigger: this happens when the revelations are recited *bayyināt*, as clear evidences. The clarity is what provokes the reaction.

**The First Charge Is About Ancestors, Not About God**

Their opening objection is worth reading carefully, because of what it does not mention. *Mā hādhā illā rajulun yurīdu an yaṣuddakum ʿammā kāna yaʿbudu ābāʾukum* — he only wants to turn you away from what your forefathers worshipped. No claim is made that the forefathers were right, that the old gods are real, or that the new message is false. The charge is disruption of inheritance. For a society organised around lineage, that was a serious accusation and did not need supplementing; a man who breaks with the fathers has broken with everything that makes a person legible. The verb they choose, *yaṣudd*, is the same one the arrogant leaders used when denying that they had blocked anyone from guidance (34:32). Here it is turned around and applied to the Prophet, who is accused of doing precisely what the leaders denied doing.

**"A Man" Again**

The sūrah opened its record of Meccan speech with the same indefinite noun — *a man who claims that when you have been utterly disintegrated you will be raised* (34:7) — and it recurs here in the same position with the same function. The strategy is consistent across thirty-six verses: strip the name, supply *rajul*, and the category of messenger has been denied without an argument being made against it. What has changed is the accusation attached. The earlier version made him ridiculous; this one makes him dangerous, with a motive and a target audience. That progression tracks something real about how opposition to a movement develops. A message that can still be laughed at is described as absurd. One that is gaining adherents is described as subversive.

**Fabrication and Magic Are Incompatible Charges**

The second and third statements cannot both be true, and putting them in one verse exposes the incoherence. *Ifk muftaran* is a fabricated lie — something composed, assembled, made up by a human being working at it. *Siḥr mubīn* is manifest sorcery — something with genuine power, operating on people beyond their ability to resist. A forgery is impotent; that is what makes it a forgery. Magic works; that is what makes it magic. The opponents needed both charges because each answers a different embarrassment. Fabrication explains where the text came from and denies it authority. Magic explains why it has such an effect on listeners, which fabrication cannot account for. Holding both at once is the mark of people working backwards from a conclusion, which the sūrah has already diagnosed in them when they offered the choice between liar and madman (34:8).

**Why Sorcery Was the Charge That Stuck**

Of the three, the accusation of magic is the most revealing about the Qur'an's reception. Sorcery was a recognised category in Arabia with a recognised social function, and to call something *siḥr* was not to call it ineffective. The word acknowledges power while denying legitimacy. What the Meccans were confronting was a text that changed people — that held listeners against their intention, that split households, that produced conversions among men who had come to mock. The Qur'an records more than one instance of opponents warning each other not to listen to it. Calling it magic was the only available explanation that accounted for the observed effects without conceding the source. That the charge is recorded here, at the end of a long argument in which every other objection has been answered, marks it as the last position available to them.

**The Phrase That Frames the Third Charge**

The final statement is introduced with a clause the others lack: *wa qāla alladhīna kafarū li-l-ḥaqqi lammā jāʾahum* — the disbelievers say of the truth, when it has come to them. Two elements are inserted before the quotation. The object is named as *the truth*, not as the Qur'an or the message, so the reader is told what is being dismissed before hearing the dismissal. And the timing is specified: *when it had come to them*, at the moment of arrival, with no interval for examination. Together these make the third statement the most damning of the three, because the verse has supplied the facts that the speakers omitted. They are calling a thing sorcery which is in fact the truth, and they are calling it that on arrival rather than after study. Al-Zukhruf records the identical reflex almost word for word, showing it as the standing reaction of the type rather than a considered local verdict. The next verse takes up the one thing that might have justified such confidence, and finds it absent.
