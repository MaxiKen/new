## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:69

> When they entered Joseph’s presence, he called his brother ˹Benjamin˺ aside, and confided ˹to him˺, “I am indeed your brother ˹Joseph˺! So do not feel distressed about what they have been doing.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Entry and a Private Moment Inside It**

The brothers have arrived again, this time with Benjamin, and the verse opens with their entry upon Joseph. The public business — provisions, measures, the condition of verse 60 — will occupy verse 70. What this verse records is something that happens inside the arrival and apart from it. Joseph takes his brother aside. The chapter gives no setting for the withdrawal: no room, no pretext, no time of day. The withdrawal is simply reported, and the report is what the whole second half of the story has been moving toward since verse 58, when one side recognised and the other did not. Everything the brothers have travelled for arrives in this scene and none of them knows it. The provisions are loaded, the condition is met, the minister has received the brother he asked for, and the recognition building since verse 58 has finally occurred — on one side only. The chapter places the reader inside the moment and keeps the brothers outside it.

**"He Drew His Brother Close"**

The verb describes a physical act of bringing someone near, and the commentators explain that Joseph used it to speak to Benjamin privately and to tell him everything that had happened to him. The disclosure is therefore not a single sentence but an account: the well, the caravan, the sale, the household, the accusation, the prison, the dreams, the granaries. The chapter compresses all of it into the words *I am your brother*, and leaves the rest to the reader's knowledge of the preceding sixty-eight verses. What the verse supplies is the fact of a full explanation given in confidence, and the fact that it was given to Benjamin before it was given to anyone else. The physical detail carries more weight than the speech attached to it. A powerful minister embracing a stranger from a foreign caravan in front of his own officials is an act requiring explanation, and the chapter supplies one: these are brothers, and one of them has been grieving for twenty years. The gesture is the identification; the words only confirm it.

**The First Explicit Identification in the Chapter**

Until this moment Joseph has been recognised only by himself. Verse 58 recorded his recognition of the brothers; verse 62 showed him acting on knowledge they did not share; verse 69 is the first time the knowledge passes to another person. The wording is emphatic — *innī anā akhūk*, I am indeed your brother — and it names the relation rather than the history. Joseph does not begin with the well or with the years of slavery. He begins with the fact that establishes everything else: the man standing in front of Benjamin is his brother. The chapter's disclosure follows the order of importance rather than the order of events. That ordering explains why the disclosure comes here rather than at the first meeting. What matters is not that Joseph recognised his brothers, which he did long before and the narrative recorded, but that the recognition is at last spoken, and spoken to the one person in Egypt who never harmed him. The verse gives the secret its proper recipient before giving it to anyone.

**"Do Not Be Grieved at What They Used to Do"**

The consolation is specific about tense. The verb form denotes repeated or continuing action in the past — what they used to do, what they have been doing. The commentators read the clause as Joseph telling his brother that these events now belong to the past and occurred according to God's will. The formulation does two things. It prevents Benjamin from being distressed by what he has just been told, which is the immediate purpose. And it states Joseph's own settled judgement on his brothers' conduct: the matter is closed, it was in the past, and it happened within a divine arrangement. Verse 92 will say the same thing publicly, in the words *there is no blame on you today*. The private form is more specific than the public one will be. Here Joseph addresses the harm done to him by the men standing outside; in verse 92 he releases them from blame in their own hearing. What is said first to a brother in confidence is said later to the offenders themselves, and the earlier version is what makes the later one credible.

**Benjamin Is Asked to Keep the Meeting Secret**

The commentators record that Joseph instructed his brother not to disclose the meeting to the others. The Qur'an does not state the instruction, but the narrative requires it: the brothers behave throughout verses 70 to 82 as though Benjamin is their brother and the minister is a stranger, and their reactions make no sense otherwise. The secrecy also explains the shape of what follows. A plan is about to be executed in which Benjamin will be detained in Egypt, and Benjamin will not appear surprised or resist. The verse's private disclosure is what makes the public events of the next thirteen verses possible, and the chapter trusts the reader to supply the silence. The request also explains Benjamin's conduct in everything that follows. He is detained, accused through a planted object and claimed under a law he knows to be false, and at no point does he protest or reveal what he has been told. His silence is not passivity but compliance with an instruction from the only brother he trusts, given here and kept for thirteen verses.

**Two Traditions About the Plan That Follows**

The commentators differ on how much Benjamin knew. One account holds that Joseph and his brother together devised the arrangement by which Benjamin would remain in Egypt — that is, Benjamin was a party to the plan of the cup. Another, from al-Ṭabarī, holds that the plan was not known to Benjamin, who was told only who Joseph was and not what was about to happen. Both accounts are transmitted and the Qur'an does not settle the question. The difference affects how verse 70 is read: as a joint operation, or as a device executed around a brother who has been told the truth but not the next step. The chapter's silence leaves the reader with the disclosure and nothing more.

**Two People in Egypt Now Know**

The verse changes the distribution of knowledge in the story, and the change is what drives the irony of everything that follows. Joseph has known since verse 58. Benjamin knows from verse 69. Ten brothers, a father in Canaan, an Egyptian administration and a caravan still do not. The reader stands with the two who know, which means the next sequence — a cup planted, a theft announced, a law invoked, a brother detained, ten men pleading — will be watched from inside a secret rather than from outside it. The chapter has been building toward this reversal for eleven verses, and it announces the reversal in a single sentence spoken privately to a boy who has just learned that his dead brother is the ruler of Egypt.

**The Consolation Is Given Before the Trial**

Note the order of Joseph's two acts in the following verses. He comforts his brother first and then arranges the cup. Benjamin is told who Joseph is, told not to grieve over the past, and asked to keep the meeting secret — and only after that does the accusation fall on him. The sequence means that the one person in the caravan who will be publicly named a thief is also the only person who knows the charge is a device. The chapter is deliberate about this: it does not put an innocent boy through a humiliation he cannot understand. What it does is place the humiliation inside a disclosure, so that the reader watches a trial whose outcome the accused has already been assured of.
