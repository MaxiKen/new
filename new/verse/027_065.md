## Sūrah an-Naml 27:65

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah. Nor do they know when they will be resurrected.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Limit the Prophet Announces About Himself**

The instruction is *qul*, say, and what the Prophet is told to say is a statement about the boundaries of his own knowledge. Nobody in the heavens or the earth knows the unseen — a phrase that includes him, and includes the angels.

Al-Qurṭubī reports that the verse came in response to the idolaters asking when the Day of Resurrection would arrive. If that occasion is right, the answer is remarkable for what it declines to do. A man building a following on claims of special access has an obvious interest in appearing to know; the reply here is a public admission that he does not, framed as a general principle rather than as a personal apology. The Qur'an has the Prophet make this kind of disclaimer repeatedly, and the consistency of it is part of the case for the book's honesty.

**What "the Unseen" Means**

*Ghayb* in the Qur'an is not simply what happens to be out of sight. It names what belongs to a different order of reality — not a thing too distant or too small to observe, but a category no observation reaches.

The term also covers events placed beyond reach by time: the remote past nobody witnessed and the future that has not occurred. Both senses are present in this verse, since the resurrection is unseen in both ways at once. The distinction matters because it separates the Qur'an's claim from a claim about current ignorance. Better instruments do not reduce the *ghayb*; a telescope extends what is witnessed and does not touch the other category at all. What is being described is a partition in the structure of knowledge rather than a frontier that research moves.

**Knower of the Absent and the Present**

The Qur'an pairs *ghayb* with *shahādah* in a formula it uses about a dozen times, describing God as Knower of the unseen and the witnessed. The pair could equally be rendered as what is absent and what is present.

Setting the two together is what makes the attribute meaningful. A being who knew only hidden things would be a specialist; the formula asserts that the same knowledge covers both categories without difference, so that the distinction between hidden and apparent is a fact about creatures rather than about God. This chapter already used the claim in the hoopoe's argument to Sheba, where God was described as knowing what is concealed and what is disclosed. Verse 50 cashed it out in narrative when nine conspirators made a plan they believed nobody could see. The abstract attribute, the historical example and this general statement are three presentations of one claim.

**Even the Angels Are Included**

The scope is *man fī al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — whoever is in the heavens and the earth. The heavens are where the Qur'an locates the angels, and they are therefore covered by the denial.

That inclusion closes off a route the surrounding argument needs closed. Meccan religion treated certain beings as intermediaries with privileged access, and the jinn in particular were credited by soothsayers with information from above. This chapter has already shown a jinn of extraordinary power volunteering in Sulaymān's court, and it will not have escaped the audience that such a creature could carry a throne across a continent. The verse says that none of these beings knows the unseen. Power and knowledge are separated cleanly: an *ʿifrīt* can move a throne and cannot tell you when the Hour comes. Sūrah Sabaʾ makes the same point about Sulaymān's jinn labourers, who kept working after his death because they had no way of perceiving that he had died.

**Why the Hour Is Kept Hidden**

The verse specifies one item of the unseen above all others: when they will be resurrected. That particular ignorance is universal and it is not accidental.

The Qur'an elsewhere has the Prophet asked directly about the Hour and told to say its knowledge is with his Lord alone. What follows from the concealment is a way of living. A known date would convert religion into scheduling, with repentance deferred until the final year and conduct calculated against a countdown. An unknown date that is nonetheless certain requires readiness now, which is the practical result the Qur'an wants. There is also a connection to the chapter's opening. Verse 3 marked the believers as those with *certainty* about the Hereafter — certainty that it will come, paired with complete ignorance of when. Those two conditions together are what produce the disposition the whole chapter has been describing, and the next verse turns to those who have neither.

**A Claim That Cost Something to Make**

Consider the position this statement puts its speaker in. The Prophet is being publicly asked for a date, the questioners are hostile, and the answer he is instructed to give is that nobody knows and he is included in nobody.

An inventor of a religion does not write that sentence. The whole value of a claimed channel to the unseen lies in what it can produce on demand, and a movement's credibility in seventh-century Arabia was measured against soothsayers who did produce, however unreliably. Declining the question forfeits that ground entirely. The Qur'an makes the Prophet decline it repeatedly and in the most exposed circumstances, and it does the same with other things he was asked and could not answer. The pattern is one of the internal features of the text that later Muslim scholars pointed to when arguing that its author was not the man reciting it.
