## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:48

> We have sent messengers only as deliverers of good news and as warners. Whoever believes and does good, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Functions and Nothing Besides Them**

The verse defines the office of the messenger in two words, and the construction is restrictive: messengers were sent only to bring good news and to warn. The Book repeats the pairing across many sūrahs, describing the prophets as bearers of glad tidings and warners (2:213; 4:165; 18:56; 25:56; 33:45; 34:28; 35:24). The Prophet himself is told to say that he is only a warner and a bringer of good news to people who believe (7:188).

The restriction answers the demands the sūrah has been recording. A messenger who is only a news-bearer cannot be required to produce a treasury, to know the unseen, or to turn the world upside down on request. Each of those demands assumes an office larger than the one described here, and the verse closes them off before the next verse states the outcome for those who accept and those who reject.

**Why Both Words Are Necessary**

The pairing is carefully balanced. Good news without warning would produce a religion of comfortable expectations, in which the mercy of God is separated from the seriousness of the accounting. Warning without good news would produce despair, and the Book states its own refusal of that result when it describes people who call upon God with hope and fear at once (32:16), and where it says that God has taken upon Himself to be merciful (6:12).

The two words therefore hold the messenger's task in a single direction. What he announces is not a general religious atmosphere but a specific news item with two aspects: the possibility of a good outcome and the reality of a bad one, and the audience is told plainly which conduct belongs to which.

**The Two Conditions Attached to the Promise**

Whoever believes and does good is the Book's standard formula for the saved, and it uses two verbs where a modern reader might expect one. Belief is inward and amendment is outward, and the Book consistently refuses to separate them. Elsewhere it states the same pairing where it speaks of those who believe in God, the Last Day, and do righteous deeds, and says that they will have their reward with their Lord, with no fear for them and no grief (2:62; 2:262).

The Book's moral psychology is visible in the pairing. Faith that produces no change in conduct is not treated as a weaker faith but as a doubtful claim, and conduct that has no faith underneath it is treated as an account that will not balance. The verse in front of us states the combination as the condition of the promise without commenting on the exceptions, and the sūrah elsewhere handles the exceptions directly.

**What It Means to Have No Fear or Grief**

The two words cover the two directions of human anxiety. Fear concerns what is coming, and grief concerns what has gone; the Book's promise removes both, which is why the formula recurs so often in descriptions of the Hereafter (7:35; 10:62; 46:13). Those who are promised it are told that the arrival of the judgment holds nothing for them to dread, and that the losses of their earthly lives are not the end of the account.

The Book also treats the promise as a present possession in some measure, since the people described as mindful are those who have no fear concerning their future and no regret over their past dealings with God. That is the practical form of the good news the messenger brings, and it is delivered in the same sentence as the warning to the people who reject him.

**The Messenger's Freedom From the Outcome**

Read together with the verses around it, this sentence also relieves the Prophet of a burden. He had been told that the punishment is not in his hands (6:47), and now he is told that his task is the delivery of news rather than the production of results. The Book repeats the point when it tells him that his duty is only to remind (88:21), and when it refuses to make him a keeper over the people (6:107).

That arrangement protects the message as well as the messenger. A prophet who could compel acceptance would destroy the moral content of the decision, and a prophet who carried the responsibility for other people's choices would be crushed by them. The verse states the boundary, and the next sentence states what happens on the other side of it.

**The Value of Naming the Office So Plainly**

The verse is a small definition with a large reach. It tells the audience what they may expect from the man addressing them and therefore what they may not demand, and it tells the messenger what he may and may not attempt. Everything the sūrah reports about the Makkan demands for signs, angels, and written books is answered in advance by the narrow job description given here.

That description also puts the audience on notice about what they are being held to. The message has been delivered as news and warning; the response is now theirs, and the following verse states what the response costs when it goes the wrong way.

The Book widens the same description beyond the Prophet's own people when it says that he was sent only as a deliverer of good news and a warner to all humanity, though most people do not know (34:28), and when it says that no community has ever lacked a warner (35:24). The wording of this verse is therefore a definition of the office in general rather than a description of one mission. Every messenger who came to a people came with the same two items and the same absence of coercive power, and the Makkans were dealing with an instance of a pattern they had already heard about.
