## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:39

> O my fellow-prisoners! Which is far better: many different lords or Allah—the One, the Supreme?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Puts Him on Their Level**

*Yā ṣāḥibayi al-sijn* — O my two companions in the prison. He does not call them servants of the king, does not use their offices, and does not address them as an instructor addresses pupils. He names the one thing they have in common, which is the cell. The construction is a dual possessive: my two companions, belonging to him as much as to them. That choice of address matters because of what preceded it. Joseph has just announced that he abandoned the creed of the society that imprisoned him, a statement that could easily have separated him from two royal servants. The vocative immediately closes the distance the confession opened. He is not preaching from a position of superiority; he is speaking to the two other men in the room, all three of them held by the same authority.

**One Question Rather Than a Lecture**

The sermon now takes the form of a single interrogative, and the interrogative is comparative: which is better? Not which is true, not which is obligatory, not which your fathers held. He asks his hearers to rank two arrangements, and the ranking is one they can perform themselves without expertise. The method is the Qur'an's characteristic way of arguing about God's unity: it does not begin from metaphysics but from preference, because preference is something every person already exercises. A listener who answers the question has already conceded the conclusion, and a listener who refuses to answer has been shown that the refusal is not intellectual. The form also respects the setting. Two men under sentence will not follow a demonstration; they will answer a question.

**Many Lords Against One**

The alternatives are stated with exact symmetry. *Arbābun mutafarriqūn* — lords many, dispersed, at odds with one another; the adjective carries the sense of scattered and divided, not merely numerous. Against them: *Allāhu al-wāḥid al-qahhār*. The comparison is therefore not between one and many in arithmetic but between coherence and fragmentation. Al-Rāzī reads the argument as the standard Qur'anic one from cosmic order: if there were multiple gods, the order of the world would show the fissures that multiple authorities always produce. The Qur'an states the same reasoning directly: *“Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted”* (21:22). Joseph's question invites the prisoners to apply that reasoning to the pantheon they live inside, where every temple holds a different lord and no two agree.

**"The One, The Supreme" Pairs Two Names on Purpose**

*Al-wāḥid* denies division; *al-qahhār* denies rivalry. The second name is an intensive form meaning the one who subdues, overcomes and holds everything under his constraint, and its presence in the sentence is what makes the comparison decisive. Many lords would have to be either unequal or in conflict; if unequal, the greatest is the only real lord, and if in conflict, the order of the world would collapse. A single lord who is also supreme over all things removes both problems at once. The pairing also answers an objection available to Egyptian hearers, whose religion distributed power among many deities precisely because no single one seemed sufficient for every domain. The names assert sufficiency: one, and able to enforce it.

**The Argument Begins With Lordship, Not Existence**

Joseph does not attempt to persuade his hearers that God exists. They are not atheists; they are servants of a king in a country full of temples, and the question of whether divine reality exists would have been meaningless to them. The dispute is about number, quality and allegiance — how many lords there are and whether they are at odds. Starting there is a lesson in method that the Qur'an follows with its own audience in Mecca, who likewise acknowledged a supreme creator while associating others with Him. The argument that such people require is not a proof of God but a comparison of gods, and the comparison is settled by asking which arrangement is better. Verse 39 is the whole of that argument, in one sentence, addressed to two men who would otherwise have spent the night discussing their sentences.

**What the Question Sets Up in the Next Verse**

The comparative form leaves an opening that verse 40 fills. Once the hearers have agreed — or been unable to deny — that one supreme Lord is better than many dispersed ones, the status of the many has to be accounted for, and the accounting is devastating: they are names. The two verses form a single argument in two movements, the first establishing that unity is superior and the second showing that the alternatives have no existence to compare. That the argument is delivered by a prisoner to two other prisoners, in the middle of a chapter about a family's betrayal, is the sūrah's way of insisting that its subject is not the family. The story carries a creed, and the creed is stated where nobody in it has anything to gain.

The comparative interrogative is also the Qur'an's habitual form for this argument, and the pattern is visible elsewhere. The sūrah of Thunder instructs the Prophet to put a series of the same shape to his audience: *“Say, ‘Can the blind and the sighted be equal? Or can darkness and light be equal?’”* (13:16). The questions are not seeking information; they are forcing a ranking the hearer cannot refuse, and once the ranking is given the conclusion follows without further argument. Joseph's question works identically. Nobody can rank dispersed lords above a single supreme one and mean it, so the question is not really open. Its function is to make the hearers say the conclusion themselves, which produces conviction of a different kind from being told. Two men in a cell are led to pronounce a judgement on the religion of the country that imprisoned them, in their own words, to a fellow prisoner.
