## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:72

> Allah has promised the believers, both men and women, Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, and splendid homes in the Gardens of Eternity, and—above all— the pleasure of Allah. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise Includes Men and Women Equally**

The promise is addressed to believing men and women together. The reward is not divided by a lesser spiritual status assigned to one sex. Both receive Allah's promise of the Garden because both were called to faith, obedience, sacrifice, and guardianship. This is the other side of the moral equality stated in the preceding verse: shared responsibility is joined to shared hope.

The inclusion also corrects a narrow view of religious reward. A person's final worth is not measured by social visibility, wealth, or the authority attached to a public role. Allah sees faith and action in every servant. The woman whose obedience is hidden and the man whose service is unseen are both included in the promise when they believe and act sincerely. The Garden is not a prize reserved for public fame.

**The Gardens Answer Human Longing Without Reducing It**

The imagery of gardens with flowing rivers speaks to life, beauty, refreshment, and permanence. Water that flows beneath the gardens suggests abundance without the fear that the source will dry up. The homes are splendid because the reward includes belonging, safety, and a dwelling worthy of rest. These descriptions address the body and the imagination in language people can understand.

The promise should not be reduced to material appetite. The Garden is a created reward from Allah, and its beauty is part of His generosity. The believer is allowed to hope for its pleasures without being ashamed of that hope. Yet the images also point beyond the limits of worldly pleasure: no earthly garden removes death, loss, or fear. The promised Garden does.

**Eternity Changes the Meaning of Home**

The verse names Gardens of Eternity. A worldly home can be beautiful and still be temporary. Families separate, buildings decay, and a person may lose the place he worked years to build. The eternal dwelling answers this instability. It offers residence without eviction, joy without the shadow of departure, and belonging without the fear that time will take it away.

This hope can make the believer less enslaved to status in the present world. He cares for his home and family, but he does not treat worldly possession as the proof that he has finally arrived. He can live simply without feeling spiritually defeated and can use wealth without making it his identity. The eternal home places every temporary home in perspective.

**Allah's Pleasure Is Greater Than the Garden**

The verse gives the highest place to Allah's pleasure. This does not make the Garden insignificant. It tells the believer that created rewards are not the summit of the relationship with the Creator. The greatest success is to be received by Allah with contentment and to know that He is pleased with the servant.

This ranking also purifies intention in worship. If a person acts only for pleasure, he may abandon obedience when the reward is delayed. If he seeks Allah's pleasure, he remains faithful even when worldly results are hidden. The Garden is a gift; Allah's pleasure is the sign that the servant has reached the purpose for which the gift exists.

**Hope Is a Moral Force in the Present**

The promise of the Hereafter is not an escape from worldly responsibility. It strengthens the believer to practice responsibility without demanding immediate payment. He can give wealth, protect another person, or endure loneliness because he trusts that Allah's reward is larger than the present cost.

Hope also protects against envy. Another believer's success does not reduce one's portion in Allah's bounty. The Garden is not a scarce resource fought over by human rivalry. This allows people to rejoice in one another's obedience and to support one another toward the same end. The community described in the previous verse becomes possible when its members hope together rather than compete for the only visible reward.

**The True Triumph Has a Clear Measure**

The verse calls this the ultimate triumph. Triumph is not defined as winning every public argument or possessing the largest home in this world. It is to reach the promised Garden, remain there, and receive Allah's pleasure. That measure gives present victories their proper place. A worldly win that costs faith is loss; a worldly loss endured in obedience may be part of triumph.

The believer still seeks justice and lawful success. He simply refuses to call them ultimate. He asks Allah for a life that leads to the final home, a heart that seeks divine pleasure above applause, and actions that make the promise believable in the present. The greatest hope is therefore also a discipline: live now as a servant who wants to meet Allah pleased with him.

The promise also teaches the believer how to use present homes. A family home should train gratitude, a workplace should be governed by honesty, and a public institution should protect dignity. These earthly places cannot become Paradise, but they can reflect a servant's hope for Paradise. He builds without worshipping what he builds, enjoys without forgetting the Giver, and gives without imagining that temporary comfort is the final triumph. Allah's pleasure remains greater because every created delight is valuable only when it is received under His approval. The heart that seeks that approval is already being trained for the eternal dwelling.

The highest reward also protects the believer from using worship as a bargain. He hopes for the Garden because Allah promised it, but he obeys Allah because Allah is worthy of obedience. This is why a servant can remain faithful when no worldly benefit appears. The promise of contentment is not a reason to despise the created world; it is a reason to place the created world beneath its Creator. Every lawful pleasure becomes safer when it leads to gratitude, and every sacrifice becomes lighter when it is offered for the One whose pleasure is greater than the reward itself.
