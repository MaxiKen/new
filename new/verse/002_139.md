## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:139

> Say, “Would you dispute with us about Allah, while He is our Lord and your Lord? We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put to the Disputants**

The verse instructs the Prophet ﷺ to address the two communities with a question, and the question concerns the ground of a dispute rather than its details. The arguers were contesting a matter that belongs to God, and the sentence asks on what basis they were doing so.

The Book's method here is to expose the assumption beneath the argument. A dispute about God presupposes that one party has a standing to settle what God has decided, and the question in the sentence removes that ground before any particular point is discussed. What the reader receives is a lesson in how the Book handles controversies: it examines the premise rather than the claims.

**Who Was Being Addressed**

Commentators identified the parties as the Jews and the Christians, and some added the idolatrous Arabs, noting that the disputes took different forms. The reports describe objections to a prophet coming from outside the children of Israel, and complaints among the Arabs that a man of higher social standing should have been chosen if a prophet were to be sent.

Both objections have the same structure, which is that they measure the message by the messenger's identity. The verse's answer displaces that criterion by naming what the two sides actually share: the same Lord, the same accountability for deeds, and the same obligation of sincere devotion. The dispute is set inside a relation common to everyone involved.

**"He Is Our Lord and Your Lord"**

The middle of the sentence states a fact that no party to the dispute would deny, and the Book uses this kind of shared premise when it wants to move a controversy onto common ground. God is Lord of all, and the community's relation to Him is not different in kind from that of its opponents, whatever the difference in their response to Him.

The statement also removes a possible reading of the dispute as a contest between two gods or two peoples. The Lord being discussed is one, and the only question left is what He has commanded. The sūrah has made the same point in several ways in the preceding verses, and this sentence states it in its simplest form.

**The Division of Accountability**

The sentence then states that each party is answerable for its own deeds, and the clause repeats the principle stated a few verses earlier about the community that had passed away. The Book returns to the point because it is the hinge on which disputes about inheritance turn: no one else's record can be entered in a person's account.

The Book states the same in its record of the Prophet's answer when he was denied: his deeds belong to him and the deeds of his opponents to them. The formula appears in the sūrah's account of Abraham's debates with his people as well, and the Book uses it as a boundary line that allows argument to end without the parties needing to agree.

**The Claim to Sincere Devotion**

The final clause states that the community is devoted to God alone, and the Arabic term describes sincerity as well as exclusive worship. The word carries the sense of purifying one's religion from any admixture, and it appears in the Book's descriptions of those whose worship is offered for God without a partner.

The clause is therefore the community's own answer to the dispute. It does not respond to every point the two communities raised; it states the position from which the believers speak, and the sūrah leaves the detailed arguments to the material it has already presented. What the community claims is not a superior pedigree but an undivided devotion.

**A Model for Handling Disputation**

The Book's records of the Prophet's disputations follow the same pattern. He is told in one passage to invite people to the way of his Lord with wisdom and kind advice and to debate in the best manner, and in another he is told that if his opponents dispute with him, he should state that he has submitted himself to God along with his followers.

The sentence in this verse belongs to that group of instructions. It shows that the community's confidence does not require the silencing of its opponents, and that a dispute can be conducted by stating the position and leaving the judgment where it belongs.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The sentence contains no insult and no threat, and the Book does not instruct the Prophet ﷺ to win the exchange. What it instructs him to do is to identify the shared ground and to state the community's own position, and to let the difference be what it is.

The sūrah will continue to address the two communities in the verses that follow, and it will appeal to their scriptures and to the record of their ancestors. The verse in this place supplies the disposition in which that appeal is made, and it protects the community from the temptation to make the argument personal.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The sentence follows the confession of faith and the statement about guidance and schism, and it precedes an appeal to the testimony the two communities possessed. The sequence is deliberate: the community states what it believes, it states the ground on which disputes are conducted, and then it asks its opponents to weigh the witness their own scriptures contain. Each step prepares the next, and the passage reads as a single argument rather than a series of separate sayings.

**The Plainness of the Sentence**

The verse's language is ordinary and its claims are few, which is what gives it its strength. The Book does not need a technical vocabulary to settle the matter, and the reader is left with three statements that are difficult to deny: the same Lord, separate accounts, and a devotion offered without partners. The sūrah's argument continues from that footing.
