## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:139

> Say, “Would you dispute with us about Allah, while He is our Lord and your Lord? We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Arguing About God With God in Common**

The question the verse puts in the Prophet's mouth is designed to stop an argument by exposing its absurd shape. Would you dispute with us *about Allah* — while He is our Lord *and your Lord*? Both sides of the dispute already stand on the same premise: there is one Lord who owns both communities. The quarrel, then, is not about whether God exists or whose God is real; it is being conducted, oddly, about God Himself — His choices, His sends, His preferences — by parties who agree on who He is. The rhetorical question names that oddity. Disputing about a person behind his back presupposes you know him; disputing about God while calling Him your Lord presupposes a knowledge of His ways that neither disputant actually possesses. The verse does not forbid the discussion; it re-frames it, moving the contested matter out of God's essence and into the only place where evidence exists: the deeds of the disputants themselves.

**What the Dispute Was Actually About**

The historical substance of the argument is recoverable from the context and the commentaries. The Jewish communities of Medina objected to a prophet arising outside the Children of Israel — revelation, in their theology, had a lineage, and this claimant came from the wrong family tree. The idolatrous Arabs, for their part, objected on grounds of social rank: if heaven were going to send a man, it would send one of higher station than the orphan of Mecca — the same protest the Qur'an quotes elsewhere, *"If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities!"* (43:31). Both objections share a structure: they are claims about how God ought to distribute His favors, and the sūrah answers that distribution is His alone — *"You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please"* (3:26). The dispute about Allah is, on inspection, a dispute about Allah's freedom. The verse's reply — He is your Lord as much as ours — concedes the relationship and denies either party a veto over it.

**Our Deeds and Yours**

Having named the true object of the quarrel, the verse disengages with a formula that recurs across the Qur'an: to us our deeds, to you yours. The same words appear when denial hardens into deadlock — *"If they deny you, then say, 'My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours. You are free of what I do and I am free of what you do!'"* (10:41) — and when submission is offered and refused: *"I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers"* (3:20). The formula is not indifference and not relativism. It is the closing of a case that cannot proceed: each party's deeds will be answered for by that party, so further argument about whose deeds please God is litigation without jurisdiction. What the formula preserves is accountability — "we are accountable for our deeds and you for yours" — and what it refuses is the merger of accounts that the disputants wanted, in which one community's standing vouches for or condemns another's.

**The Prophet Who Ended the Same Argument Before**

The pattern is older than Medina. When Abraham's people argued with him, he answered with the identical move: *"Are you arguing with me about Allah, while He has guided me?"* (6:80) — the shared God named, the guidance located in the arguer's own experience rather than in the opponent's theory, and the fear transferred from the idols to the Lord who encompasses everything in His knowledge. The sūrah of the Cow itself stages the earliest version, the man who disputed with Abraham about his Lord (2:258), undone not by superior information but by the direction of the sun. This verse places Muhammad in that exact succession: the same argument, the same adversaries in new costumes, the same answer. Reading 2:139 alongside 6:80 shows that the Qur'an understands religious dispute as a repeating event with a fixed prophetic response — name the common Lord, present your own guidance, separate the accounts, and stop.

**Devoted to Him Alone**

The final clause is the verse's real edge: *wa-naḥnu lahu mukhliṣūn* — and we are sincere, devoted, purified in intention toward Him alone. The root carries *ikhlāṣ*, sincerity in the technical sense of a motive unmixed with anything other than God. Placed at the end of an argument verse, it identifies what actually terminates disputes: not the winning of points but the purity of the disputant. A person who argues to be seen winning cannot disengage, because the argument itself has become his object; a person devoted to God alone can state the separation of accounts and mean it. The clause also answers the *shiqāq* named two verses earlier (2:137) — opposition is a mixed motive at work, and sincerity is its cure. The community's position in the quarrel is thus not a stronger claim but a cleaner heart: we do not need to defeat you, because our devotion does not run through your defeat.

**The Graceful Argument**

The method of this verse is codified in the sūrah of the Spider, in the Qur'an's standing rule for interreligious speech: *"Do not argue with the People of the Book unless gracefully, except with those of them who act wrongfully. And say, 'We believe in what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to you. Our God and your God is ˹only˺ One. And to Him we ˹fully˺ submit'"* (29:46). The resemblance to 2:139 is structural: begin from the shared — one God, one Lord of both communities; state the creed without polemic; keep the accounts separate; reserve firmness for those who argue in bad faith. Grace (*aḥsan*) is the manner, not the message: the common ground is asserted first because it is true, and the separation follows because it is necessary. This verse is that procedure in its earliest Medinan form — a dispute about God settled by pointing at God, at the deeds of the disputants, and at the sincerity that needs no verdict from either.
