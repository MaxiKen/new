## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:3

> We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the best of stories through Our revelation of this Quran, though before this you were totally unaware ˹of them˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Superlative Attached to One Narrative**

The words are *aḥsan al-qaṣaṣ*: the best of stories, not a good story, not an instructive one. The superlative is explicit and it is attached to a single narrative among all the narratives revelation could have chosen. Elsewhere the same construction is applied to the Book as a whole — *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons”* (39:23) — so the Qur'an is comfortable ranking its own discourse. The ranking here is not aesthetic in the modern sense of pleasant. *Ḥusn* in this usage means the story does its work completely: nothing in it is idle, nothing is missing, and every element returns later to complete another. A narrative earns the superlative by the tightness with which its parts interlock, and by the range of human situations it manages to hold inside one arc without breaking.

**One Unbroken Thread Where the Others Are Episodic**

The structural reason is visible on the page. Stories of Moses appear in many chapters, retold from different angles, each telling a fragment; Abraham, Noah, Lot and Jonah recur in pieces. Joseph's account is told once, from the dream in verse 4 to its fulfilment in verse 100, without repetition and without a rival version elsewhere in the Book. Nearly a hundred consecutive verses carry a single plot with a beginning, a middle and a resolved end, and no other chapter offers anything comparable. The consequence for the reader is unusual: suspense is possible. Because the story is not being restated, details introduced early — a shirt, a well, a measured supply of grain — can be stored and paid out much later, and the audience discovers their meaning only when the plot requires it.

**Why the Content Deserves the Word "Best"**

The range explains the rest of the claim. Inside one narrative sit the unity of God and the nature of prophethood; a father's partiality and a brother's envy; dream interpretation treated as a learned skill rather than a superstition; seduction, false accusation and imprisonment; court politics, granary administration and a seven-year famine; forgiveness granted by the injured party to the injurers; and the reunion of a family scattered across two countries. Creed and statecraft, the inner life and the market, all run through the same set of characters. No other chapter gathers so much of religious and worldly experience into a single continuous account, which is why the tradition has read it as a manual as much as a story: patience under trial in one half, gratitude under power in the other.

**The Ignorance Clause Is the Argument**

The second half of the verse does the evidential work. *Though before this you were totally unaware* is not a biographical aside; it is a proof offered to an audience that could check it. The Qur'an uses the move repeatedly and in the same terms. *“This is one of the stories of the unseen, which we reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. Neither you nor your people knew it before this. So be patient! Surely the ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous”* (11:49). *“This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian”* (3:44). *“You were not there ˹O Prophet˺ on the western side of the mountain when We entrusted the Commandments to Moses”* (28:44). Each time the claim is that the teller had no access. A man who never studied scripture, living in a city with no rabbinical academy, recites a Hebrew patriarch's biography with details unknown to his own people. The unawareness is the ground of the miracle, not a footnote to it. Mecca in this period had no synagogue capable of supplying such a narrative and no library at all; the Prophet could not read the scriptures of the People of the Book even had they been present. The verse asks the audience to weigh that fact against the fluency of what they are hearing. It is the same argument the chapter presses again in its last verse, where the message is called a confirmation of earlier revelation and not a fabrication (12:111).

**Two Reported Occasions, and What They Have in Common**

The commentators transmit two accounts of why this narrative was sent down. One holds that learned Jews in Mecca persuaded the Quraysh to test the Prophet by demanding a story about Joseph, on the reasoning that only a genuine prophet could supply it; the other holds that the Companions asked him to tell them of those who suffered before them, so that their own endurance might be strengthened. Neither report can be traced to a chain of the first rank [UNVERIFIED: both occasions of revelation are transmitted in the tafsīr literature without a graded chain; presented here as reported, not as established], and the two are not mutually exclusive — a challenge from outside and a need from inside could both have been met by one revelation. What they share is more interesting than which is correct. In both versions the story answers a question that had been put, and in both the answer is meant to settle something: for the challengers, whether this man knows what he cannot have learned; for the challenged, whether suffering has a shape that ends.

**Consolation Written Into the Form**

The setting supplies the third reason for the superlative. This chapter is placed by the reports in the years after Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib died, when the Prophet stood unprotected in a city that had turned violent toward him, and shortly after the journey to Ṭāʾif ended in stones. Into that moment comes the longest continuous narrative in the book, and its subject is a man betrayed by his own family, sold, slandered, imprisoned on evidence everyone knew was false, and finally raised to authority over the people who destroyed him. The form of consolation is deliberate. Not a command, not a list of promises, but a story — the shape in which a grieving mind can hold hope without having to argue for it. Calling it the best of stories is therefore a claim about function as well as craft: this is the account best fitted to do what the moment required. It also explains why the chapter keeps returning to address the Prophet directly between episodes, and why the closing verses draw the lesson out loud instead of leaving it implied. A consolation that must be inferred is not consolation. The story is handed over complete, ranked, and pointed at the one who most needed it.
