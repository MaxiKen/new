## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:21

> O humanity! Worship your Lord, Who created you and those before you, so that you may become mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Turns**

For twenty verses the sūrah has been talking about people: the reverent, the disbelievers, the hypocrites. Now it turns and speaks to someone. *O humanity* (2:21) is the first direct address in the Book's longest sūrah, and the change of grammatical person is the change of subject. Diagnosis is over; summons begins.

The word used is *al-nās* — people, humankind, everyone — and it is not qualified. The sūrah does not say "O believers" or "O people of the Book" or "O Quraysh." The address reaches everyone who can hear it, and it comes immediately after a passage that sorted humanity into three groups and described the ruin of the worst of them. The placement is deliberate: having shown what refusal produces, the sūrah now asks.

**Worship**

The command is *iʿbudū*, and its object is stated in the same breath: *your Lord*. The two words belong together. Worship in the Quran is not reverence directed at whatever a person finds impressive; it is the posture appropriate to the specific relation between a creature and the one who made it.

The word also carries more than ceremony. Its root is the same one that produces *ʿabd*, a servant, and the range of the term covers everything a servant does in recognition of his master. Prayer is part of it; so is giving, so is abstaining, so is the ordinary business of a life lived in reference to someone other than oneself. The sūrah will spend its next two hundred verses unpacking what the word contains, which is one reason the command is stated so early and so plainly.

**Who Created You**

The argument is a single clause and it is the oldest in the Book: *Who created you and those before you* (2:21). Worship is owed to the Creator, and the claim rests on a fact about origin that the Quran treats as conceded. The pagans of Makkah, asked who made the heavens and the earth, answered with the name of God; the Quran's arguments elsewhere begin from admissions its opponents already make.

The second half of the clause does the more interesting work. *And those before you* removes the two most common evasions. It removes generational pride, since the people who came before were made by the same hand and were not the source of themselves. And it removes the assumption that a person's existence is an achievement of his own era — his biology, his culture, his class — by pointing out that every previous era was produced the same way and has gone.

**So That You May Become Mindful**

The purpose clause names the goal: *so that you may become mindful* (2:21), using the same root the sūrah opened with at 2:2, where the Book was described as guidance *for those mindful ˹of Allah˺*. The circle closes here. The reverent were described in the first five verses; this is how a person joins them.

The particle translated *so that* has caused discussion, since the word carries a sense of hope or expectation that seems out of place when the speaker is God, who is not anxious about outcomes. Commentators resolved it two ways: that God's hope is equivalent to a promise, since nothing can obstruct it; or that the expectation attaches to the people addressed rather than to the Speaker. Both readings preserve the same instruction. Worship is the means and mindfulness is the end, and the two are related as practice and condition.

**Worship as the Answer to the Diagnoses**

Placed where it is, the command answers everything the preceding twenty verses described. The hypocrites' disease was a divided trust; worship is undivided trust practised. Their trade was exchanging what lasts for what does not; worship is the refusal to make the trade. Their condition was instability so severe that they could not walk by the light they were given; worship is the discipline that converts a flash into a direction.

The sequence also supplies the motive the sūrah has not yet mentioned. Nothing so far has been said about reward or punishment for the people being addressed. What is offered instead is a description of what they are and who made them, and the instruction to live in reference to it.

**A Command Addressed to Everyone**

Because the address is to humanity as such, the sūrah is making a claim about what a human being is rather than about what a Muslim must do. Worship is not presented as a system someone might opt into; it is presented as the correct posture of a created thing toward its Creator, and the failure to adopt it is described elsewhere as a failure to understand what one is.

The verse is therefore the hinge of the whole opening. Everything before it sorted humanity by how it responded to the summons, and everything after it — the challenge, the promise, the laws, the narratives — is addressed to people who have now been told what is being asked of them and why.

It also sets the terms for everything that follows in a way that is easy to miss. The sūrah's next two hundred verses contain a great deal of law: marriage, divorce, trade, inheritance, fasting, warfare, pilgrimage. Every one of those rulings is being presented under this opening command, which means the sūrah does not treat obedience as a separate department from worship. The regulations are the shape that worshipping your Lord takes in the specific business of a life. A reader who takes this verse as a preamble and the rulings as something else has misread the architecture of the whole sūrah.
