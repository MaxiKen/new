## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:21

> O humanity! Worship your Lord, Who created you and those before you, so that you may become mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Call That Addresses Everyone Present**

For the first time in this sūrah the Book speaks to the whole of humanity rather than describing a group. The Arabic address, *yā ayyuhā al-nās*, gathers everyone in hearing distance: the believers whose marks were listed at the beginning, the rejecters whose hearts were sealed, the men of the two rooms, and any reader who has recognised a fragment of himself in those portraits. After twenty verses of sorting, the text opens out and speaks to all of them.

The placement is a deliberate change of register. What precedes it is diagnosis, and what follows it is argument, and the pivot between the two is an invitation. Nobody is asked to explain his previous position first; the call arrives before any response and is addressed to the whole crowd at once. The sūrah's method here is characteristic: it identifies what is wrong with people and then speaks to them as though the identification were not the end of the matter.

**Worship Argued From Creation, Not From Threat**

The reason given for worship is the act of creation, and the Book tends to lead with this reason before any other. The Qur'an states it as a general conclusion elsewhere: *“That is Allah—your Lord! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. ˹He is˺ the Creator of all things, so worship Him ˹alone˺. And He is Maintainer of everything.”* (6:102). The command follows from a fact about origins rather than from a warning about consequences.

That ordering shapes what the verse is asking for. A demand backed by punishment produces compliance where the power is felt and silence where it is not; a demand argued from creation addresses the person's own existence, which he cannot deny having received. The Book's calls to worship return to this reasoning again and again, and they return to it in moments when the audience is least receptive, as if the argument were the point rather than the prelude.

**"Those Before You" as Part of the Evidence**

The clause about previous generations does more work than it appears to. Everyone addressed knows that he did not make himself and that the world was already furnished with occupants when he arrived, and the ancestors he can name were in the same position. The argument therefore leans on testimony the audience already accepts, and it does not require them to trust a new claim about history.

The point is pressed further elsewhere in the Book, where the audience's own admission is used against them: *“And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, ‘Allah!’ Say, ‘Praise be to Allah!’ In fact, most of them do not know.”* (31:25). The people of the sūrah were not atheists arguing that the world had no maker. They already named God as the creator of the sky, and the verse here holds them to what their own answer implies about loyalty.

**Whose Hope Is It, Anyway**

The aim of the command is *taqwā*, and the Arabic construction *laʿallakum* attaches a hopeful outcome to God's side of the sentence. Commentators discussed the difficulty in that phrasing, since hope and anxiety are conditions of creatures, and one solution they offered was to read the hope as a promise: for God, *perhaps* means *certainly*, since nothing can obstruct what He has decided. Another was to attach the hope to the human beings who are the subject of the statement rather than to God.

Either way the sentence describes a relationship in which worship leads to a state of guarded awareness, and the Book treats that state as desirable in itself rather than as a means to some other reward. The sūrah opened by describing the Book as a guide for the mindful (2:2). The command in this verse names the same quality as the goal of worship, which ties the sūrah's first description of its readers to its first universal instruction.

**Reverence as the Fruit of Worship**

The word *taqwā* comes from a root meaning to shield and protect, and the Book treats it as a practical competence rather than a mood. The person who has it keeps watch over what he does, because he is aware of being seen; the Qur'an's portrait of such people elsewhere shows the vigilance expressed in ordinary choices: *“Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets”* (2:177), after which the verse lists giving, prayer and keeping promises.

Reverence, then, is what worship produces when it is practised rather than performed. The command in this verse asks for worship and names the awareness that follows from it, which means the sentence is not a transaction in which an offering is exchanged for a benefit. It is a description of how a person changes when he directs his life toward the one who made him.

**An Open Door at the Head of the Sūrah**

The address stands directly after the Book's most severe statements about people who cannot hear, speak or see their way. Coming there, the call to humanity reads as an announcement that the diagnosis was not a sentence. The men described in the preceding parables are still in the audience, and the summons in this verse is addressed to them among everyone else, which is why the next verse continues to reason with the same audience rather than expelling it.

The Qur'an states the policy elsewhere in an instruction that governs how the message is carried: *“Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner.”* (16:125). This verse is that invitation in its shortest form. It names God as the Lord of the people being addressed, credits Him with their creation and their ancestors', and asks for one act in return.
