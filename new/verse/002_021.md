## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:21

> O humanity! Worship your Lord, Who created you and those before you, so that you may become mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Command Falls on the Whole Species**

The sūrah's opening twenty verses sorted people into files — the God-conscious, the disbelievers, the hypocrites — and the taxonomy could have bred a comfortable reading: every reader locating himself in the good file and continuing. Verse 21 forecloses that move by its address. *Yā ayyuhā al-nās* — O humanity — gathers all three files back into one room, and the first direct command of al-Baqarah is issued to the species as such, not to the believers alone. The vocative is the Qur'an's widest; it excludes no one capable of hearing it, including the men just described as deaf. The command that follows is equally universal in its object: *uʿbudū rabbakum* — worship your Lord. Not a new ritual, not a Madinan regulation, but the primordial obligation, restated at the head of the Qur'an's longest legislative sūrah so that every law that follows is received inside it. Structure before statute: the whole book of rulings will be addressed to people first defined as worshippers.

**ʿIbādah: The Verb That Contains a Whole Life**

"Worship" here is the Qur'an's comprehensive term, *ʿibādah* — the same word al-Fātiḥah put at the center of the prayer, *"You ˹alone˺ we worship"* (1:5). Its root covers servitude, obedience, and adoration at once, and its scope in Quranic usage is a life's entire orientation rather than a liturgical segment. The creation of the two orders is stated as its purpose elsewhere without ambiguity: *"I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me"* (51:56). Read with that verse, the command of 2:21 is not adding an activity to human life but naming the activity human life is for. The exegetes' standard gloss — worship as the utmost humility joined to the utmost love — keeps the term from collapsing into either fear alone or affection alone. And the command's justification is embedded in its own relative clause: *Who created you*. The Maker's claim on the made is the argument; nothing else is offered, because nothing else is needed. A thing that was made owes its making its orientation, and *ʿibādah* is that orientation with a name.

**“Your Lord”: The Title That Argues**

The command names its object *rabbakum* — your Lord — and the choice of title carries the verse's whole persuasion. *Rabb* is not primarily a word of power; it is the nurturer-owner, the one who raises what he possesses from stage to stage, the title al-Fātiḥah set over the worlds. To command worship in the name of the *rabb* is to ground obligation in received care: you are not addressed by a stranger with force but by the party who developed you from what you were. The relative clause then widens the nurture across generations — *Who created you and those before you* — so that the addressee inherits the claim along with the lineage. No one is self-originated and no one's ancestors were; the chain of makers everyone appeals to terminates in one Maker, and the verse simply walks the reader up his own family tree to the point where gratitude has an address. The prohibition that will close verse 22 — no equals — is already prepared here: a Lord who created your fathers cannot have partners invented by sons.

**Laʿalla: A “Perhaps” That Functions as a Promise**

The purpose clause — *laʿallakum tattaqūn*, so that you may become mindful — uses one of the Qur'an's characteristic particles, *laʿalla*, which classical grammarians gloss as expectation: perhaps, perchance, with the hope that. The particle raised a theological question the commentaries address directly: hope implies uncertainty, and uncertainty implies a heart in suspense — states unfitting to God. Two reconciliations dominate. One holds that God's "perhaps" is equivalent to a promise, since nothing can stand in its way; the expectation is certain from the divine side. The other attaches the hope to the subject of the sentence rather than to God: *you* are the ones for whom the outcome stands open, and the particle describes your position, not His. The second reading has the better grammar and the richer effect. The command genuinely hands the worshipper something to become: taqwā is offered as a real possibility that the act of worship genuinely produces, and the openness in the particle is the space where human freedom lives inside a divine sentence.

**Taqwā as the Destination, Not the Prerequisite**

The purpose clause performs a quiet reversal of the sūrah's opening. In verse 2 the Book is guidance *for* the God-conscious — taqwā appeared as the condition of receiving. Here worship is commanded *so that* taqwā may result — the same quality now appears as the fruit of practice. The two positions are not contradictory but sequential, and between them lies the sūrah's moral psychology: a minimum of guarding brings a person to the prayer, and the prayer manufactures the guarding it required. Taqwā is thus both door and destination, and the circle is how growth works — each turn through worship deepens the mindfulness that enabled the worship. This is also why the command is plural and general: taqwā is not reserved to a spiritual elite who arrived before the address; it is held out to humanity as an achievable state, produced by an available act. The God-conscious of verse 2 were described as if finished; verse 21 opens the workshop where they are made.

**Why the Call Comes After the Parables**

The placement of this verse is an argument in itself. Immediately before it stand the two most claustrophobic images in the sūrah's opening — an extinguished fire and a concussion of sky — and immediately after it comes the furniture of the world: earth as resting place, sky as canopy, rain as provision. The sequence moves from the fate of counterfeit light to the summons toward the real, and the summons is issued with the evidence assembled in the next breath. Diagnosis preceded prescription, so that the prescription arrives to an audience that has just seen what untreated looks like; and evidence follows the command, so that the command is not bare authority but authority that shows its work. The whole opening of al-Baqarah now reads as one designed movement: five verses of portrait, thirteen of exposure, two parables of night — and then the lights come on and the entire species is addressed by name, with the first word it is given being the first word of every prophet's message.
