## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:53

> And ˹remember˺ when We gave Moses the Scripture—the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺ that perhaps you would be ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Nouns, One Gift**

The verse records the recital's central donation: *wa-idh ātaynā Mūsa al-kitāba wa-l-furqān* — and when We gave Moses the Book and the Criterion. The two nouns stand joined by *wa*, and the exegetes have read the join three ways, each with its support. The first reading takes them as one gift under two names — the Book, which is the Criterion, the Torah itself functioning as the divider between truth and falsehood; the translation's appositive rendering ("the Scripture—the standard") follows this reading. The second takes them as two gifts: the Book, and separately the furqān understood as the concrete acts of separation that accompanied it — the parting of the sea, the rescue, the distinctions performed before witnesses, the deliverance as the Credential of the Law. The third reads furqān as the clear proofs and the victory, the evidences by which the Book was authenticated. The three readings converge on a single claim whichever way the grammar falls: the giving at Sinai was not the delivery of text alone. A book arrives with its divider — the power to separate — and a divider arrives with its book — the standard by which the separations are made. What Moses brought down from the forty nights was, in every reading, a machine for telling things apart.

**Furqān: The Word That Divides**

The noun comes from *f-r-q*, to split, part, separate — the same root as the recital's own verb two verses back: *We parted the sea*, *faraqnā*. The word-family connection is the Qur'an's theology of revelation in miniature. The sea was split with water; the furqān splits with meaning — a criterion that divides truth from falsehood, lawful from unlawful, the clean case from the corrupt, the way from the wandering. Arabic names the divider by its action: something is *furqān* not because of its material but because of what it does to a confused situation — it makes two things out of one muddle. The Qur'an generalizes the term accordingly. Sūrah 25 takes its title from the opening declaration that the Furqān was sent down to the servant — the Qur'an naming itself by the function this verse assigned to the Torah. The day of Badr is named in Sūrah al-Anfāl as the day of discrimination, *yawm al-furqān* (8:41) — the battle where the community's case was separated from its enemies'. And ʿUmar's honorific al-Fārūq — the one who divides — descends from the same root's moral sense. Across all these filings the word keeps its Sinai meaning: the gift that makes distinctions is the gift that makes a people, because a community is exactly a body that can tell its cases apart. The people of verse 51 — who could not, for forty nights, distinguish a lowing idol from the God of the sea — are the diagnostic case for why the furqān came.

**The Same Gift Filed for Two Brothers**

Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ records the donation with Aaron joined to the address: Moses and Aaron were given the Criterion, the light, and the reminder — the same threefold gift expanded (21:48). The joining matters for this verse's argument. The furqān was given to a *prophetic pair*, which is to say to a community's leadership as an instrument of government — the criterion is what deputies hold when the principal is at the mountain. Verse 51's crisis was precisely a failure of held distinction in the deputy's interval: the people had the deputy but not yet the book, and the calf's lowing divided nothing. The giving of verse 53 answers that gap in the recital's economy — after the interval's failure and its pardon, the permanent divider is installed, so the next absence will be met not by a man alone but by a standard the man and the people hold jointly. The al-Anbiyāʾ filing adds the gift's third name, the *dhikr* — the reminder — and the addition completes the machine: a criterion divides cases, a light shows them, a reminder keeps both from being forgotten between applications. The Torah at Sinai is thus described with the same triple vocabulary the Qur'an will use of itself, and the description is the sūrah's quiet claim of continuity: what Madina's scholars carry and what Madina's Prophet ﷺ recites are filings of one gift.

**The Purpose Clause: For Guidance**

The verse ends with the recital's standing particle: *laʿallakum tahtadūn* — that perhaps you would be guided. The purpose clause does three pieces of work. It states the gift's end: the Book and Criterion exist for guidance, not for possession — a furqān held and not applied has missed its purpose clause as surely as a favor held and not acknowledged missed verse 52's. It universalizes the addressee: the plural *you* covers the wilderness generation and every inheritor of the gift, which is why the sūrah can file the same donation to the descendants directly (2:87) and why al-Baqarah's own opening describes itself by the identical function — a book, no doubt in it, guidance for the God-conscious (2:2). And it subordinates the gift to the sūrah's running argument with the scholars: guidance is the Book's purpose, and verses 41–42 charged exactly the men whose profession was that purpose — selling its signs, mixing its truth, concealing what they read. A criterion in the hands of concealers produces the precise inversion the recital documents: the machine for telling things apart operated to tell them apart wrongly, distinctions manufactured rather than found. The purpose clause is the verse's built-in audit of every subsequent holder, and it is filed here, at the giving, so that no later custodian can claim the function was added by critics.

**The Book After the Pardon**

The verse's placement in the recital is itself an argument. The order runs: rescue (49–50), failure (51), pardon (52), gift (53). The Torah is given *after* the calf and *after* the ʿafw — the law arrives to a pardoned people, not to a clean one. The sequence forecloses two readings of revelation at once. Against legalism: the Book is not the condition of the pardon but its consequence — mercy came first, unearned, deleting the greatest sin on record, and the standard was installed in the cleared space. Against antinomianism: the pardon was not the end of the matter either — the pardoned were immediately handed the divider, because a wiped ledger that receives no standard will be written on again by whatever calves are moulded next. Law-after-mercy is the Qur'an's whole ordering of the covenant, and it is also the ordering of this sūrah's own legislation: al-Baqarah will spend two hundred verses detailing command and prohibition to a community already assured, at its head, that the guidance is mercy (2:64's renewals, 2:160's openings, 2:173's necessities). Verse 53 files the principle in history before the sūrah applies it in law: the standard is given to forgiven people precisely because forgiven people are the only ones who can hold a standard without either worshipping it or selling it.

**The Furqān's Last Filing**

The gift of verse 53 is the recital's pivot from memory to institution. Everything before it happened *to* the people — delivered, rescued, pardoned; the furqān is what is placed *in* their hands, and the recital's remaining movements will test the hand: the quarrels over commanded things, the refusals at the city's gate, the thirst at the rock, and finally the great charge of the following verses — the killing of prophets and the selling of signs, which are crimes of the divider's custodians, possible only where a furqān is held. The verse therefore reads forward as the enabling fact of every later accusation and backward as the answer to verse 51: the interval's failure was met with a permanent instrument, the absence's crisis with a present standard. And the instrument's history does not end in this sūrah or this community. The Qur'an's self-naming as Furqān, the Prophet's ﷺ title for the revelation he received, the sūrah's placement of this gift in its covenant unit — all file the last installment of one donation: the divider given at Sinai, renewed in every prophet's hand, and recited now in Arabic to a new custodian community whose whole commission, in verse 43's row of bowers and 3:110's charge, is to hold it and apply it. Verse 53 is where the recital hands the reader the tool the recital itself was made with — and the audit it installs runs on every holder until the Day of verse 48, when the divisions made with it are read back, case by case, before the eyes that watched.
