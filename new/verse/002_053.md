## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:53

> And ˹remember˺ when We gave Moses the Scripture—the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺ that perhaps you would be ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Things Given Together, a Book and a Criterion**

The sentence names two objects in one gift: the Scripture and the standard of judgment. The Arabic for the second is *furqān*, from a root meaning to separate or distinguish, and it describes that by which truth and falsehood are told apart. The Book uses the same word as a name for the Qur'an itself, which is why this verse is read as a statement about the function of revelation generally and not only about what Moses received.

The pairing tells the reader what a revealed book is for. It is not presented as an archive of information about God but as an instrument for making distinctions; the same verse states the purpose as guidance for the people who received it. The Book's self-descriptions use the same vocabulary, and the sūrah's second verse had already described its own contents as a guidance for a particular kind of reader.

**What the Book Says About This Gift Elsewhere**

Other passages fill out the content of the gift. One states that Moses and his brother received a light and a reminder (21:48). Another records the granting of the Scripture with its purpose attached: *“And We gave Moses the Scripture and made it a guide for the Children of Israel”* (17:2). A third describes the same revelation as completing the favour on those who do good, detailing everything, and serving as a guide and a mercy (6:154).

The most detailed statement of the Scripture's role appears in the sūrah of the Table, in the context of the judgments its judges are to make: *“Indeed, We revealed the Torah, containing guidance and light, by which the prophets, who submitted themselves to Allah, made judgments for Jews. So too did the rabbis and scholars judge according to Allah's Book, with which they were entrusted and of which they were made keepers. So do not fear the people; fear Me! Nor trade my revelations for a fleeting gain.”* (5:44). That verse names guidance and light, it names the office of those who hold the book, and it repeats the warning against selling revelation that the sūrah has already given its audience twice.

**Furqān: an Attribute or a Document**

Commentators discussed whether the standard named in this verse is a second book or a description of the first one. The sūrah of the Prophets mentions the gift as something granted to Moses and Aaron together, and the term there is joined to light and remembrance, which suggests a quality rather than a separate volume. Elsewhere the Qur'an itself is called the standard, and the Book also describes wisdom as given alongside scripture.

The readings converge on the same function. Whether the standard is a distinct document or the discriminating power of the revelation in the hands of a prophet, the effect is that what is given enables a person to tell one thing from another. The Book's practice supports this: the verses that follow in this sūrah will continue to separate the conduct that deserves blame from the conduct that deserves praise, and the reader is being invited to perform the same operation on himself.

**"That Perhaps You Would Be Guided"**

The stated purpose of the gift is guidance, and the construction attaches a hopeful outcome to it. The same purpose is attached to the revelation throughout the Book, and the sūrah's opening sentence describes the Book as guidance for the mindful. What the verse adds here is the historical claim that the guidance given to Israel came with the same intention and in the same form.

That continuity is the point of the whole passage. The Book has been reciting the favours granted to this community, and it now names the greatest of them: the arrival of a text with the means to judge by it. Anyone holding a book of this kind is in a position to recognise what comes next, and the argument of the address rests on that position being taken seriously.

**The Closing Item on the List of Favours**

The verse completes the recitation of gifts that began with the deliverance from Pharaoh. Rescue, crossing, appointment, pardon, and then the Scripture: the sequence ends with the instrument rather than with the escape, which places the community's guidance above its safety in the order of importance. The Book's way of ranking the two is consistent, and it treats a people's possession of a criterion as the greater of the two endowments.

The passage then continues into the account of later failures, and the reader who has followed the list understands what those failures cost. They were not committed in the dark. A community provided with a text that distinguishes right from wrong, and with a history of pardon after failure, had every means to act differently. The sūrah has now supplied all the material its later reproaches will assume.

**Scripture Named Last in the List of Gifts**

The recitation of favours ends with the arrival of the book rather than with the escape from slavery, and the order is deliberate. In the Book's scale of value, a community's possession of a criterion outranks its delivery from danger, because the first governs what the community does with the second. A rescued people without a text has been given a life; a rescued people with a text has been given a life and a way to live it.

That is the ground the sūrah builds for the requests that follow. The audience addressed in Madinah holds a scripture of this kind, and the Book's argument is that a text of that sort comes with the obligation to test what claims to confirm it. The list of gifts closes here, and everything the passage does after this point rests on the community having exactly what it has been told it received.
