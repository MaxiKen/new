## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:38

> We said, “Descend all of you! Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows it, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Descent Widens the Address**

The command to descend was given in verse 36; here it is repeated — *ihbiṭū min-hā jamīʿan*, descend from it, all of you — and the repetition marks a shift of register from event to constitution. The first descent expelled a family and an enemy from a garden; the second installs a species on an earth under stated terms. The plural *all of you* carries the widening: the exegetes read the address as reaching beyond Adam and his wife to their progeny — every child of the couple is a party to the descent — and in some readings Iblīs and his progeny are inside the same plural, the two camps dispatched together to one theater. The effect on the reader is immediate and personal. This is not a story being watched but a decree being served: the "you" of the descent includes whoever recites the verse, already resident on the earth, already inside the enmity, and now handed — in the same breath as the exile — the terms of residence.

**Idhā: When, Not If**

The guidance clause is built on a particle that does quiet theological work. *Fa-immā yaʾtiyannakum minnī hudan* — then *when* guidance comes to you from Me — the construction combines the conditional *in* with the emphatic *mā* and the heavy *nūn*, and the commentators read the combination as certainty wearing the grammar of contingency: guidance's arrival is not in question; its timing is left open. The preposition *minnī* — from Me — fixes the source, foreclosing every candidate substitute: no guidance arrives from the earth you descend to, from the enemy stationed there, or from the self that slipped once already. The parallel in Ṭā Hā states the outcome's symmetry: *"Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray ˹in this life˺ nor suffer ˹in the next˺"* (20:123). Guidance, in the charter of the descent, is a delivery service with a named sender and guaranteed dispatch — the earth is never left without it, and every generation lives inside some radius of a prophet, a book, or a remainder of either.

**Two Guarantees: No Fear, No Grief**

The promise attached to following is the Qur'an's standing formula of security: *fa-lā khawfun ʿalayhim wa-lā hum yaḥzanūn* — no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve. The commentators' analysis of the pair gives it its full psychology: *khawf* concerns what has not happened — the future and its threats; *ḥuzn* concerns what has — the past and its losses. The two affections between them occupy the whole of mortal time, and the formula's double negation empties both. The guided soul is described as inhabiting an unafraid present: not protected from events, but released from the two taxes the mind levies on events — dread in advance and sorrow behind. The same formula recurs as the description of the Garden's people throughout the Book — *"there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve"* (2:62) — which means the promise of this verse is not postponed to Paradise: the state it names begins in the following, here, and completes there. The descent took humanity out of a garden of provision; the charter offers a garden of the heart, portable across the enmity, to whoever follows the delivery.

**The Condition Is Following, Not Perfection**

The clause that unlocks the guarantee is small: *fa-man tabiʿa hudāya* — whoever *follows* My guidance. The verb is one of motion behind a leader, and it is doing deliberate work. The condition of safety is not sinlessness — impossible for children of the man who slipped one verse earlier and was forgiven — but following: staying behind the guidance, keeping it in front, returning to it when the enmity pulls the other way. The charter is realistic about its subjects because its drafter has just demonstrated the remedy for their failures: verse 37's received words stand behind verse 38's *man tabiʿa*, so that following includes repentance as one of its stations. The sentence's two halves also divide the covenant cleanly: from Me comes the guidance; from you comes the following. Nothing else is asked — no guarantee of results, no pledge of spotless performance. The whole religion of the descent is compressed into a source and a posture: what Allah sends, and whether the creature walks behind it.

**Four Acts Containing All Things**

The tradition preserves an expansion of this charter reported from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī: when Adam descended, Allah revealed to him that four acts contain all things, for him and his children. One is for Me — worship Me and associate nothing with Me. One is for you — the reward of what you do. One is between you and Me — you supplicate, and I respond. One is between you and your fellow humans — treat them as you love to be treated. The fourfold scheme is a complete constitution of the descent, and its correspondence to the verse is exact: worship answers the guidance's first demand; personal reward answers the following's guarantee; supplication-and-response names the living link between the descended and the Sender; and the golden rule legislates the social floor of a theater occupied by enemies. Al-Rāzī's observation on the report draws its consequence: prophecy and revelation begin with the earthly history of humanity — the religion of Adam's children is not a later addition to the descent but part of its equipment, dispatched with them.

**One Sentence, Three Standing Audiences**

The charter's grammar — whoever, all of you, when guidance comes — names no community, and the anonymity is the instrument. The sūrah's first audience hears it three ways at once. The hypocrites described in verses 8–20 are inside it: guidance came to them from Allah; they mocked it; the guarantee passed them by not because it was withdrawn but because the following was refused. The Children of Israel about to be addressed at length are inside it: guidance came to them repeatedly, in books and prophets, and the coming verses will audit their following. And the community receiving the sūrah in Madina is inside it: this very recitation is one of the deliveries the charter promised. The verse thus functions as the hinge of the sūrah's first movement — the Adamic cycle closes with terms valid for every later cycle, and the long case file of Israel that begins at verse 40 is read, from its first line, as one chapter in the charter's enforcement. The next verse states the charter's other clause — the one the followers will never need to hear about themselves, and everyone else is warned by.
