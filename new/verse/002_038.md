## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:38

> We said, “Descend all of you! Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows it, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Command, With Something Attached**

The command to descend is repeated here, and the repetition carries an addition. The first time the verse concluded with a dwelling, provision and a term; this time it concludes with a promise about guidance that has not yet arrived. The sūrah is showing the reader what the descent means: not only a change of address but the beginning of an arrangement in which revelation will keep arriving.

The addressees are named in the plural again, and the commentators took the group to include Adam, his wife, and their descendants, or at least to stand for humanity as a whole. That reading is supported by what follows, since the promise about guidance is not spoken to two individuals in a garden; it is addressed to everyone who will live on the earth.

**Guidance Announced Before It Is Given**

The sentence promises a future arrival: when guidance comes to you from Me. Nothing is said about what form it will take, when it will come or through whom, and the reader of the sūrah knows what the original hearers could not, since the Book in his hands is the thing being promised. The Qur'an treats its own descent as the fulfilment of this sentence, and it describes itself in the terms used here: the sūrah's second verse calls the Book a guide for the mindful (2:2), and another passage states the same purpose in one line: *“O humanity! Indeed, there has come to you a warning from your Lord, a cure for what is in the hearts, a guide, and a mercy for the believers.”* (10:57).

That sequence is how the Book presents the history of revelation. Guidance is promised first and delivered later, and each delivery is announced as the fulfilment of an earlier undertaking. The promise in this verse is general enough to cover every messenger who came after Adam, which is precisely the reading the Book's own history requires.

**"Whoever Follows It"**

The condition attached to the promise is not hearing, believing or admiring; it is following. The Arabic verb describes walking behind someone along a road, and it is the same verb the sūrah uses at its opening when it says the Book is a guide for the mindful who follow what has come to them. Following in the Qur'an is a physical metaphor for a deliberate life, and the Book consistently ranks it above the possession of information.

The verse therefore places the entire promise on a single act that any person can perform, and it leaves nothing in the sentence that depends on lineage, intelligence or status. Adam's descendants are addressed as individuals who will each have to decide whether to walk. The Book's later addresses keep the same condition in the same words, and the sūrah will use them again when it turns to the community of Madinah.

**No Fear and No Grief**

The promise attached to following is stated in a formula that recurs throughout the Qur'an. Fear looks forward to what may come; grief looks back at what has been lost. The Book removes both for the person who follows the guidance, and it does so in the same order every time, as in the verse that pairs the promise with the service of God: *“There will certainly be no fear for the close servants of Allah, nor will they grieve.”* (10:62).

The pairing is deliberately comprehensive. A human being's distress is usually either anxiety about the future or regret about the past, and the promise in this verse addresses both without suggesting that the follower's circumstances will be easy. What is removed is not hardship but the two emotions that make hardship unbearable, and the Book offers this as the reward of following rather than as a consequence of comfort.

**A Report About What Adam Was Taught on Earth**

Several reports describe instruction given to Adam after he came down. One account transmitted from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī says that God revealed to him that four things contain everything for him and his children: what belongs to God, which is worship without association; what belongs to the human being, which is the reward of his deeds; what lies between them, which is supplication and the answer to it; and what lies between him and other people, which is treating them as he wishes to be treated.

The reports are not of the highest grade and the sūrah does not depend on them, but they show how the earliest readers understood this verse. The promise of guidance was heard as the beginning of a whole teaching, and the four-part summary describes the same structure the sūrah itself follows: obligations toward God, obligations toward oneself, prayer, and conduct toward others.

**The Future Left Open in the Text**

The verse ends without naming the guidance, describing the messengers, or stating when the promise will be fulfilled. What it gives the reader is a condition and a result: guidance will arrive, and the person who follows it will be safe. The sūrah moves directly from this sentence to the fate of those who do the opposite, and the two destinies are placed side by side before the Book turns to the community it will address for the next eighty verses.

That transition is worth noticing for what it says about the Book's method. Before any law is given, before the history of Israel is recounted, and before the first command of the detailed teaching arrives, the reader is told what the two roads lead to. The Qur'an does not begin with legislation; it begins with a description of where each of the two possible responses ends.
