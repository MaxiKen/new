## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:50

> Say, “If I am astray, the loss is only mine. And if I am guided, it is ˹only˺ because of what my Lord reveals to me. He is indeed All-Hearing, Ever Near.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Concession No Opponent Expected**

The Prophet is instructed to entertain, out loud, the possibility that he is wrong: *in ḍalaltu fa-innamā aḍillu ʿalā nafsī* — if I have gone astray, I go astray only against myself. Nothing in the preceding forty-nine verses prepares for this. The chapter has argued, challenged, threatened and testified, and it now hands its opponents a sentence they could not have extracted by pressure. The concession is not a weakening of the claim. It is a statement about where the risk lies, and it functions as the final answer to the accusation of motive that the sūrah has been dismantling since verse 43. A man who acknowledges that his error would cost him everything and gain him nothing has removed the last reason to suspect him of an angle.

**The Asymmetry Between the Two Halves**

The sentence has two conditional branches and they are not built the same way. If he is astray, the loss falls on himself — a closed circuit, no one else implicated. If he is guided, the credit goes elsewhere: *fa-bimā yūḥī ilayya rabbī*, it is by what my Lord reveals to me. He takes full ownership of the downside and none of the upside. This asymmetry is the verse's argument, and it is the opposite of how a man defending a position normally speaks. The ordinary pattern claims credit for what is right and distributes blame for what is wrong. Reversing it is either an extraordinary rhetorical calculation or an accurate description of how the speaker understands his own situation, and the sūrah has spent the preceding verses removing the grounds for the first explanation.

**Guidance Described as Received, Not Achieved**

The second branch contains a claim about the nature of guidance that the Qur'an makes repeatedly. It does not say "if I am guided, it is because I reasoned well" or "because I sought sincerely." It says the guidance consists in what is revealed to him — *yūḥī*, in the present tense, an ongoing supply rather than a single past delivery. This connects to the sūrah's treatment of knowledge at the other end of the chapter, where those who recognised the revelation as truth were described as people who had been **given** knowledge rather than people who had acquired it (34:6). The same passive stance appears in both places. On this account the difference between a guided man and a straying one is not primarily a difference in effort, which removes the last trace of self-congratulation from the Prophet's position.

**Why the Admission Is Safe to Make**

There is a hard-headed logic under the apparent vulnerability of this verse, and the Meccan audience would have felt it. Their charge was that he had gone astray and was dragging others with him. The first half of the sentence addresses only the first part of that and concedes it hypothetically; but the concession has a consequence they may not have wanted. If his error would harm only himself, then their opposition is not a public service, and the urgency of their campaign against him loses its justification. The Qur'an states the underlying principle in general terms elsewhere: *"Whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good. And whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss"* (17:15). The sūrah applied it to both parties earlier, when neither side was to be questioned about the other's conduct (34:25). Here the Prophet applies it to himself first.

**Two Names, and One of Them Occurs Only Here**

The closing pair is *Samīʿun Qarīb* — All-Hearing, Ever Near. The first name is common in the Qur'an. The second, *Qarīb*, is used of God in several places, but searching this translation of the whole text shows that the two are joined in this combination at this verse alone. The pairing is exact for what precedes it. A man who has just said that his guidance depends entirely on what his Lord reveals is a man whose whole position rests on a line of communication, and the two names describe the two properties that line must have: someone listening, and someone close enough for the listening to matter. Hearing without nearness would be surveillance. Nearness without hearing would be presence without response.

**A Sentence That Models the Procedure It Recommends**

Four verses earlier the opponents were told to separate from the crowd and think honestly about the question in front of them (34:46). This verse shows what honest thinking sounds like when the speaker applies it to himself. He states both branches of the question, assigns the consequences of each without flinching, and declines to claim anything he cannot support. Nothing in the sentence is defensive. A man who can hold his own possible error in view while continuing to act is demonstrating exactly the disposition he asked his opponents to adopt, and the demonstration is more persuasive than the instruction was. This is also why the verse belongs in the closing sequence rather than earlier. It answers the last remaining version of the charge of madness: a man whose reason had been taken by the jinn could not construct a conditional about his own condition.

**What Nearness Means in the Qur'an's Vocabulary**

Al-Baqarah gives the fullest statement of this name's practical content, answering a question the Companions had asked: *"When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one's prayer when they call upon Me"* (2:186). The nearness there is defined by responsiveness rather than by location, and it is offered as the reason petition works at all. Placing the same name at the end of this verse ties the Prophet's situation to every listener's. He has just described himself as a man entirely dependent on a channel he does not control — and the name that closes the sentence is the one the Qur'an elsewhere attaches to ordinary people's prayers. The sūrah now leaves the Prophet's speech and turns, for its last four verses, to the moment when the party that ignored all this discovers what it cost.
