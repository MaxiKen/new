## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:17

> We ˹also˺ gave them clear commandments regarding ˹their˺ faith. But it was not until knowledge came to them that they differed out of mutual envy. Surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Commands Clear Enough to Remove the Excuse**

The clarity of what the Israelites received is stated before their division is reported, and the order is the charge. The Qur'an says the commands were clear proofs, the kind of indication that leaves a reasonable person in no doubt about what is being asked. A community in that position cannot plead that the instructions were obscure or that the messengers were vague, and the verse does not allow the reader to construct that defence on their behalf. The same standard will later be applied to the readers of this sūrah, who have been given signs in the sky and in their own bodies. The verse is therefore not only a report about a people in the past. It establishes what the Book means by a clear revelation, so that every case in which that revelation is disputed can be judged against the standard set here.

**Division That Arrives After the Knowledge**

The sequence in the verse is precise: the difference between them did not arise until knowledge had come. That ordering is repeated across the Qur'an whenever the subject of schism appears, and the repetitions carry the same diagnosis — humanity was one community, then they disputed after clear proofs had reached them (2:213); those who were given the Scripture did not dispute out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them (3:19); a people should not behave like those who split into sects and differed after clear proofs had come (3:105); and the people of the Book did not become divided until the clear proof came to them (98:4). The pattern is consistent enough to be treated as the Qur'an's considered explanation of religious conflict: disagreement is not a symptom of too little information but of something else operating after the information has arrived.

**Envy as the Engine of the Split**

That something else is named in this verse. The Arabic describes a mutual transgression or jealousy among the disputants, and the exegetes understand it as rivalry over position and leadership. The logic is uncomfortable because it is familiar: a body of knowledge creates status for those who hold it, and the holders can begin to compete for that status as though the knowledge existed to serve them. When that happens, the shared text no longer determines the outcome; the contest does. Al-Rāzī, quoted in the classical commentaries, observes that knowledge itself does not lead people to differ, but the manner in which some seek advancement through it produces the envy, and the envy produces the split. The verse does not say the disputants were ignorant. It says they were envious, and the two conditions are compatible.

**Two Ways of Reading the Clear Commandments**

Commentators differ about whose proofs are in view. Read in the light of the preceding verse, the plain clear indications belong to Moses and the Israelites, since the whole passage concerns the gifts given to them. Read in the light of the following verse, some take the phrase as a reference to the signs given to the Prophet Muhammad, including the indications that he would migrate to Madinah and find help there, with the disputes then belonging to the Jews of Madinah who rejected his prophethood out of jealousy. The first reading holds the passage together as a single account of one people's history; the second explains why the sūrah turns to the Prophet immediately afterwards. The first is the stronger fit for the verse as written, since its pronouns stay with the Israelites throughout, and the second is a legitimate application of the pattern rather than a rendering of the words.

**The Judge Who Does Not Need the Disputants' Consent**

The verse ends by stating that God will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences. That is the answer to a division which no human court could settle, since the parties each claimed the same authority and each rejected the other's evidence. The Book offers the same comfort and the same warning elsewhere, saying that God will decide between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute (2:113; 32:25). The parties to a religious dispute are not told that their disagreement will be tidied away in this world, or that one side will be vindicated in a manner visible to all. They are told that the case is already docketed, that the judge saw everything, and that no argument made from envy will survive the hearing.

**The Pattern That Watches the Reader**

This verse is not only an indictment of a people who lived long ago. It describes a mechanism that operates wherever a community inherits a revelation: knowledge arrives, authority attaches to those who hold it, and the holders begin to compete in ways the knowledge does not authorise. The sūrah's first readers had just been reminded of what was given to Israel, and the next verse turns to the Prophet with a command about the road he is to follow. The warning is placed between the two so that no reader can dismiss it as ancient history. A community that splits over the meaning of a clear text, in a dispute driven by standing rather than evidence, has reproduced the very pattern the verse condemns, and can expect the same hearing.

**Why the Book Repeats This Diagnosis**

The same finding about division after knowledge is stated in half a dozen chapters, which means the Book is teaching a recognisable mechanism rather than recording one dispute. The repetition also crosses the boundaries between communities: the Israelites who quarrelled over the Torah, the Christians who quarrelled over the Gospel, and the Muslims warned not to become like those who split into sects after clear proofs had come to them (3:105). Each community receives a clear text, sets up teachers of it, and then begins to fight over who will speak for it. The pattern is so reliable that the Qur'an can describe a group who split apart centuries after their own scripture arrived and expect the reader to recognise the sequence instantly. The verse holds that continuity up to the sūrah's audience as a mirror rather than an accusation directed only at people long dead.
