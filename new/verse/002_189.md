## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:189

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. Say, “They are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage.” Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors. Rather, righteousness is to be mindful ˹of Allah˺. So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors, and be mindful of Allah so you may be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question and the Redirect**

The verse follows the sūrah's familiar question-form — they ask you — and its answer performs a characteristic move: it declines the question as posed and answers the need behind it. The reports about what was actually being asked vary — some describe curiosity about the moon's waxing and waning, its physical mechanics; others describe a practical inquiry about the new moons' function — but the answer is the same in every case: *they are means of timing for people, and for the pilgrimage*. The redirect is the verse's method. Speculation about the celestial mechanism is not forbidden, but it is not what the community needs legislated; what it needs is to know what the moons are *for*, and the verse supplies the for: the religious calendar. The same pattern governs the sūrah's other question-verses — the inquiry about spending answered with the list of recipients (2:215), the inquiry about the sacred month answered with the hierarchy of sins (2:217) — revelation answering in the register of practice rather than theory, turning every question toward the life it must govern. The moon asked about as an object is answered as an instrument, and the instrument's user is the worshiper.

**The Moon That Is Seen, Not the Moon That Is**

The verse's word — *ahillah*, the new moons, plural of hilāl — denotes specifically the visible crescent, and the distinction is astronomical as well as legal. The Islamic month does not begin at the astronomical new moon, when sun and moon share the sky and the lunar disc is dark and invisible; it begins with the first sighting of the waxing crescent after sunset. The mechanics the tradition describes are exact: the moon's orbit is inclined to the ecliptic, so the dark conjunction-moon passes north or south of the sun unseen; as it moves eastward in its orbit its angular distance from the sun grows, and when that separation and the timing after sunset suffice, the thin crescent becomes visible above the western horizon — the hilāl whose sighting opens the month. The law attaches itself to this visibility deliberately: a calendar keyed to an invisible conjunction would require calculation invisible to the herdsman and the Bedouin, while a calendar keyed to the seen crescent can be kept by any community with clear western sky. The means-of-timing the verse names is thus a timing *for people* — the phrase is emphatic — an instrument calibrated to human eyes rather than to abstract astronomy, which is why the sighting, and not the computation, remained the law's trigger across the tradition.

**Timing and Pilgrimage: The Calendar Built**

The two functions the verse names — timing for people, and the pilgrimage — cover the religious year's architecture. The lunar months order everything the community must date: Ramadan's fast begins with a crescent and ends with a crescent (2:185's completing of the number); the hajj belongs to months appointed (2:197), whose first is another sighting; the ʿīds, the zakāh-year, the ʿiddah of widows and divorced women (2:234), the months of the truce and the sacred months — all run on the hilāl's arithmetic. The pilgrimage is named separately because it is the calendar's summit: the one act of worship whose timing is collective and continental, drawing every community's sighting into a single convergence at a single place in a single month. By making the moons the means of both daily timing and the great gathering, the verse installs the sky itself as the community's clock and its compass-measure of seasons — a calendar not legislated by priests nor adjusted by rulers but posted monthly on the western horizon, where the herdsman reads it as reliably as the astronomer. The answer to the questioners is thus larger than the question: the moon they wondered at is the instrument by which the entire worship-life of the ummah keeps its appointments with God.

**The Back Doors of Yathrib**

The verse's second half turns abruptly from sky to architecture: it is not righteousness to enter houses by their backs. The reports behind the clause describe an actual practice. Among the pre-Islamic Arabs — the Anṣār of Yathrib are named in the accounts — a returning traveler or a person in a state of ritual consecration would not enter his house by its door; he would scale the back wall or have a breach made, treating the ordinary entrance as forbidden in his condition. Other reports connect the practice to the ihram of pilgrimage or to returning from the festival rites; al-Qurṭubī collects the variants and their common shape: a scruple that had attached itself to doorways, a superstition of thresholds. The verse's verdict is total: the practice is not righteousness — not a higher piety but a misplaced one, an act of devotion performed in the wrong element. The clause then states the true substance: righteousness is the taqwā itself, the mindful reverence of God — and from that substance the practical instruction follows almost as corollary: enter the houses by their doors. The superstition is not merely tolerated-with-disapproval but routed by a definition: the back wall is not holiness, the door is, because the door is where God put the entrance.

**The Spiritual Reading: Houses and Their Doors**

The Sufi exegetes, with Ibn ʿAjība at their head here, read the clause as the allegory its concreteness invites: everything in the religious life is a house with an appointed door, and the house is not entered by its back. Ibn ʿAjība's schema names the three houses and their nine doors — the house of the sharīʿah, entered by repentance, obedience, and reverence; the house of the ṭarīqah, by sincerity, purification, and virtuous character; the house of the ḥaqīqah, by concentration, contemplation, and gnosis. The principle generalizes beyond the schema: the knowledge that comes without its discipline of study, the spiritual station sought without its obedience, the intimacy with God attempted without the prayer He appointed — each is the back wall, and each fails for the same reason the Yathrib climber's scruple failed: it approaches a true destination by an unappointed route, mistaking the difficulty of the approach for its legitimacy. The verse's genius is to make the smallest of superstitions — how one enters one's own courtyard — carry the whole epistemology of worship: there are doors, the doors are known, the doors are God's, and the climb over the wall is not extra piety but refused piety, the piety that will not accept being directed.

**Enter by the Doors, and Be Mindful**

The closing commands gather both halves of the verse into one ethic. *Enter the houses by their doors* is the general principle extracted from the specific practice — appointed means for appointed ends, the lawful route however plain against the dramatic shortcut. *And be mindful of God, that you may prosper* attaches the principle to the sūrah's governing virtue and its governing promise: taqwā as the way, success — falāḥ — as the end, the same pairing the sūrah's opening gave its very first description of the guided (2:5). The two halves of the verse now read as one lesson in two registers. The moon: a wonder asked about as spectacle, answered as instrument — the celestial body's meaning is its function in the worshiper's calendar. The door: a scruple performed as extra-devotion, answered as error — the threshold's meaning is its appointment by the Lawgiver. In both cases the verse disciplines religious imagination toward obedience: not what seems marvelous (the moon's phases, the wall's ascent) but what has been made a means (the timing, the door). The questioners who came asking about the sky are sent home with a calendar and a doorway, and the verse's last word promises that the homecoming — through the door, by the months, in mindfulness — is where the prospering lives.
