## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:190

> Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night there are signs for people of reason.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Signs Named**

The verse states that in the creation of the heavens and the earth and in the variation of night and day there are signs for those who possess understanding. The sentence opens the sūrah's closing movement, which is built on the observation of the world.

The Book invites this observation in several passages. One lists the creation of the heavens and the earth, the alternation of day and night, the ships that sail the sea, the rain, the creatures scattered throughout the earth, the shifting winds, and the clouds, and concludes that in all of it there are signs for people of understanding. Another passage states that in the alternation of night and day and in what God has created there are signs for those who are mindful. A third states that in the creation of the heavens and the earth there are signs for the believers.

**The Word Behind Creation**

The word rendered as creation can refer both to the act by which the heavens and the earth were brought into being and to the character of what was created. The commentators noted that the verse can therefore direct attention both to the making and to the nature of what was made.

The distinction widens the scope of the observation. A person reflecting on the sentence can consider the act of creation and the power it displays, or he can consider the arrangement of the created world and the order it exhibits. Both lines of reflection lead to the same conclusion, and the verse leaves room for either.

**The Possessors of Understanding**

The phrase describing those for whom the signs exist is one of the Book's terms for people who use their minds in the right way. The Book uses it in passages that commend reflection, and it uses it in passages that commend the fear of God and the acceptance of admonition.

The term is not a description of intelligence in the ordinary sense. The Book's usage connects it with the ability to draw the right conclusion from what a person observes, and the passages that use the term consistently place it close to the qualities of humility and attention. What the verse requires is not cleverness but the willingness to see what is in front of one.

**The Prophet's Practice**

The commentaries record that the Prophet recited the closing verses of this sūrah every night, and they report the occasion on which the passage was first revealed. He is said to have stood in prayer, and when Bilāl came to call the congregation and found him weeping, Bilāl asked why he wept when God had forgiven him all his sins, past and future. The Prophet answered by asking whether he should not be a grateful servant, and he named this verse as the reason for his tears.

The report adds that he said that the one who recites the verse and does not reflect on it is in a bad way. The tradition has preserved the account because it shows how the passage was received by the person who first recited it, and the community's teachers have used it ever since.


The report places reflection alongside recitation, and the community's practice has followed the distinction. The Book's verses are to be read and to be considered, and the passage before the reader is one of the places where the second activity is explicitly invited.

The verse's own structure supports the practice. It names specific features of the world and states that they are signs, which amounts to an instruction to look at them and to draw the conclusion. The invitation is repeated in the sūrah's following verses, which describe the people of understanding as those who remember God in every posture and reflect on the creation.

**The Book's Appeal to Observation**

The Book's arguments frequently begin from what its readers can see. It points to the rain, the growth of crops, the cycles of the sky, the variety of creatures, and the order of the seasons, and it asks its audience to draw conclusions about the one who arranged them.

The appeal is not to a special kind of knowledge but to the ordinary experience of anyone who lives in the world. The Book's reasoning in such passages is that the ordinary facts of existence are enough to establish what it is asking people to accept, and the verse before the reader is a compact statement of that argument for a community that had seen unusual events.

**What the Signs Lead To**

The next verses describe the people of understanding in action: they remember God while standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and they reflect on the creation and ask what it all means. The explanation of what they conclude and what they ask for continues through the close of the sūrah.

The passage therefore moves from the observation of the world to the conduct of those who observe it, and the sūrah ends with their prayer. The community is given a portrait of the quality that the Book most values in its people, and the portrait begins with the sentence in this verse and continues through the prayer that ends the sūrah.

**The Function of the Closing Movement**

The sūrah ends with this reflection and the prayer that follows it, and the placement gives the whole sūrah its conclusion. The community has been taken through the battles, the failures, the warnings about property and speech, and the arguments with its opponents, and it is left contemplating the heavens and the earth.

The close of the passage returns to the note on which the sūrah opened, where the Book described itself as a guidance for those who guard themselves against God. The last verses turn that guidance back toward the world it explains, and the community that reaches the end of the sūrah is asked to look up.
