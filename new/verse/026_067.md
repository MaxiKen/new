## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:67

> Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sea Crossing Becomes Evidence for Every Witness**

The verse calls the entire episode a sign. The sign includes the command to leave, the opening of the sea, the rescue of Moses and his companions, and the drowning of the pursuing host. No single detail carries the whole argument by itself. Together the events reveal God's power, His care for the oppressed, and the danger of treating tyranny as permanent.

A sign points beyond the event to its Lord. The sea crossing is not preserved as an adventure story whose value lies in suspense. It tells the audience that creation obeys God, that prophecy is confirmed through divine action, and that judgment can reach a ruler who seems beyond challenge. The event is historical in the narrative and theological in its meaning.

**Most Still Refuse the Meaning of What They See**

The statement that most would not believe follows a miracle of extraordinary public force. That sequence rejects the idea that every person believes once evidence becomes dramatic enough. The event can be clear while the response remains resistant. People may explain it away, refuse its demand, or admire it without surrendering their authority to God.

Most is not all. The wording leaves room for those who respond rightly, including the believers who followed Moses. It also warns against judging truth by majority. A large number of people can witness a sign and still choose the interpretation that protects habit or power. The sign's strength is not measured by the percentage of people willing to submit to it.

**A Sign Requires Moral Interpretation**

The event does not merely display power. It establishes a moral pattern. Pharaoh's oppression is judged, the Israelites' obedience is honored, and the Lord's promise is fulfilled. To believe in the sign is therefore to accept more than the physical possibility of a sea dividing. It is to recognize that God's authority should change how power, freedom, and fear are understood.

A person may accept that something extraordinary happened while resisting those implications. Such acceptance is incomplete because it leaves the heart's allegiance unchanged. The verse uses belief in a fuller sense: recognition joined to trust and obedience. The majority's refusal shows that the obstacle is not only what the eyes can see but what the will is willing to follow.

**The Refrain Places Moses Among Earlier Messengers**

The same statement about a sign and the majority's refusal will recur after the stories told in this sūrah. Its placement here closes the account of Moses and prepares the reader for Abraham and the other prophets. Each story will have different people, sins, and signs, but the pattern remains: God sends a clear proof, the messenger delivers it, and many resist.

This repetition comforts the Prophet who hears the chapter. Rejection after a sign does not mean that his mission has failed. Moses faced an army and a ruler after receiving unmistakable support, yet the audience still contained those who refused. The refrain connects the Prophet's present sorrow with the long history of revelation and gives him a measure larger than immediate acceptance.

**The Sign Judges Both the Past and the Present**

The sea crossing is a sign for Pharaoh's generation because it exposes the end of their rule. It is also a sign for later audiences who might imagine that oppression is too strong to challenge. The event judges the past by showing what Pharaoh's power led to, and it judges the present by asking whether people have learned to distrust the same claims of absolute rule.

Historical memory becomes guidance when the reader asks what the event means now. It does not license every person to assign themselves the role of Moses or to announce judgment on opponents. It teaches trust in God, resistance to false lordship, and patience when visible power appears overwhelming. The sign is carried forward through disciplined understanding.

**Majority Refusal Cannot Erase Deliverance**

The phrase most would not believe might sound like a shadow over the miracle. It is not. The Israelites were delivered whether or not Pharaoh's wider society accepted the lesson. God's action does not depend on universal applause. The sign remains true, and the rescued community remains rescued.

That distinction protects believers from measuring divine favor by public approval. A truthful community may be small, and a false interpretation may be common. The task is not to manufacture a majority through pressure. It is to respond faithfully to the sign and to present its meaning without surrendering it to the crowd's preference.

**The Event Ends One Story and Opens the Next**

The refrain gives closure without making Moses' story an isolated miracle. The sea has shown the Lord's might and mercy, and the next account will present a different setting in which idolatry must be confronted. The repeated line will allow the reader to compare the forms of resistance without confusing the stories.

For this verse, the central claim is firm. The sea crossing is evidence of divine authority and deliverance, but evidence does not cancel human refusal. The majority's disbelief becomes part of the sign's warning: even a people confronted with rescue and judgment must still choose whether to believe.

The sign remains a sign whether the audience accepts it or not. Its force is measured by what it reveals, not by the number of people willing to admit what it reveals.

That distinction keeps faith from becoming a contest for popularity. The believer seeks clear recognition and patient testimony, not forced agreement. A sign remains clear even when a crowd chooses not to follow it. Refusal changes the refuser, not the evidence.
