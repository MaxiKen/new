## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:16

> who pray, “Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive our sins and protect us from the torment of the Fire.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Clause That Tells Us Who They Are**

The previous verse ends with the phrase *All-Seeing of His servants*, and this verse picks the sentence up without a new beginning: *who pray*. The Arabic relative construction ties the people described here to the servants just named, so the reader learns that the mindful ones are identified by what they say to God when nobody else is listening.

That is the whole function of the verse in the sūrah's argument. A list of virtues could have been given; instead a prayer is given, because the Book's interest in this passage is not the catalogue of good behaviour but the relationship that produces it. The people who inherit the gardens are described in the act of addressing their Lord.

**The Qur'an Defines Groups by Their Supplication**

Naming a community by its prayer is a habit of this Book, and the habit is instructive. The people of understanding are introduced by what they ask for: *“˹They are˺ those who remember Allah while standing, sitting, and lying on their sides... ˹and pray˺, ‘Our Lord! You have not created ˹all of˺ this without purpose. Glory be to You! Protect us from the torment of the Fire.’”* (3:191). The balanced worshipper is the one who asks: *“Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire.”* (2:201). Even the angels who carry the Throne are quoted in prayer, interceding for the believers before God: *“Our Lord! You encompass everything in ˹Your˺ mercy and knowledge. So forgive those who repent and follow Your Way, and protect them from the torment of the Hellfire.”* (40:7).

The same pattern appears here. A group is named by the words it repeats, which means the reader can test his own identity by what he says when he is alone and afraid.

**Faith Stated First, Forgiveness Asked Second**

The order inside the prayer is not accidental. The suppliant begins with a declaration — *we have believed* — and only then makes a request. Faith is presented as the ground on which a person can ask at all; there is no bargaining in the sentence, no accounting of merits, no argument that the request is deserved.

This is the Qur'an's ordinary grammar of prayer. The servant states his position before God honestly, both what he has carried out and what he has failed at, and the appeal rests on who God is rather than on what the servant has done. In the prayer taught at the end of the sūrah of the Cow, the same structure holds together a long list of petitions: each one is grounded in a recognition of the servant's weakness rather than in a claim of strength.

**The Two Requests and the Shape of Human Danger**

The petition itself has two halves, and the halves correspond to the direction a person's danger comes from. What is past needs *forgiveness* — the record already written, which no effort can now change. What is coming needs *protection from the Fire* — the outcome that has not yet been decided and can still be altered by how a person lives.

Holding both in one sentence is a small education. A person who thinks only about the future becomes anxious and self-absorbed; a person who thinks only about the past becomes a defendant who has already lost. The prayer asks for both directions at once, and it does so in a single breath, which is how the Qur'an usually teaches a balanced religious psychology.

**"Shield Us": the Verb and Its Root**

The word rendered "protect us" is *qinā*, from the same root as *taqwā*, the mindfulness that verse 15 made the condition of entry. A shield is a thing that stands between a person and a blow; the verb means to place such a covering over someone.

The connection between the two verses is therefore deliberate at the level of the lexicon. The mindful person is one who has been shielded, and his mindfulness is the trace of that protection in his conduct. The same root appears in the description of the Helpers of Madinah, who were praised for giving preference to others over themselves: *“And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful.”* (59:9). The danger a person most needs shielding from turns out to be inside him, and 64:16 repeats the promise in the same words. Forgiveness covers what is done; the shield stands between a man and what he might still do.

**What "Forgive" Means in Arabic**

The verb *ighfir* comes from a root whose first sense is to cover, to conceal, to draw a screen over something. A *mighfar* is the covering a fighter wears over his head. The Arabic word for forgiveness is therefore not primarily about forgetting; it is about covering, and the covering has a purpose, since what is covered is no longer exposed to the punishment it deserved.

This is why the Book joins forgiveness with mercy so often and so tightly. The sin is not annulled as a fact — the record of a person's life is not falsified — it is covered, and the covering is an act of the one who has the right to expose. The suppliant in this verse knows what he is asking. He is not requesting a technicality or a lenient reinterpretation of his acts. He is asking the owner of the record to draw something over it.

**A Prayer Said in the Plural**

Both requests are phrased in the first person plural: *forgive us*, *protect us*. The verse is describing a group of people who speak as one even when they are alone, and the grammar repeats the opening — *our Lord* — which is the same address the well-grounded used in verse 8 and again in verse 9.

The plural does more than express solidarity. It puts the person praying inside a body of people who are all making the same admission, which removes the two temptations that attend private confession: despair at one's own case, and pride at being better than others. A servant who asks for the community is in no position to hold a rank above its members, and a servant who includes himself in the request has already admitted that the request is necessary. The community of the mindful is constituted in this verse by the words they say together.
