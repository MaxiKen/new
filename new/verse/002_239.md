## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:239

> If you are in danger, pray on foot or while riding. But when you are safe, ˹take time to˺ remember Allah for teaching you what you did not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prayer Under Fear**

The guarding-command of the previous verse is immediately qualified by its hardest case: what happens to the prayer when keeping it may cost a life? The verse answers with the condition first — *if you are in danger*, khawf, which the commentators extend across battle and persecution alike — and then with the concession: pray on foot or riding. The two modes name a prayer released from its normal architecture: no fixed stance, no stationary row, no required orientation. The exegetes detail what the release covers. The praying person faces the qiblah if he can and any direction if he cannot; the bow and prostration become the head's inclinations, nodded at the corresponding degrees; and according to some readings the prayer itself may be shortened to a single cycle where the normal count is two, three, or four. Everything the standing devotion of the previous verse commanded — the stillness, the silence, the direction — is suspended by the single word khawf, and the suspension is itself commanded.

**The Concession as Command**

The verse's grammar deserves notice: the fear-prayer is not merely permitted but prescribed — *pray on foot or while riding* is an imperative, not a license. The jurists read the implication plainly: in conditions of real danger, insisting on the full form is not piety but disobedience, since the verse has redefined the prayer's obligation to fit the danger's shape. This is the same logic the sūrah applies to fasting on a journey and in illness (2:184–185) and to the pilgrimage interrupted (2:196) — the worship is not suspended by hardship but re-specified by it, and the re-specification comes from God rather than from the worshipper's discretion. The fear-prayer thus protects two things at once: the life that would be spent praying standing, and the prayer that would be lost if it could only be performed standing. Between the two protections stands the verse's quiet claim — that the religion's forms exist for its purposes, and that God Himself redraws the forms when the purposes require it.

**The Detailed Legislation Elsewhere**

This verse gives the fear-prayer its principle; the sūrah of Women gives it its choreography. The commentators cross-reference the detailed legislation directly: *When you travel through the land, it is permissible for you to shorten the prayer—˹especially˺ if you fear an attack by the disbelievers* (4:101), and then the congregation's procedure — one group praying with the imām while armed, the second group standing guard behind them, then the rotation, the weapons carried through the prostrations, the rain-and-illness exemptions (4:102). The two passages divide the legislation the way the code often divides it: the Baqarah verse names the concession's spirit — pray however the danger allows — and the Women verses build its order, a communal prayer conducted in shifts with a guard posted mid-worship. Together they describe a community that prays under attack without ceasing to be either an army or a congregation, the verse here supplying the permission that makes that double existence lawful.

**When You Are Safe**

The concession's boundary is its second half: *But when you are safe, ˹take time to˺ remember Allah*. The dhikr commanded at safety's return has been read in two registers that the verse's wording holds together. The first is the prayer restored — the full standing, the complete cycles, the direction recovered — resumed the moment the danger lifts, since the concession was coextensive with the fear and not a moment longer. The second is remembrance in its own right: the gratitude of the survivor, the recollection that the safety itself is a gift and that the shortened prayer was prayed under the shadow of a death that did not arrive. The exegetes' gloss *take time to* — the Qur'anic verb carrying the sense of full, unhurried performance — marks the contrast the verse is built on: the fear-prayer is contracted, and the safety-prayer is expanded; what the danger compressed, the peace is to stretch back out, in the prayer's length and in the rememberer's attention.

**For Teaching You What You Did Not Know**

The verse's closing phrase attaches the remembering to its occasion: *for teaching you what you did not know*. The fear-prayer legislation is itself the thing taught — a form of worship the community could not have derived, since every instinct of reverence argues for standing still, facing the sanctuary, and completing the count, and the revelation alone could authorize the nod, the saddle, and the single cycle. The commentators read the phrase more widely too: the whole structure of the prayer — its times, its counts, its concessions — is knowledge received, not knowledge constructed, and the safety's remembrance is to include the remembrance of that reception. The clause also carries a reproach in its grammar, gentle and total: what you did not know names a former ignorance without naming a fault, since ignorance of a legislation not yet revealed is not culpable — but it makes the knower's position one of permanent debt. The safe man remembering God remembers, among everything else, that his prayer's very shape in danger was a lesson.

**The Religion of the Two States**

Taken with the verse before it, this pair describes the prayer across the two states of a life — guarded normalcy and threatened extremity — and refuses to let either state dissolve the obligation. In normalcy the prayer is watched, timed, and stood through in devotion; in extremity it is transformed, mounted, and nodded — but in neither state is it dropped. The fear-prayer is the code's clearest statement that worship survives every condition the human being can be placed in: there is no circumstance under which the connection is legislated out of existence, only circumstances under which its form changes. The jurists drew from this verse and its Women-sūrah companion a whole chapter of fiqh — the ṣalāt al-khawf — debated across the schools in its exact rotations and counts; and the Sufi commentators drew something further, reading khawf as the soul's standing before God in awe, the state in which the prayer's forms contract to their essence and the heart rides toward God on foot or mounted, by whatever means the way allows. Both readings keep the verse's center: the prayer bends; it does not break.
