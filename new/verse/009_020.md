## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:20

> Those who have believed, emigrated, and strived in the cause of Allah with their wealth and their lives are greater in rank in the sight of Allah. It is they who will triumph.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Movements Define the Higher Rank**

Verse 20 names the people who stand above the merely respected religious office described in the previous verse. They believe, emigrate, and strive in Allah's cause with wealth and lives. The sequence describes a movement from conviction to separation and then to expenditure. Faith gives the direction. Emigration shows that the direction has become more important than inherited comfort. Striving shows that the new commitment continues to cost something after the move.

The verse does not present emigration as a romantic journey. For the first Muslims, leaving Makkah could mean abandoning property, family protection, trade, and the status provided by one's clan. The Muhājirūn went to Madinah because faith required a new social home. The action created a community in which the believer's loyalty was no longer governed by the old tribal order. Their movement was therefore geographical and moral at once.

**Belief Comes Before Expenditure**

The order begins with those who have believed. Wealth and life do not create the rank by themselves. A person can give money for prestige, fight for power, or migrate for profit. The verse connects the visible sacrifices to belief in Allah, so the sacrifice has a truthful purpose. This protects striving from becoming another form of ambition.

Belief also gives the believer a source of endurance. If the cause were only political, loss would eventually make the effort seem pointless. Faith in Allah and the Last Day allows a person to value obedience even when the worldly result is uncertain. That does not make planning unnecessary. It changes what counts as success. The believer can accept a difficult outcome without calling obedience a failure, because Allah's judgment extends beyond the immediate report.

**Emigration Is a Transfer of Loyalty**

The word emigrate recalls the Hijrah, which became the beginning of the Islamic calendar. The calendar's starting point is not a coronation or a victory. It is a community leaving danger and building a life around faith. This choice explains why emigration appears between belief and striving. It is the bridge by which inward conviction becomes a new public arrangement.

In later circumstances, the moral meaning of emigration can appear in different forms. A believer may leave a place where worship is impossible, abandon a corrupt livelihood, move away from an abusive influence, or change a social circle that keeps him from Allah. These applications should not erase the historical meaning of the Hijrah, but they show the principle's reach. Emigration means refusing to let inherited surroundings have the final authority over one's relation to Allah.

**Wealth and Life Are Both Spent**

The verse joins wealth and lives, preventing a narrow idea of sacrifice. Some people are willing to give money but not time, courage, or reputation. Others speak bravely while refusing material responsibility. The Qur'anic description includes both forms of expenditure. Wealth supports the community's needs; the self is placed at risk in obedience. Neither is a substitute for the other when both are possible and required.

The pairing also creates a safeguard against spiritual boasting. A person cannot claim that his financial gift excuses him from moral courage, nor that his courage excuses him from generosity. The believers at the center of the sūrah made their resources serve their convictions. That pattern continues in the Qur'an's description of the good trade: faith and striving with wealth and selves are mentioned together (61:10–11).

**Rank Is Assigned by Allah**

The verse says these people are greater in rank in the sight of Allah. Their rank is not established by public applause, lineage, or the office they hold. Allah sees the cost, intention, and faithfulness of the act. The phrase in His sight also reminds the believer that the highest status may be hidden in this life. Someone unknown to a crowd may be honored with Allah because he gave what the crowd never saw.

The Qur'an elsewhere speaks of degrees for those who strive with wealth and selves (4:95–96). Degrees do not mean that every believer without the same opportunity is worthless. They mean that Allah recognizes differences in effort, sacrifice, and circumstance. A person who gives much from much may not be equal to one who gives little from little, and a person who bears a costly duty may not be equal to one who chooses the easy path. The scale is exact and merciful because it knows what each person could actually give.

**Triumph Is More Than Winning a Campaign**

The verse calls this group triumphant. The root of triumph can carry the sense of escaping danger and arriving at a good outcome. In the Qur'anic frame, triumph may include victory in this world, but it cannot be confined to it. Some who emigrate lose property. Some who strive are killed. Yet the verse calls them successful because their lives have reached the purpose for which they were made.

This is not a way to rename every defeat as victory. It is a way to place worldly outcomes in a larger account. A believer can grieve loss and still know that obedience was not wasted. Conversely, a person can win public power and still be a loser if the victory required selling revelation. The preceding verses rejected religious status without faith; verse 20 identifies the triumph of those who gave faith a costly form.

**The Rank Remains Open Through Faithful Effort**

For modern believers, the verse is not a command to imitate the first Muhājirūn in circumstances that no longer exist. It is an invitation to ask what must be left, carried, and spent for Allah now. Perhaps it is a dishonest income, a group that mocks obedience, a comfort that keeps the believer from serving, or a fear of losing approval. The answer will differ, but the pattern remains: believe, move toward the community of faith, and spend what Allah has entrusted.

The verse places the final verdict with Allah. That keeps sacrifice from becoming a claim of superiority over others. The one who strives is still a servant hoping for acceptance. He does not despise the person whose duty is different, and he does not turn his own effort into a new idol. Triumph belongs to those whose wealth and lives have been directed to Allah and whose rank is received from Him alone.
