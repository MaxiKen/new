## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:110

> You are the best community ever raised for humanity—you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah. Had the People of the Book believed, it would have been better for them. Some of them are faithful, but most are rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Best Community Raised for Humanity**

The verse addresses the believers as the best community brought forth for humanity, and then names the practices that define the description: encouraging what is good, forbidding what is wrong, and believing in God. The rank is stated first and the content of it immediately after.

Some commentators read the verb as describing a destiny settled in advance, so that the community is told what it has been written to be and is then expected to live up to it; others read it as describing what the community becomes by virtue of the practices named. The Book's grammar permits both, since the verb form can carry the sense of the present and of something established. The same title appears in the sūrah of the Cow, where the community is described as made upright so that it may bear witness over humanity (2:143).

**Why the Description Comes Before the Title's Content**

The order of the sentence is worth noting. The practices are named after the title, and the mention of belief comes last, after the enjoining and forbidding.

Ibn Kathīr drew the conclusion that the community is called best because of what it does for others, which is the concern of encouraging good and forbidding wrong, and that the placement of action before belief reflects that emphasis. The three items are not ranked against each other; they are the description of a community that is defined by what it does as much as by what it holds. The sūrah has just commanded the same practices to be carried out by a group within the community, and this verse states the result as the community's identity.

**The Witness the Community Carries**

The role attached to the community in the parallel passage is that of a witness over humanity, with the messenger a witness over it (2:143). That role explains why the description of the community's practices follows its title, since a witness must be visible and its conduct is part of what it witnesses to.

A community that believes in God, calls others to good, and intervenes against wrong is presenting an argument in the form of a life rather than in the form of a claim. The Book's treatment of the community's function toward other peoples matches its treatment of the Book itself, which it describes as a guardian over the earlier scriptures. The role is not a privilege of rank but a responsibility of representation.

**The Conditional Statement About the People of the Book**

The verse turns to the People of the Book and states that if they had believed, it would have been better for them. The sentence is a statement about benefit, not about blame: belief is described as something that would have improved their condition.

What the Book means by belief in this context is the acceptance of the message that had come to the Prophet, since the verse is speaking about a community that already held a scripture. The Book's arguments with them in this sūrah have been precisely about the acknowledgement of that message, and the sentence here states the consequence in the mildest terms available, which gives the following clause its force.

**"Some of Them Are Faithful, Most Are Rebellious"**

The word rendered as rebellious is used in the Book for those who abandon an obligation or step outside a limit, and the commentators noted that it is contrasted here with belief rather than with disbelief.

Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī both read the term as describing the failure of the People of the Book to be faithful to their own scriptures, which, in the Muslim reading, point toward the final message. The choice of the word is exact: it names a breaking of trust with what a person already holds rather than a simple rejection of what he has not held. The verse's description is therefore of a community that had something to be faithful to, and the accusation is that most of it was not.

**Believers Named Among Them**

The verse's statement that some among them are faithful is confirmed elsewhere in the sūrah. A later passage says that they are not all alike and describes an upright group among the People of the Book who recite God's revelations through the night and prostrate themselves (3:113). The same sūrah's closing passages speak of those among them who believe in God, in what has been revealed to the believers, and in what was revealed to them (3:199).

The Book's own categories therefore prevent its readers from converting a judgment about majority conduct into a verdict on a whole people, and the tradition records that individuals of the People of the Book accepted the message and were counted among the community's most respected members.

**How the Passage Continues**

The verses that follow draw out the consequences of the distinction the verse has made. They state what the believers may expect from the opposition they face, and then describe the upright among the People of the Book in more detail.

The passage's method is the one the sūrah has used throughout this stretch of argument. A general statement about a people is qualified by a distinction among them, and the distinction is then developed with specific descriptions of belief and conduct. The reader is left with a picture of two groups within a single community, and with the reminder that his own community's title is attached to practices he has just been commanded to carry out.

**The Readings of the Verb and What They Change**

The verb in the sentence can be read as either present or past, and al-Rāzī set out what follows from each. Read in the past, the words describe something already written: the community was named the best of peoples in the Preserved Tablet, the register the Book mentions at the end of the sūrah of the Constellations, *“˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet”* (85:22), and the sentence then calls on the community to live up to what it was named. Read as addressed to the people of the community once their lives are over, the words describe them in the garden, looking back at what they were in the world.

Read as a verb of becoming, the title is not a fixed inheritance at all. On that reading a community becomes the best of peoples by doing the work the sentence names, and the two activities it lists are the means by which the description is earned. The three readings differ about when the sentence is spoken and agree about the condition attached to it. None of them turns the title into a possession, and each leaves the community's conduct in its own hands.
