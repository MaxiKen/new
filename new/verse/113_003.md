## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:3

> and from the evil of the night when it grows dark,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ghāsiq and Waqaba Describe a Darkness That Arrives**

Two words carry the verse, and both were argued over. *Ghāsiq* is the active participle of a root meaning to be dark or black; as a noun it names the dark one, the darkening thing. *Waqaba* means to enter, to sink in, to spread over — of the sun, to set; of night, to come down and cover.

The phrase *al-ghāsiq idhā waqab* therefore describes a darkness in the act of arriving: the darkening one when it settles. Most commentators take the referent to be the night, and the evil sought against is the evil of the hours when it has fully closed in.

A second reading takes *waqaba* from a different sense of the same root — to grow cool — and understands the verse as the night when it turns cold. The grounds are lexical: the root is used of cold as well as of descent, and the night's cooling is as much a feature of it as its darkening. The dominant reading is stronger, because the participle *ghāsiq* is a word about darkness and not about temperature, and because the pairing of a dark thing with a darkening verb is the natural construction. The cooling reading survives as a legitimate minority gloss rather than as a rival interpretation.

**Why Night Is Named as a Source of Harm**

The verse is practical before it is anything else. Night is when harm arrives unseen: when the road cannot be read, when the intruder cannot be identified, when the animal in the brush is heard rather than watched. A person's capacity to guard himself falls as the light falls, and the sūrah marks the fact.

The Qur'an observes elsewhere that concealment is the condition of evil plotting. Those who oppose the messenger are described as agreeing by day and scheming when the meeting breaks up at night, and the text says that God records what they contrive in the dark. Evil that depends on being unseen depends on the night, and a prayer against the night's evil is a prayer against the operations that require it.

There is a second, less physical layer. Night is the time when the mind is least defended — when sleep approaches, when solitude is complete, when thought has no company to correct it. The tradition's nightly practice of reciting this chapter treats that vulnerability as real. The refuge asked for is not only from what happens outside the house but from what the hours do to a person inside it.

**The Moon Reading Rests on a Report From ʿĀʾisha**

A substantial body of commentary identifies *al-ghāsiq* with the moon. The evidence is a Prophetic statement, not a lexical inference. ʿĀʾisha reported: the Prophet ﷺ looked at the moon and said, "O ʿĀʾisha, seek refuge in Allah from the evil of this, for this is *al-ghāsiq idhā waqab*" — the darkening one when it darkens or sets. The report is in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3366, which al-Tirmidhī called *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ* and Dārussalām grades *ḥasan*.

That report settles a second referent for the word. It does not abolish the first. The moon is *ghāsiq* precisely when it sets and the night takes over, so the two readings describe successive moments of the same darkness: the night darkens, and within it the moon darkens.

The exegetical method here is worth noting, because it is the method the tradition uses throughout. Where a word in the Qur'an admits two referents and a report from the Prophet ﷺ attaches one of them to the word itself, both are kept. The report does not narrow the verse; it fills it out. A reciter who thinks of the setting moon and a reciter who thinks of the closing night are both inside the phrase.

**The Pleiades Reading Is the Weakest of the Three**

A minority identified *al-ghāsiq* with the Pleiades, *al-Thurayyā*, at their setting, on the ground that disease and pestilence were held to become prevalent in the season when that cluster declines.

The grounds are not textual. They rest on the medical and astronomical beliefs of the period, in which the risings and settings of particular stars were taken to govern seasons of sickness. Nothing in the verse requires the identification, no report supports it, and the word *ghāsiq* is an ordinary Arabic term for what darkens rather than a name for a constellation.

The reading is preserved in the commentaries because the tradition records minority glosses even when it does not adopt them, and because the phenomenon it appeals to was real: seasonal epidemics did cluster, and people did mark them by the stars. It is the clearest example in this chapter of an interpretation that tells the historian about the commentators rather than about the verse.

**The Sūrah Is Recited at the Hour It Names**

The practice attached to these verses places them in the dark. ʿĀʾisha reported that every night, when the Prophet ﷺ went to his bed, he joined his hands, breathed into them, recited into them this chapter, the next one, and *Say: He is Allah—One*, and then wiped over as much of his body as he could, beginning with the head, the face and the front of the body, doing that three times. That is Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī.

The other report puts the same chapters outdoors. Travelling between al-Juḥfa and al-Abwāʾ, when a wind and intense darkness came over the party, the Prophet ﷺ sought refuge reciting the two chapters and told ʿUqba ibn ʿĀmir to do the same, adding that no one has ever sought refuge with anything comparable to them (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1463).

In both cases the prayer is used at the moment its own third verse describes. The sūrah is not a text about nightfall so much as an instrument for it, and the tradition treats it that way — recited at bed, on a dark road, in wind, before sleep, at the two ends of the day.

**Three of the Four Evils Cannot Be Seen**

The list this verse belongs to has a shape. The night's darkness is a condition rather than an agent; the blowers on knots work out of sight; the envier's evil is inside him. Only the first is a natural phenomenon, and even it is named for what it hides.

That is the common feature. Each of the three specific harms operates where observation fails: in the dark, at a distance, in another person's heart. None of them can be watched for, none can be litigated, and none can be answered by force. A refuge is the only available response, which is why the sūrah is a refuge and not a set of instructions.

The first verse supplies the reason the refuge works. The Lord invoked there is the Lord who splits the night open every morning — the one who ends the condition under which all three harms operate. The prayer does not claim the darkness will be lifted at once; it claims that the darkness has a Lord.

**Night Is Also His, and That Is Why the Prayer Works**

Verse one names God as Lord of the daybreak and verse three asks for protection from the night. The two are the same governance seen from two ends, and the Qur'an says so directly: *“He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (6:96).

Night in that verse is not an invasion but an appointment — made for rest, measured, part of a design. The evil sought against in this chapter is not the night's existence but what happens inside it. The distinction keeps the prayer from becoming a complaint about creation.

The structure of the whole chapter follows from it. Every harm named is a harm that occurs within a created order God governs, and the refuge is taken in the governor. The following verse turns from the natural order to the human one.
