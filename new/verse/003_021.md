## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:21

> Indeed, those who deny Allah’s signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice—give them good news of a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Charge in Three Steps, Each Worse Than the Last**

The verse lists three acts and builds them into a single indictment. It begins with denial of God's signs, moves to the killing of prophets, and ends with the killing of people whose only distinguishing feature is that they stand up for justice. The sequence is not a list of unrelated crimes; it is a description of one temper as it advances.

The logic connecting the three is that each step makes the next possible. A person who has decided that the signs do not oblige him has removed the authority that would restrain him; a community that has killed the messengers who corrected it has established that correction is fatal; and what remains is a settled hostility to anyone, prophet or not, who says that something is wrong. The verse describes a religious establishment that has turned its own corruption into policy.

**Who Are "Those Who Enjoin Justice"?**

The middle of the verse concerns prophets, but the end concerns people who stand up for justice. The Arabic phrase describes those who command equity — a category that includes prophets and extends past them to ordinary men and women who insist on what is right in the presence of power.

The commentators found the point significant, and their evidence is prophetic. The Prophet is reported to have said that among the greatest forms of struggle is *a word of justice spoken before a tyrannical ruler* (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4344; Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2174, graded ḥasan by virtue of corroborating narrations). The Book itself provides a portrait of such a man at the edge of a city, running toward danger to support the messengers: a figure whose brief narrative ends with the reward of Paradise. The verse's indictment therefore covers more than the murder of prophets. It covers the murder of anyone who says that the powerful must be just.

**"Give Them Good News" — the Inversion of a Kind Word**

The command to announce a painful punishment uses the language of glad tidings, and the word is deliberately wrong. In the Book, *bushrā* is the announcement of good: a child to an old man, mercy to the righteous, the garden to the faithful. Here the verb is applied to the sentence of a court.

The same startling inversion appears elsewhere: *“Give good news of a painful punishment to hypocrites”* (4:138). The figure works because it refuses to soften the message at the last moment. A sentence delivered as news is spoken in the tone of a messenger reporting what has been decided, and the reader who flinches at the wording has understood the point the Book is making: the most terrible events in this verse are reported in the calmest grammar available.

**The Killing of the Prophets in the Record of Israel**

The Book presents the killing of messengers as a recurring feature of the history of the Children of Israel, and it does so without hedging. In the wilderness narrative, the punishment is traced directly to it: *“they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah’s signs and unjustly killing the prophets.”* (2:61). The charge is repeated in the account of the covenant broken and renewed: *“˹They were condemned˺ for breaking their covenant, rejecting Allah’s signs, killing the prophets unjustly”* (4:155).

The pattern is stated as normal rather than exceptional in 5:70: *“Whenever a messenger came to them with what they did not desire, they denied some and killed others.”* The verse above inherits that record and treats it as a habit that generates its own future, and the Book addresses the community that carried the habit in the present tense.

**What the Commentators Passed Down and What to Do With It**

Commentators relate that this verse was understood to refer to particular groups among the Children of Israel, and some transmit a report that forty-three prophets were killed in a single morning and that a company of men who stood up to denounce the killing were killed with them on the same day. The report is found in tafsīr works rather than in the sound collections, and its chains are not reliable.

What may be said with confidence is the pattern the Qur'an itself asserts, that persecution of the prophets ran through the generations, and that those who spoke for justice after them shared their fate. The verse gives the reader the pattern in one sentence, which is all that the argument requires.

[UNVERIFIED: the report that forty-three prophets were killed in one day, with the men who protested their killing, is preserved in tafsīr literature (Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) without a sound chain, so no ḥadīth collection or number is cited.]

**The Warning for a Community That Kills Its Conscience**

The verse closes with the punishment and leaves it there; the following verse will describe what happens to such people's deeds. Read on its own, the sentence sets out a rule that applies to any establishment in any period, religious or otherwise: the killing of the ones who correct you does not silence the judgement, it confirms it.

A community may be certain that it is defending God's religion while removing the people who keep insisting that God's religion requires something of it. The verse names that conduct, and it refuses to call it devotion. It calls it murder, and it attaches to it an announcement phrased as though the condemned were receiving a favour.

Two words in the sentence carry the weight: *unjustly*, which rules out the case of a true legal sentence carried out by a legitimate authority, and the present tense of the participles, which describes the conduct as an ongoing practice rather than a single historical crime. The verse is not reporting an atrocity for the record. It is describing a class of people whom the reader may meet in any generation: those who deny what God has shown, silence those who speak for Him, and have no doubt about their own standing while doing it.
