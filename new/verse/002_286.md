## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:286

> Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford. All good will be for its own benefit, and all evil will be to its own loss. ˹The believers pray,˺ “Our Lord! Do not punish us if we forget or make a mistake. Our Lord! Do not place a burden on us like the one you placed on those before us. Our Lord! Do not burden us with what we cannot bear. Pardon us, forgive us, and have mercy on us. You are our ˹only˺ Guardian. So grant us victory over the disbelieving people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Principle Stated**

The sentence opens with the statement that God does not require of any soul more than what it can bear, and the community's teachers read the clause as the answer to the distress that the previous verse had produced. The Book has described the account that reaches what a person conceals and discloses, and the sentence here names the limit within which the requirement is made.

The wording places the measure with the capacity of the person, so that the obligations of the law are presented as a burden that the servant is able to carry. The community's scholars read the principle with the passages that state the same rule, and they gathered the instances from the sūrah and from the rest of the Book.

The teachers drew from the clause the lesson that the mercy of God accompanies the instructions that the sūrah has set out, since the law of the dealings, the worship, and the household is given within the measure that the sentence names.

**The Earning and the Loss**

The sentence states that the good a person earns is for his own benefit and that the evil is to his own loss, and the community's teachers read the clause as the statement about the destination of every act. The Book has treated the account of the deeds throughout the sūrah, and the sentence here presents the outcome in the language of gain and loss.

The commentators explained the phrase as the statement that the soul carries the result of what it has done, so that no one bears the burden of another. The community's scholars gathered the passages on the subject, and they read the sentence as the ground of the account that the Book describes.

The wording also supplies the answer to the anxiety of the previous verse, since the sentence measures the requirement and states the ownership of the act together. The teachers used the pair of clauses when they taught the mercy and the justice of the law.

**The First Petition**

The sentence then records the words of the community in prayer, and the first request asks the Lord not to take the believers to task if they forget or make a mistake. The community's teachers read the petition as the statement about the faults that accompany the conduct of every person.

The commentators noted the two terms of the clause, since forgetfulness and error describe the failings that a person does not intend. The teachers of the community read the request with the reports about the forgiveness that the Book grants, and they used the material when they set out the subject.

The teachers also observed that the petition is placed in the mouth of the community by the Book itself, so that the words of the verse are taught to the believers rather than composed by them. The sentence in this place supplies the community with the manner in which it addresses its Lord.

**The Second Petition**

The second request asks that no burden be placed upon the community like the one that was placed upon those who came before, and the community's teachers read the clause as the reference to the severity of the earlier laws. The Book has treated the history of the earlier peoples throughout the sūrah, and the petition here turns that history into a request for the mercy of God.

The commentators explained the clause as the statement about the obligations that the earlier communities carried, and the teachers described the material that the Book has supplied about them. The jurists of the community read the petition as the recognition of what the earlier peoples met and the request to be spared the like of it.

The wording also connects the closing verses with the narratives of the sūrah, since the accounts of the earlier communities are gathered into the prayer of the believers. The passage's treatment of the history is thus completed by the supplication that the sentence places in the community's mouth.

**The Third Petition**

The third request asks that the community not be burdened with what it has not the strength to bear, and the community's teachers read the clause as the appeal to the measure that the sentence stated at its beginning. The petition returns to the principle of the capacity, so that the opening statement and the closing request belong to one another.

The commentators noted the connection, and they explained the arrangement as the statement of the confidence that the community places in the mercy of its Lord. Scholars of the community drew from the pair the lesson that the law and the petition are presented together.

The teachers also read the clause as the answer to the fear that the account of the previous verse had created, since the community is taught to ask for what it cannot bear to be lifted from it. The sentence supplies the words that the believers repeat.

**The Closing Words**

The sentence closes with the requests for pardon, forgiveness, and mercy, and with the declaration that God is the Master of the community and the petition for help against the disbelieving people. The community's teachers read the sequence as the statement of the progression by which the community addresses its Lord.

The scholars explained the three terms of the closing, and they described the requests as the covering of the fault, the removal of the record, and the provision of the kindness that the person requires. The community's preachers used the verse when they taught the words of the supplication.

The final clause places the petition for help against the disbelieving people at the close of the sūrah, and the teachers read the request within the circumstances in which the community lived. The Book's treatment of the conflict that the chapter has described is thus gathered into the prayer that ends it.

**The Standing of the Two Verses**

The reports of the community describe the standing of the final two verses, and the material states that the Prophet ﷺ described the recitation of the passage at night as sufficient for the person who performs it. Other reports describe the two verses as a gift that the community received, and the teachers used the accounts when they encouraged their recitation.

The scholars of the community also observed the five articles of the faith that the previous verse names, and they read the closing pair as the summary of the creed and the supplication that belongs with it. The material shows the two verses recited together in the practice of the community.

The sūrah's close therefore gathers the confession and the prayer, and the teachers of the community read the arrangement as the statement that the knowledge of God and the appeal to Him belong to one another. The verses were memorised and recited in the community's devotions from its earliest years.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the principle that no soul is required to bear more than it can, the outcome of the good and the evil for the person who performs them, the three petitions that the believers are taught to make, and the closing request for pardon, forgiveness, and mercy together with the petition for help. The community's teachers used the verse when they taught the mercy of the law and the words of the supplication, and the sūrah ends with the prayer of the believers, so that the chapter that began with the description of the Book and its guidance closes with the appeal of those who accepted it.
