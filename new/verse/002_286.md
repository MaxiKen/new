## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:286

> Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford. All good will be for its own benefit, and all evil will be to its own loss. ˹The believers pray,˺ "Our Lord! Do not punish us if we forget or make a mistake. Our Lord! Do not place a burden on us like the one you placed on those before us. Our Lord! Do not burden us with what we cannot bear. Pardon us, forgive us, and have mercy on us. You are our ˹only˺ Guardian. So grant us victory over the disbelieving people."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Capacity Principle**

The Book's final verse opens with the sentence the tradition quotes more often than almost any other: *Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford*. The principle has the Qur'an's standing formulation — la yukallifu Llāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā — and the commentators list its recurrences as a network: the nursing-provision legislation bound the father only within his capacity (2:233), the sūrah of the Cattle legislates full measure with the same clause (6:152), the sūrah of the Heights places it inside the Paradise-promise (7:42), and the sūrah of the Believers states it beside the record that speaks truth (23:62). Its position as the sūrah's closing legislation is the tradition's answer to the audit-verse's distress: the Companions wept at the heart's total registration (2:284), and the response revealed — these final verses — begins by bounding the obligation while leaving the knowledge total. God knows the heart's every content; God requires of the soul only its width. The distinction is the verse's whole mercy: the audit's range is infinite, the assignment's is capacity-shaped, and the community lives between the two — fully known, partially tasked.

**Earned and Perpetrated**

The verse's second sentence pairs two near-synonyms and sets them in opposite directions: *All good will be for its own benefit, and all evil will be to its own loss*. Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī mark the roots' kinship — kasabat, earned, and iktasabat, perpetrated, are from one stem, their difference contextual and deliberate — and gloss the pair as the ledger's two columns: what the soul earned is added to its merit, and what it perpetrated is what it is held to. The commentators connect the clause to the spending-section's own economics — whatever you spend is for your own good (2:272), whoever does good does it for his own soul (41:46) — and to the Day-return verse's payment-in-full (2:281): the soul's earnings travel with the soul, benefit and loss both deposited in the only account the return will open. The clause is also the capacity-principle's corollary: since the assignment never exceeds the width, everything assigned falls within the soul's power to earn — and everything earned, good or evil, returns as the soul's own. No transferred guilt, no borrowed merit, no burden carried from another's ledger: the verse states, in two clauses, the personal arithmetic the whole sūrah's legislation has assumed.

**The Three Do-Not-Petitions**

The verse's second half is the believers' prayer, and its first three petitions each open with the same call — Our Lord, Our Lord, Our Lord — a triple invocation the commentators read as the prayer's deliberate rhythm. The first: *Do not punish us if we forget or make a mistake*. Ibn Kathīr glosses its range as sins of omission — the canonical prayer forgotten until its window closes — and of commission, the wrong done in error's grip. The tradition's supporting ḥadīth is the verse's own commentary: God has absolved this community of the mistake, the forgetting, and what is done under compulsion — the three involuntary modes lifted from the ledger's punishment-column. The second petition: *Do not place a burden on us like the one You placed on those before us*. Al-Ṭabarī reads the burden as the covenant-conditions laid on earlier communities, and Ibn Kathīr states the tradition's consensus: Islam is, in much of its legislation, an alleviation and reduction of the ritual and legal obligations borne by the peoples before — the same sūrah's widow-legislation halving the mourning-year (2:234), its fear-prayer bending the ritual to the danger (2:239), its fasting legislating the night's full permission (2:187). The petition asks the alleviation's continuation: what was lightened, keep light. The third: *Do not burden us with what we cannot bear* — al-Rāzī reads it as the plea against commands and afflictions beyond the strength to uphold them, and the commentators note the petition's harmony with the verse's own opening clause: the soul tasked only within its width prays that the width be honored — the principle stated as doctrine and then begged as mercy, because the doctrine's keeper and the petitioner's needer are the same God.

**Pardon, Forgive, Have Mercy**

The prayer's fourth movement compresses three imperatives into one breath: *Pardon us, forgive us, and have mercy on us*. The commentators distinguish the three without separating them: the ʿafw, the effacement — the deed's trace removed; the maghfirah, the covering — the deed's exposure prevented; the raḥmah, the mercy — the good beyond both, given unasked. The sequence runs from the sin's cancellation to the sinner's dignification, and its placement after the three do-not-petitions completes the prayer's architecture: the negative requests (do not punish, do not burden, do not overwhelm) are answered by the positive three (efface, cover, mercify) — what the petitioner fears removed and what he hopes installed. The tradition preserves the prayer's reception in the ḥadīth the exegetes quote: after each petition of this verse — do not punish, do not burden, do not overwhelm, pardon, forgive, have mercy — God answers I have done so, or the Prophet is told through Gabriel: He has done so (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī). The report makes the verse, in the tradition's practice, a prayer already answered — recited not to move an unwilling God but to enter a granted one, and the reports of the Companions' recitation (Muʿādh ibn Jabal would say āmīn at the sūrah's completion, the exegetes note, as one says amen at the Fātiḥah's end) show the community treating the verse as the Fātiḥah's bookend: the prayer that opens the Book answered by the prayer that closes it.

**Our Guardian, Our Victory**

The prayer's final movement names its Addressee and asks its last ask: *You are our only Guardian — so grant us victory over the disbelieving people*. The mawlā-name gathers the verse's whole theology into one relation — the Master, Patron, Guardian, the One whose ownership (2:284), knowledge (2:283), capacity-principle (this verse), and answering (the ḥadīth) have just been recited — and the victory-request defines what the guardianship is asked to do in the world: naṣr over the disbelieving people. The commentators note the sūrah's ring-closure in the request: the Book opened with the disbelievers' sealed hearts and hearing (2:6–7), legislated two hundred verses of a community's formation, narrated its battles and its Bani Israil mirror, and ends with the formed community asking its Guardian for the victory the opening described as withheld from the sealed. The request is also the prayer's only petition aimed outward — the preceding six concerned the soul's ledger and the community's load — and its outward aim is the verse's final statement of what the capacity-principle produces: a community tasked within its width, forgiven its involuntary, lightened of the former burdens, does not ask for ease but for the contest's outcome. The sūrah's last word for the world is thus a battle-prayer, and its last word for the soul is the guardianship the battle-prayer leans on.

**The Two Verses from Beneath the Throne**

The tradition's verdict on this verse and its predecessor is the sūrah's own epilogue, and the ḥadīth-record states it in three reports the exegetes gather. The Prophet said of the two closing verses: whoever recites them in a night, they suffice him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 5009; also in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim) — the sufficiency read as protection, as the night's worship, as the household's covering. He said of their origin: I was given the two verses that close the sūrah of the Cow from a treasury beneath the Throne — the kanz taḥt al-ʿarsh report that places the sūrah's ending at the highest point of the revelation's geography, the same Throne āyat al-kursī (2:255) describes as encompassing all. And the Miʿrāj-tradition reports that at the Night Journey — the night the sūrah of the Night Journey opens (17:1) — the Prophet was given three things: the five daily prayers, the two closing verses of the Cow, and the forgiveness of his community's sins save the associating of partners. The three reports converge on the verse's function in Muslim practice: the sūrah that legislated the community's law ends in a gift carried down from the Throne and up from the Night Journey, recited every night by millions who may not have read the legislation it seals — the creed-verse and the prayer-verse, belief and capacity, the articles and the asking, sufficient for the night, sufficient, the tradition has always understood, for more: the whole Book folded into its last two sentences, the Cow's long road from guidance-without-doubt to victory-over-disbelief ending where every road the sūrah described ends — at the Guardian, in the prayer, before the return.
