## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:8

> **So We destroyed those who were far mightier than these ˹Meccans˺. The examples of ˹their˺ predecessors have ˹already˺ been related.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Consequence Follows the Mockery**

The verse opens with fa, "so", the particle of consequence. Verse 7 said that every prophet was mocked; this verse says what followed the mocking. The link is causal and immediate. God destroyed the mockers, and He destroyed them in spite of a fact the verse goes out of its way to state: they were stronger than the Quraysh. The word is baṭshan, from baṭasha, to seize violently or to strike with force. Ashaddu baṭshan means more violent in their grip, more formidable in attack. The earlier peoples were not weak nations who fell because they lacked the means to resist. They were the strong ones, and their strength did not save them. The verse thus removes the one consolation the Quraysh might have drawn from history. They might have thought that the destroyed peoples were destroyed because they were small or backward. The verse denies it. The destroyed peoples were greater than the Makkans in exactly the quality the Makkans prided themselves on, and it made no difference.

**Stronger, More Numerous, More Deeply Rooted**

The Qurʾān makes the same comparison in several places and each time adds a detail. In 40:82 the earlier peoples were more numerous, mightier in strength, and left firmer traces on the earth. In 50:36 they were stronger in grip and travelled through the lands, searching for a refuge from God's decree and finding none. In 30:9 they tilled the earth and built it up more than the later people ever did. The picture is of civilisations larger, richer, and more technically capable than seventh-century Makkah, a town of perhaps a few thousand living by trade in a valley without agriculture. ʿĀd built columns; Thamūd carved houses from rock; Pharaoh's Egypt raised monuments that still stood. The Quraysh had none of that. So the argument of the verse is from the greater to the lesser. If those who had power were removed when they mocked their prophets, what protection does a people without such power imagine it has? The exegetes state this plainly: the opponents of the Prophet ﷺ, weaker than the earlier generations, are wrong to think they can oppose his message and prevail.

**"These": The Quraysh Named by Pointing**

The comparison is with hāʾulāʾ, "these", and the demonstrative is pointed at the audience. The translation supplies "Meccans" to make the reference clear, but the Arabic simply gestures, as a speaker gestures at people standing in front of him. The effect is to bring the verse out of history and into the present. Verses 6 and 7 spoke of the ancients; this verse says "these" and looks at the men in the room. Al-Ṭabarī understands the pointing as directed at the Quraysh who were mocking the Prophet ﷺ, and the demonstrative carries a tone of diminishment. The mighty nations are named by their strength; the Quraysh are named by a gesture. The shift from third person to demonstrative also completes the movement that began in verse 5, where God asked whether the Reminder should be withdrawn from "you". The audience has been addressed as "you", then reminded of "them" who came before, and now the two are placed side by side with a pointing finger. The Quraysh are not being told about the past. They are being shown their reflection in it.

**Mathal al-Awwalīn: The Example Already Given**

The second sentence, wa-maḍā mathalu l-awwalīn, means the example of the ancients has passed, has already gone before. Mathal is a likeness, a pattern, a story told for the lesson in it. The exegetes read it in two ways. Ibn Kathīr allows that mathal here means the way of life and the condition of the earlier peoples: their manner of living and how it ended have already been described. The second reading takes mathal as an exemplary punishment, a fate that serves as a warning to those who come after. The sūrah itself uses the word in this second sense in verse 56, where the people of Pharaoh are made a precedent and an example for those of later times. The two senses converge. The story of the ancients is both the account of how they lived and the warning of how they ended, and the verb maḍā, "has passed", says the story is complete and on record. Nothing more needs to be added to it. The Quraysh had heard the accounts of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the rest in the Qurʾān already revealed and in their own traditions. The verse does not repeat the stories. It points at the shelf where they sit.

**Precedent as Argument**

The verse's method is legal as much as rhetorical. It establishes a precedent and applies it. The precedent is: peoples who mocked their prophets were destroyed, regardless of their strength. The application is: the Quraysh are mocking their prophet and are weaker than those peoples. The conclusion is left unstated because it is obvious, and an unstated conclusion presses harder than a stated one. The Qurʾān uses this form of argument throughout the Makkan period, and it explains why so much of the Makkan revelation is narrative. The stories of earlier nations are not decoration. They are the case law by which the present is judged, and 14:45 says as much: you dwelt in the dwellings of those who wronged themselves, and it was made clear to you how We dealt with them, and We set forth examples for you. The Quraysh lived in that verse's situation. They travelled through the ruins of the peoples whose example had passed, and the verse asks them what they thought the ruins were for. The next verse turns from history to a question they could not answer wrongly: who made the heavens and the earth.
