## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:172

> O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided for you. And give thanks to Allah if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Address to the Believers**

The verse addresses the believers and commands them to eat from the good things that God has provided, and it joins the command to a call for gratitude. The sentence follows the address to all humanity in the earlier verse, and the difference between the two is instructive.

The first command was issued to humanity as such, on the ground of what God has provided on the earth. The second is issued to the believers, on the ground of what God has provided for them, and the community's scholars noted that the two commands establish the same practice on the same basis. What the reader is shown is that the believers' response to provision is gratitude, and the sentence makes that response the condition of the worship the community offers.

**The Good Things Provided**

The term rendered as good things is the same one used in the earlier address, and the commentators explained it as what is wholesome, pleasant, and free of harm. The wording adds the idea of provision, which the sentence attributes to God, and the community's teachers read the phrase as covering everything that has been given for the use and enjoyment of human beings.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere includes the list of foods that have been forbidden, and the command in this verse occupies the space that the list leaves open. What the sentence establishes is that the believer's default position toward what he has been given is acceptance and gratitude, and that the restrictions are the exceptions rather than the rule.

**Gratitude as the Condition of Worship**

The sentence states that gratitude is to be offered if the community worships God alone, and the conditional form connects the two. The community's scholars explained the connection by noting that a person who acknowledges the giver of what he has received is someone whose worship is directed to the right object.

The Book's frequent pairing of provision with gratitude appears elsewhere in the sūrah, where the children of Israel are reminded of what they were given, and in the appeal to the Quraysh, who are reminded of the security and the provision they enjoy. The verse in this place belongs to that body of teaching, and the sentence makes the recognition of the gift a part of the worship itself.

**The Reports About What God Accepts**

The community's reports attach the same principle to the acceptance of acts of worship. The Prophet ﷺ stated that God is good and accepts only what is good, and the community's teachers used the saying when they discussed the relation between a person's earnings and the acceptance of his prayers and alms.

The report shows how the community applied the verse's command to the question of what a believer brings before God. What the sentence establishes is a link between the material conditions of a person's life and the worship he offers, and the reader is told that gratitude is not a separate act added to the worship but a condition of it.

**The Command in Its Context**

The sentence follows a passage about people who justified their practices by the custom of their ancestors, and it addresses the community that has accepted the revelation. The command to eat what is good is therefore a statement about the freedom that the revelation brings, since the restrictions the earlier communities had invented are not imposed on the believers.

The commentators noted that the passage's discussion of food is connected with the subject of authority. What the Book has established is that the standard for what is permitted belongs to God, and the sentence here draws the practical conclusion for the community that accepts that standard.

The command follows a passage about communities that forbade themselves things God had permitted, and the community’s teachers read the sentence as the correction of that practice. The Book has stated that the standard belongs to God, and the sentence draws the practical conclusion for those who accept it. What the reader receives is a statement about the freedom that the revelation restores, since a community that follows the Book is not bound by the restrictions that other communities have invented, and the sentence places that freedom within the obligation of gratitude.

**Gratitude in the Book's Vocabulary**

The Book's term for gratitude carries the sense of acknowledging a benefit and of acting in a way that shows the acknowledgement, and the community's scholars distinguished it from mere verbal thanks. The same root is used in the Book for what a person owes to his parents and for the failure of a community to recognise what it had been given.

The verse's use of the term gives the reader a task that extends beyond the act of eating, since what the Book asks for is a life that reflects the recognition of the giver. The community's teachers read the sentence as an instruction about the whole of a believer's dealings with what he possesses, and the passage continues with the list of what has been forbidden.

The words rendered as thanks and as ungrateful come from the same root, and the Book uses the pair when it describes how people receive what they are given. The sūrah’s account of the children of Israel includes the command to remember the blessings that were granted to them, and the appeal to the Quraysh reminds them of the provision and the security they enjoy. The community’s teachers read the vocabulary as an indication that the Book treats the recognition of a gift as a duty that accompanies the gift itself, and the sentence in this verse applies that duty to the food the believers have been provided.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes that the believers may eat what God has provided, that the provision is the reason for gratitude, and that the gratitude belongs to the worship the community offers. The reader is given the principle before the list of prohibitions that follows, and the two together form the Book's teaching on the subject in this passage.

The community’s teachers gathered the command in this verse with the address to humanity that precedes it and read the two as a single instruction given to different audiences. What the sentence adds is the connection between what a person eats and the worship he offers, and the community’s practice has preserved the connection in the invocation spoken over food and in the thanks given after it. The reader is given a standard that concerns the ordinary acts of his day, and the passage continues with the list of what has been forbidden and with the concession granted to those who are compelled.
