## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:172

> O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided for you. And give thanks to Allah if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Permission, Narrowed and Warmed**

Four verses earlier the command to eat went out to all humanity: eat from what is lawful and good on the earth (2:168). Here it is given again, to the believers alone, with two changes that carry the verse's whole message. The food is now described not as lawful-and-good in the abstract but as *the good things We have provided for you* — the provider named, the provision personalized, the table set by a Host. And to the permission is added an obligation the universal address did not contain: *give thanks to Allah*. The narrowing from humanity to believers is not a restriction of the food but an intensification of the relationship. All people may eat the lawful; believers are told that their eating is reception — that every meal is a gift arriving from a Giver who has furnished the earth and furnished their access to it. The verse thus converts the biological act the whole species shares into the covenantal act only the believers perform: eating as worship, the table as an altar of gratitude.

**The Ṭayyibāt: What God Did Not Forbid**

Al-Ṭabarī's definition of the good things is a definition by negation, and the negation is the doctrine: *ṭayyibāt* are the things God has not forbidden or singled out as sinful. The default of creation is permission. The earth was furnished before the prohibitions were legislated, and the legislative list — the four items the verse after next will name — is an exception carved out of an ocean of the allowed. This definition answers the occasion the surrounding verses had exposed: the tribes that forbade animals to themselves, the ancestors whose customs bound what God had loosed, the footsteps that walk pious people toward invented prohibitions (2:168–170). Against all of them the verse states the table's constitution — what God did not forbid is provision, not suspicion; the good things are good by divine manufacture, and the believer's first act toward his food is not scrutiny of its permissibility against human custom but reception of it against divine generosity. The satanic program named two verses back — making unlawful what God made lawful — is here countermanded in a single noun: the ṭayyibāt, the good things, plural and abundant, spread across an earth that was created to feed its inhabitants.

**Thanks as the Test of Worship**

The second sentence attaches gratitude to the eating with a conditional that reads as a test: *give thanks to Allah if you truly worship Him alone*. The condition does not doubt the believers' worship; it defines it. Gratitude is not an appendix to worship, a polite addition performed after the meal and the prayer — it is worship's own substance in the presence of a gift, and the verse makes the substance explicit: the one who worships Allah alone gives thanks to Allah alone, at the table where the alone-ness is most tangible. The logic binds the two halves of the creed together. Worship directed at rivals produces thanks to the rivals — the ancestors credited for the customs, the idols thanked for the harvest, the stars credited for the rain; and the invented prohibitions of the previous verses were exactly such misdirected reverence, scruples offered to sources other than God. The believer's thanks at the meal is therefore the smallest daily act of tawḥīd: the acknowledgment that this provision came from the One confessed in the creed of 2:163, and that the acknowledgment is owed to no one else. Eating without thanks is not a neutral act in this verse's economy — it is the food's provider left unnamed, which is the silence where shirk grows.

**The Verse Quoted by the Prophet**

This verse holds a distinction rare in the Qur'an: it was used as a proof-text by the Prophet himself, in a report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (1015). Abu Hurayrah narrated the Messenger of God saying: *O people, Allah is good and accepts only what is good. Allah commanded the believers as He commanded the messengers* — and he quoted the sūrah of the Believers: *"O messengers! Eat from what is good and lawful, and act righteously"* (23:51) — *and He said: 'O you who believe, eat from the good things We have provided for you'* (2:172). Then he described a man long journeying, dishevelled and dusty, who raises his hands to the sky crying *O Lord, O Lord* — while his food is unlawful, his drink unlawful, his clothing unlawful, his nourishment unlawful: *how can he be answered?* The ḥadīth reads this verse as law with consequences: the command to eat the good things governs the whole body, and the body governs the prayer. The traveler has every title to an answer — the journey, the distress, the raised hands — and one disqualifier: unlawful nourishment standing between him and the sky. The verse's table is thus load-bearing for the community's entire worship; the supplication the fasting tongue utters is funded by what the hand ate.

**Manna and the Two Tables**

The command to eat the provided good things has a precedent in this same sūrah's history of the Israelites: the clouds shaded them, manna and quails were sent down, *"Eat from the good things We have provided for you"* (2:57) — the identical formula, spoken over the wilderness table. And the verse records what followed: *the evildoers did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves* — the same provision, received by one generation with transgression and rebellion. The two tables stand beside each other in the sūrah deliberately: provision plus gratitude is this verse's covenant; provision plus self-wronging was that generation's failure. The difference is not in the food — both tables were furnished by the same Provider with the same class of good things — but in the second course, which is thanks. This is why the verse makes gratitude conditional on worship rather than optional for it: the manna generation had the permission and lost the benefit, and the believers are handed both in one sentence so that the history is not repeated at their own tables. Every meal offers the choice the wilderness offered: eat and remember the Provider, or eat and wrong the self.

**The Shortest Food Law**

Structurally, the verse is the pivot of the whole food passage, and its brevity is its jurisprudence. The permission: eat the good things provided. The obligation: give thanks. The condition: if it is Him you worship. No list, no detail, no casuistry — the verse before it described the enemy's methods, the verse before that exposed the ancestral refusal, and this verse answers both with the positive law of the table in two sentences. The detail comes next, and comes short: the four prohibitions of the following verse, framed by necessity's exemption and closed with forgiveness and mercy. The architecture is a teaching about law itself — permission vast and stated first, prohibition narrow and stated second, gratitude woven through both. A reader who holds this verse holds the spirit in which the coming list is to be read: not as a fence closing the earth's bounty but as four exceptions proving the rule, spoken by the Provider over a table He furnished, to guests whose only required speech is thanks.
