## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:15

> Allah said, “You are delayed ˹until the appointed Day˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Given to Iblīs**

The verse reports the answer to the petition of the previous verse, and the answer grants what was asked. The Book's other accounts of the scene report the same grant with the addition of the term: the sūrah of the Rocky Tract states that Iblīs is of those granted respite until the day of the time appointed (15:37–38), the sūrah of Ṣād gives the same answer in the same words (38:80–81), and the sūrah of the Night Journey reports the request for delay without the answer. The sūrah in front of us states the grant in a single sentence.

The answer settles the terms of the interval. Iblīs is not destroyed at the moment of his refusal, and he is not free of the term that has been set for him. The verse in front of us states that the delay is granted and that its end is fixed, and the narrative then reports what he does with the time.

**The Appointed Day**

The day named in the answer is the day of the resurrection, and the Book's accounts of it supply the names and descriptions that the verse leaves out. The sūrah of the Clatterer opens with the day on which people will be like scattered moths (101:4–5), the sūrah of the Inevitable describes the falling of the event that has been denied (56:1–3), and the sūrah of the Overwhelming describes the faces of the day, some humbled and some blessed (88:2–9). The term in front of us ends at the day on which those scenes take place.

The Book's statements about the timing of the resurrection place it in the knowledge of God alone, and the sūrah of the Heights itself will state that no soul knows what it has been prepared for. The answered petition in front of us fixes the outer limit of the interval that the whole history of the human race occupies.

**Respite as a Fixed Practice**

The Book describes the delay of punishment as a settled practice of its Lord, and it applies the description to the communities that refused their prophets. The sūrah of the Thunder states that many messengers were mocked before the Prophet and that God granted respite to those who disbelieved and then took them, and it asks how the taking was (13:32). The sūrah of the Family of Imran warns the believers not to think that the respite given to the disbelievers is good for them, since it is granted so that they may increase in sin (3:178). Sūrat al-Aʿrāf itself will describe the communities that were taken by surprise after their respite had run its course.

The practice serves the sūrah's argument about the audience's security. The people of Makkah lived in the enjoyment of their prosperity and their position, and the verse in front of us states the principle that governs such enjoyment: it is a delay, and the delay has an end. The Book's warnings about the destroyed towns make the same point with the ruins that stood along the audience's trade routes.

**What the Respite Is For**

The Book presents the time between the refusal and the reckoning as an opportunity that can be used in either direction. The chapter of the Cow states that God gives the wrongdoers rope to lengthen, and the sūrah of the Pen describes the same extension as a gradual drawing on. The time is granted to the audience of the Prophet as it was granted to Iblīs, and the Book's descriptions of the repentant and the obstinate show the two uses to which it can be put.

The sūrah's later passages describe people who use the time to argue about the message, to demand signs, and to mock the messenger. The verse in front of us, with the vow that follows it, presents the alternative use in its most extreme form. The tempter has been given time and resolves to spend it on the deception of others.

**The Term and the Human Being**

The Book applies the language of the appointed term to individual human beings as well as to communities and to Iblīs. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf states in its later verses that every community has a term that can neither be advanced nor delayed (7:34), and the sūrah of the Believers and the sūrah of the Heights repeat the statement in different words. The verse in front of us applies the same language to the one who asked for delay.

The reader of the sūrah is therefore placed in a position where the terms of the answer are visible. The tempter has his term, the communities have theirs, and the individual has his own. The delay in the verse is not a privilege reserved for the wicked; it is the shape of the world in which the warning is delivered.

**Divine Forbearance and the Delay**

The Book's treatment of the divine forbearance accompanies its warnings about the delay. The sūrah of Fāṭir and the sūrah of the Troops describe God as the Forbearing and the Forgiving, and the sūrah of the Family of Imran places the delay of punishment in the context of an instruction to the Prophet not to be distressed by the wealth of the disbelievers. This verse states the grant of delay without commentary, and the sūrah's reader is left to connect it with the account of the reckoning that the opening section has recently given.

The connection is the point of the narrative's placement. The chapter has described the questioning, the recounting, and the weighing; it then shows the enemy of the human race receiving the time in which to operate before that reckoning arrives. The reader is told that the interval is not a gap in the accounting but a period within its scope.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands fifteenth in the chapter, and it closes the exchange that began with the prostration. The verse that follows reports the vow of Iblīs to lie in wait for the descendants of Adam on the straight path, and the account then describes his approach from every direction. The grant in front of us is the condition under which the whole of that activity takes place.

The reader has been given the creation, the command, the refusal, the sentence, and the petition. The narrative now turns to the activity that follows from the sentence and the petition, and the sūrah's warnings about the protectors taken in place of God receive their explanation in the character of the one who waits on the path.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports that Iblīs, claiming that God has caused him to err, vows to lie in wait for the descendants of Adam on the straight path (7:16). The passage in front of us has granted him the time; the verse that follows states how he intends to use it.

The two verses together move the narrative from the sentence to the campaign. The reader is shown an enemy with a fixed term and a declared intention, and the sūrah's later passages describe the human beings who are the object of that intention and the revelation that is their defence.
