## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:91

> And they have not shown Allah His proper reverence when they said, “Allah has revealed nothing to any human being.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who then revealed the Book brought forth by Moses as a light and guidance for people, which you split into separate sheets—revealing some and hiding much? You have been taught ˹through this Quran˺ what neither you nor your forefathers knew.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah ˹revealed it˺!” Then leave them to amuse themselves with falsehood.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge of Failing to Appraise God**

And they have not shown Allah His proper reverence. The Book uses this formula in two other places, and each time it is attached to a failure of understanding. The sūrah of the Troops uses it for people who cannot conceive of God's power to hold the earth in His grip and roll up the heavens on the day of judgment (39:67), and the sūrah of the Pilgrimage uses it in a passage about those who worship beings that can create nothing (22:74). Here it is attached to a claim about revelation, and the claim is that God has never revealed anything to any human being.

The severity of the sentence comes from what the claim implies. A person who says that God has never spoken to humanity has denied the whole of the tradition he claims to honour, and the Book's answer begins by naming the one book the audience could not deny.

**The Book of Moses as the Counter-Example**

Who then revealed the Book brought forth by Moses as a light and guidance for people? The question is addressed to people who acknowledged the Torah, and it leaves them with no answer except the one they were refusing to give. The Book uses the same method of argument elsewhere, appealing to the earlier scriptures as evidence that revelation happens, since the audience accepted those scriptures as genuine.

The sūrah of the Family of Imran states that God revealed the Book in truth confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel (3:3), and the sūrah of the Cow says that Gabriel revealed the Qur'an confirming what came before it, as a guide and good news for believers (2:97). The continuity between the earlier books and this one is the sūrah's theme in these verses, and the audience's own acceptance of Moses is the ground on which the argument stands.

**The Charge of Concealment**

Which you split into separate sheets, revealing some and hiding much. The sentence accuses the custodians of the earlier scripture of selective disclosure, and the Book makes the same accusation in several places. It says that the Messenger has come revealing much of what the People of the Book used to hide (5:15); it records the covenant taken from those given the Scripture to make it known and not hide it, and their casting it behind their backs in exchange for a fleeting gain (3:187); and it says that a group of them hides the truth knowingly (2:146).

The charge matters to the sūrah's argument because it explains how an audience could claim the tradition of Moses while denying revelation altogether. The sheets had been divided, the difficult passages set aside, and the result was a religion that could be honoured in name while its content was managed. The verse describes that practice as a failure to show God the reverence He deserves.

**"You Have Been Taught What Neither You Nor Your Forefathers Knew"**

The sentence continues with a statement of what the new revelation contains, and it names the new knowledge as a gift to the audience rather than a challenge to their tradition. The Book describes the Qur'an as teaching what neither the Prophet nor his people knew before, and the sūrah of Hūd records the same claim when it says that the stories of the messengers are related so that the Prophet's heart may be strengthened by them.

The sūrah's audience was therefore being told that the message brought content their tradition had never contained, and that this content had reached them through the man they were arguing with. The statement also pre-empts the objection that the message was merely a repetition of what earlier prophets had said, since the Book holds both things at once: it confirms the earlier scriptures and it brings what those scriptures did not.

**The Command to Declare the Source**

Say, Allah revealed it. The sentence is short, and the Book places it after a long question because the answer needed to be stated plainly. The Prophet is not asked to argue the point further but to name the source of the Book he was reciting, and the declaration is the same one the sūrah has been making since its opening verses about the revelation being sent down.

The Book uses this pattern repeatedly, placing a short declaration of divine origin after an extended argument. What the audience is left with is not a demonstration to evaluate but a claim to accept or reject, and the sūrah's concern throughout has been with the response rather than with the quantity of evidence.

**The Dismissal at the End**

Then leave them to amuse themselves with falsehood. The verse ends by withdrawing the Prophet from the argument, and the instruction uses the same vocabulary the sūrah used earlier when it told him to leave those who take the faith as play and amusement (6:70). The people who have just denied that God ever revealed anything to a human being are described in the terms of the earlier passage, and the sūrah declines to make their amusement the Prophet's responsibility.

The closing instruction also prepares the reader for the verse that follows, which presents the Book as a blessed revelation sent down to warn the Mother of Cities and those around it. Having stated that revelation is real and that the earlier books confirm it, the sūrah now describes the book in the Prophet's hands and the mission it carries.
