## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:44

> and the residents of Midian. And Moses was denied ˹too˺. But I delayed ˹the fate of˺ the disbelievers ˹until their appointed time˺ then seized them. And how severe was My response!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Midian and Its Messenger**

The catalogue closes with the people of Midian, to whom the Qur'an sent Shuʿayb. The account is preserved in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf: *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord. So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order’”* (7:85).

Midian is the last community on the list whose messenger is named in the Qur'an by name besides the five already given, and it is the one whose offence was economic — fraudulent measures, commercial cheating — which broadens the catalogue again. These are not all idolaters, and they are not all guilty of the same thing. What they share is the refusal.

**Why Moses Is Named Differently**

The construction changes at Moses: *wa-ukhdhiba Mūsā* — and Moses was denied. The verb is passive and the subject is the messenger himself rather than his people.

The shift is meaningful. For every other community on the list, the deniers are named. Here the denied one is named, and the deniers are left as an unnamed subject in the passive voice.

The commentators took this as deliberate. Moses' people — the Children of Israel — were not destroyed in the way the other communities were; they were delivered, and their denial of him was persistent rather than terminal. Naming him rather than them keeps him out of the destruction verdict that follows, which belongs to the communities that were seized.

**"But I Granted the Disbelievers Respite"**

*Thumma akhadhtuhum* is preceded by *fa-amlaytu li-l-kāfirīn* — I gave the disbelievers respite. The verb *amlā* means to give rein, to let run on, to allow more length.

This is the sentence the whole catalogue was building toward, and it is the point the sūrah needed to make at this juncture. Nine communities were named, and the thing said about all of them is that they were given time. The delay is not an oversight or a reprieve; it is the standing procedure.

The sūrah has been preparing this since verse 1. The Hour is *coming* (22:7) rather than arrived; Iblīs was *delayed* in the parallel sūrahs; the man who wanted the punishment hurried is told in the next verse that a day with God is a thousand years. Delay is the sūrah's most consistent claim about how God runs the world, and this verse states it as a matter of history.

**"Then I Seized Them"**

*Thumma akhadhtuhum* — then I took them. The verb is plain and physical, and *thumma* places it after an interval of unspecified length.

The combination is what makes the verse frightening, and it is the same combination the sūrah opposed in verse 40 to the aggression of the Quraysh. Wrongdoing is met, but not on the schedule the wronged party would choose. The delay is long enough for the deniers to conclude that nothing is coming.

**"And How Was My Denial"**

*Fa-kayfa kāna nakīr* — and how was My *nakīr*? The word is the verse's difficulty, and the commentators recorded two readings of it.

*Nakīr* comes from the root *n-k-r*, which gives both "to deny" and "to disapprove, to alter something into a state one dislikes." On the first reading the phrase means "how was My denial of them" — God's rejection answering theirs, the same word used for their refusal. On the second it means "how was My rebuke," or "how terrible was the change I wrought."

Both senses are present in the Arabic, and the ambiguity is a deliberate piece of lexical justice. They denied; the response is described with the same root. The punishment is named as a matching refusal rather than as an unrelated act of force. The same closing question appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in the same form, which confirms that the phrase is a fixed formula: *“Then I seized those who persisted in disbelief. How severe was My response!”* (35:26).

**Two Verbs, Nine Communities**

The whole catalogue of verses 42 to 44 comes down to two verbs at the end: *amlaytu*, I gave rein, and *akhadhtu*, I took.

Everything else in those three verses is a list of names. The sūrah is not interested in the differences between the communities — their different sins, their different messengers, their different centuries. What it extracts from all nine is a single sequence: time given, then time ended.

That economy is the point of the catalogue as a literary form. A historian would want the particulars. The Qur'an wants the rule, and it states the rule in a form its audience can apply to itself. The community reading this verse in Makkah was, in the sūrah's telling, in the first half of the sequence.

**The Catalogue's Verdict, Delivered Once**

Nine communities, three verses, one verdict. The grammatical structure of verses 42 to 44 is what carries the argument: everything is suspended until the final clause, and then respite and seizure are announced for all of them together.

A reader who has been following the list is left with a single principle instead of nine anecdotes. Communities that reject their messengers are not answered on the spot. They are given length, and then they are taken — and the question the verse ends on is not whether it happened but *how*, which is a question the ruins were supposed to answer.
