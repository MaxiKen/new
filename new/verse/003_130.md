## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:130

> O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over. And be mindful of Allah, so you may prosper.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command About Money in a Passage About War**

The verse addresses the believers about interest and forbids them to take it in multiplied form, and it closes by telling them to be mindful of God so that they may prosper. It follows a passage about battles, aid, and the fate of opponents.

The placement is characteristic of the sūrah. Having described matters of life and death, it turns to transactions, and the turn makes the point that the community's conduct in the market is part of the same religion as its conduct in the field. The sūrah's later passages continue in the same register, with instructions about spending and about forgiveness in the middle of the aftermath of a battle. The prohibition arrives in the middle of a sūrah that has been discussing warfare and its aftermath, and the juxtaposition is deliberate: integrity in dealings belongs to the same religion as conduct in the field.

**What the Term Covers**

The Arabic word for interest describes an addition charged on a loan, and the Book's treatment of the practice is worked out at length in the sūrah of the Cow. There it states that God has permitted trade and forbidden interest (2:275), and then commands the believers to give up outstanding interest if they are believers, warning them that persistence means a war with God and His Messenger (2:278-279).

The seriousness of the language is unmatched in the Book's treatment of economic matters. The two verses in al-Baqarah state the prohibition, the alternative of keeping the principal, and the warning, and the verse before the reader supplies the command in the context of the community's life.

**"Multiplying It Many Times Over"**

The phrase describes a debt that grows by doubling when it is not repaid, and the practice was one that left borrowers in a position they could not escape. Commentators discussed whether the qualifier limits the prohibition to the compounded form or describes the practice as it was known.

The second reading was the one favoured by most of the classical scholars, who held that the prohibition covers any guaranteed addition for the mere passage of time, and the Book's later verses in al-Baqarah state the rule in general terms. The description in this verse records a shape the practice commonly took rather than a boundary of the prohibition.

**The Book's Contrast Between Interest and Charity**

The Book sets the two practices against each other in a way that makes the prohibition a matter of principle rather than of amounts. One passage states that whatever a person lends seeking interest will not increase with God, while whatever is given as charity seeking His pleasure multiplies its reward (30:39).

Another states directly that God makes interest fruitless and makes charity fruitful (2:276). The pair of sentences treats the two uses of wealth as producing opposite results, and the result is measured not in the world's terms but in the accounting the Book has been describing throughout the sūrah.

**"So That You May Prosper"**

The closing clause attaches the command to the word for success, the same word that opens the call to prayer and the same one the sūrah used when it described the group that calls others to good. The Book's promise of prosperity is not attached to accumulation.

That choice of word is the verse's answer to the appeal of the practice it forbids. A person takes interest because it looks like gain, and the sentence tells him that the gain is not the success the Book means. Between the two uses of the word, the reader is being taught to attach the term to the outcome described elsewhere rather than to the amount in his hand.

**The Warning the Book Attaches to the Practice**

The prohibition in the sūrah of the Cow carries the strongest warning in the Book on an economic matter, informing those who persist that they should expect a war from God and His Messenger. The language has led the commentators to treat the practice as a serious offence rather than as a commercial irregularity.

The verse before the reader does not repeat the warning, and its brevity leaves the reader to supply the context from the rest of the Book. That is how the sūrah's instructions work elsewhere: a single command is given, and the framework that makes it binding has already been established in the passages around it.

**Why the Sūrah Raises It Here**

The passage that follows this verse continues with commands about spending, patience, and the pursuit of forgiveness, and the whole stretch describes the conduct of a community that has just been through a testing.

The connection between the battle narrative and the rules about money is not incidental. The community's ability to function depended on its internal dealings, and a practice that concentrated wealth in the hands of lenders at the expense of debtors would have divided the very community the sūrah has been trying to hold together. The command belongs to the same project as the commands about unity and patience.

**The Instruction and the Community's Practice**

The prohibition's effect on the early community was substantial, since credit arrangements of the kind described were common in the region. The Book's method was to state the rule, describe the two alternatives, and leave the community to organise its affairs accordingly.

The tradition records the community's engagement with the question in detail, and the sūrah's contribution is the command in its shortest form. What the verse requires is that a community's dealings be conducted without the practice it names, and the closing command to be mindful of God places the whole matter under the awareness the sūrah has been asking of its readers throughout the passage.
