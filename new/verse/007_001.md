## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ṣãd.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Alif Lām Mīm Ṣād**

Four Arabic letters stand at the head of this sūrah, and they are known as the separated letters, the letters that appear singly at the openings of twenty-nine chapters of the Book. The combination in front of us is used once and once only: no other sūrah begins with Alif Lām Mīm Ṣād, just as no other sūrah begins with the five letters of Maryam (19:1) or the two letters of Ṭā Hā (20:1). The letters are recited before a single word of the sūrah's message is given, and the reciter meets them as sound rather than as statement.

The collection of these openings is a fixed feature of the Book's arrangement. Alif Lām Mīm stands at the head of the Cow (2:1), the Family of Imran (3:1), the Spider (29:1), the Romans (30:1), Luqmān (31:1), and the Prostration (32:1); Alif Lām Rā appears at the head of Jonah (10:1), Hūd (11:1), Joseph (12:1), Abraham (14:1), and the Rocky Tract (15:1); Alif Lām Mīm Rā opens the Thunder (13:1); and Ḥā Mīm opens the seven sūrahs from the Forgiver (40:1) to the Sand Dunes (46:1). The letters of this sūrah belong to a family that runs through the whole Book.

**What the Letters Signal**

The letters are followed in almost every case by a reference to the Book, and the pattern tells the reader what the opening is for. The letters of the Cow are followed by the words, This is the Book, no doubt therein (2:2); the letters of the Family of Imran are followed by the statement that God has revealed the Book to the Prophet in truth (3:3); the letters of Ṣād are followed by an oath sworn by the Quran, full of reminders (38:1); and the letters of the Pen are followed by an oath sworn by the pen and by what people write (68:1). The sūrah in front of us continues the same pattern, since its letters are followed immediately by the mention of a Book sent down to the Prophet.

The sequence places the reader in front of the recitation itself before it places him in front of its content. The Book is presented as a text that is recited, memorised, and written down, and the letters at the head of a sūrah are the audible sign of that. The sūrah of the Cave states that God has made the Quran upright and has not placed any crookedness in it (18:1), and the sūrah of the Troops says that the Book was sent down in truth. The letters that open the chapter in front of us introduce a text of that kind.

**Interpretations of the Letters**

Commentators have not settled on a single meaning for the separated letters, and the range of their suggestions is part of the tradition. Some have held that the letters are a name of God, others that they stand for the greatest name of the Divine, and others that they are the name of the sūrah or of the Book itself. The Companion Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that the letters are among the ambiguous verses of the Book, and that the believing response to them is to accept them and to leave their meaning to God.

The differences among the explanations have not produced a dispute about the text, because the letters are recited and preserved as they stand in all readings of the Book. The community receives them as it receives the rest of the revelation, and the discussion of their meaning belongs to the study of the Quran's language rather than to the determination of its commands. The letters in front of us are recited in every prayer that uses this sūrah, and they are written at the head of every copy of it.

**The Opening of a Sūrah That Argues**

The sūrah that follows these letters carries an argument, and its opening is built for the audience that the argument addresses. The sūrah's introduction states that it is a late Makkan revelation, and its content concerns the consequences of rejecting the signs and messengers of God. It recounts the fate of communities that denied their prophets, from the people of Noah to the people of Pharaoh, and it sets that history in front of an audience that refused to believe in the prophethood of Muhammad.

The letters at the head of such a sūrah place the revelation before the debate. The audience is presented with the recitation first, and the letters are the point at which the reciter's voice begins. The sūrah's first ten verses then state the program: the Book has been sent down, the community is to follow it, the communities that were destroyed are a warning, and the reckoning will be just.

**The Name of the Sūrah**

The chapter takes its name from the Heights mentioned in its middle section, the barrier that stands between the people of Paradise and the people of the Fire (7:46; 7:48). The name is a description of the sūrah's scenery, and it has been used by the community since the earliest copies of the Book. The letters at the head of the chapter are not part of its name, although they are inseparable from the opening of the recitation, and the sūrah is known both by the word for the Heights and by its place in the sequence of the Book.

The title the community uses describes a scene from the Hereafter, and the sūrah's first verses speak of the world that is passing. The Book's habit of naming its chapters after an image or a theme inside them means that the title is a signpost rather than a summary, and the name of this chapter directs the reader to the middle of a long argument about the last things.

**Letters, Recitation, and the Claim of the Book**

The letters stand at the head of a Book that challenges its audience on the ground of its own eloquence. The sūrah of the Cow closes its longest passage on the signs with the challenge to produce a single sūrah like the Book (2:23), and the sūrah of the Night Journey states that if humanity and the jinn were to gather together to produce the like of the Quran, they would not do it (17:88). An audience that prided itself on its poetry was addressed in the sounds with which its poetry began, and the letters that open this sūrah are that address in its plainest form.

The Book's use of letters as an opening has been read by some commentators as a statement that the materials of the recitation are the letters the audience already knew, arranged in a manner that none of them could produce. The claim is not that the letters are unfamiliar but that the arrangement is beyond them, and the sūrah that follows develops the claim at length.

**Where the Verse Stands**

The verse is the first element of the sūrah's opening section, and it stands before the mention of the Book that its letters introduce. The ten verses that follow state the themes of the chapter: the sending down of the Book, the warning to the community, the protection that belongs to God alone, the fate of the destroyed communities, the questioning of the recipients of the message, the weighing of deeds, and the provision that has been placed on the earth.

The reader who begins the sūrah with these four letters is given the recitation before he is given the argument. The Book is placed in his hands, the warning and the reminder are named, and the long account of the prophets and the peoples that rejected them begins from the next passage. The letters are the door through which the whole chapter is entered.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The verse that follows describes the Book as sent down to the Prophet, tells him to carry no constriction in his breast because of it, and states that he is to warn by means of it and that it is a reminder for the believers (7:2). The letters of the opening are thus tied to the message they precede, and the reader passes from sound to statement in the space of a single verse.

The arrangement gives the chapter its shape from the outset. The revelation is named, the messenger is addressed, the warning is stated, and the community of believers is placed alongside the audience that is warned. The four letters that begin the sūrah are followed by a Book that has been sent down, and everything that follows in the chapter is an unfolding of that sending.
