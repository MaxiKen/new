## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:13

> They made for him whatever he desired of sanctuaries, statues, basins as large as reservoirs, and cooking pots fixed ˹into the ground˺. ˹We ordered:˺ “Work gratefully, O family of David!” ˹Only˺ a few of My servants are ˹truly˺ grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Inventory Rather Than a Marvel**

The conscripted workforce of the previous verse is now shown at its output, and the output is listed like a building schedule: sanctuaries, statues, basins, cooking pots. No single item is described in detail, none is called wondrous, and the effect of the list is deliberately prosaic. A king with jinn at his disposal might have produced anything, and what he produced was infrastructure. Two of the four items are for feeding people at scale and one is for washing or storing water at scale. The Qur'an is not usually interested in inventories, so a list this concrete is itself a statement. It brings a story that could have drifted into legend back onto the ground where Solomon's authority actually operated — a working state with public buildings, a water system and kitchens large enough to serve a crowd. The phrase *mā yashāʾ*, whatever he desired, sits at the head of the list and gives it its scope. Unlimited capacity, spent on this.

**maḥārīb: A Word That Travelled**

The first item is *maḥārīb*, plural of *miḥrāb*, translated here as sanctuaries. The word has a history worth tracing because it explains the range of meanings commentators have found in it. In pre-Islamic usage a *miḥrāb* was the private chamber of a house or the innermost, most honoured part of a large tent — a place of retreat, and therefore a place of standing. The Qur'an uses it that way for the chamber where Mary was found supplied with provision (3:37) and for the place from which Zachariah emerged to signal to his people (19:11), and it uses it of the enclosure David's two plaintiffs climbed into (38:21). From that sense of an honoured inner space the word later settled onto the prayer niche in a mosque. Early authorities offered two further derivations: that the place is named for the war (*ḥarb*) waged there against Satan and one's own appetite, or because the worshipper in it is cut off — *ḥarīb* — from the concerns of the world. Applied to Solomon's builders, the word has been read as palaces and halls of state, and as places set aside for worship with the direction of prayer marked. Both readings keep the underlying sense: a space set apart and given honour.

**Statues in the Hands of a Prophet**

*Tamāthīl*, images or statues, is the item that has provoked most discussion, since sculpture of living forms is prohibited in Islamic law and the prophets are not exempt from law. Several answers have been offered, and they divide on what the statues represented. One line holds that they depicted inanimate things — trees, buildings, objects — which falls outside the prohibition as generally formulated. Another holds that images of angels and earlier prophets were permitted under Solomon's law and abrogated later, which is a coherent position since the Qur'an itself records rulings that changed between communities. The verse gives no detail, and the honest reading declines to invent one. What the verse does establish is that the objects were commissioned, not smuggled in: they belong to a list the Qur'an reports without censure, in a passage whose whole argument is that Solomon used his powers correctly. A commentary that turns the item into a fault has to work against the grain of the passage it sits in.

**Basins Like Reservoirs, Pots That Do Not Move**

The last two items are domestic objects scaled up until they are civic. *Jifān ka-l-jawāb* — great dishes like *jawābī*, the word used for the cisterns and watering troughs dug for camels. *Qudūr rāsiyāt* — cooking pots that are *rāsiyah*, the same adjective the Qur'an applies to mountains as anchored and immovable, so these are pots too large to be lifted, built into their setting and filled where they stand. The picture is of continuous public catering: water held in tanks, food cooked in fixed cauldrons, quantities that assume a permanent crowd. This is the least glamorous portion of the list and probably the most revealing. A ruler's greatness is being illustrated by the size of his kitchens. For the Meccan audience of this sūrah, whose own aristocracy measured itself by feeding pilgrims and guests, the detail would not have needed explanation.

**The Command That Interrupts the List**

The inventory breaks off and a voice cuts in: *iʿmalū āla Dāwūda shukrā* — work, family of David, in gratitude. The construction can be read two ways in Arabic, and both are instructive. *Shukrā* may be an accusative of purpose, giving *work in order to give thanks*, or it may be the object of the verb, giving *perform thanks* — with gratitude itself as the work to be done. The second reading makes the sharper point. Gratitude in the Qur'an is rarely an emotion and frequently a labour; it is something performed with the hands and the estate, not felt in private. The addressee is again the household rather than the king, the same plural used when the armour was commissioned (34:11), which ties the two halves of this family's story together. David was told to work righteousness while he worked iron; Solomon's house is told to work gratitude while it builds. Neither instruction separates the craft from the duty attached to it.

**"Few of My Servants Are Grateful" — A Verdict Without Softening**

The verse ends on a sentence with no comfort in it: *wa qalīlun min ʿibādiya al-shakūr*. The form *shakūr* is intensive — not merely one who says thank you but one whose whole disposition is thankfulness — and the claim is that such people are rare. The observation is not new in the Qur'an; it is exactly the prediction Iblīs made when he was given his reprieve: *"then You will find most of them ungrateful"* (7:17), a prediction this sūrah will report as having come true a few verses later (34:20). Placing the verdict here, immediately after a list of spectacular favours, gives it its force. Gratitude is hardest precisely where gifts are most abundant, because abundance normalises itself and what is normal stops being noticed. Ibrāhīm states the stakes of the choice: *"If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe"* (14:7). The sūrah is about to demonstrate both halves of that sentence on a single people.
