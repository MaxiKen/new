## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:162

> They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺.

**Abiding in the Sentence**

The pronoun that opens the verse reaches back for its antecedent, and al-Ṭabarī holds that it covers the curse pronounced in the previous sentence together with the Fire that the curse leads to; al-Jalālān states both plainly, and Ibn Kathīr describes the curse continuing through the life of this world until the Day of Resurrection, after which the Fire becomes the place of the abiding. The word *khālidīn* is chosen for permanence, and the exegetes explain it by the absence of any term, any interval, and any amendment of the state it names. The sūrah has used the same word before for a different company — those who abide in the Fire (2:39) — and the repetition is deliberate: the two passages measure the same duration for the same condition.

**A Punishment That Is Not Lightened**

The second clause names the least conceivable relief and denies it. Al-Rāzī observes that the wording does not say the punishment is great but that it is not reduced, which addresses the hope that has no ground: the fear of the condemned is not that the fire is heavy but that it may become lighter, and the verse closes that hope in its first word. Al-Jalālān gives the measure as the blink of an eye, a relief so small that it would pass unnoticed, and denies even that. The Book states the same formula at the end of the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān, where the curse, the punishment, and the absence of relief are joined in one sentence (3:88), and again in the sūrah of the Bee, where the evildoers are told that their punishment will not be lightened nor will they be given respite (16:85).

**No Reprieve**

The last word *yunẓarūn* belongs to the vocabulary of debt and delay. The sūrah uses it earlier to command the creditor to grant a debtor in difficulty time to pay (2:280), and the Book uses it of the wrongdoers who ask for one more season of life to correct what they have done. Al-Ṭabarī explains the closing clause as the removal of that possibility: no delay in which to repent, no interval in which to offer an excuse, no postponement of the sentence for an hour. The sūrah of the Originator gives the formula its fullest form, telling the disbelievers that they will cry out for release and be told that they will not be released and that they were given warning in their lives (35:36–37). The world is the place where delay is granted; the verse announces the place where it is not.

**The Two Doors the Sūrah Leaves Open**

The severity of the sentence is bounded by two statements that keep the reader from the two errors it could produce. Despair is forbidden in the sūrah of the Companies, where God tells His servants who have transgressed against their own souls not to despair of His mercy and states that He forgives all sins (39:53). Presumption is forbidden in the sūrah of the Heights, where the question is asked who can feel secure from the plan of God except a people already lost (7:99). Al-Saʿdī reads the passage as the pairing of hope and fear throughout: the exception of the previous verse keeps the door open in this life, and the sentence of this one describes the state of those who leave through no door at all.

**Placed Before the Declaration of God's Oneness**

The next verse of the sūrah announces that the God of the community is one God, with no deity besides Him, the Compassionate and the Merciful (2:163). Al-Rāzī notes the arrangement: the passage on the concealers of the signs and their end is closed before the sūrah turns to the plain statement of divine unity that governs everything after it. The two verses on punishment stand as the boundary of the argument, holding the reader at the edge of a warning whose purpose is the declaration that follows. The sūrah does not introduce mercy as a contrast to the sentence but places the sentence where the reader will accept the mercy it names.

**The Lightest Punishment Among the People of the Fire**

The reports describe the least of the Fire's states so that the verse is read with a measure attached. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim carry the Prophet's ﷺ statement that the most lightly punished of the people of the Fire is a man wearing sandals and laces of fire from which his brain boils as a cauldron boils, and al-Bukhārī records his word to al-ʿAbbās about Abū Ṭālib, that he occupies a shallow place in the Fire from which his brain boils, and that without him he would have been in the lowest depth. Al-Qurṭubī brings both reports forward when he explains this verse. If the description of the Fire at its lightest reaches that point, then a sentence announcing that the punishment of the condemned is never reduced names an end rather than a figure of speech.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The condition described is permanent and the state admits no mitigation, a reading the exegetes take from words chosen to exclude every interval and every reduction. Relief is denied in the smallest measure imaginable, and the delay that this world grants to debtors and wrongdoers is announced as finished. The sentence is bounded by the open door of the previous verse and the open door of divine mercy later in the Book, so that the warning serves the reader rather than crushing him. And the passage closes where the sūrah turns to the oneness of God, the declaration toward which the whole argument has been moving.
