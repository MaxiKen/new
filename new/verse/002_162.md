## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:162

> They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence in Three Clauses**

The verse executes the curse of its predecessor in three flat statements: abiding in the Fire, no lightening of the punishment, no respite from it. There is no rhetoric in the verse and no narrative — the sūrah has moved from description to sentence, and sentences are delivered in clauses. The first fixes duration: *khālidūn fīhā*, abiders in it, dwellers with no term. The second fixes intensity: the punishment will not be lightened — no mitigation, no anesthesia, no interval of relief inside the sentence. The third fixes execution: nor will they be delayed — no postponement, no stay, no gap between the verdict and its landing. Together the three clauses close every avenue a prisoner of any court scans for: an end date, a commutation, a delay. The verse's power is that it closes all three in eleven words and moves on, the way the next verses will move on to the creed that all this sentence exists to vindicate.

**A Formula the Qur'an Repeats**

The middle clause is a fixed formula, and its recurrence is deliberate. The traders of the Hereafter for this world: *"their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped"* (2:86). The cursed of Āl ʿImrān, in wording identical to this verse: *"They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed"* (3:88). The wrongdoers of the Bee, when the punishment comes: *"it will not be lightened for them, nor will they be delayed"* (16:85). The disbelievers of the Originator, with the harshest addition: in the Fire *"they will not be ˹allowed to be˺ finished by death, nor will its torment be lightened for them"* (35:36) — and the sūrah of Ornament repeats the same pairing at 43:75. The repetition is not redundancy but jurisprudence: the same verdict stated in the same words across sūrahs establishes that this is one settled ruling, not a local threat. When the Qur'an sentences, it sentences identically everywhere, and the identical wording across Meccan and Medinan revelation marks the ruling as constitutional.

**Abiding: The Question the Tradition Argued**

The first clause — forever — carries the heaviest theology, and the classical tradition argued it rather than merely asserting it. The sūrah had already met the rival claim in its early verses: the assertion by some among the Scripture-people that the Fire would touch them only for numbered days (2:80), an assertion the sūrah answered by demanding their proof. On the other side stood philosophical speculations, some tracing to early voices, that the Fire's torment might eventually exhaust itself or that its inhabitants might cease. The exegetes answered from the text's own machinery: al-Ṭabarī argued from the verse of regret a few verses ahead — the disbelievers' wish to return will not remove them from the Fire (2:167), and a wish that never ceases marks a state that never ends; the abiding-verses state their duration without exception, and the exceptions the Qur'an does contain ("except as your Lord wills," 11:107) were read by the mainstream as covering the disobedient believers who exit, not the settled disbelievers who do not. The mainstream position held: for those who die in rejection, the Fire is without term. The argument's shape matters as much as its conclusion — the tradition treated the duration of Hell as a question answerable from revelation's grammar, and this verse is the grammatical anchor: *khālidūn*, abiders, a participle with no clock attached.

**No Lightening: The End of Hope's Arithmetic**

The second clause deserves precision, because lightening is what suffering naturally hopes for. The punished do not first hope for release — release is unimaginable from inside a sentence of abiding; they hope for reduction, a lighter day, a numb hour. The verse closes that hope first, before it can form. And 35:36 closes its back door: they will not be finished by death — the one release every sufferer retains, the body's collapse, is withdrawn, so that the capacity to suffer is maintained at full strength inside a punishment never lightened. What remains, in the Quranic scenes, is only the wish of 2:167 — the second chance that will not come — and the conversations of the Fire's inmates cursing one another (7:38–39), speech without remedy. The clause is thus the cruelest of the three by mercy's own logic: hope exists to be answered or abandoned, and a punishment defined as unlightenable leaves its subjects the single dignity of knowing their case exactly, which is what the verse, by stating it plainly here, extends to every reader while there is still a verse like 2:160 to act on.

**No Respite: The Term Expired**

The third clause — nor will they be delayed — uses the language of respite, *yunẓarūn*, from the same root as the respite granted to Iblīs at humanity's first temptation: delayed until the appointed Day. The contrast is the verse's hidden argument. The whole drama of this world runs on delay — the sinner respited, the tyrant's rope lengthened, the test administered across a lifetime of granted time. Respite is the world's constitution; its withdrawal is the Hereafter's. The Day of Judgment is elsewhere called the Day on which no soul can avail another and no intercession is accepted, and the delay that structured probation ends at its door. Those described in this verse spent their respite as the previous verses described — hiding the proofs, dying in rejection — and the sentence now states the consequence in the respite's own vocabulary: the time granted is used up, and what was delayed *for* them is delayed *from* them no longer. The punishment arrives at the exact moment the probation ends, with nothing between.

**Why the Severity Sits Beside the Creed**

The verse's placement is part of its meaning. It closes a four-verse sequence that opened with the curse on concealment and passed through the open door of repentance; and two verses later the sūrah will state, in the calmest sentence imaginable, *your God is one God* (2:163), followed by the seven signs of creation and the analysis of love. The severity and the creed are not juxtaposed by accident. The unlightened, undelayed, endless sentence is what is at stake in the confession — the Fire is the cost of the wrong answer to the question 2:163 asks, and the passage wants the reader to feel the weight of the question before it is posed. This is the Qur'an's standard architecture of ultimacy: mercy and wrath stated at full strength, neither diluted by the other, both real, both published, with the door of repentance standing open between them for as long as the reader has breath. The eleven words of this verse are the wall behind the door — and the door, in the previous verse, was described in the first person of God.
