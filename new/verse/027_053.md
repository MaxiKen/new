## Sūrah an-Naml 27:53

> And We delivered those who were faithful and were mindful ˹of Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Completes the Accounting**

Verse 51 said the destruction covered them and their people all together. This verse states the exception, and without it the previous claim would describe an indiscriminate catastrophe. The believers were taken out.

The placement is deliberate. The Qur'an states the destruction first, in the strongest available terms, and supplies the qualification two verses later — long enough for the weight of the ruin to land, close enough that the accounting is completed within the same passage. Every destruction narrative in the Book follows this structure: a people ends, and those who believed among them do not. The pattern is what distinguishes these accounts from stories of divine temper. A punishment that fell on everyone present would be a disaster; one that sorts is a judgment.

**Two Conditions, Not One**

The deliverance is granted to those who *believed* and who *were mindful* — *alladhīna āmanū wa kānū yattaqūn*. Two verbs, and the second is not a restatement of the first.

*Īmān* is the inward assent: accepting that what Ṣāliḥ brought was true. *Taqwā* is the resulting carefulness, the conduct of someone who lives as though the assent were real. Arabic builds the word from a root meaning to guard oneself, and the Qur'an uses it for a practical vigilance rather than a feeling. Pairing them here does the same work the chapter's third verse did, where believers were identified by prayer, alms and certainty. A claim about the inside is given an outside. In a city run by nine notables whose business was corrupt lending and public humiliation, *taqwā* would have had an obvious daily content, and the people who practised it would have been visible.

**The Verb of Rescue**

*Najjaynā* comes from a root meaning to pull something clear, to bring it out of a place where it would have been lost. The same verb is used of Nūḥ on the ark, of Lūṭ leaving his city, and of Mūsā's people crossing the sea.

What the word describes is extraction rather than protection. The believers were not made immune where they stood; they were removed from where the destruction fell. That distinction matters for how the Qur'an presents divine rescue throughout. It generally involves motion, timing and a warning acted upon — Lūṭ's household leaves at night, Nūḥ builds for years before the water. Deliverance in these accounts is something people participate in, and the participation is the evidence that the belief was real. The verse's two conditions and its verb of extraction fit together: those who believed and were careful were the ones in a position to be pulled clear.

**A Believing Minority Existed Throughout**

This verse confirms retrospectively what verse 45 reported: the nation split into two parties, and one of them accepted Ṣāliḥ. That party existed through everything that followed — the demand for punishment, the accusation of ill omen, the conspiracy of the nine.

Their situation is worth registering. They lived in a city whose leading men were organising a murder, held a position that made them a named source of misfortune, and had no power to alter any of it. The Qur'an does not record them arguing, resisting or being heroic. What it records is that they believed and were careful, and that this was sufficient. There is a realism in that which the destruction narratives are often read past. Most people inside a failing society cannot change its direction; what remains available to them is their own position, and the verse says that their own position was enough.

**Where They Went**

The Qur'an says nothing about what became of the survivors, and the commentators filled the silence in different ways. Al-Ṭabarī transmits that those saved with Ṣāliḥ settled in Palestine. Al-Qurṭubī records an alternative, that some four thousand went to Ḥaḍramawt in southern Arabia, where Ṣāliḥ died, and that the place is named from that event since the word can be read as *death has come*.

That etymology is one of several legendary derivations offered for the name and should be treated as such. [UNVERIFIED: the accounts of the survivors settling in Palestine or Ḥaḍramawt, the figure of four thousand, and the etymology of Ḥaḍramawt come from tafsīr tradition without chains I could verify; the Qur'an gives no destination.] The Book's reticence is consistent with how it has handled every detail in this chapter that does not bear on the argument — Sheba's queen has no name, her gift no contents, and now her counterpart's survivors no address. What is recorded is that they were brought out. The chapter turns to its fourth and last prophetic narrative in the following verse.

**The Rescue Is Stated in a Single Line**

Seven verses went to the plot and its consequence. One goes to the deliverance, and it names no individuals, no numbers and no circumstances. The Qur'an spends its words on the warning and summarises the outcome it actually wants.

That imbalance is characteristic of the destroyed-nation narratives, and it follows from who they were told to. The stories are told to people who have not yet decided, and what such an audience needs at length is the sequence of refusals that led to the end — the demand for punishment, the accusation of ill omen, the nine men and their oath. The rescue needs only to be certain, not detailed. There is also a structural effect. A single short verse of deliverance placed after a long account of ruin leaves the reader with the ruin's weight and the rescue's fact, which is the proportion the chapter has maintained since it described the Book as guidance and good news for those who take it.
