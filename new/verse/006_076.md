## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:76

> When the night grew dark upon him, he saw a star and said, “This is my Lord!” But when it set, he said, “I do not love things that set.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Setting of the Sky**

The verse opens with the night darkening over Abraham, and the scene is the one his people knew best. The Makkans travelled by the stars, divided their calendar by the moon, and associated the heavenly bodies with the deities they served, and the region's older religions had made the sun, the moon, and the stars objects of worship. The sūrah's audience would have recognised the setting immediately, and the argument that follows is built from the objects they considered sacred.

The Book elsewhere denies the heavenly bodies any share in divinity while maintaining their place in the created order. The sūrah of Fussilat says that among God's signs are the day, the night, the sun, and the moon, and commands the reader not to prostrate to the sun or the moon but to prostrate to God who created them (41:37). The sūrahs of the Bee, the Prophets, and Ibrāhīm all describe the sun, the moon, and the stars as subjected by God's command (7:54; 14:33; 16:12; 21:33), which is the position Abraham states in the verses that follow.

**"This Is My Lord" and the Difficulty It Raises**

When Abraham saw the star he said, This is my Lord, and the sentence raised a question for the commentators that they answered in more than one way. Some held that Abraham did not mean it sincerely but said it to draw his people into the argument, since the Book's purpose in the passage is to show how the argument against the heavenly bodies can be made using the heavenly bodies themselves. Others held that the words record a genuine process of reasoning in which Abraham tested each candidate and rejected it, and they placed that reasoning early in his life, before his prophethood and before he was accountable.

The sūrah itself resolves the question at the end of the section when it calls Abraham's discourse the argument God gave him against his people (6:83). Whichever reading of the individual sentences is adopted, the Book presents the sequence as a demonstration that belongs to Abraham's mission rather than as a record of a mistake he made and corrected.

**The Argument From Setting**

But when it set, he said, I do not love things that set. The argument is compressed into that phrase, and it takes the form of a test that anyone can apply: a god should not be subject to disappearance, change, or decay. The Book states the same principle about the created order when it describes the sun as travelling for its fixed term and the moon as passing through phases until it looks like an old curved palm stalk, and when it says that the sun cannot catch up with the moon and that the night cannot outrun the day, since each is travelling in an orbit of its own (36:38–40).

The audience's gods shared the defect of the objects they were identified with. Whatever could rise and set, appear and vanish, was a creature running its course, and a creature's course can be interrupted. The verse states the conclusion in the language of affection, which is why the phrase has stayed in the memory of every generation that has read it: what he rejected, he rejected as something he could not love.

**A Concession the Audience Would Have Made**

The Book records that the Makkans themselves admitted who had made the heavenly bodies when they were asked directly. The sūrah of the Spider says that if they were asked who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon, they would certainly say Allah, and it follows the admission with a question about how they could then be deluded (29:61). The sūrah of Luqmān and the sūrah of the Troops record the same answer to the same question (31:25; 39:38), and the sūrah of the Ornament adds that they would name the Almighty, the All-Knowing as the maker (43:9).

Those admissions are the ground on which Abraham's argument stands in this verse. The audience already accepted that the sun and the moon were creatures under God's command; what they had not done was follow the concession to its conclusion. The verse shows the founder of their tradition doing exactly that.

**The Star Among the Objects of Worship**

The star is the first of the three candidates and the smallest of them, so the argument begins with the least impressive and rises through the sequence toward the sun, which the verse after next calls the greatest. That order shows the method at work: Abraham moves from one object to the next, and at each stage the same defect appears, until the audience has been shown that the greatest of all the visible powers has the same weakness as the smallest.

The Book's account of the sun and moon's subjection gives the same argument a longer form, stating that both were created and commanded and that their regularity is itself a proof of the design they serve rather than of any authority of their own. Against the background of those verses, Abraham's nightly observation is not merely a story about an individual's search. It is the narrative version of the sūrah's claim that the created order points beyond itself.

**What the Verse Establishes Before the Argument Continues**

By the end of this verse the reader has watched Abraham test and reject one candidate, and the pattern is now available to be repeated with the other two. The sūrah has also established the connection between the demonstration and the reader's own situation, since the Makkans watched the same sky and their ancestors had drawn the opposite conclusion from it.

The next verse takes Abraham to the moon, and the language of the search is repeated with a difference: the conclusion this time is not a rejection but a plea for guidance. The sequence is building toward the declaration Abraham makes when he has finished with the greatest of the heavenly bodies, and the sūrah treats that declaration as the destination of his reasoning.
