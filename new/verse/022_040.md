## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:40

> ˹They are˺ those who have been expelled from their homes for no reason other than proclaiming: “Our Lord is Allah.” Had Allah not repelled ˹the aggression of˺ some people by means of others, destruction would have surely claimed monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which Allah’s Name is often mentioned. Allah will certainly help those who stand up for Him. Allah is truly All-Powerful, Almighty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grievance, Stated in One Sentence**

*Alladhīna ukhrijū min diyārihim bi-ghayri ḥaqqin illā an yaqūlū rabbunā Allāh* — those who were driven out of their homes without any right, except that they say, "Our Lord is God."

The offence for which these people were expelled was a sentence. Not a raid, not a theft, not a sedition: a creed stated aloud. The verse frames it that way because the framing is the argument. Whatever else the Quraysh believed they were doing, the charge against the believers reduces to a claim about who God is.

The exception particle *illā* does the work: they were expelled for nothing *except* saying this. A person can be lawfully expelled for a crime; there is no lawful expulsion for a sentence of worship.

**The Expulsion Was the Grievance the Qur'an Cited Most**

The commentators note that this grievance is among the most frequently cited in the Qur'an against the Quraysh. The same expulsion is named in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where it is the ground of the permission to fight: *“Kill them wherever you come upon them and drive them out of the places from which they have driven you out. For persecution is far worse than killing”* (2:191).

The repetition matters legally as well as rhetorically. In the Qur'anic framework, expulsion from one's home for one's religion is not one injury among many; it is the paradigm injury, and the permission granted in verse 39 is tied to it specifically rather than to hostility in general.

**Four Words for Houses of Worship**

*Lahuddimat ṣawāmiʿu wa-biyaʿun wa-ṣalawātun wa-masājidu* — cloisters, churches, synagogues and mosques would have been pulled down.

The commentators disagreed over the assignment. *Ṣawāmiʿ* is usually taken as the cells and monasteries of Christian monks, though some linked it to the Sabians. *Biyaʿ* is generally Christian churches, possibly Jewish temples. *Ṣalawāt* is usually understood as Jewish synagogues, with some linking it to the Sabians. *Masājid* — literally places of prostration — is taken by most as the mosques of the Muslims, though one reading takes it as a general term covering houses of worship of any kind.

The disagreement is historically interesting, and theologically the point survives it regardless of which community owns which word: what is being protected is worship as such, in all its forms, in buildings belonging to several religions at once.

**A Defence of Other People's Worship**

This is the feature of the verse that is hardest to overstate. The permission to fight is being granted to a community that has been expelled, and the reason given for the permission includes the preservation of buildings belonging to other faiths.

That is not incidental colour. The verse's logic is that unchecked aggression destroys *worship*, and that the destruction is a loss regardless of who was praying in the building. The protection of monasteries and synagogues is cited as a good that the believers are being authorized to secure — the same good that secures their own mosques.

The jurists drew the consistent conclusion: the houses of worship of peoples under treaty are inviolable within and outside Islamic territory, and damaging them is forbidden. The verse's plural vocabulary produces that ruling directly.

**The Content of the Sentence They Were Expelled For**

*An yaqūlū rabbunā Allāh* — that they say: our Lord is God.

The creed is worth parsing, because the sūrah chose these words rather than others. The term used is *rabb*, Lord — the word for master, owner, sustainer and the one to whom a person is responsible. The sentence is not a claim about God's existence; the Quraysh did not deny that a high god existed. It is a claim about who runs what, and about who a person answers to.

That is precisely why it was intolerable. A sentence about existence can be added to an existing arrangement without disturbing it. A sentence about lordship cannot, because lordship is not divisible between God and the patrons, intercessors and tribal authorities that the arrangement depended on. Saying *our Lord is God* removes the middlemen, and the people who were expelled were expelled for saying it in the hearing of the people who profited from them.

**"Had God Not Repelled Some People by Others"**

*Wa-law-lā dafʿu Allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin* — and were it not for God's repelling of people, some of them by means of others.

The principle is a sober one. The world is not preserved by the victory of the good; it is preserved by a balance of restraint in which aggression is met and checked. The agents in the sentence are unnamed and undifferentiated: *some people by means of others*. Nobody is assigned the role of the righteous party.

This is the same root the sūrah used in verse 38 for God's defence of the believers, and the two are one doctrine. God pushes back against aggression through whatever and whoever He wills, and the survival of anything fragile — a community, a building, a rite — is the result.

**"God Will Help Those Who Help Him"**

*Wa-la-yanṣuranna Allāhu man yanṣuruhu* — God will certainly help whoever helps Him.

The phrasing is deliberately paradoxical. God has no need of help, and the commentators resolve it in the obvious way: helping God means helping His religion and His messenger.

The paradox carries a warning, though. "Helping God" can be claimed by anyone with a cause, and the sūrah has just specified what it means in this case: standing up for a religion whose followers were expelled for saying that their Lord is God, and whose defence includes the buildings of other people's worship. Any other claim to be helping Him is not covered by this promise.

The verse closes with two names — *qawiyy*, strong, and *ʿazīz*, mighty — and the point of closing there is that the help promised does not depend on the strength of the people being helped.
