## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:11

> Their fate will be like that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them —they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins. And Allah is severe in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Comparison That Is Also a Verdict**

The verse answers the previous sentence about wealth and children by naming a case, which is the Qur'an's standard method for confirming a principle: it points at a defeated power and asks the audience to look. What happened to the people of Pharaoh is not being recalled as ancient history for its own sake. It is offered as the fate that repeats.

*Daʾb*, the word behind "fate" in this translation, means a wont, a regular way of proceeding, the manner in which something habitually happens. The same comparison appears with the same vocabulary at 8:52: *“Their fate is that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them—they all disbelieved in Allah’s signs, so Allah seized them for their sins. Indeed, Allah is All-Powerful, severe in punishment.”* By calling the destruction a habit, the Qur'an presents it as a law that anyone can verify in the record of nations, not a stroke of unusual severity.

**"And Those Before Them" — the Chain That Makes the Lesson General**

Pharaoh is a name every audience knows, but the verse refuses to leave him alone as an example. It adds *those before them*, extending the pattern backwards to unnamed generations whose ruins were still visible in the region. The listener is being told that no part of history has been exempt from this rule.

The same method appears in the list of consumed nations: *“So We seized each ˹people˺ for their sin: against some of them We sent a storm of stones, some were overtaken by a ˹mighty˺ blast, some We caused the earth to swallow, and some We drowned. Allah would not have wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves.”* (29:40). *“like the fate of the people of Noah, ’Ȃd, Thamûd, and those after them. For Allah would never will to wrong ˹His˺ servants.”* (40:31). The variety of punishments matters less than the uniformity of the charge, and the closing sentence of 29:40 removes the only defence that could be raised: the accusation at trial is against the accused, not the judge.

**One Charge, Stated Over Every Nation**

The verse makes no mention of military weakness or economic decline. The whole case is one sentence long: *they rejected Our signs*. The signs are the revelations and the messengers that summoned those nations, so what the peoples are charged with is not ignorance but refusal — the same refusal that, in the sūrah's argument, greets a new revelation from the same God.

The pattern is spelled out in the fate of the Egyptians, where the signs are named one by one: *“So We plagued them with floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood—all as clear signs, but they persisted in arrogance and were a wicked people.”* (7:133). Their doom is stated as a direct consequence of that persistence: *“So We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them in the sea for denying Our signs and being heedless of them.”* (7:136). The Book ties a nation's destruction to a decision it kept making in the presence of evidence.

**"Allah Seized Them" — Punishment as Undramatic**

The verb *akhadha* means simply to take. Arabic chronicles use it for a seizure in war; the Qur'an uses it for God's punishment quietly, without a thunderclap, as though the matter required no extra description. What is being conveyed is that the punishment was not a new policy. It was the removal of a people who had exhausted their opportunity.

The verse also keeps the agency where the Book always keeps it. It does not say the Egyptians were destroyed by a tide, a plague, or a rival empire; it says Allah seized them. The causes were visible and the order came from above them, which is the pattern the sūrah will apply to its own audience: armies assemble, hunger spreads, dynasties fall, and behind the machinery stands the one who set it running. A reader who grasps that stops assigning final weight to either the strength or the weakness of the people in front of him.

The related verb in 8:54 tells the same story with the sea as the instrument and the sins as the reason: *“That was the case with Pharaoh’s people and those before them—they all rejected the signs of their Lord, so We destroyed them for their sins and drowned Pharaoh’s people. They were all wrongdoers.”* Nobody in the narrative is ambushed. Each generation receives, refuses, and is taken, and the taking is described as calmly as a settlement of accounts.

**"Severe in Punishment" — Why the Sūrah Keeps Saying It**

The verse closes by naming the attribute again, as 3:4 closed, and the repetition is part of the sūrah's work. A book that spends long passages inviting people to reflect, and arguing with them where they disagree, keeps reminding the reader what refusal costs. The warning is a consequence of mercy, not its opposite; a teacher who concealed the stakes would not be honest.

The same attribute is attached to God in contexts about stewardship, since He is exact as well as severe: *“He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you. Surely your Lord is swift in punishment, but He is certainly AllForgiving, Most Merciful.”* (6:165). Severity and forgiveness are named together in the same verse, because the reader needs both facts in view before deciding what to do with what he has inherited.

**The Word That Can Mean Either a Case or a Way**

The noun rendered as affair carries two senses in Arabic, and the commentators registered both. It can name a matter or a case, which is how the verse is usually read: the fate of these peoples is being cited as an example, and the comparison is between the message and the response it met. It can also name a wont, a settled way of acting, which turns the sentence into a statement about something that happens repeatedly rather than about one event in the past.

The illustration the commentators attached to the warning comes from the story of Egypt, where the Book lists the floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood that were sent as clear signs while the people remained arrogant (7:133). The verb rendered as seized was explained through its use elsewhere, where being taken by God means being punished, as in the demand made of Moses for a visible proof and in the warning given to the people of Thamūd about the she-camel (4:153; 7:73). The choice of the verb is therefore not neutral: the sentence describes an outcome that was decided rather than an accident that befell the nations.
