## Sūrah an-Naml 27:50

> And ˹so˺ they made a plan, but We too made a plan, while they were unaware.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Verb, Used Twice**

The Arabic repeats the same word deliberately: *wa makarū makran wa makarnā makran*. They plotted a plot, and We plotted a plot. The verb and its cognate object appear on both sides of the sentence, and the mirroring is the whole point.

The construction is a known rhetorical device and the Qur'an uses it in several places where human scheming is answered. It produces an effect no paraphrase can reproduce: the divine response is described in precisely the vocabulary the conspirators used for their own act, so that their word comes back at them unchanged. Whatever they thought they were doing, something of the same name was being done about them at the same time. The verse's last clause seals it — *wa hum lā yashʿurūn*, while they did not perceive. They had a plan and no idea they were inside one.

**Does God Plot?**

The word *makr* in Arabic normally carries deception, and applying it to God raises an obvious difficulty. The Qur'an nonetheless uses it, here and elsewhere, and the solution the commentators reached is a linguistic one rather than an evasion.

*Makr* at root means a covert stratagem — a plan whose workings are hidden from the one it concerns. Deceit is a frequent feature of human stratagems, not the meaning of the word itself. When the Qur'an says God devised a plot, the hidden quality is what transfers: the conspirators could not see what was being arranged. Nothing false was told to them, and no promise was broken. The Book elsewhere calls God the best of planners and states that all planning belongs to Him. Read together, these passages describe sovereignty over outcomes rather than a divine talent for lying. The rhetorical repetition here is what forces the reading — the word is chosen because they chose it.

**The Response Is Simultaneous, Not Subsequent**

Both verbs are in the past tense and the sentence presents them as concurrent. God's plan is not described as a reaction arriving after theirs matured. It was running while theirs was being sworn.

That simultaneity is what makes the closing clause devastating. The nine had spent their meeting arranging a night attack and rehearsing an alibi, confident that the only parties to the matter were themselves, Ṣāliḥ, and an heir they intended to deceive. The verse says a third party was present throughout. Their secrecy was real with respect to the city and worthless with respect to what actually determined the outcome. The hoopoe's speech in verse 25 had already named the attribute at work — God knows what is concealed and what is disclosed — and here the abstract claim is cashed out in a specific conspiracy.

**What Their Plot Cost Them**

The Qur'an does not describe the mechanism in this chapter. The next verse states the result, and the other accounts of Thamūd supply the destruction that fell on them. What this verse establishes is the shape of the reversal rather than its physics.

Al-Qurṭubī notes that the commentators offer various versions of how the plot was foiled and how the nine met their end, and that none of them is definitive. That admission is worth repeating, because the gap invites filling. [UNVERIFIED: the detailed accounts in the tafsīr literature of how the conspirators died — collapsing rock, an angel's blow, and other variants — have no chain I could verify and are not in the Qur'an.] What the text gives is sufficient for its purpose: they planned, something else was planned, and they did not know. The absence of detail keeps the attention on the structure rather than on the spectacle.

**Secrecy Is the Assumption Being Attacked**

Strip the episode to its logic and the conspirators' error is a single premise. They believed that an act performed in darkness, agreed under oath, and covered by coordinated testimony would be, in the relevant sense, unobserved.

That premise is the practical form of disbelief in the Hereafter, which the chapter's fourth verse identified as the root of everything else. A man certain of a final accounting cannot plan an unwitnessed crime, because the category does not exist for him; a man who is not can plan one, and will, whenever the balance of advantage recommends it. The nine are the demonstration the chapter set up eighty verses earlier. Their oath by God, their alibi, and their emphatic claim to truthfulness are all the behaviour of people managing an audience they believe is the only audience. The result of the plan, and what remains of their city, is stated next.

**The Qur'an's Broader Language for This**

The idea in this verse recurs across the Book in related vocabulary. God is called the best of planners in two places, and elsewhere it is stated that all planning belongs to Him entirely. A parallel set of passages uses the language of *kayd*, scheming, and asserts that the divine scheme outmatches the human one.

Collected together, these verses form a consistent position rather than a scattering of images. Human beings genuinely plan, and their plans genuinely operate; the Qur'an never suggests the conspirators here were doing nothing. What it denies is that any such plan is the final determinant of what happens. The formula in this verse is the compressed version, and the structure of the destroyed-nation narratives is the extended one: in each, a scheme is laid, proceeds, and arrives somewhere other than its intended destination. Thamūd's nine are the clearest case in the chapter, because their plan is reported in full detail exactly one verse before its collapse is announced.
