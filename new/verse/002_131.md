## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:131

> When his Lord ordered him, “Submit ˹to My Will˺,” he responded, “I submit to the Lord of all worlds.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One-Word Command**

The verse files the patriarch's defining moment in its smallest possible form: a command of one word. *When his Lord said to him: Submit* — *aslim*, the imperative of the same root whose participle the builders' prayer requested at verse 128 (*make us submitters*). The command's singularity is its content: not a list, not a ritual, not a doctrine — the whole religion in one verb, the orientation before the specification. The recital does not date the scene; the tradition's accounts place this command at various stations of the patriarch's life, and the undated narration is the verse's method — the moment abstracted into pattern, every prophet's commission reduced to its root. The root-command also explains the prayer of verse 128 in retrospect: the patriarchs asked to be *made* muslim because the making had been *commanded* — the imperative arrives first, and the submission that answers it remains, at every station of its holder's life, a thing asked for. The verse's structure is thus the religion's own in miniature: Lord, command, servant, answer — four terms, no fifth, and the whole of what the recital has legislated across a hundred verses stands inside the exchange. The commands fulfilled at verse 124 — the purities, the rites, the trials — are this command's specification; the fire and the knife were its tests; and the one word remained the sum, which is why the verse can state the patriarch's entire standing in one sentence of dialogue.

**“I Have Submitted”: The Answer in the Perfect Tense**

The response is as short as the command and carries one grammatical feature the exegetes mark: *aslamtu* — I have submitted — the perfect tense, the completed act. The answer does not say *I will submit* and does not negotiate terms, delay, or conditions; it reports the submission as already accomplished at the moment of speaking, the response and the command meeting without interval. The recital's audience knew the contrast-answer: the community of verse 93, commanded under the raised mountain to hold firmly and obey, replied *we hear and disobey* — the same command-structure, the split response. Between the two answers the recital's whole moral geography lies: the perfect-tense *I have submitted* and the inverted *we hear and disobey*, the patriarch's file and Israel's file, the millah and its rejection. The answer's object completes its scope: *to the Lord of all worlds* — the submission directed not to the command's occasion but to the Commander's universal lordship. The phrase — *rabb al-ʿālamīn* — is the recitation's own opening address, the Fātiḥa's second line (*all praise belongs to Allah, Lord of all worlds*), and its appearance in the patriarch's mouth ties the Muslim's daily prayer to the patriarch's defining answer: every reciter of the Fātiḥa speaks the object of Abraham's submission in every prayer of his life. The worlds named are the plural of every realm — human, jinn, angelic, seen, unseen — and the submission is to the Rabb of the whole plurality, which is the verse's quiet completion of the millah-contest: a faith whose object is the Lord of all worlds cannot be any single community's property, because its object owns the claimants.

**Islām Before Islam**

The verse's verb is the recital's evidence for a claim the whole Qur'an makes: the submission named here is older than the name the Muslim community carries. The tradition states the principle plainly — *islām*, while the proper name of the religion brought by Muhammad ﷺ, is generalized in the Qur'an to mean the primordial religion, the true religion as such, the fundamental orientation of the soul toward God that is universal and stands among the bequests the prophets leave their children. The verse is the principle's founding exhibit: Abraham, centuries before the Mosaic law and centuries more before the Qur'an, receives the command *aslim* and answers *aslamtu* — and the exchange is the same religion the recital's own community bears by name. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān legislates the identity's exclusivity from the other side: whoever seeks a way other than islām, it will not be accepted from him, and in the Hereafter he is among the losers (3:85) — the submission-pose named as the only accepted orientation across every dispensation. The verse thereby performs the recital's genealogical politics in one dialogue: the patriarch is not a proto-Jew, not a proto-Christian, not an Arab ancestral cult-figure — he is a muslim in the Qur'an's precise sense, the first word of whose religion is the word commanded to him, and every community that claims him claims a submitter, which is a description of posture rather than of party. The command's universality is its inheritance-law: the millah passes to whoever answers it, and the answer has never been restricted to a lineage — the same lesson verse 124's covenant-condition stated in legal form, here stated in dialogue.

**Submission as Response, Not Achievement**

The verse's deepest instruction is in its sequence, and the sequence reverses the usual grammar of religious accomplishment. The command comes first; the submission is the answer. Abraham's aslamtu is not a self-generated attainment — not a discipline achieved, a state climbed to, a perfection manufactured — but a response to being addressed, the servant's word after the Lord's. The theology inside the sequence is the recital's own at every station: guidance descends before response rises (the signs sent down, the Book revealed, the messenger raised), and the human act that saves is reception rather than production. The petition of verse 128 now reads as the sequence's prayer-form — *make us submit* — asking from Allah what Allah commanded toward: the command creates the obligation, the grace creates the ability, and the perfect-tense answer holds both in one word. This is why the patriarch's submission can be commanded to increase (128) without contradiction: the achieved state is itself a received state, and reception continues for as long as the address continues. The verse leaves its reader with the exchange as model. The command arrives in every life in its own forms — the ruling that costs, the direction that changes, the truth that requires the perfect tense — and the verse files the only two answers the recital recognizes: *I have submitted*, spoken to the Lord of all worlds, or the inversion verse 93 preserved. Between the two lies the whole distance the sūrah has been measuring since its opening verses, and the patriarch's answer stands at the far end of it — one word commanded, one word returned, and the worlds' Lord named in the returning, which is the shortest complete religion the recital ever files.
