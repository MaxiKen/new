## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:131

> When his Lord ordered him, “Submit ˹to My Will˺,” he responded, “I submit to the Lord of all worlds.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command and the Answer**

The verse records a single exchange, and it belongs to the series of scenes in which Abraham's obedience is presented. The command is given in one word and answered in one sentence, and the brevity of the exchange is part of its effect.

The Book elsewhere describes Abraham as one who fulfilled what was commanded of him, and it lists his trials among the proofs of that description. The scene in this verse supplies the pattern of his life in its smallest form: a command and an immediate acceptance, without argument and without delay. The scene also illustrates the meaning of the term the sūrah has been using throughout the passage, since submission is presented here as a response to a command rather than as a general disposition. The Book's vocabulary for the religion is therefore given a concrete instance in the words of the man it praises.

**The Command to Submit**

The Arabic of the command uses the same root as the term for submission and the name of the religion, and the commentators noted that the word carries the sense of surrendering one's affairs entirely. The command therefore concerns the whole orientation of a life rather than a particular act.

The Book presents submission as the substance of the religion of every prophet, and the sūrah has used the term in the prayers at the House, in the request for a community of submitters, and in the scene that follows this one where the advice given to the children is to die in that state. The verse here supplies the pattern of the response on which all of those depend.

**"I Submit to the Lord of All Worlds"**

Abraham's reply names the one to whom he submits, and the phrase describes God as the Lord of everything that exists. The addition of the universal title is significant in a scene where the demand laid on him was absolute, since the answer identifies the authority being accepted as the authority over all creation.

The Book's use of the title elsewhere connects it with the provision and governance of every created thing, and the sūrah's opening chapter opens with it. Abraham's reply therefore states both his own act and the ground of it, and the reader is shown a submission that rests on the recognition of who God is. The reply also distinguishes submission to God from submission to any other authority, since the title names the Lord of everything and leaves no room for a rival claim. The words are brief, and the whole content of the religion the sūrah is defending is contained in them.

**Submission Before the Revelation of the Qur'an**

The commentators noted that the term used here describes a condition that existed long before the mission of Muhammad ﷺ, and that the Book applies it to the prophets of earlier generations and to their followers. The religion the sūrah is defending is therefore presented as the original one rather than as a novelty that came with the final revelation.

That reading governs the way the passage answers the claims of the communities that possessed scriptures. The Book does not argue that Abraham was a Muslim in the later, technical sense of belonging to the community of Muhammad ﷺ; it argues that the position he held, which the Book calls submission to God, is the one the revelation restores.

**The Speed of the Response**

The verse places the command and the answer side by side, and the Book does not record any hesitation between them. The same pattern appears in the account of his obedience regarding his son, where the Book states that he acted on what he had seen and that his son consented, and in the account of his migration and his confrontation with his people.

The sūrah's use of this scene is therefore exemplary rather than biographical. The Book is showing the reader what an answer to the command of God looks like, and it presents it in the words of the man whose faith the passage has been defending against the claims of later communities.

**What the Verse Contributes to the Argument**

The sentence supplies the definition of the faith the previous verse called on the reader to accept and warned against rejecting. The content of Abraham's religion was submission to the Lord of all worlds, and the remainder of the passage presents the same religion being passed to his children as a bequest.

The verses that follow record that Abraham left the advice with his descendants and that Jacob did the same, and they place the words in the mouth of the dying patriarch as well. The sūrah's argument rests on the continuity of this single position from Abraham through his line, and the exchange in this verse is its clearest statement. The sentence also provides the definition that the following verses apply to the descendants, since the bequest recorded next is an instruction to remain in the state Abraham took up. The narrative chain that runs from his answer to Jacob's deathbed is built on that single position.

**Submission as a Response to a Command**

The Book presents the religion it teaches as a response rather than as a system, and this scene shows what that means. God commands, and the servant answers; the answer is the religion, and the command is its occasion. The sūrah has been describing communities that received commands and did not answer, and it now places the opposite case in the mouth of the man both of them honoured. The brevity of the exchange also protects it from becoming a discussion. There is no negotiation, no request for delay, and no proposal of alternatives, and the Book records the words without commentary, leaving them to speak. The verses that follow carry the same content into the next generation, since the advice given to the children is to die in the state their father entered. What the reader receives from the scene is the shape of the obedience that the Book calls submission, stated in two short sentences that the community has recited ever since.
