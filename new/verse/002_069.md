## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:69

> They said, “Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour.” He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should be a bright yellow cow—pleasant to see.’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Round and the Same Distance**

The negotiation continues in the grammar established by the first question: *call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour*. The distancing pronoun returns — your Lord, not ours — and its return after the first round's rebuke (*so do as you are commanded*) is the verse's diagnostic of escalation. A rebuke received would have ended the exchange; a rebuke routed around produces the second question. The subject of the question also shifts register. The first round asked about the animal's age — a functional mark, the difference between a working beast and a spent one, arguably the kind of specification a sacrificer might need. The second asks about colour, and colour is not functional but visible: the specification of an object as spectacle. The movement from age to hue traces the questioners' actual relation to the command — less and less about performing it, more and more about surveying it. The answer, like the first, is given in full: Allah says, and the specification arrives. The divine side of this exchange never stalls, never refuses, never rebukes a second time. It answers — and each answer, in the economy the negotiation has entered, adds weight to the object being described.

**Ṣafrāʾ Fāqiʿ: The Colour at Full Saturation**

The specified cow is *ṣafrāʾ* — yellow — and the verse does not leave the yellow generic: *fāqiʿun lawnuhā*, its colour intense, deep, vivid. The Arabic participle *fāqiʿ* belongs to the language of saturation: a colour at full strength, undiluted, unmuted — the word used of a yellow so complete it holds the eye. The specification then states its effect in a second phrase: *tasurru al-nāẓirīn*, pleasing to those who look. The two clauses together describe an animal of striking beauty — not merely conforming to a colour-code but beautiful in the colour, the kind of creature people stop to look at. The Qur'an's precision here is characteristic of its handling of concrete detail: when it specifies, it specifies exactly, and the exactness does interpretive work. A bright, saturated, beautiful cow is a distinctive cow — the description is the narrowing the questioners ordered, and the narrowing runs, this round, through beauty. The commanded sacrifice is being defined into uniqueness, and the defining mark chosen is the one that attaches the eye: of all the properties a cow could be specified by — strength, breed, size — the answer selects the visible splendour that will make this particular animal, once seen, unforgettable and unreplaceable.

**Why Rarity Is the Hidden Clause**

The exegetical reports converge on the specification's consequence: a cow of this description was rare — in some accounts unique in the region — and rarity has a price. The tradition preserves that the animal eventually found was owned by a person positioned to know its worth and to charge for it; the obedience that began with *any cow* was heading, through the colour clause, toward *the cow, at its price*. The mechanism deserves stating plainly because it is the verse's hidden pedagogy: each round of questioning transfers cost from the command to the questioner, and the colour round transfers more than the age round, because beauty is what markets price. A plain cow is cheap everywhere; a beautiful one is expensive somewhere, and the somewhere is wherever the questioners must now search. The reports of the price and the search are tradition's furniture rather than the verse's text, but the verse's own trajectory supports them: a specification stated in superlatives (*intense, pleasing to see*) describes an object the ordinary herd does not contain. The questioners asked Allah to define their sacrifice, and the definition is arriving in the terms they chose — visible, exceptional, and therefore dear. What they experience as clarification is functioning, in the exchange's deeper arithmetic, as appraisal.

**The Beautiful Sacrifice Across the Scriptures**

The colour specification places this scene inside a wider scriptural pattern. The Torah's own cow-ordinance — the red heifer of Numbers 19 — specifies an animal by hue: red, without blemish, on which no yoke has come, sacrificed outside the camp and burned with cedar, hyssop, and scarlet, its ashes kept for the purification of those who have touched the dead. The two ordinances share their decisive marks: a cow defined by a specified colour, unblemished, unworked — and both connect the animal to death's pollution, the biblical rite by statute and the Qur'anic narrative by event, since this cow's slaughter will be joined to a murder case in the recital's close. The differences are equally instructive: Numbers files a permanent ritual law; al-Baqarah files a single commanded act embedded in a story about how commands are received. Neither text quotes the other, and the parallel's value is not source-criticism but theology: both testaments agree that the sacrifice offered for the gravest matters is a *specified* one — marked out, unmistakable, costly in its very definition. Islamic practice keeps the same principle in its own sacrificial law: the animal for the ʿīd is to be sound, unblemished, and the best within the offerer's means — beauty and soundness as the standing marks of what is fit to be given to Allah.

**Asking Again After a Rebuke**

The verse's psychological datum is the second question's timing. The first answer had closed with *so do as you are commanded* — a redirection from inquiry to action, the door back to cheap obedience left explicitly open. The second question walks past the open door. Whatever the questioners' conscious intent — genuine perplexity, habitual caution, stalling — the exchange now has a momentum of its own: questions beget answers, answers beget specifications, specifications beget narrower questions, and the participants find themselves deeper in a negotiation none of them planned. This is the anatomy of every delayed obedience. No one decides to make a command expensive; the expense accumulates one legitimate-sounding question at a time, each question defensible in isolation, the sequence ruinous as a whole. The Qur'an's narration preserves the sequence exactly — question, answer, question, answer — so the reader watches the trap close from outside it. The colour round is the trap's second tooth, and it is the one that engages the appetite rather than the intellect: after this answer, the questioners are not merely informed but invested, having seen in description an animal worth seeking. Commands delayed long enough acquire glamour; the obedience that was a duty becomes a quest, and quests have costs duties never carried.

**The Definition of What You Love**

Read at its longest range, the verse states a rule about divine definitions. The questioners asked Allah to specify the object of their sacrifice, and the specification that arrived describes beauty — an animal intense in colour, pleasing to the eye, singular among its kind. The Qur'an elsewhere ties righteousness to exactly this structure: giving of what one cherishes, the love of the object being the measure of the gift (3:92). A sacrifice costs what its object is loved for, and the colour clause is quietly assembling a sacrifice of maximum cost — not because Allah requires expense but because the questioners required definition, and the defined is always dearer than the generic. The verse's pastoral transfer is direct. Every reader carries commands stated in indefinite nouns — give, forgive, speak, stop — and every reader knows the first question of the negotiation: specify for me exactly what, exactly which, exactly how much. The cow's answer stands over all of it: the more precisely you ask Allah to define the sacrifice, the more precisely the definition will find what you love. The bright yellow cow, pleasing to see, is not the Qur'an's description of a difficulty but of a gift — the kind of object worth giving — and the tragedy of the negotiation is only that its conductors reached that description by the expensive road.
