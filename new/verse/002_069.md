## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:69

> They said, “Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour.” He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should be a bright yellow cow—pleasant to see.’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third Question, and a Further Specification**

The community asks for a colour, and the answer describes an animal whose yellow is emphatic and whose appearance gives pleasure to those who look at it. The Book's wording doubles the intensity of the colour, so that what is being described is not a pale or modest shade but something that stands out in a field.

The question adds a new category of detail. The first inquiry concerned the animal's condition, and it produced a specification that narrowed the choice; this one concerns appearance, and it produces a description that is easier to apply but harder to satisfy. The community was incrementally defining an animal that may not have existed in anyone's herd, and the sūrah's narrative lets the reader see the process complete itself.

**Yellow as a Description With Weight in the Book**

The Book uses the same colour word in its account of the heifer elsewhere in its descriptions of creation, and it applies the word to the yellow of a cow in this verse. The colour is not decorative in the narrative; it is a criterion the community itself requested, and it changes the search from a general slaughter into the identification of a specific beast.

The specific pleasure of the description is worth noting: the animal is pleasant to the eye. The Book's narratives about offerings rarely comment on appearance, and the phrase here is supplied in answer to a question about appearance. What the community asked to be told was what the animal looked like, and the answer gives them a sight they would have recognised if they had seen it.

**The Escalation the Community Chose**

At this point in the exchange the task has been narrowed twice, and the community has not yet made a move toward performing it. The sūrah's arrangement of the four questions produces an effect that would be lost if the narrative were compressed: the reader watches a simple duty become a search, and the search is conducted by the people who were supposed to be doing the duty.

The Book's account of the community's history as a whole follows the same curve. The sūrah has reported their demand to see God, their complaint about food, their change of words at the gate, and now their insistence on an ever more precise specification of an animal. The pattern that connects the incidents is a reluctance to accept a command on the terms in which it was given.

**What Moses Keeps Doing in the Exchange**

The answer in this verse is reported by Moses, and he delivers it without commentary. The Book shows him carrying each question upward and each answer back, and it does not describe him refusing the community's requests or protesting at their delays. His function in the narrative is exactly what the sūrah's other passages say about messengers: he conveys what he is given.

That consistency is what makes the passage readable as instruction about prophethood as well as about obedience. The messenger in the Book is not the author of the requirements he announces and not the arbitrator of the community's objections. The community's conversation is being conducted with someone who is not a party to it, and every answer in this exchange ends by pointing back to the one who issued the command.

**The Colour That Made the Animal Rare**

The description in this verse contributes to the practical difficulty the community created for itself. Reports preserved in the commentaries describe a search that ended with a single animal in the possession of one owner, sold at a heavy price; the Book itself does not narrate the search, and it does not need to, because the sequence of questions tells the reader what kind of animal was being sought.

The sūrah's point is not the market. It is the relationship between a command and the response to it. The community that received a two-word instruction and converted it into a specification with conditions ended up buying an animal at a price it would not have paid for an ordinary cow, and the narrative treats this as the natural result of its own approach rather than as a penalty imposed from outside.

**A Question With One Left to Ask**

The community has now asked about the animal's age and its colour, and one further question remains in the sūrah's account. The pattern is established, and the reader who reaches the next verse expects the exchange to continue. The Book leaves the sequence in place because the mounting precision is the point, and the instruction that had accompanied each answer is still waiting to be carried out.

**Appearance in the Book's Descriptions of Provision**

The Book describes the appearance of things when it wants a reader to notice a maker. It invites attention to the sky and asks whether any flaw can be found in it, it describes the earth spread out and furnished, and it describes the cattle from which people derive warmth and food. The description of the animal's colour in this verse belongs to that habit, and it comes in answer to a question about the animal's appearance.

What the community did with the information is another matter, and the sūrah records that separately. The colour was requested as a way of narrowing the search, and it was given as a description of beauty. The Book leaves the two purposes side by side, so that the reader can see what a community gets when it asks a question about a command and what it could have had if it had simply performed the act.
