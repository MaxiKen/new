## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:100

> Whoever emigrates in the cause of Allah will find many safe havens and bountiful resources throughout the earth. Those who leave their homes and die while emigrating to Allah and His Messenger—their reward has already been secured with Allah. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Whoever Emigrates Finds Many Refuges and Abundance**

The verse opens *wa man yuhājir fī sabīli Allāhi yajid fī al-arḍi murāghaman kathīran wa saʿah* — whoever emigrates in the cause of God will find in the earth many safe havens and bountiful resources, spaciousness. *Murāgham* — safe haven, place of turning away from hostility, ploy against the oppressor’s nose in the dust (*raghama anfuh*) — was explained as a refuge where the emigrant can live openly, as Madinah became for those leaving Makkah, and as Abyssinia briefly was, and as any land where belief can be practiced. *Saʿah* — spaciousness, abundance, ample provision — was read as breadth of earth and of rizq, the same spaciousness the angels cited — *was not God’s earth spacious enough?* (4:97) — now answered as found. The promise *yajid* — will find — was explained as certainty for the one who moves: the earth that was asked about as question becomes answer as discovery. The sūrah has shown that the oppressed cry for a deliverer from the land of oppressors (4:75) and that the helpless who cannot find *ḥīlah* or *sabīl* are hoped to be pardoned (4:98–99); here the one who can find device and way and uses them finds not desert but refuge and abundance. The verse generalises emigration beyond the historical Hijrah from Makkah: the Hijrah to Madinah after its conquest was declared ended — *lā hijrata baʿda al-fatḥ* (Bukhārī 3080; Muslim 1353) — yet the broader hijrah — abandoning what God forbade, *al-muhājir man hajara mā nahā Allāhu ʿanhu* (Bukhārī 10) — remains, and for it the promise of many havens still holds wherever a land permits God’s worship.

**Those Who Leave Homes and Die — Reward Secured**

The second clause *wa man yakhruj min baytihī muhājiran ilā Allāhi wa rasūlihī thumma yudrikhu al-mawtu fa qad waqaʿa ajruhū ʿalā Allāhi* — those who leave their homes emigrating to God and Messenger, then death overtakes them, their reward has already fallen upon, been secured with God — was explained as the sūrah’s assurance that intention and movement complete the wage even before arrival. *Yakhruj min baytihī* — leaving home — echoes the earlier hypothetical — *“had We commanded them to sacrifice selves or abandon homes few would have done so”* (4:66) — now shown as praised where done for God. *Muhājiran ilā Allāhi wa rasūlihī* — emigrating to God and Messenger — was read as the intention that distinguishes hijrah from mere travel; the same distinction the Prophet ﷺ drew: *“actions are by intentions, and the emigrant’s reward is according to what he emigrated for”* (Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907). *Thumma yudrikhu al-mawtu* — then death overtakes — was explained as dying on the way before refuge is enjoyed; *fa qad waqaʿa ajruhū ʿalā Allāhi* — his reward has fallen upon God, is secured — was read as a completed right upon divine generosity, not upon a treasury. Early reports attached the verse to a man — named as Damrah ibn Jundab or similar — who left Makkah for Madinah while ill, died at Tanʿīm, and was feared to have lost reward, upon which this assurance came. The legal point drawn was that the reward is secured at departure with right intention, not at arrival, which is why those who die after leaving are not counted among those who wronged themselves by staying (4:97).

**And God Is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful**

The seal *wa kāna Allāhu Ghafūran Raḥīmā* — And God is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful — was explained as the suitability to both clauses: forgiving the emigrant’s past sins in the land left, faults in the manner of leaving, and the incompleteness of a journey cut by death; merciful in giving many havens where one was expected and in securing reward where death intervened. The same pair sealed the pardon hoped for the helpless (4:99) with *ʿAfuww Ghafūr*, and the striver’s ranks (4:96) with *Ghafūr Raḥīm*; here again *Ghafūr Raḥīm* covers both Movement and mortality. The sūrah has said *“God does not wrong even an atom, and if it is good He multiplies”* (4:40) and *“had they done what they were advised it would have been better and would have brought great reward”* (4:66–67); emigration is that advice made spatial, and the multiplication is many havens and secured wage even when death cuts short. The pairing also shows that emigration is not presented as heroic autonomy but as forgiveness and mercy received: the emigrant does not earn spaciousness by might; He finds it because the Ever-Forgiving opens it.

**A Verse That Answers the Angels’ Question With Geography and Grace**

Placed after the angels’ *“was not God’s earth spacious enough?”* (4:97), the exception for the helpless (4:98), and the hope for pardon (4:99), this verse answers the question with geography and grace. To those able to devise and find a way, the answer is *yajid* — you will find — many havens and abundance; to those whose way was cut by death, the answer is *waqaʿa ajruhū* — reward secured. The sūrah that opened from a single soul spread across earth (4:1) and that said God wills to make clear, to guide to noble ways, and to turn in mercy (4:26–27), now makes clear that the earth’s width is not an abstraction but a set of actual refuges where prayer can be shortened without fear (4:101) and where the burdens lightened for the weak (4:28) are lightened further by place. The ethic extends beyond place to intention: the emigrant to God and Messenger is secured even without arrival, as the martyr is secure even without victory — *whether they achieve martyrdom or victory We honour with great reward* (4:74). For the community commanded to go forth in groups or together (4:71) and to be sure before fighting (4:94), the verse lightens departure: you will find refuge and abundance, and if death finds you first, your wage has already fallen upon the One who never wrongs.

**The Hijrah That Remains**

Though revealed about the Hijrah from the land of oppressors, the verse was read as remaining principle: wherever a land makes faith unlivable and another makes it livable, the instruction to find *murāgham* holds. The Prophet’s declaration after the Conquest (8 AH) that no further Hijrah from Makkah as emigration from unbelief’s abode was required (Bukhārī 3080) did not close the wider meaning: the emigrant is one who abandons what God forbade, and such abandonment still finds many havens. The verse therefore holds geography and morality together: to leave a place where the self is wronged for a place where the self can obey is not flight from God’s decree — both good and evil are destined (4:78) — but the means by which the evil that is from the self is refused. The next passage will apply the same spaciousness to prayer itself, permitting its shortening when travel brings fear of attack from those who are your sworn enemies (4:101).
