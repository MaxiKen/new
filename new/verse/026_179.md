## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:179

> So fear Allah, and obey me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Trustworthiness Calls for Obedience**

Shuʿayb has identified himself as a trustworthy messenger. The response is now direct: reverence God and obey the messenger. The people are not asked merely to admire his fairness or agree that dishonest conduct is unfortunate. They are asked to let divine guidance govern their own dealings.

The order protects the source of authority. Fear belongs to Allah, while obedience to Shuʿayb follows because he faithfully conveys God's command. The messenger is not asking the people to worship him or to replace God with a human judge. He is directing them back to the Lord.


Reverence for God is often spoken of as an inner state, but Shuʿayb's call gives it an economic form. A person who fears Allah does not alter a measure when no one is watching, hide a defect from a buyer, or use power to make another accept an unfair exchange. Taqwa makes ordinary commerce morally visible.

That visibility is not meant to create paranoia. It creates honesty. The trader and customer remain human beings who can make mistakes, but deliberate deception is no longer protected by the thought that only human eyes matter. God's knowledge reaches every transaction and every concealed intention.

**Obedience Is More Than Formal Compliance**

A person may follow a rule outwardly while searching for a loophole that preserves the same injustice. Shuʿayb's command requires more than technical compliance. It asks the people to obey the moral purpose of God's guidance: honesty, fairness, and protection from exploitation.

This is why obedience begins with fear of Allah. Without reverence, a person may treat rules as obstacles to outsmart. With taqwa, he asks what justice requires even where a narrow reading might allow him to take advantage. The spirit and form of obedience belong together.


Shuʿayb does not call the people to obey him so that he can manage their markets or seize their wealth. He calls them to Allah, whose authority covers both spiritual worship and public exchange. His role is to convey a standard, not to become a new merchant prince.

This distinction matters whenever religious leadership and economic power meet. A true messenger makes people more accountable to God, not more dependent on his personal business. Obedience to Shuʿayb should produce honest dealings among the people rather than a new hierarchy centered on Shuʿayb himself.

**A Parallel Links Worship With Social Repair**

Quran 29:36 records Shuʿayb calling Midian to worship Allah, hope for the Last Day, and avoid corruption in the land. The parallel shows that reverence and obedience are not separate from public reform. Belief in the Last Day gives present conduct lasting significance.

The community cannot claim to fear God while treating the marketplace as a zone without accountability. Nor can it claim to repair society while ignoring worship. Shuʿayb's command joins both: recognize the Lord, then obey the guidance that protects people from one another's greed.


In an unequal exchange, the stronger party can often make the weaker accept terms he would not choose freely. Fear of Allah limits that advantage. It tells the one with wealth, information, or authority that capacity to pressure another person is not permission to do so.

This protection is a practical expression of mercy. A just transaction is not one in which both sides merely sign a document; it is one in which neither party is deliberately deceived or coerced. Shuʿayb's call gives economic life a moral center that human power alone cannot supply.

**Taqwa Makes Hidden Conduct Serious**

Public reputation can reward a successful cheat for a time. He may be praised for profit while victims remain unheard. Obedience to God introduces a judgment beyond reputation. The hidden reduction in a measure and the concealed alteration in a contract are not hidden from the Lord.

This unseen accountability is especially important where institutions are weak. It does not replace good law or honest enforcement, but it gives the individual a reason to remain just when no penalty seems likely. Fear of Allah makes integrity possible in the space between human inspections.


Some people may benefit from dishonest practice and therefore resist Shuʿayb. Others may fear losing customers or social standing if they become honest first. Obedience may require a community to accept short-term loss in order to recover trust and blessing.

A faithful society supports those who choose honesty. It does not mock them as foolish or force them back into corruption. The command belongs to everyone, but leaders and influential traders bear special responsibility for making obedience easier rather than more costly for the vulnerable.


The verse is short because the decision is clear. Will the people allow fear of Allah to regulate what they buy, sell, measure, promise, and conceal? Or will they obey the appetite for profit and call the result success?

For the reader, Shuʿayb's command extends beyond commerce. It reaches every place where one person can take advantage of another. Reverence is real when it restrains the hand, and obedience is real when the messenger's call changes the structure of daily dealings.

The command also guards against a false division between religious and economic life. A person cannot reserve obedience for words of worship and claim independence in contracts, weights, employment, or public power. The Lord who hears a prayer also knows who receives less than is due. Shuʿayb's call joins the two spheres because truth must govern the whole person. It reaches the hand that counts, the voice that promises, and the authority that approves a contract. No corner of life is too ordinary for obedience, and no profit is too small to be examined when another person bears the loss. Economic honesty is therefore part of worship, not a separate concern reserved for public sermons.
