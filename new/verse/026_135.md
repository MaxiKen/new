## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:135

> I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hūd's Warning Comes From Fear for Them**

After recalling the gifts given to ʿĀd, Hūd explains why he has urged reverence and obedience. He fears for them because their rejection places them before a tremendous day. The sentence reveals the emotional source of the warning: not envy of their prosperity, but concern about what their choices will bring.

A messenger who sees danger and remains silent would not be trustworthy. Hūd has already stated that he seeks no payment. Here he shows that his motive is protective. He wants the people to escape a consequence that their vanity and violence are making more likely.


The word tremendous gives the coming day a scale that exceeds ordinary human disputes. ʿĀd may think only in terms of rivals, buildings, and present security. Hūd places their lives before a judgment larger than any authority they recognize on earth. Their wealth cannot make that day small.

A serious warning should not be softened into a vague suggestion that every choice has harmless effects. Some actions destroy lives and corrupt communities. God is merciful, but mercy does not make accountability unreal. The fear Hūd expresses protects the people from treating moral consequence as an empty threat.

**Fear and Hope Are Not Opposites**

Hūd's warning occurs amid a reminder of provision. This pairing matters. The people are told both that God has cared for them and that He will judge their response. Hope in divine generosity should lead to gratitude, while fear of judgment should prevent gratitude from becoming complacency.

A balanced heart needs both directions. Fear without hope can produce despair, and hope without fear can produce presumption. Hūd speaks to a people in danger of presumption. Their abundance has made them feel secure, so he restores the seriousness that their comfort has erased.


Hūd does not say that the punishment is desirable or that he takes pleasure in imagining it. He says he fears for them. His words carry urgency because delay is dangerous, but they do not celebrate the people's destruction. The true guide can oppose a people's conduct while still wanting its repentance.

This distinction matters when warning is delivered in public life. Correcting wrongdoing is not permission to despise the wrongdoer or enjoy his downfall. Hūd's fear keeps the warning morally clean. He wants justice to be avoided through return, not witnessed as entertainment.

**A Verified Parallel Shows the Same Warning**

Quran 46:21 describes Hūd warning ʿĀd at the sand-hills and saying that he fears for them a tremendous day's punishment. The parallel confirms that this fear belongs to the heart of his mission. It is not an exaggerated phrase added only after the people's destruction; it is spoken before the outcome is visible.

That passage also situates Hūd among warners who came before and after him. The warning is therefore part of a recurring mercy: God sends guidance before judgment so that a people can recognize its danger while a response remains possible.


ʿĀd's horizon has been narrowed to what it can build, defend, and command. Hūd opens it again. The tremendous day makes present decisions transparent. A monument, a castle, or a victorious attack cannot be considered apart from the account that follows life.

This wider horizon does not make earthly duties irrelevant. It makes them more serious. The way people use provision, treat dependents, and respond to warning will matter beyond the applause of their contemporaries. A society that remembers the final day will not be satisfied with looking successful while harming others.

**Fear Is a Form of Mercy**

Many people regard fear as the opposite of care. Hūd shows that appropriate fear can be protective. A child warned away from fire is not being denied love; he is being preserved by it. In the same way, the warning of punishment is mercy when it turns a person away from a path of ruin.

The warning becomes harmful only when it is detached from truth, proportion, and hope. Hūd's message has all three. He identifies the Lord, recalls real blessings, names the people's conduct, and points to the consequence. His fear is disciplined by revelation rather than by personal anger.


Hūd speaks of the future day before it arrives. That timing is crucial. A warning after judgment would confirm the sentence but would not offer the same opportunity to change. The present verse stands at the threshold where ʿĀd can still exchange pride for gratitude and tyranny for justice.

The people may think that continued prosperity proves there is no danger. Hūd teaches the opposite: time before punishment is a chance to repent, not proof that accountability has been cancelled. The longer the warning is resisted, the more serious the refusal becomes.

**The Warning Includes Every Gift They Have Received**

Cattle, children, gardens, and springs are not unrelated to the tremendous day. They are part of the evidence that the people were cared for and part of the trust for which they will answer. Blessings increase responsibility because they give a society more capacity to do good.

ʿĀd cannot say that it lacked means to live differently. It had provision, strength, and guidance. Hūd's fear arises because a richly supplied people is using its gifts against the purpose for which gratitude would have directed them. The warning calls the gifts back to their Giver before they become witnesses against their owners.


Hūd has spoken as a brother, a trustworthy messenger, and a servant who seeks no reward. Now he speaks as one who foresees danger. The combination should make ʿĀd pause. Their familiar countryman is not flattering them, and he is not inventing a threat to gain money. He is asking them to take God's judgment seriously.

For the reader, the verse asks whether warnings are heard only when consequences become visible. The wise response comes earlier. Fear the tremendous day by living gratefully now, obeying the Lord before the opportunity to change is gone.
