## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:22

> Whenever they try to escape from Hell—out of anguish—they will be forced back into it, ˹and will be told,˺ “Taste the torment of burning!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Desire to Leave Never Stops**

*Kullamā arādū an yakhrujū minhā min ghammin* — every time they want to get out of it, out of anguish. The construction is *kullamā*, whenever, every time: a repeated action, not a single attempt.

That little word is the verse's most frightening detail, and it is easy to read past. The punishment is not described as a state into which a person settles and grows accustomed. It is described as a loop: the desire rises, the attempt is made, the return happens, and the desire rises again. Whatever else the Fire does, it does not dull the wish to leave it.

*Ghamm* is the word for grief that presses in, the distress of being enclosed. It is the emotion of a trap, and its presence here explains the attempt. These are not people exploring; they are people trying to get out because they cannot bear to be in.

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ builds the same loop around the same word: *“Whenever their skin is burnt completely, We will replace it so they will ˹constantly˺ taste the punishment”* (4:56). *Kullamā* in both verses — whenever, every time — with the renewal defeating any hope of exhaustion. Burnt skin replaced, escape attempted and reversed: the loop is the Qur'an's grammar for a state that neither ends nor dulls. The desire to leave and the skin that keeps returning are two forms of one permanence.

**Returned, and by Whom?**

*Uʿīdū fīhā* — they are returned into it. The verb is passive, in the plural, with no agent named.

The grammar is worth pausing on. Elsewhere in the Qur'an the keepers of the Fire speak and act, and al-Ṭabarī takes the accompanying command as theirs. Here, however, the return is stated without a guard, without a blow, and without a hand. The Fire is described as running on its own logic: a person who wants out is in, and wanting is not a mechanism of exit.

That impersonality is part of what makes the description unbearable. A prison with a warder can be negotiated with, resisted, or escaped. A state of affairs cannot.

Sūrah al-Sajdah stages the identical scene with the verdict attached: *“Whenever they try to escape from it, they will be forced back into it, and will be told, ‘Taste the Fire's torment, which you used to deny’”* (32:20). The same passive return, the same tasting, with the addition this verse leaves implicit — *which you used to deny*. The denial named at the moment of tasting is what answers the question of agency: no guard needs naming, because the return is the denial arriving at its destination.

**The Words That Accompany the Return**

Then comes the address: *dhūqū ʿadhāba al-ḥarīq* — taste the punishment of the burning. Al-Ṭabarī and others take this as spoken by the custodians of the Fire, which fits the pattern of the Qur'an's judgment scenes, where the announcement is made to the person rather than merely happening to him.

The imperative *dhūqū* picks up the vocabulary the sūrah used in verse 9, where God said *We will make them taste* the torment of burning. The same verb, in the same sūrah, eight verses apart — once as a warning of what is coming, once as the thing itself, now being said to a face.

The address is also, in its way, an explanation. The word *ḥarīq* is fire that burns and spreads. What is being tasted is not a temperature but a verdict, and the sentence tells the sufferer what he is experiencing so that he cannot experience it as accident.

**Why an Escape Attempt Appears in the Text At All**

Modern readers sometimes treat the attempt-and-return as a narrative detail, as though the Qur'an were describing a scene with a doorway in it. It is not that. The function of the *kullamā* construction is to state permanence in the only way a language can state it without a flat assertion.

The Qur'an could have said that they will remain there forever. It says instead that every attempt to leave is followed by a return, and it says it in a form that implies the attempts never end. The reader draws the conclusion, and drawing it is more forceful than being told. The same device appears throughout the Qur'an's descriptions of both destinations: rather than abstract statements of eternity, a repeated action that never resolves.

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the wish and its frustration in the barest terms: *“They will be desperate to get out of the Fire but they will never be able to. And they will suffer an everlasting punishment”* (5:37). Desperate, unable, everlasting — the loop of verse 22 reduced to its three terms. The *kullamā* construction says the same thing by showing rather than telling: the attempt made, the return enforced, the attempt rising again. Both forms refuse the reader any version in which wanting out becomes a way out.

**"Returned" Is the Resurrection's Own Word**

*Uʿīdū* — they are returned — is from the same root the Qur'an uses for the resurrection of the dead. These people were returned to life, and now they are returned to the Fire.

The echo is deliberate and it is bleak. The verb that names the great hope of the sūrah's opening verses — that God will raise those in the graves (22:7) — reappears here with a different destination attached. The same act that brought them back now holds them in.

Read that way, the verse is the furthest point the sūrah reaches from its opening promise, and the distance is measured in one repeated root.

Sūrah Ṭā-Hā gives the root its full cycle, from dust to dust to rising: *“From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you back again”* (20:55). Created, returned, brought back — the three motions of *ʿāda* across a human history. Verse 22's *uʿīdū* is the root turned one more time, after the cycle should have ended: returned to life at the graves, then returned to the Fire at every escape. The resurrection's verb, made into the punishment's.

**The Keepers Who Ask Questions**

The Qur'an's Fire has custodians, and their words are recorded in two passages that illuminate this one. At the arrival, *“its keepers will ask them: ‘Did messengers not come to you from among yourselves, reciting to you the revelations of your Lord and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?’ The disbelievers will cry, ‘Yes ˹indeed˺! But the decree of torment has come to pass against the disbelievers’”* (39:71). Sūrah al-Mulk records the same exchange in a harder form: *“almost bursting in fury. Every time a group is cast into it, its keepers will ask them, ‘Did a warner not come to you?’”* (67:8).

The question is always the same, and it is never "why did you do this?" It is "did anyone warn you?" The exchange establishes the thing that makes the punishment just: a warning arrived, was heard, and was refused. The confession recorded in 39:71 is an admission that it arrived.

That is the context in which the command to taste should be read. It is not the first word spoken to these people. It is the last.

**Anguish Is the Punishment, Not Its Side Effect**

The final thing to draw out is the placement of *min ghammin*, from anguish, inside the clause about wanting to leave.

The punishment here is not only what the Fire does to a body. It is the frustration of a will that keeps operating and never gets what it wants. The sufferer is intact enough to desire, to attempt, and to be disappointed — and that is the horror the verse is built around. A person who had been reduced to nothing would not be trying to get out.

This is consistent with the sūrah's whole anthropology. Human beings were described in verse 18 as the only part of creation that can be on either side of the line, and in verse 11 as creatures whose response to events decides them. What is preserved in the Fire is the thing that made them human to begin with: a will, still reaching, still refused.

Sūrah Ibrāhīm describes the same preservation of the sufferer at its limit: *“Death will overwhelm them from every side, yet they will not ˹be able to˺ die”* (14:17). Death arriving from all directions and never arriving — the will intact, the exit sealed. Verse 22's *min ghammin* is that condition from the inside: anguish enough to want out, life enough to keep wanting, and a return that answers every attempt. What is preserved is precisely what makes the preservation unbearable.
