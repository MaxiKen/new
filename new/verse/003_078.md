## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:78

> There are some among them who distort the Book with their tongues to make you think this ˹distortion˺ is from the Book—but it is not what the Book says. They say, “It is from Allah”—but it is not from Allah. And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Charge About Speech**

The verse describes a group among the People of the Book as distorting the book with their tongues, in a way that makes what they say sound as though it came from the book, when it does not, and they say of it that it is from God when it is not. The final clause names what they are doing: attributing lies to God while knowing.

The subject is therefore not the copying of a text but the recitation of one. The image is of men reading aloud, bending words as they go, so that a listener without the text in front of him hears something that the text does not contain. The Book's charge concerns the space between what is written and what is said about it, which is a space every religious community occupies whenever it explains its scripture.

**The Tongue as the Instrument**

The phrase about the tongue is exact. A distortion made with the tongue can be a change of wording, a shift of emphasis, or an explanation attached to the reading so that the hearer cannot separate the two. What all three share is that the original remains intact behind the performance, and the person performing knows where the difference lies.

The Book's other account of the same conduct uses the language of writing: woe to those who write the book with their own hands and then say that it is from God, seeking a small gain (2:79). The two verses supply the two channels by which a scripture can be made to say what it does not — the pen and the voice — and the charge in each case is the same deliberate substitution of a community's position for the text it holds. What the Book objects to is not interpretation; it is the presentation of interpretation as revelation.

**"You Think It Is From the Book"**

The clause about the hearers is what makes the offence a public one. The people being addressed are not deceiving themselves; they are managing the impression of a third party, and the verse names the intended effect.

The commentators described several occasions for such conduct, including the presentation of texts to audiences that could not read for themselves and the recitation of passages with the meanings adjusted. What all the accounts have in common is an asymmetry of knowledge: the person reciting knows the book, and the audience does not. The Book treats the possession of that advantage as a trust, and this verse is one of the places where it says what happens when the trust is spent on the speaker's own position.

**The Two Denials in the Sentence**

The verse states the same act in two ways: it is not what the Book says, and it is not from God. The first denial concerns the text, and the second concerns the source, and the Book places them in the order in which a person would check the claim.

A reader who wanted to test such a statement would begin with the book and ask whether it contained what was being attributed to it. If the text did not carry the claim, the question of its origin would be settled, and that is the sequence the verse follows. Both denials are stated without qualification by the one who sent the book down, which is the only authority competent to settle what it contains, and the verdict is delivered without the argument that produced it.

**Attributing Lies "Knowingly"**

The verse's last words return to the phrase that has run through this whole passage. The adverb is what separates the conduct described here from an error of reading.

The Book's consistency on this point is notable. It accuses the communities it criticises of many things, and the charge of ignorance is not among them; the recurring accusation is that of acting against knowledge. A person who has misread a text is to be corrected, and the Book corrects its own readers in passages that ask whether they do not understand. A person who has decided that his community's standing is worth more than the accuracy of what he attributes to God has made a different kind of decision, and the sūrah reserves its sharpest language for that one. The next verses explain what such people are trying to protect, and the answer has to do with a claim about who may be worshipped.

**Reading the Verse Inside a Community That Recites**

The Book addresses this charge to a group within the People of the Book, and then hands the same account to a new community that will recite it for centuries. The sūrah has already warned its readers about the ambitions of the group it describes, and the warning's usefulness depends on the reader's recognising the conduct rather than the people.

The offence named here is available to anyone who speaks about scripture with authority, which means it is available to the community reading the verse. What the Book provides against it is the text itself, preserved and recited in public, along with the repeated instruction not to conceal what one knows. A community that keeps its scripture audible and its explanations of it distinguishable from the text has closed the door this verse describes, and the verse's presence in that community's own book is part of the lock. The Book's own example of the alternative is worth noting as well: it reports the covenant taken from those who were given the Scripture, obliging them to make it known to people and not to hide it (3:187). The obligation to publish stands against the practice of bending, and the two verses describe the same community at the two ends of the same choice.
