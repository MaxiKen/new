## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:3

> If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. This way you are less likely to commit injustice.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Justice to Orphans Before Permission for Marriage**

This verse links two matters that seem separate — care for orphans and the number of wives a man may take — by the single concern of justice. The opening *wa in khiftum allā tuqsiṭū fī al-yatāmā* — if you fear you might fail to give orphan women their due rights — recalls the previous verse’s warning about mixing and consuming their wealth, now in the specific case where the orphan is a girl under a guardian’s authority. A guardian who also acts as marriage guardian could exploit that double role: marry the girl to himself with a reduced dowry, marry her off without her consent to secure her wealth, or block her marriage entirely to keep control of her property. The Qur’an interrupts that line of temptation with a clear exit: if you fear you cannot be just there, marry other women. The permission that follows is therefore not an encouragement to add wives but a redirection away from a particular injustice. Al-Ṭabarī records this as the core occasion, transmitted from ʿĀʾishah, who explained that men holding orphan girls would covet their beauty and wealth and then short-change them in dowry and upkeep. The verse tells such a man to leave that girl alone and choose elsewhere, where consent and valuation can be more honestly assessed.

**Two, Three, or Four — A Limit, Not a Target**

The phrase *mathnā wa thulātha wa rubāʿ* — two, three, or four — sets an upper bound, not a recommended number. Before this sūrah, there was no Qur’anic ceiling stated in this form; pre-Islamic custom allowed a man to take many wives without fixed limit. By capping the number at four, the Qur’an limited what was previously open-ended, just as it elsewhere limited retaliation, divorce pronouncements, and waiting periods. The choice of two, three, then four with the conjunction *wa* was understood by grammarians to mean any number up to four, singly or in combination, not eight or nine. The same sūrah later adds the inner condition that makes the outer number meaningful: *“You will never be able to be perfectly just between wives, even if you try”* (4:129). That later verse does not cancel this one, but it deepens its warning. A man who can manage justice outwardly — in support, housing, time — may still fall short in affection, and he is told to remain aware of that limit. The sūrah’s aim across both verses is to keep marriage inside the capacity for fairness, whether fairness to the orphan girl not yet married or to the wives already taken.

**When One Is the Just Choice**

The condition *fa in khiftum allā taʿdilū fa wāḥidah* — but if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then one — makes monogamy the safeguard for those who doubt their ability to be equitable. Justice here was explained by the early authorities to cover material provision, division of nights, and decent companionship, not only coin. The Qur’an does not treat the number of wives as a display of status; it treats it as a bearing of responsibility, and it advises the lighter burden when the heavier one risks wrongdoing. The clause *aw mā malakat aymānukum* — or those bondwomen in your possession — addressed a seventh-century institution that the Qur’an elsewhere encourages to dissolve through manumission and marriage, specifying that such women, though entitled to kind treatment, did not carry the same financial and conjugal claims as a free wife (4:25). The verse’s logic is practical: a man who cannot afford the obligations of a free marriage should not incur them and then fund them from an orphan’s property, which the previous verse had already forbidden. That economic thread connects verses 2 and 3 directly: overextension of dependents tempts a man to reach for wealth that is not his.

**The Meaning of Not Committing Injustice**

The verse closes with *adhālik adnā allā taʿūlū* — this way you are less likely to commit injustice. The verb *taʿūlū* was read by most early lexicographers as to act unjustly or to deviate, related to *ʿawl* as oppression, while a minority read it as to be overburdened with dependents. Both senses converge: limiting wives keeps a person nearer to justice and farther from being overwhelmed. That dual sense matches the sūrah’s broader pattern of keeping law inside human weakness. Two verses later it will say *“Allah wishes to lighten your burdens, for humankind was created weak”* (4:28), and here it already lightens the load by telling the anxious man to take one. The Qur’an does not treat the desire for more as shameful in itself; it treats the risk of wronging others through that desire as the decisive factor. A man is asked to judge his own case honestly before acquiring new duties. That self-assessment — am I able to be just? — is itself an act of piety, because it puts the rights of yet-unmet wives ahead of present preference.

**A Reading That Protects Consent and Dowry**

The widespread report from ʿĀʾishah ties this verse to verse 127, where the Qur’an returns to the same group: *“They ask you about women. Say, Allah answers your query concerning them, and what is recited in the Book about orphan girls whom you do not give what is ordained for them though you desire to marry them”* (4:127). That later verse was presented as clarification: men were withholding the prescribed bridal gift from orphan girls under their care while wanting to keep them. Read together, verse 3 and verse 127 draw a consistent boundary: the guardian’s authority over property does not extend to discounting the woman’s due at marriage. The dowry belongs to her, as verse 4 will immediately confirm: *“Give women their due dowries graciously”* (4:4). The connection also shows how the Qur’an reforms a power asymmetry step by step. First it orders return of wealth, then it redirects marriage away from exploitation, then it secures the woman’s personal gift, ensuring that at no point does the guardian’s stewardship become ownership of the person. The sequence is intentional and cumulative.

**Justice as the Sūrah’s Steady Measure**

This verse makes justice — *qisṭ* and *ʿadl* — the measuring stick for family formation. The same standard will recur for inheritance shares where male and female each receive a fixed portion (4:7, 4:11–12), for treatment of wives where kindness is commanded even when affection wanes (4:19), and for arbitration where mediators are sent from both families (4:35). By opening the marriage discussion with a warning about orphans, the verse teaches that entry into marriage is not only about attraction or alliance but about aptitude for fairness. That aptitude is not assumed; it is to be examined. The Qur’an pairs permission with caution in a way that keeps the law humane: what is allowed is immediately bounded by whether it can be done justly, and what is just is measured by whether the weakest party’s right remains intact. A man who hears *“two, three, or four”* and hears only an allowance has misheard; the sentence began with fear of injustice to a girl who could not argue her own dowry, and it ends with the choice that stays nearest to avoiding that wrong.
