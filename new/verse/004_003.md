## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:3

> If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. This way you are less likely to commit injustice.

### Expanded Commentary

**The verse begins with the vulnerable, not with desire**

This passage is often quoted as though it were an isolated permission concerning marriage, but its grammar and context begin elsewhere: with fear of failing orphan women in one’s care. In pre-Islamic practice, a guardian could marry a wealthy orphan girl himself, diminish her bridal gift, or prevent her from marrying someone who would take her property outside his control. The reported explanation from ʿĀʾishah, preserved in the canonical hadith collections, identifies the remedy: if a guardian cannot deal fairly with a girl under his authority, he must marry someone else rather than turn guardianship into a route to exploitation.

**Limitation rather than an unlimited innovation**

The permission to marry two, three, or four is bounded by the immediate condition of justice and by the final instruction to marry only one when justice is feared. It therefore limited an existing and sometimes unrestricted practice rather than introducing an unqualified ideal. The number is a ceiling, not a target. The order of the verse is ethically significant: the text does not first celebrate plurality and add justice as a footnote; it places the protection of vulnerable women at the entrance and justice at the exit.

Classical law understood justice between wives to include maintenance, housing, time, and the outward rights that can be measured. It does not require a man to manufacture identical feelings, since verse 129 states that complete emotional equality is beyond human capacity. That limitation does not excuse unequal treatment. It distinguishes involuntary inclination from deliberate favoritism and demands that the latter be controlled.

**The social burden of marriage**

Marriage is represented here as a responsibility with financial and legal consequences. A man who takes several wives while unable to feed, house, or protect them may be tempted to consume the estates of orphans or other dependants. The verse therefore connects polygyny to fiduciary ethics: a choice is not responsible merely because it is formally permitted; it must be supported by the rights of the people affected by it.

The phrase concerning those “in your possession” belongs to the legal world of slavery in which the revelation first addressed its audience. It cannot be detached from the Qur’an’s wider regulation, emancipation incentives, and insistence on humane treatment. Modern readers should not turn a historical category into a casual recommendation, nor should they use it to evade the verse’s controlling principle: no dependent person may be treated unjustly.

**Justice as the governing test**

The concluding rationale—being less likely to commit injustice—makes the verse a moral examination, not a license for appetite. Where justice cannot be credibly maintained, monogamy is the commanded safeguard. The passage also shows how Qur’anic legal guidance works: it engages an existing institution, restricts it, attaches duties to it, and directs the conscience toward the protection of those with less power. Any reading that makes the wealthy guardian’s preference more important than the orphan girl’s rights has inverted the verse’s purpose.

**The justice test in practice**

A marriage proposal involving a woman whose property is controlled by a guardian should be examined for conflict of interest. Does she have independent advice? Is her dowry fair? Can she refuse without losing shelter or inheritance? These questions give practical form to the verse’s concern. The legal maximum cannot turn an exploitative arrangement into a righteous one; justice remains the condition that governs every permission.
