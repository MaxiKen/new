## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:23

> O believers! Do not take your parents and siblings as trusted allies if they choose disbelief over belief. And whoever of you does so, they are the ˹true˺ wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith Can Conflict With Family Loyalty**

The verse addresses believers with a command that touches the deepest human bonds: parents and siblings. These are not casual friendships that can be set aside without pain. Family provides identity, affection, shelter, and the sense of belonging that shapes a person's earliest life. The command therefore does not pretend that choosing faith over a hostile family preference is easy. It states that no created relationship may occupy the place of ultimate loyalty when it asks the believer to prefer disbelief over belief.

The forbidden role is expressed through trusted alliance, not through ordinary kindness. The believer is not told to hate a parent or abandon every duty toward a sibling. The issue is who receives the authority of walāyah: who is trusted to direct the believer's deepest commitments and defend a way of life opposed to Allah. Family love remains real, but it cannot become the court that overrules revelation.

**The Qur'an Distinguishes Kindness From Allegiance**

Another passage sets the distinction clearly. If parents strive to make a child associate partners with Allah, the child must not obey them in that matter, yet must keep their company in the world with kindness (31:15). This verse and 31:15 belong together. Disobedience to a parent in a matter of faith does not authorize cruelty, insult, or neglect. The believer refuses the false demand while preserving whatever kindness remains lawful.

This balance is harder than either extreme. Some people use family loyalty to excuse disobedience; others use religious loyalty to excuse harshness. The Qur'an allows neither. Parents retain rights of respect and care, but they do not have the right to command disbelief. Siblings may be loved and helped, but their approval cannot become the believer's inner qiblah. Faith sets a limit on obedience without turning the heart into stone.

**Trusted Alliance Means More Than Affection**

Walāyah includes nearness, protection, and reliance. It can describe the person whose counsel is admitted into the inner circle and whose values shape collective action. The verse does not prohibit a believer from working fairly with non-Muslim relatives or receiving their advice in worldly matters. It prohibits making their rejection of belief the basis of a governing alliance against faith.

That difference helps prevent the verse from being misapplied. A Muslim may have a non-Muslim parent, spouse, colleague, or neighbor. Justice, gratitude, and good treatment remain required. What the believer cannot do is make disbelief the standard by which he chooses truth, or join a campaign that seeks to uproot belief because the campaign is led by relatives. The question is not whether affection exists. The question is whether affection has been promoted into obedience against Allah.

**The Historical Pressure Was Real**

The first believers faced families who could pressure them to stay in Makkah, reject the Messenger, or return to the old religious order. Migration could mean separation from parents and brothers who controlled protection and property. A person who obeyed the family might preserve immediate safety while losing the community of faith. A person who followed Allah might be called disloyal by the people who had taught him loyalty.

The verse speaks into that pressure without denying its emotional weight. It gives the believer a higher reference so that the cost of obedience cannot be decided by the family group alone. This does not turn every disagreement with relatives into a test of faith. The condition is specific: they choose disbelief over belief and the believer takes them as the trusted allies who govern his loyalty. The text addresses a conflict of ultimate commitments, not normal family difference.

**Wrongdoing Is Misplaced Loyalty**

The verse calls those who make the forbidden alliance wrongdoers. Qur'anic wrongdoing often carries the idea of putting something where it does not belong. Family belongs in the sphere of love, care, and lawful respect. Allah belongs in the position of ultimate worship and obedience. When family approval is placed above Allah, the order is reversed. The person may feel loyal, but the loyalty has become unjust because it has displaced the rightful claim.

This helps the believer diagnose subtle compromises. A person may not be asked to deny the shahādah openly. He may simply be expected to hide prayer, accept a forbidden income, approve a false belief, or remain silent when family status depends on injustice. Each case requires wisdom, but the principle remains. The fear of disappointing relatives cannot decide what Allah has made clear.

**Honoring Parents Within the Boundary**

For modern believers, the verse should lead to careful practice rather than dramatic estrangement. Serve aging parents, speak with gentleness, keep family ties, and accept ordinary differences with patience. At the same time, refuse requests that require disbelief, injustice, or abandonment of an established duty. Explain the refusal without contempt. A boundary stated with kindness is closer to the Qur'anic balance than a public performance of hostility.

The believer may also need support from the believing community when family pressure becomes dangerous. Emigration in the broad moral sense can mean moving away from an influence that makes obedience impossible, while still preserving lawful ties. The verse does not promise that every family will approve. It promises that the loss of human approval cannot make a believer truly wronged when he protects the place of faith. The pain of separation is real, but it is not proof that the higher loyalty was mistaken. The believer may grieve the family conflict while refusing to make grief the judge of revelation.
The command protects the community from a loyalty that quietly asks faith to surrender. Believers can remain just to relatives while refusing to let family pressure decide whom they obey.
