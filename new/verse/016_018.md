## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:18

> **If you tried to count Allah’s blessings, you would never be able to number them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Uncountability As Proof Of Abundance**

Wa-in taʿuddū niʿmata Allāhi lā tuḥṣūhā — if you try to count Allah's blessing, you will not number it. Taʿuddū from ʿ-d-d means to count, enumerate, list. Niʿmah is blessing, favor, gift. Tuḥṣūhā from ḥ-ṣ-y means to encompass by counting, to compute fully, to arrive at total. The verse says even if you attempt to count, you will fail to reach an end. Al-Ṭabarī explains that niʿmah here is singular but means plural, all blessings, or that even one blessing contains within it many blessings that cannot be counted. For example, the blessing of sight includes eye, light, brain, recognition, all of which contain further blessings. Al-Rāzī gives a mathematical argument: blessings are of many types — creation, preservation, guidance, forgiveness — and each type has many individuals, and each individual has many aspects, so counting is impossible. The verse does not say blessings are many; it says they are uncountable, which is stronger. Many can be counted with effort; uncountable cannot.

**Why This Verse Comes After The Catalogue**

Verses 5-16 listed perhaps twenty blessings in detail: cattle, warmth, food, beauty, transport, horses, unknown creations, guidance, rain, drink, pasture, crops, olives, palms, grapes, all fruits, night, day, sun, moon, stars, earth colors, sea, tender meat, ornaments, ships, mountains, rivers, paths, landmarks, stars. After such a list, a listener might think, now I have heard the blessings. Verse 18 says, you have not. What you heard is a sample, not a total. If you tried to count, you would fail. This serves two purposes. First, it prevents pride: you cannot say I have thanked enough because you cannot even count what you must thank for. Second, it magnifies gratitude: if blessings are uncountable, then gratitude must be continual, not occasional. The verse is therefore both humbling and encouraging. Humbling because it shows human inability to encompass divine favor; encouraging because it shows divine generosity exceeds human capacity to list.

**All-Forgiving, Most Merciful After Uncountability**

Inna Allāha la-ghafūrun raḥīm — surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. Why forgiveness after counting blessings? Al-Zamakhsharī explains that since you cannot count blessings, you cannot thank adequately, so you need forgiveness for shortcomings in gratitude. The verse anticipates the feeling of inadequacy that uncountability produces and answers it with two names. Ghafūr, from gh-f-r, means covering, forgiving, protecting from consequences of shortcomings. Raḥīm means merciful, granting more despite shortcomings. The pairing tells the believer: you will fail to count and fail to thank fully, and Allah knows this and forgives and continues to be merciful. Al-Qurṭubī adds that ghafūr here also refers to Allah's forgiveness of those who were ungrateful but then turn, and raḥīm refers to His continued provision despite ingratitude. The same uncountable blessings that prove Allah's generosity also prove need for His forgiveness, because every blessing used without thanks is a fault that needs covering.

**A Verse Used As A Name For The Sūrah**

Al-Qurṭubī reports that some early scholars called this sūrah Sūrat al-Niʿam, the chapter of blessings, because of this verse and the surrounding catalogue. The verse is often quoted in Friday sermons and personal supplications to remind of gratitude. Its message is universal: every human, regardless of wealth, is surrounded by uncountable blessings — breath, heartbeat, sleep, water, food, family, guidance. The Qur'an elsewhere says *And He gave you of all that you asked of Him, and if you count Allah's blessing you will not number it; indeed man is unjust and ungrateful* (14:34). That verse ends with mention of human injustice and ingratitude, while this verse ends with divine forgiveness and mercy, showing two sides: human failure to count and thank, divine readiness to forgive and continue mercy. The pairing of 14:34 and 16:18 is instructive: same statement about uncountability, different seals, one highlighting human fault, one highlighting divine mercy.

**The Psychology Of Counting**

The verse invites an exercise: try to count. A person who tries will quickly realize the task is impossible. Even a single blessing like water contains within it rain, rivers, wells, purification, circulation in body, growth of plants, and so on. Each of those contains further blessings. The attempt to count leads to awe, not to a number. This is the Qur'an's method of teaching gratitude: not by demanding a feeling, but by inviting reflection that naturally produces feeling. Al-Rāzī notes that counting blessings is itself a blessing, because it increases awareness and gratitude, which in turn brings more blessings, as 14:7 says: *If you are grateful, I will surely increase you* (14:7). The verse therefore does not discourage counting; it encourages it while warning that you will never finish, so you should continue. Gratitude is not a task to complete but a state to live in.

**Linguistic Note On Niʿmah And Iḥṣā'**

Niʿmah from n-ʿ-m means softness, ease, blessing, from same root as naʿīm, bliss, and anʿām, cattle, which are soft blessings. The singular niʿmah can be collective, meaning blessings in general, or it can mean even one blessing is uncountable in its details. Tuḥṣūhā from ḥ-ṣ-y means to count with pebbles, to encompass, to compute. Iḥṣā' implies thorough counting, not approximate. The negation lā tuḥṣūhā therefore means you will not achieve thorough counting, even if you try. The conditional wa-in, and if, suggests a hypothetical attempt: even if you try, you will fail. The verse is thus not saying you should not try, but that trying will show you your limit and Allah's limitlessness. The two names at end, Ghafūr and Raḥīm, are both intensive forms indicating abundance of forgiveness and mercy, matching abundance of blessings.
