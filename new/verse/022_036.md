## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:36

> We have made sacrificial camels ˹and cattle˺ among the symbols of Allah, in which there is ˹much˺ good for you. So pronounce the Name of Allah over them when they are lined up ˹for sacrifice˺. Once they have fallen ˹lifeless˺ on their sides, you may eat from their meat, and feed the needy—those who do not beg, and those who do. In this way We have subjected these ˹animals˺ to you so that you may be grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Camels as Emblems**

*Wa-l-budna jaʿalnāhā lakum min shaʿāʾiri Allāhi* — and the sacrificial camels, We have made them for you among the emblems of God. The word *budn* refers to the large sacrificial animals; the jurists differed over whether it is restricted to camels or includes cattle, and both readings are defensible.

The classification is the point. An animal is being called a *shaʿīrah*, an emblem of God — the same word the sūrah used two verses before for the thing whose magnification comes from the reverence of hearts (22:32), and the same word used in Sūrah al-Baqarah for the two hills of Ṣafa and Marwah (2:158).

A camel is therefore in the same category as a hill: an ordinary creature that has been made to point at something. Nothing about the animal changes when it is dedicated. What changes is that it now carries a meaning, and the meaning is not the owner's to redefine.

Sūrah al-Baqarah applies the same word to the two hills of the pilgrimage walk: *“Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah”* (2:158). Hills and camels under one term — *shaʿāʾir* — which shows what the category holds: ordinary features of the world, stone and beast, taken up as pointers. The hill does not change when the pilgrim walks it, and the camel does not change when it is dedicated. Emblemhood is not a property added to the thing; it is a meaning the thing is made to carry.

**"In Them Is Good for You"**

*Lakum fīhā khayrun* — for you in them is good. The statement precedes the command to sacrifice, which is unusual and deliberate.

The animal is not being taken away. Before anything is demanded, the verse says that the arrangement benefits the person making it, and it says so in the widest available word — *khayr*, good, rather than any particular reward. The sūrah will repeat this structure in the verse after next, when it explains what actually reaches God.

Sūrah al-Naḥl itemises the good before any rite is mentioned: *“And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits”* (16:5). Warmth, food, benefits — the animal profitable to its owner in every direction before a single one is slaughtered. Verse 36's *lakum fīhā khayr* is that sentence recalled at the moment of sacrifice: the command to kill arrives only after the Creator has restated, in the widest word available, that the whole arrangement favours the giver.

**"When They Are Lined Up"**

*Fā-dhkurū isma Allāhi ʿalayhā ṣawāffa* — so mention God's name over them as they stand in rows. Camels are slaughtered standing, with the left foreleg tethered, and the word *ṣawāff* pictures a line of them waiting.

The detail fixes the moment of the invocation: not at the purchase, not at the dedication, and not afterwards, but at the killing. Slaughter is the one act that cannot be performed gently, and the Qur'an surrounds it with the name of the One who gave the animal rather than leaving it as a bare utility.

That is a claim about killing generally. The animal's life is taken at a named moment, for a named purpose, with a name pronounced — the three conditions that separate a sacrifice from a killing, and that the Qur'an enforces in its dietary law.

The rule for eating is stated as a test of belief itself: *“So eat only of what is slaughtered in Allah's Name if you truly believe in His revelations”* (6:118). The name at the killing is what makes the meat edible — not the hunger, not the occasion, not the animal's condition. Verse 36 fixes the invocation to the standing rows for the same reason: the moment of taking life is the moment the name must be over it, and everything eaten afterwards depends on that sentence having been said.

**"When They Have Fallen on Their Sides"**

*Fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā* — then when their sides have fallen, eat of them. Camels fall onto their sides when they are slaughtered, and the verse uses that as the marker of the moment after which the meat may be eaten.

The permission to eat was a correction of practice, as the commentators note: in the pre-Islamic period, some groups would not eat the meat of what they had sacrificed. The verse permits it, and the jurists drew the further ruling that eating is desirable rather than obligatory while feeding others is the binding part.

That asymmetry is worth noticing. The sacrificer is allowed the meal and required to give it. The act that looks like a transaction with God turns out to be a provision for other people, with the sacrificer permitted a share.

Sūrah al-Insān describes the feeding at its most costly, where the food is loved and given anyway: *“and give food — despite their desire for it — to the poor, the orphan, and the captive”* (76:8). Despite the desire — the giving that costs the giver his own appetite. Verse 36's asymmetry belongs to the same economy: the sacrificer permitted his share, the poor commanded theirs, and the binding part the part that leaves the household. The meal is shared because the provision was.

**"The One Who Asks and the One Who Does Not"**

*Wa-aṭʿimū al-qāniʿa wa-l-muʿtarr* — and feed the contented one and the one who asks. The two words describe two different poor people, and the pairing is standard in the Qur'an.

*Al-qāniʿ* is the one who is content with what he has and does not ask, whose need is therefore invisible; *al-muʿtarr* is the one who presents himself and asks. Sūrah al-Baqarah describes the first kind in a passage that is almost a commentary on this phrase: *“˹Charity is˺ for the needy who are too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land ˹for work˺. Those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need ˹of charity˺ because they do not beg”* (2:273).

The legal and moral consequence is sharp. Charity that waits to be asked for reaches only half the poor. The verse commands the sacrificer to seek out the person whose silence would otherwise cost him his share.

**"Thus We Have Subjected Them to You"**

*Ka-dhālika sakhkharnāhā lakum* — in that way We have made them subservient to you. The verb *sakhkhara* is used throughout the Qur'an for the created order placed at human disposal: *“He has subjected the ships for your service, sailing through the sea by His command”* (14:32), *“And He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon”* (16:12), and the same claim for animals, *“so that you may sit firmly on their backs, and remember your Lord’s blessings once you are settled on them”* (43:13).

The word carries an asymmetry: the subjected thing is stronger than the subject. A camel outweighs a man by a factor of six and could kill him; the sea is bigger than any ship. Subjugation here is a gift, not a conquest, and the verse draws the conclusion itself — *so that you may be grateful*.

Gratitude is the response the sūrah names, and it is the right one for a gift that the recipient did not arrange. A person who slaughters an animal that outweighs him, that could not have been subdued by force alone, and that he did not create, is being asked to notice what has been done for him.
