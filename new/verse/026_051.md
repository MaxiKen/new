## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:51

> We really hope that our Lord will forgive our sins, as we are the first to believe.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**New Faith Begins With Hope for Forgiveness**

The magicians move from accepting the Lord to asking for forgiveness. Recognition of the sign has not made them imagine that their previous service to Pharaoh was harmless. They know that their craft was used in a contest against a messenger and that their loyalty had been tied to a false claim. Belief opens their eyes to the need for repentance rather than giving them a reason to congratulate themselves.

Their hope is strong but not presumptuous. They do not demand forgiveness as though a single declaration makes every past matter irrelevant. They hope because the Lord they now recognize is capable of mercy, and they ask because their sins require pardon. This combination of hope and humility gives the confession depth. A person who sees truth clearly also sees his own need for repair.

**The Plural Sins Includes More Than One Failure**

The magicians speak of sins in the plural. The word gathers their earlier allegiance to Pharaoh, their participation in the spectacle, and the practices through which they had deceived the people. Repentance does not select only the most embarrassing act and leave the rest unnamed. It turns the person toward God with awareness that wrongdoing can form a pattern.

At the same time, the plural should not be used to erase the difference between actions. Some sins involve the abuse of power, some the use of skill to mislead, and some the inward pride that makes correction difficult. The magicians do not offer a legal inventory in this short verse. They place the whole past under God's mercy, which is the beginning of a truthful moral accounting.

**Hope Is Directed to Their New Lord**

They say our Lord. The possessive expresses belonging, but not ownership in Pharaoh's sense. God is their Lord because He created, guided, and will judge them; they are His servants because they return to Him. The phrase also marks the personal change in their allegiance. Pharaoh had promised to bring them near, but the magicians now seek forgiveness from another Lord.

This is why their hope can survive the ruler's threat. Pharaoh can withdraw his reward and harm their bodies, but he cannot close the path to the Lord they name. Their relationship with God is not a privilege granted by a court. It is the truth of their existence, newly recognized and publicly confessed.

**First Can Mean Earliest or Foremost**

The expression first to believe has been understood in several ways. It may mean the first among the Egyptians to accept this sign, the first of Pharaoh's people to believe in Moses' message, or the first sorcerers to make the confession. In another usage, first can carry the sense of being foremost rather than earliest in time. The immediate setting favors a local meaning: they are the first in this public contest to respond openly.

That interpretation preserves the force of their statement without turning it into a claim that no one before them ever believed in God. They are first in relation to the event and the audience now gathered. Their position gives their faith public significance. The people see the specialists who were expected to defend Pharaoh become the first witnesses to submit.

**Public Recognition Increases the Cost of Repentance**

The magicians do not repent in private after leaving the arena. They speak before Pharaoh and the crowd. Their hope for forgiveness is therefore joined to a visible break with their former role. They accept that the world may judge them as deserters, fools, or traitors. The cost does not make the repentance invalid; it shows that they are no longer measuring truth by public approval.

This is important because people sometimes want forgiveness without changing the relationships that depend on sin. The magicians cannot keep Pharaoh's reward and also deny the authority that reward represented. Their confession moves them into a new position, and their hope is tied to that movement. Forgiveness is not payment for a performance of regret. It is the mercy sought by people who have turned away from the service of falsehood.

**Hope Does Not Cancel Accountability**

The magicians' hope prevents despair, but it does not erase responsibility for what they did before recognizing the sign. They may still face consequences, and their victims may still have claims that require repair. The verse does not give a complete legal account of those duties. It shows the spiritual first step: bring the past honestly to God instead of defending it.

That balance protects repentance from two extremes. Despair says that past wrongdoing makes return impossible. Presumption says that saying the right words removes every obligation. The magicians stand between those errors. They hope for forgiveness while naming themselves as sinners. Their hope is credible because it is joined to humility and a willingness to bear the cost of the truth.

**Forgiveness Is Sought From the Lord, Not the Court**

Pharaoh has offered status as a reward and punishment as a threat. Neither can forgive the moral wrong the magicians now recognize. Only the Lord can judge and pardon their sins. The court's nearness cannot cleanse them, and the court's anger cannot define their final worth.

Their sentence therefore completes the movement begun by prostration. Their bodies bowed, their tongues named the Lord, and their hearts turned toward forgiveness. The contest that Pharaoh arranged has become an encounter with repentance. The first believers do not present themselves as flawless victors. They present themselves as servants hoping that the Lord will receive them.

Hope also gives their public break with Pharaoh a future. They are not defined only by the role they played in the contest or by the punishment they may face. They can become people whose first decisive act after recognition is to seek pardon. That hope does not erase their history; it prevents history from becoming an excuse to return to the same false allegiance.
