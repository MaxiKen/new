## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:24

> Moses replied, “˹He is˺ the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, if only you had sure faith.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses Answers the Question Through God's Works**

Moses does not try to contain God in a definition of essence. He points to the heavens, the earth, and all that lies between them. This is not a retreat from knowledge. It is a disciplined way to speak about the Creator through the created order that Pharaoh can see and cannot claim to have made. Divine lordship is made known through sustaining and governing the total field of existence.

The answer also avoids making God a rival within the universe. Pharaoh's authority belongs to a place, a court, and a historical moment. The Lord named by Moses is the Lord of the realms above and below and of the interval joining them. He is not one power among other powers. Everything Pharaoh uses to display grandeur already depends on the Lord whose scope surrounds it.

**Heavens and Earth Enclose Every Human Claim**

The pair heavens and earth forms a comprehensive frame. Whatever Pharaoh can point to as evidence of his rule lies inside that frame. His palace, river, army, ancestry, and land are not outside God's ownership. The words do not need to list every created thing because the pair marks the whole visible order, while everything in between closes the gaps that a ruler might try to reserve for himself.

This answer turns cosmology into moral argument. If God owns and sustains the setting in which Pharaoh rules, Pharaoh's authority is delegated and limited. He may administer, but he cannot become absolute. He may possess a title, but he cannot claim the right to enslave people as though their Lord were absent. The extent of creation sets the extent of God's claim over human action.

**Everything Between Removes the Escape of the Middle Realm**

The phrase everything in between is not filler. It blocks a common attempt to divide reality into sacred and neutral zones. Pharaoh might concede that a god created the heavens while treating the earth and human politics as his private domain. Moses denies that division. The Lord's care and authority reach the spaces where people live, labor, worship, and suffer.

That has consequences for religious language. Worship cannot be confined to ritual while injustice is declared outside God's concern. Nor can political rule be treated as a realm where the command of the Creator stops at the palace door. The middle realm belongs to God as surely as the sky and the ground do. Moses' answer brings the oppressed Israelites and the ruling court into the same created field.

**Certainty Is the Required Response**

The closing appeal, if only you had sure faith, identifies the obstacle. Moses has offered a sign of God's lordship through the structure of creation, but Pharaoh's problem is not merely lack of vocabulary. He lacks yaqīn, settled certainty that allows knowledge to govern conduct. A person can repeat that God created the heavens and earth while acting as though his own will is final. Sure faith is the point at which recognition becomes dependable orientation.

The phrase is also a restrained rebuke. Moses does not say that Pharaoh is incapable of understanding. He says that certainty is missing. The appeal leaves open the possibility of a response while making clear that argument alone will not change a ruler who refuses to let the argument judge him. Certainty is not a mood of confidence; it is a moral steadiness rooted in what one knows about God.

**The Answer Does Not Pretend to Exhaust God**

Some theologians and commentators used this exchange to explain why God cannot be reduced to a complete definition of essence. Moses gives signs of lordship without claiming that the human mind can surround the Lord's reality. This is the right balance. God is knowable through revelation and His acts, yet He is not made finite by the descriptions through which creatures come to know Him.

That balance protects faith from two errors. One error makes God so unknowable that no command or worship can be grounded. The other makes God into an object whose nature can be mastered like a physical thing. Moses' answer rejects both. It gives Pharaoh enough truth to understand the moral claim and preserves the transcendence that makes the Lord different from every created ruler.

**Creation Gives the Messenger Common Ground With the Court**

Moses answers in terms that do not depend on Pharaoh accepting Israelite history or Moses' personal authority. The heavens and earth are common to the whole audience. The ruler may reject the messenger's credentials, but he cannot escape the created order to which the answer points. This is wise public reasoning: begin with what can be seen, then draw out the authority it implies.

The verse therefore teaches how a universal claim can be brought into a hostile conversation. Do not begin by flattering the court's assumptions. Name the reality that surrounds the court and sustains it. Pharaoh's power is not denied as a fact; it is placed within a larger fact. The one who rules a corner of the earth must answer to the Lord of the heavens, the earth, and everything between them.

**Creation Is Evidence Against Absolute Human Rule**

The argument does not depend on denying that Pharaoh has administrative power. It denies that administrative power is self-originating or unlimited. A king can command an army, collect wealth, and shape public speech, but he cannot create the heavens, sustain the earth, or bring existence into being. His dependence is not removed by the scale of his palace. It is exposed by the scale of the world around him.

This is why Moses begins with creation rather than with a contest of political strength. If the dispute were decided by force, Pharaoh would appear to have the advantage. By naming the Creator and Sustainer, Moses changes the measure. The strongest ruler remains a dependent creature, and the apparently weak messenger speaks from the authority of the One on whom both ruler and messenger rely.
