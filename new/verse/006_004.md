## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:4

> Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Whenever" Is the Whole Diagnosis**

The verse describes no single incident. Its Arabic frame joins a negated past to an excepting clause — never does a sign reach them but that they turn aside — and the result is a portrait of a habit rather than a moment of weakness. The same construction appears where the Book reports that whatever new reminder comes from the Most Compassionate, they always turn away from it (26:5), and where it says the messengers were mocked before this Prophet and the mockers were then overtaken by the thing they had mocked (21:41). The complaint is not that a particular sign failed to convince. It is that the arrival of a sign has become the trigger for withdrawal.

A habit needs no new motive each time it operates. Once refusal has been rehearsed often enough, it runs on its own, and the evidence that should reopen the question instead closes the door faster.

**Turning Away Is a Decision, Not a Failure to Notice**

The Arabic verb behind "turn away" is *iʿrāḍ*, the act of turning one's side toward someone — a deliberate social gesture. The Book never treats it as inattention, and it never treats it as an accident. Who does more wrong than the one who is reminded of Allah's revelations and then turns away from them, and who is met with punishment for it (32:22)? The question assumes that both halves of the act are the person's own: the reminder came, and the turning was chosen.

That is why the charge laid here is graver than a simple failure to believe. A man who has not heard may plead ignorance. A man who has been addressed, understood, and rotated his shoulder away has made a statement with his body, and the Qur'an reads that statement as a verdict on the sign rather than on himself.

**Signs Already Standing in Their Own Landscape**

What had reached the Makkans already? The Book answers with the world they walked through. The dawn is caused to break and the night is made for rest, the sun and moon travelling with precision (6:96); the stars are set as guides through the darkness of land and sea (6:97); and the alternation of day and night, the rain that revives dead ground, and the winds that shift across the sky are all counted among the signs for people of understanding (2:164).

Their caravans depended on this inventory. Navigators who read a star to find a road at night were being asked to concede that the same sky carried a message. The refusal in the verse is therefore not the refusal of people who had seen too little; it is the refusal of people who had seen a great deal and preferred the sky as a tool to the sky as a claim.

**The Demand for Proof as Postponement**

Alongside the signs already granted sits a standing request for something more impressive. The disbelievers ask why no sign has been sent down from his Lord (13:7), and they are told that signs belong to God and the warner has only his warning (29:50). Other demands are recorded in the same register, and not one of them is a request for information: each asks for a spectacle whose acceptance or rejection would settle the question in public.

The verse quietly explains the demand. A person who has resolved to turn away needs a reason that arrives after the refusal rather than before it, and an absent miracle is exactly that: a reason that cannot be examined. The sign already delivered has one disadvantage for the refuser — it can be believed today, and today is inconvenient. A request for evidence that keeps being renewed is therefore not a question awaiting an answer; it is a way of holding the question open, and the Book names the tactic plainly when it says the demanders have no knowledge and that their predecessors asked the same thing because their hearts are alike (2:118).

**When the Turning Becomes a Policy**

The Book records the refusal in its organised form, and the record is blunt. A group of them advised one another not to listen to the recitation of the Qur'an, but to drown it out with noise so that they might prevail (41:26). That is not a man shrugging off an argument; that is a meeting deciding that the argument must not be heard at all. Once the decision is made collectively, the sign loses its chance before it is delivered, and the listeners are protected from the very thing the verse says kept arriving.

A second tactic sits alongside the noise. Those with no knowledge ask for a sign to be sent to them, or for God to speak to them directly (2:118), and the Book notes that earlier generations said the same thing, since hearts resemble one another. Both devices produce the same result: the question stays open because the evidence is always scheduled for later. The verse describes people for whom no arrival is ever the arrival they meant.

**What the Refusal Leaves Behind**

Turning away does not cancel the sign, and it does not cancel the appointment with it. The messenger and the reminder go on being sent, and the one who turned his side keeps a record of the turning. The next verse states where that record is filed and what it finally produces for those who treated the message as material for jokes; one sentence is enough here, because the verse in front of us is already complete. It names the pattern — arrive, then away — and leaves the consequence to be spelled out by the same sūrah, in the order God chose to reveal it.
