## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:30

> He has guided some, while others are destined to stray. They have taken devils as their masters instead of Allah— thinking they are ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Division Among People**

The verse states that God has guided some people while others have been marked out for error, and it places the division at the head of the passage about the practices and beliefs of the children of Adam. The Book states the same division in several passages, and the sūrah of the Cattle says that if God had willed He would have gathered all people to guidance (6:35), while the sūrah of the Bee states that God guides whom He wills and leaves in error whom He wills. The verse in front of us presents the two outcomes as a settled arrangement rather than as a momentary condition.

The commentary tradition treats the statement in the context of the theology of guidance. The discussion concerns whether the guidance and the misguidance are rewards and punishments attached to what people have already chosen or determinations that precede their choices, and the sūrah's own words in the following clause give the account that resolves the passage for the reader.

**Those Who Took the Devils as Masters**

The verse names the reason for the condition of those who went astray: they took the devils as masters in place of God. The language is the same as the command in the sūrah's third verse, which forbids taking protectors besides God, and the passage therefore closes the circle that the chapter's opening opened. The devils in front of us are the satans of the previous verses, and the sūrah's narrative of the first pair has shown the kind of mastery they seek.

The Book's descriptions of those who take the enemy as a patron appear in the sūrah of the Cow, the sūrah of the Women, and the sūrah of the Cattle, and the sūrah of the Jinn describes those who sought refuge with the jinn and increased them in error. The verse in front of us states the choice that produces the condition, and it names the patrons that the audience's practices had taken.

**Thinking Themselves Guided**

The verse closes with the statement that those who went astray thought they were on the right path, and the clause describes the state of those who have exchanged the revelation for their own traditions without noticing the exchange. The Book uses the same description elsewhere, and the sūrah of the Cave states that those whose efforts went astray in the life of the world will think that they are doing well (18:104). The verse in front of us applies the description to the audience's contemporaries.

The attribution of good guidance to oneself is presented in the Book as a consequence of having turned away from the source of guidance. The sūrah of the Troops states that God leaves in error those who do not believe, and the sūrah of the Ant describes the deeds of the deluded as adorned for them. The clause in front of us states the confidence that the wrong path produces in those who walk it, and the sūrah's later narratives show the communities pursuing their customs to destruction.

**The Guidance and the Practices**

The verse's placement between the statement of what God has commanded and the passage on dress and food gives it a practical function. The commands of the previous verse are the guidance, and the condition described in front of us belongs to those who have adopted another standard. The practices of the audience, including the restrictions on clothing and food that the sūrah is about to address, are presented as the fruit of the exchange.

The Book's habit of describing religious error in terms of the practices it produces appears throughout the sūrah, and the passage on the forbidden things that follows the verse states the commands in contrast with the invented prohibitions. The reader who follows the argument from the command to the response to the practice sees the sequence in the sūrah's arrangement.

**The Tradition's Discussion of Guidance**

The commentary tradition divides over the theology of guidance, and its division is reflected in the treatment of this verse. Those who held that human beings determine their own destiny read the guidance as a reward for the choice of the guided and the misguidance as a punishment for the choice of the straying, and they pointed to the clause about taking the devils as masters as the indication of the choice. Those who held that the divine will determines all things read the verse as evidence of the divine control of the outcomes.

The two readings agree that the responsibility for the choice belongs to the people who made it, since the verse states what those who went astray did. The Book's language about guidance throughout the revelation includes both the divine grant and the human response, and the verse in front of us places them together in a single sentence.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands thirtieth in the chapter, and it follows the statement of what God has commanded and precedes the passage on the adornment and the food. The address to the children of Adam continues with the description of the two groups, and the practices of each are described in the verses that follow.

The reader is given the command, the two responses to it, and the condition of those who responded wrongly. The sūrah then turns to the specifics of dress and diet that divided the audience from the guidance it had been offered.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse addresses the children of Adam and commands them to wear their adornment at every place of worship, to eat and drink without excess, and it states that God does not love the wasteful (7:31). The condition of those who thought themselves guided without guidance in front of us gives way to the practical commands that the audience's practice had ignored.

The two verses together move from the general account of guidance to the conduct that follows it. The sūrah's passage on clothing, food, and the practices of the pilgrimage is set inside the framework that the verses before it have established.
