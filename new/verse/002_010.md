## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:10

> There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Disease With No Fever**

The verse changes register from courtroom to clinic: *fī qulūbihim maraḍ* — in their hearts is a sickness. *Maraḍ* is the general Arabic term for a morbid state that weakens and unsettles whatever it occupies, and the commentators identify the heart's version as doubt and double-mindedness — the divided condition of a person running two beliefs at once. The Qur'an consistently thinks about the soul in medical terms: hearts sicken, and hearts are cured. The Book names itself the treatment — *"a cure for what is in the hearts"* (10:57) — and states the differential effect of the same dose: *"We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss"* (17:82). Whether revelation heals or aggravates depends on the organ it meets. The guarded heart of verse 2 receives it as medicine; the divided heart receives it as irritant, and the irritation is recorded as increase. A disease with no fever is still a disease — invisible symptoms do not make health.

**The Increase Attributed to Allah**

The second clause assigns the disease's growth to divine action: Allah *increases* them in sickness. The attribution parallels the sealing of verse 7, and the mechanism is the same shape — confirmation rather than manufacture. Each lie widens the wound; each performance deepens the split between the said self and the lived self, and the widening is decreed as penalty while operating as nature. The Qur'an has a name for this pattern in its doctrine of *istidrāj*: the sinner is given latitude that feels like success while it accumulates ruin, drawn on stage by stage toward an end he cannot see. Unpunished sin, on this picture, is not paused sin but compounding sin — the interval of apparent immunity is itself part of the sentence. The verse refuses to let evil stagnate. Sickness left to itself does not hold steady; it grows, and its growth passes through the divine will as everything does, here in the mode of justice: what they chose to become is being ratified as what they are becoming.

**Two Readings of the Cause: Lying, or Denying**

The final clause grounds the punishment in a speech act, and the Arabic allows two forms of it. Read as *yakdhibūn*, the cause is "their lies" — falsehood told. Read as *yukadhdhibūn*, the intensified form, it becomes "their denying" or "their calling the truth a lie." The exegetes weighed the readings, and al-Ṭabarī's preference for the first has textual support inside the Qur'an: the sūrah named for the hypocrites flatly declares them liars in the plain sense — *"Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are truly liars"* (63:1). The distinction is not pedantry. On the first reading the crime is the verse-8 claim itself: the recited creed was a lie the moment it left the mouth. On the second the crime extends to rejecting the truth they privately recognized. Both readings describe the same people, but the first ties the punishment more tightly to the passage's sequence — claim (2:8), deception (2:9), and now the sickness named as the wage of the original lie.

**Alīm After ʿAẓīm: The Adjectives Escalate**

Compare the two verdicts the passage has issued. The disbelievers of verse 7 received *ʿadhāb ʿaẓīm* — a tremendous punishment, an adjective of scale. The hypocrites receive *ʿadhāb alīm* — a painful punishment, an adjective of penetration. The shift is a judgment about the two conditions. The sealed disbeliever's loss is vast and outer: faculties closed, a tremendous penalty answering a tremendous refusal. The hypocrite's penalty is designed for an interior crime — a pain that reaches where the disease lives, since the sickness was itself an inner thing. The adjectives also track the sūrah's argument that hiddenness aggravates rather than softens guilt: the open denier sinned in daylight; the hidden one sinned while professing the opposite, adding treachery to rejection. The Qur'an's later verdict on the group confirms the direction of travel — Allah *"condemned them and prepared for them Hell"* for harbouring evil thoughts of Him (48:6). Painful is the fitting adjective for a crime committed against one's own heart.

**The Diagnostic Question Asked Again**

The Qur'an comments on itself, and its own later passage supplies the symptom list for this verse's disease. When a decision is referred to Allah and His Messenger for judgment, the sūrah al-Nūr observes the divided heart recoiling: *"Is there a sickness in their hearts? Or are they in doubt? Or do they fear that Allah and His Messenger will be unjust to them?"* (24:50). The verse equates *maraḍ* with doubt and then gives it a behavioral test — the reaction to being judged by the truth one professes. Health welcomes the verdict even when it costs; sickness negotiates, delays, resents. This is how a disease with no fever becomes visible: not by inspection of the heart, which belongs to Allah, but by the flinch at the moment of decision. Al-Baqarah names the condition in verse 10; al-Nūr hands the community its symptom. Between them the portrait needs no clairvoyance — only occasions when the truth asks for a choice.

**From Act to State to Trajectory**

The three verses have now completed a pathology, and its stages deserve to be seen together. Verse 8 recorded an act: they said what they did not believe. Verse 9 recorded a practice: the saying hardened into a scheme of deception aimed at Allah and the community. Verse 10 records a state and its direction — the practice has become a disease, and the disease is increasing. This is sin read as medicine rather than as bookkeeping: not a ledger of infractions but the biography of a condition, with each stage generated by the last. The punishment clause then reads less like retribution than like prognosis — the painful end of a painful process, continuous with it rather than bolted onto it. The moral weight of the sequence falls on the earliest stage. A lie is still cheap to retract at verse 8; by verse 10 the retraction would have to fight a disease that Allah is increasing. The passage is telling its reader where the only easy exit stood, and how quickly it narrowed.
