## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:10

> There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Disease Named**

The previous verse described a sealed heart. This one describes a sick one, and the difference matters. A seal is a closure; a sickness is a process. Something is still happening inside these people, and the process is running in one direction.

The medical metaphor is chosen with precision. Disease is not a wound — it is a departure from a working state that the body cannot correct on its own, that distorts what the sufferer perceives, and that gets worse without intervention. A heart in this condition does not stop working; it works wrongly. It receives the truth and reports back a distorted version of it, which is exactly the condition of a person who hears a warning and concludes that he is fine.

The classical commentators identify the sickness as doubt, and that identification is sharper than it sounds. Doubt here is not the absence of belief; it is the presence of two of them. The hypocrite is not an unbeliever who happens to be standing inside the community, nor a believer who is having a hard week. He is a person whose trust is divided between God and something he considers more reliable — his own calculation, his standing, his safety — and what makes the condition a disease rather than a weakness is that he does not experience the division as a division. He experiences it as prudence.

**Who Increases It**

The clause that has troubled readers is the one in the middle: God *lets their sickness increase* (2:10). Taken crudely, it makes God the author of the disease, and a God who infects a heart and then punishes it is not the God this sūrah has been describing.

The Quran supplies its own reading of such verbs. A few sūrahs away the sequence is stated in full: *We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance* (6:110). The verb belongs to God and the refusal belongs to the person, and the two are not in competition, because the same truth that softens one hearer hardens another. That is the rule here. The increase is what the message does to a heart that has already decided against it, and the sūrah has already said as much about the people in question: they lied, and the lie required further lies to sustain it, and each one made the next easier.

**Lying, or Denying?**

The Arabic of the final clause can be read two ways — *because they lied* (*yakdhibūn*) or *because they denied* (*yukadhdhibūn*) — and the choice is not cosmetic. On the first reading the punishment attaches to the gap between what they said and what they were. On the second it attaches to their unbelief as such.

The first is stronger, for two reasons. The sūrah's whole passage is about people whose profession contradicts their state, and a verse that ended by punishing mere unbelief would break the thread. It is also the reading the Quran supports elsewhere, where the hypocrites are condemned specifically for the falsehood of their oath: *Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are truly liars* (63:1). What is punished here is not that they failed to believe but that they said they had.

**Sickness and Seal Are Not the Same**

Holding this verse against 2:7 produces the most useful thing either verse has to offer. A sealed heart is finished: the instrument is closed, the account is settled, and the Quran speaks of it in the past tense. A sick heart is not finished. Disease has a direction, but it also has a remedy, and by naming the condition an illness rather than a closure, the verse leaves the door open.

The sūrah is consistent about this. It goes on addressing these people, arguing with them, and warning them for another dozen verses, which would be pointless if the state described were terminal. The sickness is serious precisely because it is still treatable — and serious because the treatment is the thing they are refusing.

**A Punishment Matched to the Crime**

The punishment is *for their lies* (2:10), and the specification is deliberate. The Quran elsewhere describes punishments that fit: theFire that has people and stones for fuel (66:6), the mockery thrown back at those who mocked (9:79). Here the correspondence is between the lie in this life and the exposure in the next. The hypocrite's entire project was the maintenance of an appearance; the punishment is the collapse of the appearance, in front of everyone.

The adjective attached to the punishment — *painful* — is the Quran's ordinary word for it, and its ordinariness is part of the point. The hypocrite spent his life avoiding pain: he avoided the cost of commitment, the risk of being counted with a despised minority, the loss of standing. What he is promised is precisely the thing he organised his life to escape, delivered by the only authority he could not manage. A person who has spent decades arranging not to be hurt is, on this account, the worst candidate for being hurt.

**Why the Diagnosis Returns**

This is not the last time the sūrah's people are described this way. The phrase recurs whenever the Quran wants to mark a group that cannot commit: *when the hypocrites and those with sickness in their hearts said, “Allah and His Messenger have promised us nothing but delusion!”* (33:12); *those with sickness in their hearts staring at you like someone in the throes of death* (47:20). The repetition is the point. A sickness is what keeps a person from doing the thing he can see he ought to do, and the sūrah identifies it wherever it appears.
