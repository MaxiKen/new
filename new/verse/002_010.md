## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:10

> There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sickness Is Doubt, Not Infection**

The commentators who examined this verse in the earliest generations were unanimous about one thing: the disease is not physical. Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid, followed by al-Ṭabarī, read *maraḍ* here as doubt and hypocrisy — a defect in the religion of the person rather than in his body. The Qur'an uses the same word for the same condition elsewhere, and one of those passages supplies the definition directly: *“But as for those with sickness in their hearts, it has increased them only in wickedness upon their wickedness, and they die as disbelievers.”* (9:125).

The choice of vocabulary is a diagnosis. A person whose heart holds doubt about what he professes is ill in the place where belief is produced, and the rest of the verse treats that illness as active rather than static. Sickness in this imagery does not sit still. It grows, and the growth is what the second half of the sentence is about.

**Increase as Consequence, Not Infliction**

God is the subject of the increase: *lets their sickness increase*. The Qur'an states the same law in several places, and it consistently presents the increase as the outworking of a direction already chosen. Sūrah 9 says of the same people that revelation *“has increased them only in wickedness upon their wickedness”* (9:125). Sūrah 74 describes a sign given *“so that those ˹hypocrites˺ with sickness in their hearts and the disbelievers will argue”* (74:31), where the same disease produces the same argument.

The pattern is one of correspondence. The Book does not describe a disease injected into a healthy person as a test; it describes a condition that feeds on what the person does with it, so that every further choice makes the condition harder to reverse. The verse's grammar keeps God as the agent of the increase, which means neither the outcome nor the process escapes His government — and the sūrah leaves both facts standing together.

**A City Where Profession Was Required**

The setting of these verses explains why hypocrisy is treated as an illness of a community rather than an individual failing. In Madinah after the migration, the community had become a body with a treasury, an army and a legal order, and the price of membership was a public profession. People whose interests lay elsewhere had reason to make that profession and no reason to mean it.

The Book's response to this situation is not a screening procedure. It is a description so precise that the people involved could recognise themselves in it, if they chose to look. The illness it names is the corrosion of the inner life under the pressure of outward conformity, and the sūrah treats that corrosion as a public danger precisely because it is invisible to the public.

**Punishment for Lying, in the Distinctive Sense of the Verb**

The verse ends with a painful punishment *for their lies*. Two readings of the final word have been transmitted: *yakdhibūn*, they lie, and *yukadhdhibūn*, they deny. Al-Ṭabarī preferred the first, and the Qur'an supports him elsewhere: the opening of Sūrah 63 says of the hypocrites that when they testify to the Prophet's mission, *“Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are truly liars”* (63:1).

Lying, in this usage, is not a matter of incorrect facts. A person who says what he does not hold to be true is not making an error about the world; he is misrepresenting himself, and the Book names that act by its proper name. The punishment corresponds to the offence in the precise way the sūrah has been arranging from its opening: the liar is measured against the truth he covered.

**Illness Named So That It Can Be Treated**

The Qur'an does not describe spiritual illness only to condemn it. Other passages use medical vocabulary for revelation itself: *“O humanity! Indeed, there has come to you a warning from your Lord, a cure for what is in the hearts, a guide, and a mercy for the believers”* (10:57), and *“We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers”* (17:82). The Book that diagnoses the disease also presents itself as its cure.

Read together, the diagnosis in al-Baqarah and the healing announced elsewhere belong to one argument. Sickness in the heart is treatable while the person still perceives it, which is why the sūrah's refrain about awareness — repeated at the close of three consecutive portraits — carries such weight. The verse states that a condition exists and grows; it does not state that the growth is beyond interruption.

**The Order in Which the Sūrah Tells the Story**

The sequence from verse 8 to verse 10 narrates a decline in three steps. A claim is made and denied; an attempt at deception is described and reversed; then the condition that produced both is named and its direction established. The Book begins with what the hypocrites say, moves to what they do, and only then states what they are.

That order is a rhetorical strategy as much as a diagnosis. The reader of the sūrah meets the people before the disease, and the portrait is recognisable from behaviour that anyone can observe rather than from a condition that only God can inspect. By the time the illness is named, the evidence has already been laid out, and the punishment announced in the last line reads as the natural end of what the reader has just been shown.

**A Description Written for the Community That Reads It**

The portrait of the hypocrites serves the community as much as it serves the people described. A body that cannot name a disease cannot decide how to treat it, whether with argument, patience or distance. The sūrah names the condition, traces its growth and states its price, and it does all of this at the head of a book of law.

The sequence is worth noting in a sūrah that runs to 286 verses. The longest chapter of the Qur'an teaches its readers to recognise a divided heart before it begins to legislate for the community that contains one. Everything that follows — the rules on marriage, debts, fasting, warfare and inheritance — will be administered by people whose inner condition the Book has just taught everyone else to identify.
