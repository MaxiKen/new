## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:10

> There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Heart Described as an Organ That Can Be Ill**

The sūrah moves from the hypocrite’s saying (2:8) to his seeking to deceive (2:9) to the interior that makes both possible: *“In their hearts is a disease”* (2:10). The heart (*qalb*) has already been described as sealed (2:7) for the persistent rejector; here it is described as sick. The two images are not interchangeable. A seal closes an intact surface; a disease corrupts an organ from within. For the rejector, the instrument of understanding is shut; for the hypocrite, it is inflamed. That is why the same organ can be rusted — *“rust has covered their hearts for what they earned”* (83:14) — hardened (2:74), veiled (6:25; 17:46), or made to go blind (22:46). The Qur'an treats the heart as the locus of knowing and sincerity, not merely of sentiment, so that its sickness is not a metaphor for sadness but a description of a cognitive-moral lesion. The lesion has a name in the commentary: doubt (*shakk*) that has become habitual, so that the heart can no longer tell its own profession from its own condition.

**Why the Disease Is Said to Be “In” the Heart**

*Fī qulūbihim maraḍ* — “in their hearts is a disease” — places the disease *inside* the organ that was meant to carry certainty. The preposition matters because the hypocrite’s profession had presented the disease as outside: he displayed outward assent while withholding inward assent, as if the inside and outside could be kept separate without consequence. The verse says the inside has itself become diseased. That interiority explains why the deception of (2:9) folded back upon the deceiver. A deception practiced often enough ceases to be a technique and becomes a constitution, and a constitution that says “We believe” while not believing must generate a heart that can sustain contradiction without feeling it. The disease is the name for the heart’s accommodation to that contradiction — its learned ability to hold a sentence and its negation at once.

**God Increased Them in Disease — Increase as Recompense**

*Fa-zādahumu Allāhu maraḍā* — “and Allah has increased them in disease.” The increase is attributed directly to Allah, with the same active grammar that attributed the sealing (2:7). The causal question that arose there arises here with greater intimacy: does God cause sincerity to decay, or does persistent insincerity produce a decay that God then confirms? The sūrah, by sequencing the hypocrite’s saying and self-deception before the increase, favors the second hearing without excluding the first. The disease was theirs; the increase is His — but the increase is the disease’s own momentum, now divinely ratified. The same pattern of recompense-through-increase governs the sealing, and the Qur'an names the opposite pattern as well: *“whoever is granted wisdom has been granted much good”* (2:269) and the prayer *“My Lord, increase me in knowledge”* (20:114) treat increase as the form divine generosity takes when the recipient is receptive. Here, receptivity has been replaced by contrivance, so increase takes the form of the contrivance’s own growth.

**A Variation in Reading That Sharpens the Verdict**

The source notes a well-known variant in the final clause: *yakdhibūn* (they lie) versus *yukadhdhibūn* (they deny/belie). The first reads *“for having lied”* — *bi-mā kānū yakdhibūn* — directly referencing the profession *“We believe”* (2:8) that was not true. The second reads *“for having denied”* — *yukadhdhibūn* — referencing the broader denial that underwrites the lie. The sūrah of the Hypocrites, which rebukes the hypocrites for lying — *“Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are indeed liars”* (63:1) — lends weight to the first reading, and the commentator Ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī (Ṭ) favors it for that reason. Yet the two readings converge in effect. The lie is the specific utterance; the denial is the settled posture that makes the utterance necessary. The painful punishment that closes the verse attaches to both, because the lie is not a slip of the tongue but the verbal form of a denial that has already been chosen.

**Painful Punishment for a Painful Profession**

*ʿAdhābun alīm* — “a painful punishment” — uses the adjective that evokes felt pain rather than mere magnitude. The “tremendous punishment” of the sealed (2:7) was *ʿaẓīm*, vast; this punishment is *alīm*, aching. The variation fits the disease image. A sealed heart is a vast privation; a diseased heart is an aching one. Hypocrisy is painful even before it is punished because it requires the continuous labor of managing two faces (2:14) and because that labor is sustained by a heart that is itself inflamed. The punishment that is promised is therefore not an arbitrary addition; it is the pain of the profession brought to its natural term. To claim belief while withholding it is to live with a wound and to treat the wound by pressing on it, so that the pressing — the very saying “We believe” — deepens the wound that the saying was meant to hide.

**Disease That Spreads to Perception Itself**

The sūrah has already said the hypocrites deceive themselves without perceiving (2:9). The disease explains that lack of perception. A heart that is diseased does not merely make false claims; it loses the ability to feel that its claims are false, and therefore it also loses the ability to seek the remedy that would heal it. That is why the next verses will show the hypocrites calling corruption “reform” (2:11) and intelligence “folly” (2:13) without irony. They are not performing a cynical script; they have come to believe the script’s inversions because the organ that would have detected inversion is itself inverted. The sūrah will later generalize the pattern in a principle: those who do not know (*lā yaʿlamūn*, 2:13) and those who do not perceive (*lā yashʿurūn*, 2:12) are not excused by not knowing; they are described as not knowing because they have made knowing unwelcome.

**Why the Verse Says “Their Lies,” Not “Their Doubts”**

To end with *“for having lied”* rather than “for having doubted” is to keep responsibility at the level of speech. Doubt can be suffered; a lie is enacted. The sūrah insists that the hypocrite is not a victim of uncertainty about God and the Last Day — after all, he has chosen to profess precisely those two — but an agent who has chosen to utter what he does not hold. That insistence matters because it keeps the door to return ajar. A disease can be healed, but only if it is acknowledged as disease and not as sophistication. By naming the pain’s cause as lying, the verse names the act whose cessation would be the beginning of healing: to stop saying what is not held, and to hold what must be said. Until that act is taken, the increase continues, and the increase is itself the punishment that is already underway.

**A Corresponding Increase on the Other Side**

Finally, to hear this verse only as threat is to miss its symmetrical promise. If Allah increases disease in the heart that cultivates diseased saying, He also increases other things in the heart that cultivates their opposite — *“Allah increases those who are guided in guidance”* and *“whoever is guided, He increases him in guidance.”* The same verb *zāda* that darkens here brightens there. The moral world of al-Baqarah is one of increase in the direction already chosen. That is why the mindful were described as prospering (*mufliḥūn*, 2:5) — their tilling is met with yield — and why the hypocrite’s tilling of contradiction is met with more contradiction. The verse therefore completes the moral physics of the opening: hearts are not static containers; they are fields, and what is sown in them is made to grow.
