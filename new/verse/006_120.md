## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:120

> Shun all sin—open and secret. Indeed, those who commit sin will be punished for what they earn.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Shun Sin**

Shun all sin, open and secret. The verse moves from the specific dietary rule to a general command, and it covers the two ways in which a person's wrong conduct can be conducted. The open sin is visible to others and is judged by them; the secret sin is hidden from the community, and the Book states that both are equally subject to the command.

The sūrah's treatment of the audience's conduct throughout the chapter has dealt with the hidden as well as the visible. It said that God knows what they conceal and what they disclose, and it described the whispering of the satans among humans and jinn as a hidden activity (6:112). The command in this verse addresses the whole field in which those activities occur.

**What the Book Means by the Two Kinds**

The commentators explained the open sin as the wrong that is done in public and the secret sin as what a person hides in his own conduct, and some applied the pair to the acts of the limbs and the intentions of the heart. The distinction appears in the Book's other passages about conduct, and the Book's word for the hidden covers both the concealment of an act and the inner intention behind it.

The Book treats the two together because its account of accountability includes intention as well as action. The sūrah of the Cow states that God will bring to account what people reveal and what they conceal, and the sūrah of the Family of Imran states that He knows what is in the hearts of His servants. The command in this verse assumes that the concern of the law reaches the place where no witness from the community can see.

**The Reason the Command Is Stated So Broadly**

The verse is stated broadly because the dietary question that precedes it was a fragment of a larger system. The audience had built rules around food while other conduct went unchecked, and the sūrah's argument has been that the religion is not a matter of selected observances. The command to shun sin in both its forms states the scope of the requirement.

The Book's addresses to the community elsewhere make the same point about partial observance. The sūrah of the Cow criticises those who accept part of the Scripture and reject part, and the sūrah of the Women records the same complaint. The command in front of us sets the whole of a person's conduct under the rule.

**The Statement of Punishment**

Indeed, those who commit sin will be punished for what they earn. The second sentence supplies the consequence and states it in the Book's vocabulary of earning. The Book frequently describes reward and punishment as the produce of what a person has collected for himself, and the same language is used in the sūrah's accounts of the day of judgment, when each person is confronted with what he has sent ahead.

The sūrah has described the audience's reckoning in several earlier passages: God knows what they do, their deeds will be recorded, and they will be informed of what they used to do (6:60; 6:108). The sentence in this verse states the same fact in terms of the sin itself, and it applies it to the open and the hidden alike.

**The Pairing of the Verse With Its Neighbours**

The verse stands between the dietary command and its defence and the prohibition of what has not been consecrated to God. The three verses form a single unit in which food, conduct, and ritual are treated by one standard. What God has permitted is permitted; what He has forbidden is forbidden; and the whole of a person's conduct, visible or concealed, is subject to the command.

The Book's habit of placing an ethical generalisation beside a ritual rule appears throughout the chapter, and it serves to show that the two belong to the same authority. The sūrah has already joined the confession of God's unity to commands about food, and it will continue to move between the two registers, since its argument is that both come from the same Lord.

**What the Verse Says About the Concealed Act**

The mention of the secret sin is significant in a passage addressed to a community that lived in close association with people who watched it. A community concerned only with its public standing could satisfy the visible requirements and leave the hidden ones untouched, and the Book's mention of the hidden sin prevents that reading. Nothing in the audience's conduct escaped the One who was being worshipped.

The sūrah has stated the same fact about knowledge in the passage on the keys of the unseen, where God knows the secret and the disclosure, the leaf that falls, and the grain in the darkness (6:59; 6:60). The command in this verse rests on that knowledge, and the punishment it states is the consequence that knowledge produces.

**The Transition to the Following Verse**

The next verse returns to food and forbids what has not had the Name of God mentioned over it, describing that as disobedience and connecting it to the whisperings of the devils and their associates (6:121). The shift back from general conduct to diet shows that the sūrah is treating the dietary system of the audience as one item in the register of conduct the Book is correcting.

The two verses together supply the standard for assessing the practices the sūrah will describe in the passages about livestock and harvests. Sin, whether open or hidden, is what the command excludes, and the following verse states how the concrete practice of the audience stands in relation to it.
