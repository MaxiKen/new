## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:4

> It has been decreed for such devils that whoever takes them as a guide will be misguided and led by them into the torment of the Blaze.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What Has Been Decreed, Precisely**

*Kutiba ʿalayhi* — it has been written for him, decreed for him. The verb carries the sense of a settled inscription, and the verse then states what the inscription says: whoever takes him as a guide, he misguides him and leads him to the torment of the Blaze.

Notice the shape of what is decreed. The decree is not a list of names. It is a conditional sentence: whoever does X, Y follows. What God has written is the *connection* between taking a devil as patron and ending in the Fire, not the identity of the people who will take one. The verse predestines a law, not a population.

That distinction is the whole answer to the objection the verse invites. If the decree were a roster of the damned, patience and effort would be theatre. As a conditional, it is the opposite: it is the strongest possible warning, because it tells the reader exactly which act carries which consequence and leaves the act to him.

**Two Antecedents for One Pronoun**

The pronoun in "it has been decreed for him" is grammatically available to two referents, and the commentators split accordingly.

The first reading takes it as the devil of the previous verse: it is decreed for every rebellious devil that he will misguide whoever follows him. The clause that follows then reads smoothly — "whoever takes him as a guide" refers back to the same devil, and the subject of "he misguides him" is the devil throughout.

The second reading takes it as the follower: it is decreed for the person who follows a devil that he will be misguided. This yields a harder, more predestinarian sense, and it is recorded by al-Rāzī among others.

The stronger reading is the first. The nearest antecedent in verse 3 is *shayṭānin marīdin*, the rebellious devil, and the pronoun in "takes him (*hu*) as a guide" has to be the devil for the sentence to make sense at all. Readings that make the follower the subject of the decree must supply that reference across a clause boundary, and they sit awkwardly beside verses that place the choice squarely with the human being: *“˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve”* (18:29). The coherence of the Qur'an's moral language is a real argument here, and it favours the reading that keeps the decree a law rather than a list.

**The Irony in the Word "Guide"**

The verb used for leading to the Fire is from *h-d-y*, the same root that gives *hudā*, guidance. The devil "guides" his follower — to the torment of the Blaze.

The Qur'an uses this irony repeatedly and always to the same point: guidance is not a feeling of being led somewhere confidently. It is a destination. A man who is certain, directed, and utterly sure of his path has been guided in the verbal sense while being misguided in the sense that counts. The verse refuses to let *hudā* be judged by its manner. It is judged by where it ends — and the Blaze is where this one ends.

**Patronage: a Word Borrowed From the Marketplace**

The verb translated "takes as a guide" is *tawallāhu*, from the same root as *walī*. In pre-Islamic Arabia a *walī* was a protector — the man or clan to whom you attached yourself for safety, who answered for your blood and whose standing covered you. Clientage was a real institution with real consequences, and everyone hearing this verse understood the transaction.

Using that vocabulary for a spiritual relationship is a deliberate transfer. The disputant of verse 3 is not merely persuaded by a devil; he has entered into an arrangement with one, trading his dependence for protection. The Qur'an's point is that the arrangement is recognized — it is not a metaphor for influence but a description of who the man now belongs to.

What makes the transfer cutting is that clientage was entered into for safety, and this clientage delivers the opposite. The follower takes a protector because he wants cover, and what the protector delivers is the Blaze. Patronage that kills the client is not patronage at all, which is why the verse's conditional is so severe: the law it decrees is that protection sought outside God reverses itself.

**What the Devil Himself Will Say**

The relationship described here is a relationship of patronage: the man takes the devil as *walī*, a protecting friend and manager of his affairs. The arrangement is presented as if it were a contract with benefits.

The Qur'an lets the other party describe it later, after judgment has been passed, in words that undo the contract entirely: *“I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves”* (14:22). The patron of verse 4 turns out to have had no power to deliver anything, and his own testimony is that the follower walked in on his own feet.

Read together, the two verses settle the question of coercion from both ends. The decree of verse 4 guarantees that the path leads where it leads; the confession of 14:22 establishes that nobody was dragged along it.

**Fire as a Destination Rather Than a Mood**

The closing phrase is *ʿadhāba al-saʿīr* — the torment of the Blaze. *Saʿīr* is kindled fire, fire set going, and it is the Fire's most common proper name in the Qur'an's eschatological vocabulary.

The precision defuses a modern misreading. The Qur'an is not describing a state of mind or a absence; it is describing a place with a temperature, reached by a route. The verse's conditional structure and its concrete destination belong together: a law that connects an act to an end, and an end that is stated as plainly as the act.
