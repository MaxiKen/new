## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:50

> The King ˹then˺ said, “Bring him to me.” When the messenger came to him, Joseph said, “Go back to your master and ask him about the case of the women who cut their hands. Surely my Lord has ˹full˺ knowledge of their cunning.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Release Offered and Not Taken**

The king's order is unqualified: bring him to me. The messenger arrives at the prison with the authority to empty it, and the prisoner refuses to leave. Nothing in the verse explains the refusal as a negotiation, a demand for terms or a display of pride. Joseph does not ask for an office, for money, for an apology or for the punishment of anyone. He asks that a question be put: what was the matter of the women who cut their hands? The request is for an enquiry, and until the enquiry happens he stays where he is. A man who has spent years in a cell on a charge that was disproved inside his own household declines freedom because freedom without correction would leave the record as it stands.

**The Prophet's Own Comment on the Refusal**

The tradition treats this moment as the high point of Joseph's patience. Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ saying, after mentioning Abraham's request for reassurance and Lot's wish for a strong support: if I had remained in prison as long as Joseph did, I would have answered the caller (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3372). The comparison is in Joseph's favour and is explicit. The remark identifies what the refusal cost and what it required: a man who had been held for years, offered release at last, chooses to remain inside until his name has been cleared publicly. The statement is not a criticism of anyone else's weakness; it is a measure of the standard Joseph met, delivered by the one person in the tradition qualified to set it. That is why the comment sits beside the narrative rather than against it. It honours the length of the waiting by refusing to pretend that anyone else would have endured it, and it locates Joseph's distinction not in his purity at the locked door but in his patience across all the years that followed the accusation.

**"Ask Him About the Case of the Women" Names the Evidence**

*Mā bālu* — what is the state of, what happened with — introduces the specific matter to be investigated: the women who cut their hands. The reference is to the banquet of verse 31, where the female establishment of the city wounded themselves on seeing Joseph and declared him an angel rather than a man capable of indecency. Joseph chooses that evidence deliberately. The torn shirt of verse 26 was examined inside a household and never published. The husband's verdict of verse 28 was spoken in a room nobody else entered. The banquet was attended by dozens of women of standing, all of whom saw the same thing and all of whom said the same sentence aloud. He points the king to the only testimony in Egypt that was given in public.

**Why the Women, and Not the Wife Alone**

The request is plural. Joseph does not ask for the ʿAzīz's wife to be interrogated, although she is the person who accused him and the only one who can confess. He asks about the women as a group, which includes her. The reason is evidentiary: a single recantation can be explained as pressure, as fear or as favour, while the agreement of a whole group cannot. The chapter shows the strategy working in the following verse, where the women answer first and the wife answers after them. Her confession arrives inside a chorus of exculpation rather than alone, which is what makes it conclusive. Joseph has asked for the strongest available form of testimony, and he has asked for it while standing in a prison he could have walked out of.

**"My Lord Has Full Knowledge of Their Cunning"**

The word is *kayd*, the chapter's term for concealed planning, used of the brothers in verse 5, of the women in verse 28, of the same women in Joseph's prayer at verse 33, and of God's turning of it away at verse 34. Here it is applied to the whole female establishment collectively. The clause has a double function. It states that nothing about the affair was hidden from God, which is the ground on which Joseph has lived since the well. And it declines to pronounce judgement himself. He does not call the women liars, does not describe what they did, and does not ask for their punishment. He refers their planning to a knower and asks a king to ask a question. Both halves of that sentence are necessary to it. Without the appeal to God's knowledge the demand for enquiry would read as an accusation; without the demand the appeal would be a private consolation. Together they allow Joseph to hold his case open before God while pursuing it, in the same breath, before a human court that can actually do something about it.

**Vindication, Not Revenge**

The distinction governs everything that follows. Joseph's demand produces an enquiry whose outcome is a public statement of his truthfulness (12:51) and a woman's admission of her own guilt. It produces no execution, no confiscation, no dismissal and no humiliation of anyone. When the brothers eventually stand before him, decades later and in a famine, he does not use his power to settle the older account either: *“There is no blame on you today”* (12:92). The pattern is consistent. Joseph seeks correction of the record and nothing beyond it, and the sūrah presents that restraint as strength rather than as weakness — the strength of a man who does not need another person to suffer in order to be free. It is also a strategic choice. A demand for punishment would have made his release conditional on someone's ruin and given the court every reason to delay; a demand for the truth made it conditional only on an enquiry, which a king can order at no cost to himself. Joseph asks for the cheapest available form of justice, and he receives it.

**The Cost of the Demand Is Paid in Full**

The verse does not say how long the enquiry took, and the narrative moves directly from the demand to its outcome. What is certain is that Joseph remained in the prison until the king's investigation was complete, since he is summoned again in verse 54 after the women have testified. He paid for his insistence with the only currency he had. The chapter records the payment without remarking on it, which is how it records all of Joseph's costs: the well, the beating, the sale for twenty dirhams, the years in the cell, and now the additional time bought by a refusal to leave it. The reward arrives in the next few verses, and it arrives in a form the verse has already earned. The form matters as much as the arrival. He is not merely freed; he is cleared, and cleared by the testimony of the women who ruined his reputation and by the confession of the woman who accused him. What he waited for is precisely what he receives — a public record of innocence rather than an administrative release from custody.
