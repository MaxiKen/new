## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:30

> Lot prayed, “My Lord! Help me against the people of corruption.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Point at Which a Prophet Turns to Prayer**

Lot's prayer follows the mockery of his people, and the sequence is deliberate. He had spoken, argued, and been answered with a demand that the punishment be brought forward. When the conversation reached that ceiling, he stopped addressing the town and addressed his Lord, and the Book records the change without comment. This is the pattern of prophetic careers in the Qur'an. The messenger does not answer contempt with curses or with schemes; he hands the matter to the one who sent him, and the prayer marks the end of his responsibility for the outcome. What the sentence leaves unsaid is equally important: Lot does not state what should be done to the town, how it should be done, or when. He asks for help and leaves the method open.

The prayer also marks the point where argument ends. Lot had presented his case and had received an answer in the form of a threat against his guests, and the Book's prophets turn to supplication at that point rather than before they have tried to persuade.

**The Meaning of Corruption in the Book's Vocabulary**

The word Lot uses belongs to a root that means to spoil and to ruin, and the Book applies it to a wide range of damage. It is used of violence and bloodshed, of fraud in trade, of the ruin of crops and livestock, and of the general decay that follows a society's departure from what it was set up to be. Sūrah al-Rūm gives the widest statement, that corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people's hands have earned, so that they might taste the consequences of some of their deeds and perhaps return (30:41). The general principle running through these passages is that corruption is not merely a private moral failing; it is a public condition whose effects spread beyond the people who produce it, and the petition of this verse names an entire community by that description.

The vocabulary also covers conduct that goes unpunished in a town's own courts. Cutting a road, robbing a caravan, and intimidating a visitor are corruptions of the security a settlement exists to provide, and the Book's word gathers those offences together with the moral disorder that accompanies them.

**Violence Hidden Behind the Language of Peace**

The Book's portraits of corrupters repeatedly show them describing themselves in the opposite terms. When they are told not to spread corruption in the land they answer that they are only peacemakers, and the reply comes back that they are the corrupters and do not perceive it (2:11–12). Elsewhere the same type is described as one who, once he gains power, travels the land to spread mischief and destroy crops and cattle (2:205). The sins named in those passages are concrete injuries to other people's lives and livelihoods, and the account of Sodom in this sūrah fits the category precisely: the town had made its streets unsafe and its guests fair game. Lot's prayer identifies the community by the damage it caused, which is why the request is intelligible even to readers who know nothing about the town's other practices.

The name given to the opponents in this prayer is the one the Book uses for those who break the order of a community from inside it, and its range covers fraud, injustice, and the corruption of worship. That breadth is the reason the prayer is defensible. Lot is not asking for the removal of a tribe he dislikes; he is naming conduct that makes life in the town impossible for anyone who will not join in, and the Book's accounts of the city supply the conduct. The verdict in the following verses is an answer to a statement about behaviour rather than a clash of loyalties.

**A Request for Help, Not a Demand for Punishment**

The wording of the petition is restrained, and the restraint is part of its authority. Lot says "help me against" the people, which is the language of a man who needs support, not of a prosecutor asking for a sentence. The Book's prophets are consistently presented in this posture, and the pattern protects them from the charge of vindictiveness: they do not take the judgment into their own hands and they do not itemize what their opponents deserve. What the prayer asserts is a fact about the situation rather than a wish about the defendants. Lot is powerless against a town that has refused every argument, and he asks the one who is not powerless to take charge of the affair.

The Book's prophets ask for relief rather than for the punishment of their neighbours, and the request is aimed at the trouble rather than at the people causing it. Noah had already used the same posture in an earlier account, asking to be helped rather than answered with fire, and the destruction that follows is reported as God's decision rather than as the prophet's petition granted. What the pattern shows is the limit on a messenger's role: he argues, he warns, he prays, and the verdict remains with the one being petitioned.

**The Same Prayer in the Mouth of Noah**

The Book records the same movement in Noah's career, where the prophet cries out to his Lord that he has been overwhelmed and asks for help (54:10). Two men, several centuries apart, with completely different audiences, produce petitions that could be mistaken for one another. The similarity is one of the Book's ways of showing that the difficulty is structural rather than personal. Every messenger who delivers a message that people reject arrives at the same wall, and the Book supplies the same instrument for getting past it, which is to ask for help rather than to obtain a hearing by other means. The prayers also mark the transition from the period of warning to the period of consequence, without giving the prophet any role in the transition itself.

Noah's case also shows how long the interval between a prayer and its answer can be. The Book reports his call and then the flood that followed it, with a long ministry of argument in between, so a reader who takes this verse as a demand for immediate action will find the pattern corrected by the earlier story.

**What a Prayer of This Kind Does Not Do**

A reader should notice what the sentence does not authorize. It does not permit a believer to avenge himself, and it does not license the community to take the judgment into its own hands; the help requested is specifically divine, and the Book's narratives guard that boundary carefully. Nor does the prayer make the town's fate a private quarrel between two parties, with one side winning. What it does is place the situation in front of the only authority competent to settle it, and to place it there in the plainest possible words. The town had asked for the punishment to be produced if the warning were true. The messenger's answer is a request for help, and the Book's answer to both is the arrival of messengers who carry the sentence. The chapter will report that arrival in the verses that follow, and the report is framed entirely from the side of the One who sends.

The prayer also declines to name anyone. No individual in the town is singled out, and the request is aimed at a condition rather than at a list of persons, which keeps the prophet's appeal on the side of the community's welfare even as it announces that community's end.

**Naming the Opponent by What He Does**

Lot calls his opponents a people of corruption rather than a tribe, a city, or a family, and the choice of label keeps a door open that a curse would have closed. A person can cease to be a corrupter; a person cannot cease to be a member of a clan. The wording of the prayer therefore leaves the matter of their identity in the hands of their own conduct, and it defines the struggle in terms of the harm they cause rather than the blood they carry. The Book's prophets address their communities the same way, which is why their condemnations are consistently aimed at practices and never at peoples as such. Lot asks for help against a condition, and the Book supplies the help when the condition has run its course.
