## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:93

> All food was lawful for the children of Israel, except what Israel made unlawful for himself before the Torah was revealed. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement About Food and a Challenge**

The verse states that all food was lawful for the children of Israel except what Israel forbade for himself before the Torah was revealed, and then instructs the Prophet to call on his opponents to bring the Torah and recite it if their claims are true.

Both halves of the sentence are aimed at a specific argument. The verse before this passage concerned the giving of what a person values, and the passage now turns to a dispute about religious law. The claim being answered is that the dietary restrictions of the Jewish law go back to Abraham, and the verse answers it by pointing at the history of the restrictions and at the document the claimants themselves accept.

**Who Israel Was**

Israel is the name of Jacob, the son of Isaac, and the third of the figures the sūrah named in its creed several verses earlier. The verse's reference to him as Israel rather than Jacob is deliberate, since the people being addressed are the tribes descended from him and the name is the one their own tradition uses for their ancestor.

The verse's claim about him is a historical one, and its importance lies in what it excludes. If the food laws that the Israelites observed went back to Jacob's own lifetime and were recorded in the Torah, then they form part of a divine legislation given to him. The verse says instead that he forbade certain foods for himself, which means that the restriction originated with a person rather than with a revealed law, and that it predates the book that the claimants treat as the source of their practice.

**The Narratives About the Forbidden Foods**

The commentators collected several explanations of what Jacob imposed on himself and why. One account describes an illness from which he sought relief and a vow to give up certain foods if he recovered. Another reports that physicians advised him to avoid certain foods, which he then renounced in a formal undertaking. A third describes the restriction as an act of asceticism with no external cause.

The narratives differ in their details, and the verse does not require any of them. What the sentence establishes is the fact on which all the accounts agree and which the argument depends on: the restriction was his own undertaking, made before the Torah, and the practice of his descendants inherited it. The commentators also noted that the Torah later included those prohibitions among the laws given to the Israelites, which is why the practice and the book are consistent with each other even though the practice came first.

**The Challenge to Bring the Torah**

The instruction to bring the Torah and recite it is a demand for evidence from the opponents' own book, and the Book uses this method at points where the dispute concerns a document both parties can examine.

The argument's structure is worth following. The Prophet is told not to appeal to his own revelation for the answer, but to invite the other side to produce their, and the invitation is issued in the confidence that the text will support him. The Book repeats the technique in other passages where it tells the Prophet to ask the People of the Book about the things they have been taught, and in the verse that tells him to bring proof for the claim that God has forbidden certain things if they are truthful. The challenge is a test of honesty rather than of memory.

**The Reading of the Passage as One Argument**

Several commentators read this verse together with the two that follow it as a single unit. On that reading, the sequence answers the claim that Abraham was subject to the food laws the Jews practised: the laws did not exist in Abraham's time, and what existed was a restriction Jacob imposed on himself, which the Torah later recorded.

The sūrah has already made the same point about Abraham in another key, when it asked why the People of the Book argued about him, since the Torah and the Gospel were revealed only after him. The two passages use different evidence and reach the same conclusion, and the pair shows how the sūrah builds a case: a fact about chronology, a document that both parties accept, and a conclusion about who the ancestors of the faith actually were.

**Why This Matters for the Sūrah's Argument**

The dietary question looks narrow, and its stakes in the passage are large. If the restrictions of the Jewish law were always in force, then a religion that does not carry them cannot be the continuation of Abraham's way, and the whole claim with which the sūrah opened would be damaged.

The verse's answer is that a legislation given to one people after their ancestor's time is not the measure of a faith that looks back to that ancestor. The Book's treatment of the food laws elsewhere is consistent with this: it states that the restrictions were imposed on the Israelites as a penalty for their transgressions, and that they came with the Torah rather than before it. What remains of Abraham's legacy in the sūrah's account is the orientation toward God that the next verse names, and the passage's next sentence states it directly.

**The Evidence Within the Israelites' Own Tradition**

The verse's argument has a further support that the commentators noticed, in the description of Jacob's injury in the book of Genesis, where the Israelites are said to abstain from a particular cut of meat because of his lameness. The detail is incidental to Genesis, and it confirms what the verse claims: practices among the Israelites could have their origin in Jacob's own life and be remembered without being part of a revealed dietary code.

A reader who follows the argument to this point sees how the sūrah uses the opponents' own sources. It does not ask them to abandon their tradition; it asks them to read it, and the reading does the work of the argument. The verses that follow draw the conclusion about where the truth stands and whom the community should follow.
