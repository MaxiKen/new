## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:95

> But they will never wish for that because of what their hands have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Challenge**

The verse reports the outcome of the proposal in the previous sentence, and it reports it as a certainty. No one from the community took up the challenge, and the Book states that no one ever will. The form of the sentence is unusual because it forecloses the future as well as describing the present, and the reason given for the refusal explains why the prediction holds.

The pattern the sūrah is constructing now has three parts. A claim is made, a test is offered, and the test is declined for a reason that contradicts the claim. The next verse will add the community's attachment to life as further evidence, and the argument will be complete. The certainty of the prediction is part of the verse's argument. A statement that a group will never do a particular thing, made in a revelation its members could falsify at any moment by stepping forward, is either a proof of divine knowledge or an invitation to refute the Book, and no one has taken up the invitation.

**"Because of What Their Hands Have Done"**

The reason given is not doubt about God or hesitation about the Hereafter. The verse attributes the refusal to the community's own actions, and it uses the image of hands because hands are what carry out a person's decisions. What the community knows about itself prevents it from making a request that would bring it before God immediately.

The same awareness appears elsewhere in the Book when it states that a person will be a witness against himself even as he offers his excuses, and when it describes people who recognise their own record and are silent (75:14–15). The verse here treats that inner knowledge as a fact of human experience rather than a special privilege of one community.

**The Force of the Prediction**

The Book rarely predicts human behaviour in advance, and when it does, the prediction is part of an argument about knowledge. A claim that a group will never do a particular thing, stated in a revelation that the group can test by acting, is either a proof of divine knowledge or a challenge that any member of the community could falsify by stepping forward.

The fact that the challenge has stood for fourteen centuries is part of how the verse has been read. Commentators noted that the community's descendants inherited the same position and the same reluctance, and that the verse's prediction has never needed revision, which is itself a claim the sūrah makes on the reader's attention.

**What the Hands Have Earned**

The phrase about what the hands have done prepares the ground for the next verse, which will describe a people clinging to life. The Book uses this language consistently when it explains a refusal or a punishment: a person's conduct becomes part of his own situation rather than remaining an external record, and what he has done shapes what he wants.

The verses that follow in the sūrah apply the same reasoning to the community's relation to the revelation, and the language recurs in the Book's descriptions of those who rejected messengers they recognised. In each case the accumulated actions of a people determine the shape of what they can accept, and the verse here states the principle in four words.

**Knowledge of the Wrongdoers**

The closing clause states that God has perfect knowledge of the wrongdoers, and the placement of the attribute is deliberate. The community's refusal is known to God in its motive as well as in its form, and the Book's habit of attaching this attribute to its descriptions of conduct removes the possibility that a private calculation escapes notice.

The sūrah has used the same ending for several of the charges in this passage, and the effect accumulates. Each time a community's conduct is described, the sentence closes with a reminder that the description is not the sūrah's inference. What the reader receives is a statement of knowledge rather than a verdict. The attribute also anticipates the following verse, which describes the same people clinging to life, and it tells the reader that the description is based on knowledge rather than on observation from outside. The two sentences together present a portrait delivered by the One who knows both the conduct and the motive behind it.

**The Challenge Repeated Elsewhere**

The Book repeats this exact test in another chapter with the same community, where they are addressed as those who claim to be God's chosen people apart from all humanity and are invited to wish for death if they are truthful (62:6). That verse continues with the same reason for the refusal, describing what their hands have sent ahead of them.

The repetition strengthens the argument the sūrah is making, since it shows that the position described here was a standing feature of the community's belief rather than an isolated claim. The Book returns to it in more than one address, and both times the test is the same and the answer is the same. The repetition establishes that the position described was a settled feature of the community's teaching rather than a remark made once in a heated exchange. The Book returns to it in more than one address, and each time the test is the same and the answer is the same.

**A Test That Reaches Its Own Readers**

The sentence is directed at a specific claim, and its underlying lesson applies more widely. Anyone who believes he is secure regardless of his conduct has made an assertion that his own habits will contradict, and the Book's practice is to point out the contradiction rather than to argue about the doctrine. The verse does not ask the community to change its belief about the Hereafter; it simply notes what it is not willing to do in light of that belief. The sentence is directed at a particular claim, and the lesson beneath it applies to anyone who believes his standing is settled. The Book's practice is to point out the contradiction between such a belief and a person's habits rather than to argue about the doctrine, and the test it applies here is available in every generation.

**What the Silence Demonstrates**

The verse does not describe an argument that the community lost; it describes a test that nobody attempted. That outcome carries more weight than a debate would have, because the challenge was designed to be settled by an action rather than by a reply. A person who believed what the community claimed to believe had only to make a request, and the Book states that no one ever made it. Commentators drew attention to the reason the sūrah supplies: the community knew its own record, and the knowledge was enough to keep it silent. The Book preserves that inner knowledge as a fact of human experience rather than as an accusation aimed at one people, since a person's awareness of what he has done is exactly what prevents him from treating his future as settled.
