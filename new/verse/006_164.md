## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:164

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Should I seek a lord other than Allah while He is the Lord of everything?” No one will reap except what they sow. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another. Then to your Lord is your return, and He will inform you of your differences.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question About Another Lord**

Say, Should I seek a lord other than Allah while He is the Lord of everything? The verse gives the Prophet a question to put to the audience, and the question returns to the ground on which the whole sūrah has stood. The Book's opening command to the Prophet in this sūrah was to declare that he would not take any guardian other than the One who originated the heavens and the earth (6:14), and the sentence in front of us states the same position in the form of a question.

The Book uses the question in other sūrahs as well. In the sūrah of the Troops, the audience is asked whether it is urging the Prophet to worship anyone other than God, and it is addressed as ignorant (39:64); the sūrah of the Forgiver records the Prophet's declaration that he has been forbidden to worship what they worship besides God since the clear proofs have come to him. The verse in front of us belongs with those passages, and it puts the matter in the form that leaves the audience to answer for itself.

**The Lord of Everything**

The question rests on the statement that God is the Lord of everything, and the sūrah has established the claim throughout its account of the signs. The stars, the rain, the harvest, the herds, and the human beings themselves have been presented as the provision of one Lord, and the sūrah has stated that God is the Creator of all things and the Maintainer of everything (6:102). The phrase in front of us supplies the reason no other lord can be sought.

The Book's statements of the same fact appear in its descriptions of God's relation to creation, and the sūrah has supplied them in its accounts of the origin of human life and the ordering of the heavens. The sentence in front of us applies the fact to the question of worship, and it leaves the audience with the choice between the Lord of everything and the beings who own nothing.

**No One Reaps Except What He Sows**

No one will reap except what they sow. The sentence states the arrangement of the reckoning, and the Book attaches the same principle to the accountability of every person. The sūrah of the Star declares that each person will have only what he endeavoured towards (53:39), and the sūrah of Fussilat says that whoever does good does so for his own benefit and whoever does evil does so to his own loss (41:46). The sūrah of the Cattle itself has stated the two measures of reward and punishment in the verse before this passage (6:160).

The image of reaping describes the relation between conduct and outcome, and the Book uses it to place responsibility on the person who acted. The sūrah has described the audience's appeal to its ancestors and its reliance on the practices it had inherited, and the sentence in front of us removes the support that such an appeal provides.

**No Soul Bears Another's Burden**

No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another. The sentence states the same principle in the language of carrying, and the Book repeats it in several places. It is stated in the sūrah of the Star that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another (53:38); the sūrah of Fāṭir says the same and adds that a soul crying out for help with its burden will not be relieved, even by a close relative (35:18); the sūrah of the Troops states that no bearer of burdens bears another's (39:7); and the sūrah of the Night Journey gives the same statement in its account of the responsibility of every person (17:15).

The Book's rejection of substitution extends to the intercession that the audience expected from its idols. The sūrah has already stated that no protector or intercessor besides God will help the people who are warned (6:51) and that the intercessors the audience claimed will be absent on the day of judgment (6:94). The sentence in front of us states the principle that underlies those passages, and the sūrah of the Cow's warning about the day when no soul will be of help to another adds the same list of things that will not be accepted (2:48).

**The Return and the Settlement of Differences**

Then to your Lord is your return, and He will inform you of your differences. The sentence closes the verse with the statement of the outcome, and the Book uses the same formula in its accounts of the disputes among the people of the earlier books. The sūrah of the Table states that God will inform people of what they differed about (5:48), the sūrah of the Pilgrimage says that God will judge between them on the day of judgement regarding their differences (22:69), and the sūrah of the Troops states that their differences will be settled before their Lord.

The sūrah's own treatment of the divisions among the communities appears in the previous passage, where the judgment of those who split into sects is left with God (6:159). The sentence in front of us completes that arrangement by stating that the differences which occupied the people of the earlier books will be presented and explained, and it leaves the community with the revelation it has received.

**What the Verse Answers in the Sūrah**

The verse answers the audience's position on three points that the sūrah has been examining. It answers the association of other lords with God by naming Him the Lord of everything; it answers the reliance on inherited religion and on intercession by stating that each person reaps only what he sows; and it answers the disputes among the communities by referring them to the day when God will inform people of what they differed about. The Prophet's declaration in the verses above is followed by the terms on which everyone else is judged.

The Book's habit of closing a long argument with a summary of accountability appears in several sūrahs, and the sūrah of the Cattle supplies its own version here. The reader who has followed the argument from the signs to the dietary rules and the covenant is left with the plainest statement of what a person carries to his Lord.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that God has placed people as successors on the earth and elevated some of them in rank above others so that He may test them with what He has given them, and it closes the sūrah with the declaration that the Lord is swift in punishment but All-Forgiving, Most Merciful (6:165). The sentence takes up the theme of the ranks and the test, and it supplies the two attributes that frame the sūrah's final word.

The two verses together bring the chapter to its end. The accountability of the individual is stated, the difference of ranks is explained as a test, and the attributes of the Lord are named. The reader is left with the Book, the path, and the reckoning that the sūrah has described from its opening verses.
