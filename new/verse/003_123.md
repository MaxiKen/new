## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:123

> Indeed, Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were ˹vastly˺ outnumbered. So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Victory Recalled Before a Trial**

The verse reminds the believers that God gave them victory at Badr when they were few, and it tells them to be mindful of God so that they may be grateful. The reminder is placed between the recollection of the march to Uḥud and the promises of angelic aid that follow.

The position is the point. The sūrah is about to describe a day of loss, and it first recalls a day of victory in which the same community was vastly outnumbered. A reader who holds the two together learns that neither outcome was determined by numbers, and the instruction at the end of the verse tells him what does determine it.

**The Disproportion at Badr**

The sūrah has already described the two armies at Badr and has stated that the believers saw their enemy at twice their number (3:13). The report of the Prophet's supplication on that day, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (1763), describes him calling on God with the words that if the small band with him were destroyed, none would remain to worship God on the earth.

The community's later memory placed its own numbers at three hundred and thirteen against roughly a thousand of the enemy. The verse's phrase about their being few matches that memory, and its use of the plural verb addresses the whole community rather than the Prophet alone. What happened at Badr is presented as something the believers did and something God gave.

**The Victory Attributed to the Giver**

The verse's construction places God as the agent and the community as the recipients. The Book presents this pattern in its other accounts of the battle, where it states that the believers called on their Lord and were answered with the promise of support.

Attribution in the Book is not the same as denying the community's part. The believers marched, fought, and held their ground, and the Book says so elsewhere. What the sentence under discussion does is refuse the inference that their own effort accounted for the outcome, and the four words it uses make the point in the plainest possible way.

**The Pivot to Mindfulness**

The second half of the verse turns the recollection into an instruction, telling the believers to be mindful of God so that they may be grateful. The connection between mindfulness and gratitude is the verse's own logic: a person who lives with awareness of God recognises where his successes come from.

The same pairing occurs in the sūrah's earlier command, where the believers are told to be mindful of God in the way He deserves, and in the passages that tie God's help to the community's state. The Book treats gratitude as a practice rather than as a feeling that occurs on its own, and it attaches the practice to the awareness that produces it.

**Why the Earlier Victory Is Named**

The sūrah's use of Badr in this passage is not nostalgia. It is the provision of a case that the community could check against its own experience. The men being addressed had been at Badr; many of them had been at Uḥud as well, and the two events together were the material from which the sūrah's lessons were drawn.

The Book's habit of arguing from events its audience had lived through gives the passage its force. A community that has won against the odds and then lost when the conditions were met differently is in a position to understand a lesson about obedience and help that no amount of abstract instruction could supply, and the verse names the earlier event for exactly that purpose.

**Gratitude in the Book's Vocabulary**

The Book's word for gratitude carries the sense of acknowledging a benefit, and its opposite is the refusal to recognise what has been received. Gratitude in this vocabulary is an act of recognition that shows itself in conduct.

The verse's phrasing therefore makes the community's future conduct the measure of its gratitude. What is being asked for is not an emotion at the memory of a victory but a life shaped by the awareness that the victory was given. That is why the command to be mindful comes first in the sentence: the awareness is the root, and the gratitude is its fruit.

**The Verse's Role in the Passage**

The passage that follows describes the promise of angelic support, the divine purpose behind the alternation of days, and the testing of the community. The reminder of Badr supplies the premise beneath all of it.

The reader is being taught to read his own history as the Book reads the community's. Defeats are examined for their causes, victories are attributed to their giver, and both are used to establish a way of living. The verse's brevity is what allows it to serve as a hinge between the recollection of the march and the series of promises that follows.

**One Battle Mentioned Twice**

The sūrah refers to Badr in two places. The first presents the two armies as a sign and states that the believers saw their enemy at twice their number (3:13). The second, in the verse before the reader, names the same event as a reminder of help already given.

The two uses serve different purposes. The first belongs to the sūrah's opening argument about the fate of those who reject the message; the second belongs to the passage that prepares the community for another battle. The Book's habit of returning to a single event with a new application shows how its history functions: the same fact can be evidence in one argument and encouragement in another, and the community was expected to know the event well enough to follow both uses.
