## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:68

> Indeed, those who have the best claim to Abraham are his followers, this Prophet, and the believers. And Allah is the Guardian of those who believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Who Has a Claim on Abraham**

Having said what Abraham was not, the verse says who belongs to him. Three parties are named: those who followed him, this Prophet, and the believers. The order runs from Abraham's own contemporaries to the messenger of the sūrah and then to the community that has accepted him.

The structure of the claim is the point. Belonging is defined by following, and the verse's first category consists of the people who followed Abraham while he was alive and afterward. Everything that follows is measured by the same standard, which is why the two later parties can be named in the same sentence as the first. What is being described is a continuous body of people united by a way rather than by blood.

**Follower Before Ancestor**

The disputes the sūrah has been answering turned on ancestry, and this verse replaces ancestry with adherence. The Arabic term used for the followers describes those who came after a person on a road, taking his direction, and the Book uses it for the relation of a community to a prophet throughout.

The replacement has consequences that the sūrah's readers would feel. A claim resting on descent can be inspected in a genealogy; a claim resting on following can be inspected in conduct. The Book consistently prefers the second test, and it does so without denying that the first exists, since the descent of the Prophet of this sūrah from Abraham through Ishmael is part of the tradition. What the verse refuses is the notion that the genealogy settles the matter. A descendant who does not follow the ancestor's religion is not being described here as the ancestor's, and the Book makes that point elsewhere in explicit terms when it denies that a covenant extends to wrongdoers (2:124).

**"This Prophet" — the Manifest Reference**

The second party named is the Prophet of the sūrah, and the phrase used is the standard designation for Muhammad in the Qur'an's argument with the scriptured communities. It marks the presence of a living claimant to Abraham's way in the middle of the dispute.

The wording also carries a claim the sūrah has already made in the passage about Abraham. If following is the criterion, then the messenger who has brought the clearest statement of the oneness of God and the rejection of all partners stands closest to the man who did the same in Ur. That is the argument's logic, and the sūrah states it directly here rather than leaving it to be inferred. The claim is not that Abraham was a Muslim of the later community, which the previous verse explicitly denies; it is that the religion of Abraham and the message of this Prophet are one, and that the people who follow that religion are his people.

**The Believers Included**

The third party is the believing community, and its inclusion extends the claim beyond the messenger. Nobody in the community has Abraham's lineage, and none of them lived in his era, yet the verse names them among those who have the best claim to him.

The extension is what makes the verse a statement about the religion rather than about a personal succession. A community assembled from different tribes and backgrounds is told that its relation to the founding monotheist is established by what it holds, and the same doctrine appears in the Book's account of Abraham's prayer for a nation to be raised from his descendants who would submit to God, which the Book presents as answered in the community of the Prophet. The sūrah's claim is that the answer is recognisable in the present community's confession rather than in its ancestry.

**God as Protector of the Believers**

The verse ends with a statement about God's relation to the believers: He is their guardian. The word used is the same one the sūrah employed in the passage about taking disbelievers as guardians, and the placement of the two passages within one sūrah is not accidental.

What the closing clause does is to state the source of the community's protection once it has renounced the protection of other parties. The sūrah warned the believers against seeking security from those who did not share their faith; it now tells them whose protection they hold in exchange, and it states it as a fact about God's relation to them rather than as a promise of what will happen. The community's confidence does not rest on its size or its allies, and the verse supplies the alternative in five words.

**A Definition That Reads Both Ways**

The verse's three parties are not merely names of groups who belong to Abraham; they are described as people who would recognise themselves in the description. Reading it, a believer is told that his standing rests on the same thing as Abraham's, and the sūrah leaves him to check whether that is where his confidence sits.

That makes the verse a criterion as much as a statement of fact, and it applies to the dispute the sūrah has been conducting as well as to its own audience.

It also explains the order in which the three parties are listed. The followers come first because they set the standard, the messenger comes second because he is the living instance of the same way, and the believers come last because they are the community being formed by it. A reader who works through the list finds his own name in the third position and his own standing in the first, since the question the verse leaves with him is whether he belongs among the people who followed. What follows in the sūrah is a description of the people who had the opportunity and did something else.
