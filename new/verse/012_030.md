## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:30

> Some women of the city gossiped, “The Chief Minister’s wife is trying to seduce her slave-boy. Love for him has plagued her heart. Indeed, we see that she is clearly mistaken.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instruction to Conceal Lasts One Verse**

Verse 29 closed with a settlement: Joseph to be silent, the wife to seek forgiveness, the matter to end. Verse 30 opens with the matter being discussed across a city. Nothing in the text describes the leak. No servant is named, no rumour is traced, and no explanation is offered for how a scene confined to a locked room became public property. The chapter simply cuts from the household to the street, which is how gossip actually behaves — it does not arrive through a narrated channel, it is discovered already in circulation. The juxtaposition of the two verses is the sūrah's comment on the husband's method. Reputation is not a possession that can be secured by instruction, and the man who tried to close the case by telling two people what to say has no control over what a city says.

**"Some Women of the City" Locates the Speakers by Class**

The phrase is *niswatun mina al-madīnah* — women from the city, a partitive construction indicating a group within a larger population rather than the population itself. The commentators identify them as the wives of notables, and the following verses confirm the identification: she invites them to a banquet, they come, and their judgement carries weight enough that she feels compelled to answer it. This is not street talk. It is the conversation of the class that sets opinion among the households of the ruling establishment, which is why the woman's response in verse 31 takes the form of a reception rather than a denial. She is not defending herself to the public; she is defending herself to her peers, and the peers are the audience whose verdict she can neither ignore nor control.

**What They Say Is Accurate**

The content of the gossip deserves notice because it is true. She has been attempting to seduce her slave-boy, and the verb they use is the same one the narrator used in verse 23 and Joseph used in his defence in verse 26. The city's account matches the evidence exactly. That fact complicates the usual treatment of gossip in moral instruction, and the chapter does not resolve the complication. Speech that is accurate can still be damaging, and it damages here: the woman's response is a public demonstration that will end with a man in prison. The sūrah records the accuracy without endorsing the circulation, and in doing so it distinguishes between the truth of a statement and the rightness of repeating it — a distinction the household in verse 29 tried to make in the opposite direction and failed.

**"Love Has Plagued Her Heart" Uses an Anatomical Idiom**

The phrase *shaghafahā ḥubban* is built on *shaghāf*, the membrane surrounding the heart. The idiom describes a love that has reached the covering of the organ and penetrated it — not an affection held in the mind but one that has taken the body. Commentators gloss it as love that has occupied the deepest recess of her heart and become all-consuming, and the Qur'an's choice of a physical term over an emotional one is deliberate. What the women are describing is a condition with symptoms, visible in behaviour: the locked doors, the pursuit, the accusation, the demand for a penalty. The verse thereby confirms something the story has already shown. Her feeling is not a passing fancy that could be talked out of; it is an occupation, and it will drive the next four verses.

**"Clearly Mistaken" Is the Brothers' Phrase**

The women's verdict is *fī ḍalāl mubīn*, in manifest error — the identical formula the brothers used about their father in verse 8: *“Indeed, our father is clearly mistaken.”* (12:8) The parallel is exact and it is not accidental. In both cases a group observes an intense attachment to Joseph, judges the attachment to be a deviation, and says so in the vocabulary of religion. Jacob was called misguided for loving his son too much; the ʿAzīz's wife is called misguided for desiring a young man too much. The chapter is tracking a pattern: love of Joseph produces an accusation of error from bystanders, and in both cases the bystanders are wrong about the nature of what they are seeing. Jacob's love was prophetic insight. The woman's love will become, in the end, the occasion of a public confession that clears Joseph's name (12:51).

**Gossip as Strategy, and the Woman's Reading of It**

The next verse describes what the women did as *makr*, plotting or scheming, even though the text has just recorded them as merely talking. The commentators explain the classification: their speech was conducted in secret, which makes it scheming rather than open criticism, and al-Rāzī adds a sharper motive — they wanted to see Joseph for themselves and calculated that their talk would force the woman to show him to them in order to clear her name. On that reading the gossip is an instrument rather than an opinion, and the sūrah classifies idle speech as a form of planning. The classification is not a moral exaggeration. Talk conducted in secret about a person who cannot answer it produces consequences for him that he did not choose and cannot contest, which is the definition of a scheme. What the women wanted was access; what their talk set in motion was a chain that ends with the subject of it in a cell. The chapter treats concealed speech as an act with a victim, and it does so by naming it.
