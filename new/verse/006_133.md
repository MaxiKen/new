## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:133

> Your Lord is the Self-Sufficient, Full of Mercy. If He wills, He can do away with you and replace you with whoever He wills, just as He produced you from the offspring of other people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Self-Sufficient Lord**

Your Lord is the Self-Sufficient, Full of Mercy. The verse states two attributes together, and the pairing carries the argument of the passage. The Self-Sufficient describes a being who has no need of anything He has made, and the sūrah of Fāṭir addresses humanity with the same contrast, telling them that they are the ones who stand in need of God while He is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy (35:15). The sūrah of Luqmān states that whatever is in the heavens and the earth belongs to God and that He is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy (31:26).

The two attributes appear together in the Book's treatment of human ingratitude and divine patience. The sūrah of Ibrāhīm records Moses saying that if the people and everyone on earth were ungrateful, God would still be Self-Sufficient and Praiseworthy (14:8), and the sūrah of the Cow states that kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury, since God is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing (2:263). The verse in front of us names the same independence and joins it to mercy, and the combination is what makes the sentence that follows intelligible.

**The Power to Replace a People**

If He wills, He can do away with you and replace you with whoever He wills. The sentence states the consequence of God's self-sufficiency for a community that rejects His message. The Book makes the same statement in several places: the sūrah of the Women says that if it is God's will He can remove humanity altogether and replace them with others, and that He is most capable of that (4:133); the sūrah of Fāṭir says that if He willed He could eliminate them and produce a new creation (35:16); and the sūrah of the Repentance tells the believers that if they do not march forth, God will afflict them and replace them with another people, and that they will not harm Him at all (9:39).

The Book's statements about replacement are addressed both to the community that receives the message and to the community that rejects it. The sūrah of Muhammad tells those who are invited to spend that whoever withholds does so to his own loss, and it states that God is self-sufficient while they are the ones in need. What the verse in front of us states is the possibility of replacement as a fact about God's freedom rather than as a threat that depends on the audience's importance.

**The Precedent of the Generations**

Just as He produced you from the offspring of other people. The clause supplies the evidence for the power being described, and the Book returns repeatedly to the succession of generations. The sūrah of the Cattle states at its end that God placed human beings as successors on earth and raised some of them above others in rank as a test (6:165), and the sūrah of the Human states that God created them and perfected their form and that if He willed He could replace them with others (76:28).

The Makkans' confidence rested on their position in the region and on the continuity of their community, and the Book's answer is that the same power which brought them into being after another people can bring another people into being after them. The clause treats the present generation as one in a series rather than as a permanent arrangement, and the sūrah has been describing the fate of the earlier communities throughout the chapter.

**What the Verse Says About the Audience's Standing**

The verse removes the assumption that the community's existence matters to God in a way that would make the judgement negotiable. The Book states that God has no need of His creation and that the mercy by which He deals with it is a gift rather than an obligation. The two attributes in the first sentence carry the whole argument: the self-sufficiency means the judgement is not a transaction, and the mercy means that it is not arbitrary.

The Book makes the same point in its address to the people who imagine that their wealth or their power gives them standing with God. The sūrah of Saba' states that God gives provision abundantly to whom He wills and measures it out, and the sūrah of the Cattle states that God has elevated some people in rank in this life, which is itself a test. The verse in front of us supplies the principle behind those statements.

**The Comfort Contained in the Sentence**

The verse is addressed to the Prophet, and it is one of several passages in which the sūrah prepares him for the possibility that his people will not believe. The statement that God could replace them is not a threat directed at the Prophet; it is the assurance that the mission is not dependent on the response of a particular community, and that if the message is rejected God's purpose is not defeated.

The sūrah has given the same assurance in other forms. It told the Prophet that he was not a keeper over the people (6:107), that God knows best where to place His message (6:124), and that the opening of hearts belongs to God (6:125). The verse in front of us states the same fact about the outcome of the mission, and it grounds the statement in the attributes of the One who sends it.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that what the audience has been promised will certainly come to pass and that they will have no escape (6:134). The two verses form a pair: the Lord is self-sufficient and free to replace them, and the promise he has made will be fulfilled regardless of their response. The passage then turns to the instruction given to the Prophet about how to conduct himself among the people who persist in their ways (6:135).

The sūrah's argument in this section is therefore complete. The reckoning is just because the warning was delivered, the ranks correspond to deeds, and the power of God is not constrained by the community that denies Him. The verse in front of us states the middle term of that argument and connects the freedom of God to the mercy by which He acts.
