## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:58

> Say ˹also˺, “If what you seek to hasten were within my power, the matter between us would have already been settled. But Allah knows the wrongdoers best.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence That Answers the Challenge by Granting It**

The verse takes the Makkan demand seriously for one clause and then shows where it leads. If the timing of the punishment had been in the Prophet's hands, he tells them, the matter between us would already be finished. The sentence is built as a hypothetical, and the hypothetical is the argument: it demonstrates what the audience's own request would have produced if it had been granted.

Nothing in the sentence needs elaboration for the Makkans to feel its force. They had been crowding around the Prophet asking him to bring down what he was warning them about, and the verse lets them imagine a world in which he could. Their next breath would have been their last, and the fact that they were still arguing was evidence about who held the decision, not evidence about the message.

**What the Hypothetical Reveals About the Delay**

The sentence explains the delay as the product of a decision that has not yet been reached rather than as a failure of the threat. The Book makes the same point at length when it describes how the wrongdoers are drawn on step by step in ways they cannot comprehend, and how their end is postponed while the planning proceeds (7:182–183). The delay is described elsewhere as a period in which people are given time, and the Book warns against reading the postponement as approval (3:178).

For the Prophet's own audience, that meant the shouting matches of the Makkan market were still possible because they were being tolerated. The verse turns the delay into a fact about God's patience rather than a fact about the Prophet's weakness, which is the whole purpose of putting the statement in his mouth.

**The Verse Pair and the Difference Between Them**

The two verses stand together as a single answer. The first says that the punishment is not in the Prophet's power and that judgment belongs to God, who declares the truth and is the best of judges (6:57). The second says what would have happened if the first statement had been false. Together they form a demonstration that the audience had asked for, delivered in the form of a condition.

That structure is a favourite of the Book: it makes the audience's own demand the instrument of their correction. A question raised against the messenger is answered by showing what the answer would cost, and the audience is left with the only conclusion available.

**"The Matter Between Us Would Have Been Settled"**

The phrase describes a dispute brought to its end by a ruling, and the sūrah has just described God as the one who decides. The Book uses similar language where it speaks of judgment being passed between people in fairness, with no one wronged (10:47), and where it says that God will judge between those who differ. The settlement in view here is final and total, and it includes the people who are asking the questions.

The expression also implies that the dispute was real and that it had two sides. The verse does not present the Makkans' position as a misunderstanding to be cleared up, and it does not present the Prophet's position as a preference. It treats the matter as a case awaiting a decision, which is exactly how the Book describes the relation between a messenger and a people who reject him.

**"Allah Knows the Wrongdoers Best"**

The closing clause might look like a step down from the high statement of judgment that preceded it. It is not. The sentence states that the parties to the dispute are known, and that the one who will judge has the full record of every one of them. The Book uses the same construction where it says that God knows the wrongdoers best, and elsewhere when it says that He knows best who has strayed from His path and who is rightly guided (6:117).

Knowledge of that kind removes the last remaining hope that any party could argue its case on the day. The Makkans could accuse the Prophet in the market, and he could answer them, and the Book's response to the whole exchange is that the judge already knows what both sides have done.

**The Prophet's Position After the Two Verses**

After making these two declarations, the Prophet has said publicly that he stands on evidence, that his people have denied it, that the punishment is not in his hands, that God decides its time, that God declares the truth, that God is the best of judges, and that the punishment would already have fallen if the timing had been left to him. There is nothing left for the audience to ask him for, and the sūrah moves straight from this point into one of its most expansive statements about the reach of God's knowledge.

That statement begins with a claim no creature shares, and it is introduced without any transition, since the audience has just been told that the decision rests with the One who knows everything that exists.

The difference between the two positions is stated by the sūrah a few verses later, where God is described as knowing best who has strayed from His way and who is rightly guided (6:117). The Makkans judged by the standards of their market and their ancestry, and the Prophet spoke from a proof he had received. The verse's last words place the assessment of both parties in the hands of the one who needs no evidence to reach a conclusion, which is the reason the passage can end on a note of confidence rather than on a threat.
