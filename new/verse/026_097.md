## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:97

> “By Allah! We were clearly mistaken,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condemned Swear by the God They Once Denied**

The deviant begin their confession with an oath by Allah. The oath is striking because they had treated other objects as worthy of worship. At judgment, the name of the true Lord becomes unavoidable. They do not swear by their idols; they invoke Allah while admitting that their former allegiance was wrong.

The oath does not turn the confession into a new act of obedience that saves them. It gives the admission solemnity. They recognize the authority that should have governed them all along, but they recognize it after the life of choice has ended. The name they used too late is the name they should have served in the world.

**The Error Is Called Clear**

They say that they were clearly mistaken. The word clear removes the shelter of ambiguity. They are not describing a minor miscalculation or an unfortunate outcome that no one could foresee. They recognize that the error was exposed by the signs, the weakness of the idols, and the truth of the messengers.

This clarity also judges their former excuses. They had inherited worship, followed leaders, and relied on social custom. Those influences may explain how the error spread, but they do not make it sound. The confession now separates explanation from justification. What they did may have been common, but common error remains error.

**Confession Arrives After the Chance to Change**

An admission of guilt can be the beginning of repentance when life remains. Here the context is the Hereafter, where the condemned are crying and disputing. Their confession is truthful, but it does not function as the return that would have changed their standing. The timing exposes the tragedy of postponement.

A person often knows enough to begin correction before possessing perfect understanding. Waiting for the final display of consequences is not wisdom. It is the loss of the very opportunity that makes confession fruitful. The verse lets the living hear a true admission from people who can no longer turn it into a changed life.

The speakers say we were mistaken. Their words acknowledge shared participation without erasing individual responsibility. They worshipped within a community, repeated its practices, and allowed its authorities to shape their allegiance. The plural confession shows that error can be social as well as personal.

Yet the word we must not become a way to hide behind the group. Each speaker is confessing his own inclusion in the community that turned away. A crowd can influence a person, but it cannot make a final choice for him. The confession is honest because it admits both dimensions: the social order was wrong, and we belonged to it.

**The Oath Reverses the Earlier Religious Order**

In worldly life, the idols received devotion while Allah's claim was ignored or divided. In the Fire, the speakers swear by Allah and name their error. The order has been reversed, but only as a confession. The true center appears when the false center has already failed.

This reversal is a warning against postponing monotheism until crisis. A person may acknowledge God verbally while giving practical authority to wealth, ancestors, leaders, or desire. The oath of the condemned shows that words become empty when the heart has spent its life serving rivals. Allah's name must govern the choices made before judgment, not merely the regret expressed after it.

Quran 67:11 records the inhabitants of the Blaze confessing their sins. That parallel confirms that recognition of wrongdoing can appear in the final scene without becoming a path out of it. The confession is morally accurate, but it follows the loss of the worldly period in which repentance, repair, and renewed worship were possible.

The point is not to discourage confession now. It is to encourage it before the heart becomes fixed in denial. A living admission can lead to forgiveness when joined to return. A dead admission shows only that the truth was eventually recognized.

**Clarity Should Produce Action Before the End**

The condemned call their error clear, but the verse gives the living a chance to call it clear earlier. Abraham's argument has already shown why idols cannot hear, benefit, or save. The final confession confirms the argument, but believers should not need the Fire to persuade them.

Clear knowledge becomes valuable when it changes allegiance. A person who sees that a created thing cannot be a lord should withdraw worship from it. A person who sees that custom is not proof should examine inherited practice. The verse demands more than agreement with the confession; it asks the living not to repeat the life that produced it.

The speakers finally use accurate language. They do not say that the idols were unlucky or that God treated them unfairly. They say that they were mistaken. The moral vocabulary is restored, but restored too late. Truthful speech is therefore shown as a gift that should be used while choices remain open.

A believer should practice this honesty before crisis: name a wrong as wrong, admit dependence on God, and resist the need to protect the self through excuses. Such truthfulness may be painful, but it is a form of mercy. The final oath shows where every honest account must end: with recognition that God alone deserved their allegiance. That recognition should arrive through repentance, not through the forced clarity of punishment. Honest speech while life remains can repair what a late confession can only describe. It can lead to changed worship, restored rights, and a different direction. The oath of the condemned shows what truth sounds like after those opportunities have closed, before judgment arrives.
