## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:12

> ˹There is˺ also ˹the example of˺ Mary, the daughter of ’Imrân, who guarded her chastity, so We breathed into her ˹womb˺ through Our angel ˹Gabriel˺. She testified to the words of her Lord and His Scriptures, and was one of the ˹sincerely˺ devout.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Model, and a Woman With No Husband at All**

The verse continues the sentence begun in the previous one, so that Mary is joined to Pharaoh's wife as the second example set forth for the believers. The progression across the chapter's four women is deliberate. Two were married to righteous men and were lost. One was married to a tyrant and was saved. The fourth was married to nobody.

That last step completes the argument. Having shown that a good household does not save and a bad one does not condemn, the chapter produces a case in which the household is absent entirely, and the woman is commended in her own name. For a sūrah that opened inside a marriage, spent five verses on marital friction, and warned about replacement wives, closing on an unmarried woman held up as a model for all believers is a considered ending. The marital frame that organised the first half is set aside in the last line.

**Guarding What Was Publicly Doubted**

*“who guarded her chastity”* (66:12) is the first thing said of her, and it is said in answer to something. Mary's people accused her when she returned carrying a child, and the Qur'an preserves their words and her vindication in the nineteenth sūrah. The same description appears there in the parallel verse: *“And ˹remember˺ the one who guarded her chastity, so We breathed into her through Our angel, ˹Gabriel,˺ making her and her son a sign for all peoples”* (21:91).

The repetition of the description across sūrahs is a defence entered into the permanent record. What her community concluded about her was wrong, and the correction is issued not once but wherever she is mentioned. This has a bearing on the chapter that contains it. The sūrah began with a private matter in the Prophet's house becoming public, and ends with a woman whose private life was publicly misjudged and publicly cleared. In both cases the verdict that stands is the one God issued, not the one the neighbours reached.

**The Spirit Breathed In, and What the Commentators Dispute**

*“so We breathed into her ˹womb˺ through Our angel ˹Gabriel˺”* (66:12) states the conception without explaining it, and the Arabic says literally that We breathed into it of Our Spirit. What *Our Spirit* refers to has been read three ways. Many take it as Gabriel, the angel who appeared to her, and the translation follows this reading. Others understand it as a spirit among the spirits God creates, here the spirit of Jesus himself. A third reading takes the phrase at its most literal, as the Spirit of God.

The disagreement is old and the stakes in it are doctrinal rather than merely linguistic, since the Qur'an is emphatic that Jesus was a servant and messenger and warns against going to extremes in exactly this matter: *“The Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger of Allah and the fulfilment of His Word through Mary and a spirit ˹created by a command˺ from Him”* (4:171). The Gabriel reading has the advantage of fitting the narrative of the annunciation and of avoiding any suggestion of a divine component entering a human body. What all three readings preserve is the grammar of the sentence: the action is God's, Mary is its recipient, and the possessive *Our* attaches the breath to Him without making the child anything other than created.

**Belief in Words and in Books**

*“She testified to the words of her Lord and His Scriptures”* (66:12) describes her faith in two registers. The commentators read the scriptures as the revealed books generally, so that she believed in all that had been sent down before her. The words of her Lord are usually read as His commands and decrees, including the ones addressed personally to her, which in her case were extraordinarily demanding.

The distinction matters because the two are not the same test. Believing the Books is a general commitment shared by every person of faith in her tradition. Accepting the word spoken directly to her required something else: she was told she would bear a child without a husband, in a society certain to draw the obvious conclusion, and her recorded response is a question about how this could be rather than a refusal. The verse credits her with both — the general faith and the particular submission — and puts the words before the Books, which is the order in which she actually met them.

**"Among the Devoutly Obedient"**

The final phrase, *min al-qānitīn*, closes the chapter. The word is the same one used earlier in the sūrah for the better wives God might supply — devoutly obedient, constant, standing in worship (66:5) — and applying it to Mary settles how it should be read there. A woman with no husband is described by the term, so the obedience it names is owed to God and to nobody else.

Two further details are worth drawing out. The form used is masculine plural, *al-qānitīn* rather than *al-qānitāt*, which the grammarians explain as placing her among the devout as a class rather than among devout women specifically; some read the phrase as indicating that she came from a devout lineage, the family of ʿImrān whom the Qur'an names among those God chose above all people (3:33). Either way, the chapter ends by locating a woman inside the general body of the faithful rather than in a category of her own. That is the conclusion the whole passage has been building toward. The sūrah that began by correcting a household ends by establishing that the unit which is finally weighed is the person.
