## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:99

> Indeed, We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ clear revelations. ˹But˺ none will deny them except the rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement About the Revelation Itself**

The verse names the revelations sent down as clear, and it then describes who denies them. Both halves are brief, and together they make an argument the sūrah has been building since its opening: the difficulty being described in this passage is not a shortage of evidence but a form of conduct.

The word rendered clear is used across the Book for signs that carry their own proof, and it applies both to the verses of the revelation and to the events that accompanied the messengers. In this sentence the two senses converge, since the community being addressed had access to the recitation and to the record of what its own prophets had brought. The claim carried a specific force in a community whose scholars expected a prophet and knew the criteria for recognizing one. What the verse asserts is that those criteria were met, and the denial it describes is therefore a judgment about conduct rather than about the clarity of what was presented.

**What the Clear Signs Were**

Commentators understood the signs mentioned here as the marks of prophethood themselves: the Prophet's character, the knowledge he had been given, and the content of what he recited, all of which the learned among the People of the Book were in a position to assess. The sūrah's own argument depends on that assessment being possible, since it repeatedly asks the community to compare the revelation with its own scripture.

The Book elsewhere treats the clarity of revelation as one of its defining features, stating that it has been made easy to understand and that its verses are well expounded. The claim carried a specific force in a community whose scholars expected a prophet and knew the criteria for recognizing one.

**Who Denies Them**

The sentence says that none will deny the clear signs except the rebellious, and the Arabic term describes a person who steps outside a boundary he knows. The Book uses the word for conduct rather than for opinion, and its appearance here identifies denial as a decision rather than a conclusion reached by reasoning.

That identification is the sūrah's consistent approach to unbelief. Elsewhere it states that signs do not benefit people who do not believe, and it describes denial that follows recognition rather than preceding it. The verse in this place is a summary of that position: the revelation is clear, and what stands against it is a disposition that has already crossed a line. That identification is the sūrah's consistent approach to unbelief, and it appears again in its descriptions of people who declined what they had already recognised. The word chosen here describes conduct rather than opinion, and it locates the offence in a decision rather than in a conclusion reached by reasoning.

**The Record of Covenants Behind the Statement**

The next verses list the community's pattern of casting aside covenants and following what the devils had promoted, and the sentence about the rebellious prepares the reader for that account. The sūrah is not describing a group that lacked information; it is describing conduct that had repeated itself across generations and that its own history documented.

Read in that sequence, the verse functions as a heading. It states the moral character of the denial, and the verses that follow fill in the details: the messenger who came confirming their scriptures, the book they threw behind their backs, and the practices they adopted instead. **Denial and the Will**

The Book's treatment of denial as a matter of will rather than of evidence runs through this sūrah, and the verse states it in its most compact form. The Qur'an's invitations are addressed to people who are asked to act on what they already know, and its rebukes are aimed at those who know and decline. This is why the Book's descriptions of unbelief so often include a reference to knowledge.

The same principle gives the sūrah's address to the believers its shape. When the commands and warnings reach the Muslims later in the chapter, they are phrased as reminders rather than as new information, and the verses about the earlier community serve as the evidence that knowledge alone does not produce obedience.

**The Clear Signs Already Recited**

By this point in the chapter the reader has encountered the signs of the sūrah itself: the accounts of the covenant, the sea, the mountain, the manna, the springs, and the raising of the dead. Each was given as an argument, and each was met with a response the Book records. The statement that the revelations are clear lands after that record has been assembled.

That placement matters for how the verse is heard. It is not a claim made in the abstract about the quality of revelation; it is a claim made about a specific message delivered to a specific community whose history the reader has just been shown. The sentence therefore closes one section of the argument and opens another in which the community's conduct is described in detail.

**What the Verse Rules Out**

The sentence rules out the possibility that the dispute in this passage was about the evidence. Whatever else the community's position involved, it did not involve a lack of clarity in what had been sent down, and the Book names the alternative explanation in a single word. **Clarity and the Burden of Proof**

A revelation described as clear places the responsibility for its rejection on the one who receives it rather than on the message. The Book makes this claim repeatedly and applies it to its own hearers as well, telling them that the truth has come and that what stands against it is a disposition rather than a missing argument. The verse in this place prepares the reader for the account that follows, in which the community's conduct is set out clause by clause: the covenants cast aside, the messenger met with objection, and the book that was thrown behind the back. Each item in that account confirms the sentence here, and the account itself serves the same purpose for the community now holding the revelation. What the sūrah establishes is that clarity is not a property the reader confers on revelation by accepting it; it is a property of the revelation that the reader is answerable for.
