## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:99

> Indeed, We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ clear revelations. ˹But˺ none will deny them except the rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Signs Sent Down, Clear by Description**

After the angel-exchange, the recital states the message's own quality in a single declarative sentence: *We have sent down to you clear revelations*. The noun is the Qur'an's standing word for its units — *āyāt*, signs — and the adjective *bayyināt* is its most honored qualifier: signs that are clear in themselves and clearing in their effect, the things that make plain. The commentators specify what the clarity consisted of for the verse's first audience: the signs of Muhammad's ﷺ prophethood — his personal qualities, the knowledge he possessed, the confirmation his message carried — evidence that should have been apparent, Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī note, to any believing Jew, meaning to anyone holding the Torah with the expectation the Torah itself installs. The descent-verb keeps the verse's chain-logic from the previous exchange intact: these signs are *sent down*, from the Sender the objection tried to reach past its couriers — and their clarity is therefore not a human achievement but a divine provision, the evidence supplied with the same Will that supplied the messenger. The address is to the Prophet ﷺ — *to you* — but the clarity is for everyone: a sign sent down to one heart is posted to the world, and the verse's second half treats it as publicly legible, which is the premise the whole sentence runs on.

**None Deny Them Except the Rebellious**

The restriction clause is the verse's judgment, and its exclusivity is total: *none will deny them except the rebellious*. The sentence does not say most deniers are rebellious, or that rebellion often produces denial — it makes rebellion the necessary condition of this denial, the only interior from which these particular signs can be refused. The epistemology installed here is the recital's own, consistent from its first movement: where evidence is clear, rejection is never evidentiary. Verse 75 charged distortion *after understanding*; verse 95 traced the unspoken wish to the hands' record; verse 97 exposed the angel-objection as a costume for lineage-grievance. Verse 99 gathers the pattern into a law: the āyāt are bayyināt, so denial is not a verdict about them but a disclosure about the denier. The clause's future tense — *none will deny them* — extends the law past its occasion: this is not a report about one delegation's refusal but a standing description of every refusal to come. And its logic carries a comfort as exact as its severity: if only the rebellious deny clear signs, then the persistence of denial proves nothing against the signs' clarity, and the believer's confidence in the revelation never has to borrow strength from its acceptance. The verse separates the message's quality from its reception completely — the first fixed by its Sender, the second diagnostic of its audience — and having separated them, assigns each its owner.

**Fisq: The Word for Exiting**

The class-name the clause uses is *fāsiq* — the rebellious one, the transgressor — and the word's root is worth its weight here. *F-s-q* carries the sense of exiting, of coming out from an enclosure; classical usage preserves the image in the mouse leaving its hole, and the Qur'an's moral vocabulary builds on exactly that image: the fāsiq is one who has come out from obedience, stepped outside the enclosure of the command. The choice of word locates the denial precisely. It is not error — the mistaken remain inside, looking; not ignorance — the ignorant have not been shown; not doubt — the doubting stand at the boundary. It is exit: a departure performed by the will after the signs have done their clearing work. The recital has watched the exit in stages through this passage: the covenant cast aside (a departure from the bond), the Book thrown behind the back (a departure from the inheritance), the messenger disliked for his message (a departure from the delivery), the angel befriended selectively (a departure disguised as loyalty). Each stage is fisq in the root's literal force — the same body stepping further outside — and the verse names the accumulated motion at last. Its diagnostic value for the reader is the mirror of its indictment: denials of clear revelation are not intellectual events to be argued with but departures to be recognized, and recognizing the category changes the response the recital counsels, from persuasion to witness — the signs remain sent down, clear, and the exit remains the exiters'.

**Clarity Does Not Compel**

The verse's two halves hold a tension the recital never resolves, because the tension is the truth it teaches: the signs are maximally clear and the denial is fully real. Clarity, in the Qur'an's epistemology, is not coercion. The bayyināt do their work on the eye and the mind; the will keeps its governance over what the person does with the seeing. This is the same structure verse 94's challenge exposed — a claim tested not by argument but by desire — and the same structure the hardening-verses filed: signs accumulate, responses divide, and the division happens in the organ that signs inform but do not commande. The verse's restriction clause is therefore not a limitation on the signs but a statement about their audience: given clarity this complete, the remaining variable is the receiver's posture, and the only posture that produces denial of the clear is the exited one. The pastoral consequence for the believing reader runs in two directions. Toward the denier: the verse forbids despairing of the signs' power when denial persists — the persistence is fisq's fingerprint, not the message's defect — and it equally forbids flattering the denier by treating his exit as a respectable alternative reading of clear evidence. Toward the believer: the verse makes reception a matter of staying put. The enclosure the fāsiq exits is where the bayyināt land, and faithfulness at this station is largely the refusal to step outside when a sign costs something — the discipline the challenge-verses tested by desire, restated as position rather than wish.

**The Consolation Addressed to the Messenger**

Read in its place, the verse is also pastoral speech to the Prophet ﷺ at the close of a long exchange of refusals. The angel-objection has just been answered; the challenges have been issued and their silence predicted; and this verse arrives to settle the messenger's own relation to the denial he lives inside daily. The settlement is in the sentence's two clauses: what you carry is clear — the defect your opponents allege in your message is a defect the Sender's own description excludes; and those who deny it are the exited — the refusal you absorb is testimony about the refusers, filed by Allah in their record rather than yours. The sūrah's consolations run in this register throughout: do not grieve yourself to death over denial (18:6); Allah guides and leaves astray by His knowledge, so do not consume yourself over them (35:8). Verse 99 adds the epistemic form of the same comfort: the clarity is a divine attribute of the message, not a human achievement of the messenger, and therefore the messenger's standing does not rise or fall with the audience's response. For every later carrier of this recitation the verse installs the same posture — the evidence's quality held as received fact, the denial's meaning read as diagnosis, and the self-location fixed inside the enclosure the rebellious exited: sent down to you, clear, and denied by none who stayed.
