## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:138

> They say, “These cattle and crops are reserved—none may eat them except those we permit,” so they claim. Some other cattle are exempted from labour and others are not slaughtered in Allah’s Name—falsely attributing lies to Him. He will repay them for their lies.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rules About the Herds and the Crops**

They say, These cattle and crops are reserved, none may eat them except those we permit, so they claim. The verse continues the sūrah's examination of the audience's dietary system, and it records a rule the community had adopted about its own property. Certain animals and certain produce were set aside, and access to them was restricted to the people the community designated, which meant that the poor and the outsiders were excluded from what the system had reserved.

The phrase so they claim again marks the rule as an invention, and the Book's treatment of the practice follows the pattern established in the preceding verse. The audience attributed the restriction to a divine command, and the sūrah states that the attribution was their own.

**The Cattle Exempted From Labour**

Some other cattle are exempted from labour and others are not slaughtered in Allah's Name. The sentence names two further features of the system, and the commentators explained them as rules about which animals might be used for work and which might be killed without the invocation. The exemption from labour placed certain animals in a category of their own, and the omission of the Name placed others in a category that the sūrah's earlier verses had forbidden.

The two rules together show how far the community's practice had moved from the principle the sūrah stated when it commanded the believers to eat of what has been slaughtered in the Name of God (6:118–119). The system the audience had inherited produced both an excess of restriction and a licence to eat what had not been consecrated.

**The Charge of Attribution**

Falsely attributing lies to Him. The sentence states the offence that underlies the whole system, and the Book uses the same charge throughout the chapter. The sūrah has said that the idolaters invent lies against God (6:21), that those who fabricate revelation are the greatest wrongdoers (6:93), and that the audience attributes to God sons and daughters out of ignorance (6:100). The practices described here are the practical form of the attribution: what was forbidden by custom was presented as forbidden by God.

The Book's objection is not to the existence of rules but to the claim of divine authority made on behalf of rules that God had not given. The sūrah has already established the standard for such claims when it stated that God has explained what He has forbidden (6:119), and the verse in front of us measures the audience's system against that standard and finds it wanting.

**The Promise of Repayment**

He will repay them for their lies. The verse closes with the statement of the consequence, and the Book's language of repayment describes the correspondence between conduct and outcome. The audience had constructed a system of permissions and prohibitions and had placed the authority for them with God, and the sentence states that the false attribution will be answered.

The sūrah uses the same closing formula in the verse that follows, where the audience's rule about the offspring of the herds is answered with the same promise of repayment (6:139). The two verses form a pair, and the repetition of the formula binds the passage together as a single indictment of the community's dietary rules.

**What the System Revealed About the Community**

The rules described in these verses show a community in which the management of food had become a way of maintaining status. The animals reserved for the community's own people, the produce withheld from general use, and the categories created by custom all served to distinguish those inside the system from those outside it, and the Book's criticism reaches the social arrangement as well as the theology behind it.

The sūrah's treatment of the meals of the community elsewhere makes the same point from the other side. The sūrah of the Cow instructs the believers to eat of the good things provided and to give thanks (2:172), and the sūrah of the Bee commands the same and forbids the practices that restrict what God has given. The verses in front of us state what those commands were replacing.

**The Place of the Verse in the Section**

The verse stands between the description of the shares assigned to God and to the idols (6:136) and the rules about the offspring of the herds (6:139), and it deals with the same system from the standpoint of the community's own table. The sūrah has been examining the audience's religion piece by piece since it turned from the argument about the signs, and this passage examines the part of it that governed daily life.

The treatment is not incidental to the chapter's purpose. The sūrah's claim throughout has been that the authority to permit and forbid belongs to God, and the passage that begins with these verses shows what happens when that authority is claimed by custom instead.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse records the rule the audience applied to the offspring of their herds, reserving it for the males and forbidding it to the females, with the qualification that a stillborn animal could be shared by all (6:139). The detail completes the survey of the system, and the verse closes the section with the statement that God will repay the audience for their falsehood and with the declaration that He is All-Wise, All-Knowing.

The passage that follows moves to the audience's practice of killing their children and forbidding what God has provided, and it states that those who do so have strayed (6:140). The sūrah's survey of the inherited system is therefore complete once these verses are read together, and the reader is left with the account of a religion that had replaced the law of God with the rulings of custom.
