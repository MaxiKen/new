## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:9

> **If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth, they will certainly say, “The Almighty, All-Knowing did.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Admission Already in Their Mouths**

The verse states a fact about the Quraysh that the Quraysh would not have denied. Asked who made the heavens and the earth, they would answer that God did. The Makkans were not atheists. They believed in Allāh as the creator and the high God, they swore by Him, they called on Him in danger at sea, and they called the Kaʿbah His house. Their error was not in denying the Creator but in placing others alongside Him, as intercessors and lesser lords, and in denying that He would raise the dead. The verse exposes the gap between what they admitted and how they lived. The same admission is put in their mouths in 29:61 and 29:63, 31:25, 39:38, and again at the end of this sūrah in verse 87. The Qurʾān keeps returning to it because it is the strongest lever available. A people who concede that one God made everything have already conceded the premise from which everything else follows; the argument with them is not about whether God exists but about whether they will let the admission govern their worship.

**Answering in God's Own Names**

The striking detail is the form of their answer. The verse does not have them say "Allāh" but "the Almighty, the All-Knowing", al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm. Two readings of this are possible. One is that the Quraysh literally used these names, which is plausible, since al-ʿAzīz was known to them as a divine title. The other, favoured by several exegetes, is that God reports their answer in His own words, so that the meaning of their admission is stated more fully than they would state it. On the second reading the verse does something subtle: it takes the bare concession, "God made it", and spells out what the concession contains. The maker of the heavens and the earth must be ʿazīz, mighty beyond resistance, or He could not have made them; and He must be ʿalīm, knowing in every detail, or He could not have ordered them. Those two attributes are then the answer to the two things the Quraysh denied. A God who is mighty can raise the dead; a God who is knowing can know who deserves what. The names are chosen to turn the admission against the admitters.

**The Contradiction Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī Identify**

Ibn Kathīr draws out the inconsistency. The idolaters acknowledged that one God alone was responsible for creation, and then worshipped other beings alongside Him. Al-Rāzī sharpens the point in a different direction: the same people who granted that God made the heavens denied that He could bring the dead back to life, though raising a body is a smaller thing than making the sky. Both exegetes are describing a failure to follow through. The Quraysh held a true premise and refused its conclusions, and the verse's function is to state the premise loudly enough that the refusal becomes visible. This is the pattern of argument in the whole passage that follows. Verses 10 through 14 will list what God did with the earth, the rain, the pairs of creatures, the ships and the animals, and each item is an instance of the creative power the Quraysh had just admitted. The list is not addressed to people who doubt that God made the world. It is addressed to people who admit it and then behave as if they had not.

**A Second Reading: The Prophets Speaking**

Al-Rāzī records another way of taking the pronouns. On this reading, "them" and "they" refer not to the idolaters but to the prophets mentioned in verses 6 and 7. Ask the prophets who created the heavens and the earth, and every one of them will say: the Mighty, the Knowing. The verse would then be affirming that all the messengers taught the same doctrine of creation, and verses 10 through 14, which describe God's works in the first and third person, could be read as a continuation of that shared prophetic testimony. The reading has something in its favour. The nearest plural antecedent is the prophets of verse 7, and the content of the answer, with its precise divine names, sounds more like a prophet's confession than an idolater's. But the majority reading is stronger for two reasons. The parallel passages in 29:61, 31:25, 39:38, and 43:87 all put this answer in the mouths of the idolaters, and the rhetorical force of the verse depends on the admission coming from opponents, not allies. An argument from what your enemies concede is stronger than an argument from what your friends affirm.

**Fitrah: The Knowledge Beneath the Idolatry**

The verse assumes something about human beings that the Qurʾān states elsewhere as a principle. Beneath the idols, beneath the inherited customs, the Quraysh knew that there was one Maker. They did not need to be argued into it; they needed only to be asked. This is what the Qurʾān calls the fiṭrah, the original constitution on which God made mankind (30:30), and it is the reason the Makkan sūrahs so often proceed by question rather than proof. The truth is already present; the question brings it to the surface. The verse also explains the tone of the sūrah's argument against idolatry, which is less a demonstration than a reminder. The Quraysh are not being taught that God exists. They are being asked to notice that they already believe it, and to see what the belief costs them if they hold it honestly. Everything they said about the angels, the daughters of God, and the intercession of idols would have to be measured against the one sentence they could not avoid saying: the Mighty, the Knowing made the heavens and the earth. The verses that follow begin that measurement.
