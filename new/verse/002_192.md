## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:192

> But if they cease, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Verse With the Largest Reach**

This verse is a single clause — and if they cease, then Allah is Forgiving, Merciful — and it carries the entire exit of the war-law the two preceding verses built. The cessation it names is the enemy's: the fighters who waged the war, who expelled the believers, who persecuted them in their faith, who perhaps fought at the Sacred Mosque itself — if they cease, the verse says nothing about their past except that God is Forgiving and Merciful. The two names are the same pair that closed the bequest-legislation (2:182) and stand throughout the Qur'an as the comprehensive formula of pardon, and their deployment here is the war-law's deepest legislation: the permission to fight expires at the cessation, and what replaces it is not punishment, not retribution for the thirteen years, not even a demand for accounting — but the divine names of forgiveness and mercy, extended over the enemies' whole record. The commentators read the clause's grammar carefully: the answer to *if they cease* is God's forgiveness and mercy, meaning the cessation itself is met, on God's side, with pardon — and on the believers' side, as the next verse makes explicit, with the end of enmity.

**Ceasing as the Turning**

The word for cease — *intahaw* — is the same root the sūrah used for the prohibition's obeying: *and if they desist, then cease* patterns the Qur'an's conditional peace-legislation throughout. The cessation the verse accepts is the cessation of the fighting and, in the fuller statement of the next verse, of the persecution — the fitnah that was named as greater than killing. The tradition asked what suffices for the desisting: the mere laying down of arms, or the abandonment of the persecution's object as well; and the two verses read together supply the answer — the war exists against fitnah (2:191), so its end is the end of fitnah, and the enemy who ceases the aggression has removed the permission's entire ground. The verse's brevity is itself the lesson: no conditions are enumerated, no reparations demanded, no probation imposed. The enemy ceases; God is Forgiving, Merciful. Every elaboration the jurists would later legislate — treaties, dhimma arrangements, prisoner ransom, safe conduct — operates under this clause's atmosphere, the atmosphere of a war whose ending requires nothing of the vanquished but the stopping of the wrong.

**The Forgiveness Offered to the Persecutors**

The names at the verse's end do specific work against the specific enemy addressed. These were not neutral belligerents: they had tortured the early believers, boycotted their clan, driven them from their homes, killed some and apostatized others, barred them from the Sacred Mosque, and fought them at it. The verse that offers them God's forgiveness when they cease is offering, in effect, the pardon of everything the war-legislation just described — and it is the same forgiveness the sūrah extended to the concealers who repent (2:160), to the eaters of usury who desist (2:275, in the legislation ahead), to the wrongdoers who return. The Qur'an's economy of pardon is uniform: the sin is named at its full weight, and the door is left open at its full width, and the two statements never cancel each other. The fighters of 2:190–191 are addressed at full weight — the killing permitted against them, the expulsion answered, the fitnah ranked above murder — and the door is then opened by two divine names, so that the passage's last word about the enemy, before the war's object is stated, is not the sword but the mercy. A war-law that ends its permission in forgiveness is legislating the war as a means whose end is peace with the enemy himself, not merely peace from him.

**The Mercy That Precedes the Battle**

The verse also retroactively conditions the two before it. If the cessation is met with Forgiving and Merciful, then the fighting permitted before the cessation was never directed at the enemy's destruction as such — it was directed at the fitnah, and the enemy's person was always, in principle, a candidate for pardon. The mercy is not the war's aftermath but its premise: the permission to fight exists inside a scripture that has already decided, in its names for God, how it wants the fighting to end. This is why the tradition could legislate from these verses the whole conduct-code of restraint — the non-combatants spared, the sanctuary held, the excess forbidden (2:190), the asylum granted and escorted (9:6) — each rule an application of the same premise: the enemy is fought while he fights and received when he stops, and the receiving is divine before it is communal. The verse's placement between the hardest permission (kill them wherever you find them) and the war's final object (fight until there is no fitnah) is thus architectural: the mercy-clause stands at the hinge, so that the reader carries the forgiveness into the object-statement and the object-statement back into the permission — the war is fought to end persecution, and it ends, for the persecutor who ceases, in the names of pardon.

**The Community's Imitation of the Names**

The verse addresses God's disposition, but its legislation binds the community in the same direction. The next verse will make the binding explicit — if they cease, then no enmity except against the wrongdoers — and the whole treaty-context of the passage's descent, at Hudaybiyyah, is a lived instance: a community that had been barred from its sanctuary settled for a truce whose terms looked, to some of the Companions, like humiliation, and accepted them as the Prophet accepted them. The names at this verse's end supply the ethic behind that acceptance: a community whose Lord meets the ceasing enemy with forgiveness and mercy is a community forbidden from meeting him with remembered injury. The sūrah will legislate the same imitation elsewhere — forgive and overlook, until God brings His command (2:109); pardon the People of the Scripture's provocations and bear with them (5:13); repel evil with what is better (41:34) — and the war-passages are the hardest case the imitation is asked to carry. The verse therefore does double duty: it states the divine response to cessation, and it trains the believers' response to be its image — the enmity ending exactly where the aggression ends, the pardon offered because the Pardoner has already offered His, and the two names standing over the battlefield as the war's real conclusion.
