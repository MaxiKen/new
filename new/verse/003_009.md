## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:9

> Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer Whose Second Half Is a Statement of Fact**

The verse continues the words of the well-grounded, and it changes register halfway through. The first clause addresses God directly — *Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity* — and the last clause stops addressing Him and speaks about Him: *Surely Allah does not break His promise.* The Arabic shifts from the second person to the third, a move the Qur'an uses often to turn a private prayer into a public declaration.

The effect is that the supplicant is not bargaining. He is confirming out loud what he already knows, and in confirming it he commits himself to live by it. The gathering of all humanity is not the object of a wish; it is a fact the prayer is built on, and the one praying states it as a fact before he asks for anything.

**Mīʿād: the Appointed Meeting**

The word translated "promised Day" is *mīʿād*, a noun for an appointment, a place or time fixed in advance when two parties meet. It is related to *mawʿid* with the same sense, and both come from the root that gives *waʿd*, a promise, and *waʿīd*, a threat.

The Qur'an uses the family of words to say something specific about the Last Day. It is not an event that happens; it is an event that is scheduled. *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘A Day has ˹already˺ been appointed for you, which you can neither delay nor advance by a ˹single˺ moment.’”* (34:30). The Qur'an attaches the same language to the good news for the righteous: *“But those mindful of their Lord will have ˹elevated˺ mansions, built one above the other, under which rivers flow. ˹That is˺ the promise of Allah. ˹And˺ Allah never fails in ˹His˺ promise.”* (39:20). Threat and promise share one vocabulary, which is how the Book trains a reader to treat both as appointments rather than moods.

**"All Humanity" — No One Is Exempt**

The gathering is universal, and the universality is stated twice, here and in the parallel verses. *“Allah, there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He will certainly gather ˹all of˺ you together on the Day of Judgment—about which there is no doubt. And whose word is more truthful than Allah’s?”* (4:87). The Book of the Covenant puts the same claim in God's own voice: *“He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. He will certainly gather ˹all of˺ you together for the Day of Judgment— about which there is no doubt.”* (6:12).

Universal language does what a partial gathering cannot: it removes every category a person might shelter in. Nobody's lineage, and nobody's book, and nobody's record of religious observance survives the sentence, because the gathering is of *humanity*, not of a denomination or a nation. The sūrah has already listed the descents of the Torah and the Gospel; it will go on to argue with the communities that held them. This clause is the frame in which all of that argument happens.

**"About Which There Is No Doubt" — Certainty With a Warning in It**

The phrase attached to the Day recurs in verse after verse in the Book, always in the same form. It is stated in the context of resurrection: *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves.”* (22:7). It is stated to those who think the question can be postponed: *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ gives you life, then causes you to die, then will gather you ˹all˺ on the Day of Judgment, about which there is no doubt. But most people do not know.”* (45:26).

The repetition of the formula is not decoration. A doubt-free statement that people nonetheless doubt puts the problem where the Book always puts it: not in the evidence, but in the will that prefers not to consider the evidence. The phrase "about which there is no doubt" is therefore a warning addressed to the one who is doubting, not a description of the doctrine's universal reception.

**The Confidence That God Keeps His Word**

The prayer ends with the grounds of its own confidence: *Surely Allah does not break His promise.* God's truthfulness is not a trait fastened on to His power; it is what makes His promises worth building a life on. The Book says it of protection as well as of threat — *“So do not think ˹O Prophet˺ that Allah will fail to keep His promise to His messengers. Allah is indeed Almighty, capable of punishment.”* (14:47) — and 13:31 closes with the same assurance after describing the disasters that will pursue the deniers.

The well-grounded state it once more in their longer prayer later in this sūrah, where the appeal is to the promise itself: *“Our Lord! Grant us what You have promised us through Your messengers and do not put us to shame on Judgment Day—for certainly You never fail in Your promise.”* (3:194). Faith in this Book is not credulity about God's generosity. It is confidence in a speaker who has never had to be taken back, and the prayer of verse 9 is what that confidence sounds like when a believer says it out loud.

Notice what the confidence does to the fear of the gathering. A person who expected no accounting might forget it; a person who expected an arbitrary one would dread it. The prayer names the Day and then names the reliability of the one who fixed it, and the two statements together make the Day a thing to prepare for rather than a thing to bargain with. The companions of the Prophet said the same in their own words at 3:194, asking that the promise be granted and that they not be disgraced.

The claim is also the answer to a doubt that runs through the sūrah. People who deny the message are shown in the following verses trusting in what they have gathered. The well-grounded trust in a promise that cannot fail, and the verse above is their reasoning, in full, in two sentences.
