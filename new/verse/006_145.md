## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:145

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I do not find in what has been revealed to me anything forbidden to eat except carrion, running blood, swine—which is impure—or a sinful offering in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—then surely your Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Declaration of What Is Forbidden**

Say, I do not find in what has been revealed to me anything forbidden to eat except carrion, running blood, swine, which is impure, or a sinful offering in the name of any other than Allah. The verse gives the Prophet the words that answer the audience's dietary system, and the answer is drawn from the revelation rather than from inherited custom. The four categories named here are the prohibitions the Book states in its other dietary passages as well: the sūrah of the Cow lists carrion, blood, swine, and what has been slaughtered in the name of other than God, and it adds the exception for the person compelled (2:173); the sūrah of the Table gives the same list; and the sūrah of the Bee repeats it with the same qualification (16:115).

The familiar phrase I do not find in what has been revealed to me places the whole question within the revelation. What the Prophet is instructed to report is what has been sent down, and the audience's rules have no place among its contents.

**The Four Categories**

The verse names the prohibited things in an order that moves from the animal that died without slaughter, to the blood that pours from a slaughtered animal, to the swine, and finally to the animal offered to a being other than God. The Book's word for the third item describes it as impure, and the fourth is described as an offering made in disobedience. The sūrah has already forbidden the eating of what has not been slaughtered in the Name of God (6:121), and the list in front of us states the same principle in its full form.

The categories show what the Book's dietary law actually contains. It is not a system of inherited taboos about particular animals, and it does not rest on distinctions between the males and the females or on the condition of the unborn. It rests on the condition of the animal at the time of its death and on the name invoked over it, which is the principle the sūrah stated when it commanded the believers to eat of what has been slaughtered in the Name of God (6:118).

**The Exception for Necessity**

But if someone is compelled by necessity, neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need, then surely your Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The exception states the two conditions that qualify it: the person must be driven to eat by necessity rather than by appetite, and he must not take more than the immediate need requires. The Book's other dietary passages state the same exception more briefly, and the version in front of us adds the description of the person who may use it.

The Book's treatment of necessity in its legal passages shows the purpose the exception serves. The rules are not intended to destroy the person who is forced to break them, and the qualification keeps the permission from becoming a general licence. The names that close the sentence, All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, state the ground on which the exception rests.

**What the Verse Answers in the Sūrah's Argument**

The verse answers the audience's system with a list rather than with a rebuttal, and the list is short. The sūrah has spent several verses describing the shares assigned to the idols, the reservation of the herds, the killing of children, and the rules about the offspring of the animals, and the declaration in front of us states the four prohibitions that God has actually given. The difference in length is part of the answer.

The Book's treatment of the earlier communities' laws makes the same point from the other side. The verse that follows this one describes the restrictions placed on the Jewish communities and the reasons for them (6:146), and the sūrah of the Women states that God forbade the Jews certain foods that had been lawful for them because of their wrongdoing and their hindering others from the way of God (4:160). The community addressed in this sūrah was receiving a law that was lighter than what had preceded it, and the declaration in front of us states its substance.

**What the Verse Says About the Revelation's Authority**

The sentence's reference to what has been revealed to the Prophet is the ground on which the whole dietary question is settled. The audience had appealed to the customs of their ancestors, and the Book answers with the content of the message. The sūrah has stated that God has explained what He has forbidden (6:119), and the verse in front of us states what that explanation contains.

The Book's use of the formula in other contexts shows that it is not a denial of the revelation's completeness but a statement of where the rulings are to be found. The sūrah of the Cattle itself will later say that the Book is fully explained and that nothing has been omitted from it (6:38), and the declaration in front of us belongs to that account of a revelation that states what it requires.

**The Place of the Verse in the Section**

The verse stands between the challenge to the audience's dietary system (6:143–144) and the account of the restrictions imposed on the Jewish communities (6:146), and it supplies the answer that the challenge required. The sūrah's examination of food is complete once the positive statement of the law has been given, and the verses that follow show how the community's law relates to the laws given before it.

The passage that continues from here returns to the theme of the audience's arguments, with the polytheists claiming that God's will was behind their practices and the Prophet told to answer that God has the conclusive argument (6:148–149). The verse in front of us therefore closes one movement of the sūrah and prepares the reader for the next, and it does so by stating the law in its simplest form.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse describes the restrictions that were imposed on the Jewish communities, naming the animals with undivided hoofs and the fat of oxen and sheep with the exceptions attached to it, and it states that the restriction was a recompense for their violations (6:146). The contrast with the four brief prohibitions of the previous verse is the point of the passage, and the sūrah will go on to state that the community addressed in the Qur'an was receiving a lighter burden.

The Book's treatment of the earlier laws appears in several of its passages, and the sūrah of the Family of Imran states that all food was lawful for the children of Israel except what Israel made unlawful for himself before the Torah was revealed (3:93). The verse in front of us supplies the ruling for the community that received the Qur'an, and the passage that follows explains why the ruling differs from what the earlier communities were given.
