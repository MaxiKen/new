## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:13

> And when they are told, “Believe as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith Given a Public Shape**

The command in this verse names a standard rather than a content. The hypocrites are told to believe as the people believe, and the commentators took *al-nās*, the people, to mean the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ, whose faith had a visible and shared form. The Qur'an does not treat belief as an inner state that each person is free to compose for himself; it treats it as a transmitted practice with a public shape.

That understanding fits the Book's habit of addressing faith in the plural. The prayer of the believers speaks in the first person plural, the community is addressed as one body, and the revelation is described as a guide for a people rather than for isolated individuals. The command in this verse is therefore not conformity for its own sake. It is an instruction to enter a practice that already exists, since the alternative on offer is not a private faith but no faith at all.

**The Insult and Its Return**

The reply uses one word: *sufahāʾ*, rendered here as fools. The Arabic noun describes the person whose judgement is unsound, whose understanding of consequences is light. The hypocrite uses it as a status weapon, implying that the believers' acceptance of a man of their own city was naive. The verse then takes the word and reassigns it: *it is they who are fools*.

The same term is used twice more in the sūrah, each time by the Qur'an for the people who sneered at the believers. At 2:142 the Book anticipates the objection to the change of prayer direction by saying that *the foolish among the people* will ask about it. At 2:130 the criterion is stated outright: *“And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool!”* (2:130) In every case the word is applied to a refusal, not to an intellectual limit.

**Foolishness as a Moral Word**

In the Qur'an's usage, a fool is not stupidity's technical name. He is a person who has misvalued something: who trades an enduring good for a passing advantage, or smothers what he already knows to be true. That is why the Book can apply the word to the learned and the powerful as easily as to the simple, and why the people mocked in Mecca and Madinah were mostly without rank, whose faith had cost them a great deal.

The criterion behind the word is a practical one, as 2:130 implies: the fool is the one who rejects a way he has already heard about. Knowledge, in this Book, is measured by what a person does with what has reached him, and honesty about the limits of one's own position is part of it. The reply in this verse is that rare thing, a self-description by the person the Qur'an has already described, and the two accounts agree less than the speaker thinks.

**Mockery as a Form of Status**

The reply is aimed at an audience, not at the person giving the command. Calling a group foolish is a way of claiming the superior position in a conversation, and in a city where status had recently been redistributed, the claim carried material weight. The Companions had lost the standing they once held in the Quraysh's hierarchy, and attachment to them brought no rank.

The Qur'an answers the insult with an inversion rather than a defence. It does not argue that the believers were intelligent, and it does not list their merits; it assigns the word they used to the people who used it. The sūrah's method is consistent throughout its portrait of this group: their own vocabulary is taken from them and returned in the correct arrangement.

**The Three Endings and Their Precise Verbs**

Three consecutive portraits close with the same kind of comment: the deceivers *fail to perceive it* (2:9), the corruptors *fail to perceive it* (2:12), and the fools *do not know* (2:13). The Book uses its verbs of cognition carefully. Perception concerns noticing what is in front of a person; knowledge concerns the settled understanding of it. The first failure is a problem of attention, and the second is a problem of what attention has been used to build.

The three endings also track a decline. The claim made in 2:8 was about what the speakers believed; the deception in 2:9 was an attempt to manage the community; the charge in 2:13 is a public insult. As the conduct becomes more visible, the warnings move from a private deficiency to a public one, and the sentence that names it becomes correspondingly direct.

**When the Accusation Cannot Be Refrained**

The verse does not allow the readers of the sūrah to borrow its verdict. The word *fool* belongs to God here, and the Book's general rule about the terms refuses the community's own use of them: it never authorises its followers to dispatch their opponents' characters, and it repeatedly commands the believers to answer an insult with something better. What the verse records is a divine description of a state, not a licence for name-calling.

The distinction matters for how a person reads the sūrah. The portrait from 2:8 to 2:13 was assembled to be recognisable, and any reader may find in it something of himself. The Book's verdicts are stated so that the reader will submit to the description before he applies it, and the verse's closing clause, that they do not know, is the reason no outsider can settle the question from the outside.
