## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:13

> And when they are told, “Believe as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Contempt Offered Instead of an Argument**

The invitation they are given is mild — *Believe as others believe* (2:13) — and the reply does not engage it. Nothing is said about God, revelation, or the Hereafter. What they answer is a question about the company: shall we become like *the fools*? The claim is not refuted; the claimants are.

The manoeuvre is recognisable, and its advantage is that it costs nothing. A person asked to accept a truth can always refuse on the ground that the people holding it are unimpressive, and there is no arguing with taste. Contempt is what replaces reasoning when reasoning would be expensive, which is why the Quran treats it as evidence rather than as a position.

Notice also what the reply concedes. *Shall we believe as the fools believe?* does not deny that there is something to believe; it does not call the message false or the messenger a liar. It objects to the company. The unbelief, on the evidence of this sentence, is not intellectual at all — it is social, and the creed is being declined for the same reason an invitation to dinner would be.

**Who the Fools Were Meant to Be**

In the first instance the insult was aimed at the Companions — the people who had believed. But the verse does not name them, and the indefinite phrasing is what has let the commentaries read it generally: the attitude on display is the permanent one of the person who declines an invitation because he will not take it from these people.

One commentarial note deserves to be kept, because it sharpens the insult and explains why it stings. Some said the speakers did not actually believe the believers were foolish; they knew perfectly well that the believers were people of intelligence, and called them fools to advertise their own superiority. On that reading the contempt is not even a mistake about the world — it is a social manoeuvre performed at the expense of the truth.

**The Reversal**

The answer is exact and uses their own word: *Indeed, it is they who are fools* (2:13). The Quran's vocabulary of folly is not about intelligence. The root denotes lightness — a mind that has not weighed what it is handling, and so misjudges value. The fool in this scheme is the person who trades something permanent for something immediate, and the Quran's most withering description of the type puts it in terms of capacity rather than error: *They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with. They are like cattle. In fact, they are even less guided* (7:179).

That is the standard being applied here. The believers are called fools for valuing the Hereafter; the people calling them fools are ruining the only thing that will outlast them. Whoever is misjudging value is the fool, and the insult returns to its sender with the definition attached.

**The Third Repetition of the Diagnosis**

*But they do not know* (2:13) appears for the third time in five verses, and the accumulation is the argument. These people are wrong about what they are doing to the world (2:12), wrong about what they are doing to themselves (2:9), and wrong about who is worth imitating — and in none of the three cases do they know it.

The repetition also removes the last defence. A person who is mistaken once can be corrected by information; a person whose faculty for correction has stopped cannot be. The sūrah is not insulting them when it says they do not know. It is describing the reason the argument has not landed and will not land on its own.

**Why the Company Matters More Than the Claim**

There is a reason the invitation was phrased as it was. *Believe as others believe* asks a person to join a community, and the refusal is almost always about the community rather than the creed. Few people reject a religion because they have weighed its arguments; most reject it because they have weighed its adherents and found them unimpressive.

The Quran's counter is not to defend the believers' brilliance but to relocate the question. The measure of a claim is not the standing of the people who hold it, and the measure of a person is not his estimate of other people. Both are the same error, which is why the verse answers an insult about company with a judgement about value.

**The Recurrence of the Pattern**

The Quran records this reply wherever a messenger is sent, and it notes that the objection is manufactured rather than discovered: *And so We have made for every prophet enemies—devilish humans and jinn—whispering to one another with elegant words of deception* (6:112). The elegance is the point. Insults delivered in polished language pass as judgements.

What the verse asks of a reader is to check whether he has ever declined something because of who else believed it. The answer, for most people, is yes — and that is precisely the mechanism the sūrah is describing.

It is worth adding that the sūrah does not respond by flattering the believers. It nowhere claims that the community is impressive, and the Quran is blunt about its faults elsewhere. The argument is simply that the worth of a claim does not rise or fall with the standing of its holders. A person who rejects the truth because of the company it keeps has made the weakest possible objection and, on this verse's account, has chosen his own company.
