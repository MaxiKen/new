## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:13

> And when they are told, “Believe as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Summons, A Second Renaming**

The pattern established in (2:11-12) recurs with a new content. There the hypocrites were told, “Do not corrupt,” and they renamed their corruption as reform. Here they are told, *“Believe as the people have believed”* (2:13), and they rename the believers as fools: *“Shall we believe as the fools believe?”* The parallelism is deliberate. The sūrah is showing that hypocrisy does not produce a single error but a habit of renaming: whatever is commanded is relabeled so that refusal feels like discernment. *Amana al-nās* — “the people have believed” — is a striking collective noun. It does not say “believe as the pious believe” or “as the learned believe”; it says “as *the people* believe,” appealing to the consensus of a community that is visible and whose belief has already been described (2:3-4). The hypocrite’s reply does not engage that description; it dismisses the describable community with a single epithet — *sufahāʾ*.

**Who Are “The People” They Are Asked to Imitate?**

The source notes that by *al-sufahāʾ* the speakers mean the Companions of the Prophet, but the more universal reading, parallel to the generalization permitted for the corruption verses, extends the type. *Al-nās* in *“believe as the people believe”* gathers whoever has exhibited the five marks of the mindful: unseen conviction, standing prayer, released provision, assent to descent, certainty about return (2:3-4) — and the standing that results (2:5). To be told to believe *as* they believe is not to be told to copy manners; it is to be told to enter the same structure. The hypocrite hears the invitation as a demand to join a crowd he has already judged. His question — *a-nuʾminu kamā āmana al-sufahāʾ* — uses the interrogative *a-* to convey incredulity, as if the proposal refutes itself: shall *we* — the reasonable, the connected, the mediating — believe as *they* do? The *we/they* split is the social form of the disease diagnosed as being in the heart (2:10).

**Foolishness as a Qur'anic Category**

*Safah* — folly — in Qur'anic usage is not low intelligence but misplacement of value: to put what is weighty in the place of what is light and what is light in the place of what is weighty. The Qur'an calls squandering folly, entrusting property to the unfit folly, and refusal to follow the way of Abraham folly. That is why the commentators cited in the source observe that the ignorant among the hypocrites *knew* the believers were not fools in intellect, yet called them so to assert superiority. The name does not describe the believers; it performs the hypocrite’s self-elevation. To call the mindful fools is to claim that prudence lies in hedging, that intelligence is the art of keeping both doors open (cf. 2:14), and that the ones who commit to one door — who say *“You alone we worship”* (1:5) and spend *from* what they were given — have been duped. The Qur'an’s definition of *safah* judges that prudence as the true miscalculation.

**“Indeed, It Is They Who Are the Fools” — The Same Emphatic Reversal**

As in the previous repudiation, the divine reply mirrors the restrictive syntax: *alā innahum hum al-sufahāʾ* — “No! Indeed, it is *they* who are the fools.” The *alā* of alert, the doubled *hum*, the definite *al-sufahāʾ* all recur from (2:12). The symmetry is not decoration; it is jurisprudence. The hypocrite’s attempt to fix a name on others is returned to himself with the same grammatical force, so that the name he dispensed becomes the name he bears. That the two verses — corruptors (2:12) and fools (2:13) — share the same form suggests they describe one reality from two angles: socially, the hypocrite corrupts; intellectually, he is foolish. The corruption is not clever; the folly is not harmless. Both are predicated of the same agent, and both are denied to his self-description.

**“But They Do Not Know” — A Failure of Knowledge, Not Sensation**

The previous repudiation closed with *lā yashʿurūn* — they fail to perceive (2:12); this one closes with *lā yaʿlamūn* — they do not know. The source’s distinction is sustained by the Arabic lexicon. *Shuʿūr* is immediate feeling; *ʿilm* is reflective, articulated knowing. To fail to perceive corruption is to miss the first prick; to fail to know one’s own folly is to miss the account that would have explained the prick. The hypocrite’s disease (2:10) has now dulled both registers. He does not feel that calling reform what is corruption is off, and he does not know that calling believers fools is a misvaluation that inverts folly and prudence. That is why the sūrah alternates the two closures: the hypocrite is described as imperceptive in what he does and ignorant in what he judges, so that neither his hand nor his mind remains a reliable witness to himself.

**Why the Foolishness Is Not Obvious to the Fool**

If the hypocrites know, at some level, that the believers are not unintelligent, why does the verse say they *do not know* they are the fools? Because *safah* is not an IQ score; it is a ranking error, and ranking errors are invisible to those who have adopted the wrong scale. The hypocrite measures intelligence by the ability to keep options open, to be counted among the believers when believers prosper (2:5) and among the rejectors when rejectors seem ascendant. The believer measures intelligence by the ability to be upon guidance (*ʿalā hudā*, 2:5) even when guidance costs. Each scale makes the other look foolish. The divine verdict — *it is they who are the fools* — does not resolve the dispute by appeal to a third, neutral scale; it declares the believer’s scale to be the true one, because it measures by provision (*mimmā razaqnāhum*, 2:3) and by return (*bi-l-ākhirati hum yūqinūn*, 2:4), not by immediate advantage. The hypocrite does not know he is the fool because the very thing that would have taught him — certainty about the Hereafter — is the thing his double life has made expendable.

**The Collective “People” Versus the Individual “We”**

The contrast between *al-nās* — the people — and *naḥnu* — we — does social work. *The people* is an open, inclusive noun; *we* is a closed, self-referential pronoun. To be told “believe as the people believe” is to be invited into an open membership whose only qualification is the five marks. To answer “shall *we* believe as the fools believe?” is to close membership around a *we* that has already decided its superiority. The sūrah’s earlier portrait of the mindful had emphasized *they* (*ulāʾika*) as a definite class upon guidance; the hypocrite’s *we* is a counter-class that defines itself by not being that class. The verse therefore does not merely correct an insult; it corrects a sociology. The believers are not a sect among sects; they are *the people* insofar as peoplehood is defined by response to guidance. The hypocrite’s *we* is, on that definition, not a people at all but a posture.

**An Insult That Completes the Disease’s Logic**

Finally, to call the believers fools after having called corruption reform completes the disease’s logic. First the hand’s work is renamed (2:11), then the mind’s worth is renamed (2:13). What remains is the tongue’s two faces, which the next verses will describe: *“When they meet those who believe they say, ‘We believe,’ but when alone with their satans they say, ‘We are with you’”* (2:14). The insult of this verse is the bridge between the renamed act and the doubled tongue. The hypocrite who believes his own renaming must eventually despise those who do not need to rename, and that contempt, once uttered as *sufahāʾ*, makes the double life feel justified. The verse closes the bridge by returning the name: the contempt, indulged, becomes the very folly it projected.
