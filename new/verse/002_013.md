## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:13

> And when they are told, “Believe as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Plainest Command, the Scornful Refusal**

The charge of corruption is followed by a simpler sentence, and the reply to it unmasks the group more completely than any argument could. The command carries no content to dispute: *believe as the people believe* — join the faith of the community around you, the faith already tested in lives you can see. No doctrine is expounded, no proof is demanded, no condition is attached; the invitation is to company. And the answer refuses precisely the company: *shall we believe as the fools believe?* The interrogative form in Arabic — *a-nuʾminu* — is rhetorical, and rhetorical questions of this shape carry scorn rather than inquiry. The verse thus locates the root of this unbelief with clinical accuracy. It is not evidential — no objection to a sign or a proof appears anywhere in the reply. It is social: the objection is to the believers. When faith's cost is association with the despised, the costume of verse 8 becomes unbearable to wear in public, and the contempt that the costume was hiding speaks.

**“The Fools” Aimed at the Best on Earth**

The referent of "the fools" is fixed by the historians and the commentators: the Prophet's ﷺ Companions — the community of the early believers, who in Madinan and Makkan terms were largely the poor, the young, the clients, and the unprestigious. The sneer is one of sophistication: faith read as vulgarity, belief as the resort of simple people who could not manage doubt. Some exegetes add a harder layer — that these speakers privately recognized the intelligence of the believers and applied the word "fools" anyway, to perform superiority before their circle. On that reading the insult is deliberate slander layered over private knowledge, which would place the speakers beyond mere blindness. The verse's own verdict, however, ends with *they do not know*, and the commentaries weigh this: a purely strategic slanderer knows exactly what he is doing. The stronger reading makes the contempt genuine — they truly ranked the Companions beneath themselves — which is why the verdict can pronounce them fools without reservation. You cannot strategically slander your way into sincerely missing the worth of the best people on earth; only a broken scale does that.

**The Verdict Flips in the Same Breath**

The retort reuses the machinery of verse 12 — *alā*, *inna*, the fronted pronoun — and aims it at the insult itself: *indeed, it is they who are the fools*. The word thrown at the Companions returns, with restriction, onto the throwers: they, and no others, hold the title. The term deserves its lexicon. *Sufahāʾ* is the plural of *safīh*, from a root meaning lightness — the light-witted, the weak in judgment, and in legal Arabic the person placed under interdiction because he squanders his estate. The *safīh* is not stupid in the modern sense of raw capacity; he is a bad evaluator who misprices things and spends accordingly. That is the exact shape of the verse's reversal. The Companions, called fools for buying the eternal with the temporal, made the only solvent purchase on the market; the sophisticates, congratulating themselves on the deal they refused, were the ones trading at a loss — squanderers of the only capital a self owns. The Qur'an does not answer the insult with a counter-insult; it answers it with an audit.

**From Unperceiving to Unknowing: The Closure Deepens**

The passage's cycle closes on a shifted negation, and the shift is a descent. Verse 9 ended: they do not *perceive* — *lā yashʿurūn*, the failure of fine sensation. Verse 12 repeated it. Verse 13 ends: they do not *know* — *lā yaʿlamūn*, the failure of the cognitive faculty itself. Perception is the surface instrument; knowledge is the settled possession. A man who does not feel the rot under his peace-making might still, in principle, be shown it; a man who does not know his own folly has lost the organ by which showing would work. The two negations map the passage's whole trajectory — from costume (they say) through operation (they deceive) and disease (sickness increased) to the completed closure of judgment. The escalation also explains the sequence's arrangement: the earlier verses described acts a person could stop; this verse describes a condition in which stopping has become unthinkable, because the thinker is the casualty. The cycle of the first diagnosis is finished, and its subject now carries both negations at once.

**Safah as the Qur'an Defines It**

The reversal works only with the Qur'an's own definition of folly, and that definition is consistent wherever the root appears: *safah* is misvaluation — the selling of the lasting for the fleeting, the preference of a moment's position for an eternity's substance. The fools of this passage are not mocked for ignorance of facts; they are convicted of ignorance of worth. The Companions knew what they were buying; the despisers did not know what they were refusing, and their refusal was priced by rank, fashion, and the opinions of their circle. This is why the Qur'an can call knowledge and folly by these inverted names throughout: the person who knows the market of the eternal is the intelligent one whatever his social standing, and the person ignorant of it is the *safīh* whatever his sophistication. The verse legislates a revaluation of the word itself. "Fool" ceases to name the simple and begins to name the misplaced — and by that statute, the sneer of verse 13 was a confession.

**An Egalitarian Epistemology**

What remains is the verse's largest claim: truth is not graded by the social standing of its carriers. The objection "as the fools believe" is an objection to believers, not to belief — an epistemology of status, where a proposition's weight equals the prestige of its holders. The Qur'an's answer dismantles the instrument. If belief were validated by the rank of its adherents, the earliest Muslims — poor, young, unprestigious — would have carried the weakest creed in Arabia, and the chiefs of Quraysh, who made exactly this objection in exactly these terms, would have been its soundest critics. The verse sides permanently against that reasoning: the company of truth is often the company the world despises, and the reflex to measure a message by its messengers' standing is here named what it is — the lightness of the *safīh*. The closing negation makes the diagnosis final. They do not know — and a person who does not know what knowledge is cannot be argued into knowing; he can only be watched, by readers warned that the same scale operates, silently, in every age.
