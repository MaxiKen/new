## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:108

> So fear Allah, and obey me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fear of Allah Comes Before Obedience to the Messenger**

Noah joins two commands: fear Allah and obey me. The order protects the meaning of prophetic obedience. People obey Noah because he is God's messenger, not because he possesses independent authority. Taqwa turns the heart toward God first, and obedience to Noah becomes the practical expression of that turn.

If the order were reversed in the heart, the messenger could become a rival object of devotion. Noah prevents that confusion by placing reverence for Allah at the foundation. His own command is legitimate because it serves the Lord. He does not ask the people to become loyal to a personality detached from divine guidance.

Obedience can be produced by fear of a ruler, pressure from a group, or attraction to a leader's charisma. Noah asks for another kind. The people should fear Allah, meaning they should recognize His authority and guard themselves from disobedience. Their following of Noah must therefore be conscious, truthful, and accountable.

This makes obedience more than outward compliance. A person may imitate a messenger's community while resisting God's claim in the heart. Taqwa joins the outer act to inner awareness. The people follow Noah because they want to please the Lord, not because they are trying to secure a place in a social movement. The inward reason determines whether the outward following is worship or mere conformity.

**The Messenger's Obedience Is Derivative**

Noah says obey me, but his authority is not self-generated. He is a messenger and trustworthy, so obedience to him is obedience to the instruction he brings. If a messenger were to call people to his own worship or to a command opposed to God, the basis of obedience would disappear.

This distinction is important for every religious community. Respect for a teacher, imam, or leader cannot become absolute. Created authority must remain measured by revelation and truth. Noah's command is strong because he stands under God, not above Him. The messenger directs people to the Lord and does not collect their devotion for himself. That limit turns obedience into service of truth rather than surrender to a personality.

A leader can demand obedience by threatening exclusion, promising status, or claiming special access to the unseen. Noah's formula offers a different foundation. The people are called to fear God, so the final audience for their conduct is God. Noah cannot manipulate them into treating him as an untouchable power.

Such obedience is morally serious because it includes the freedom to examine the message. The people should not follow Noah merely because he is forceful. They should recognize that his call accords with God's lordship and then obey as an act of worship. Taqwa makes the follower responsible rather than merely submissive to a human will.

**The Two Commands Form One Response**

Fear Allah and obey me are not unrelated instructions placed side by side. The first supplies the reason for the second. If the people fear God, they will not dismiss the messenger He has sent. If they obey Noah sincerely, their obedience will show that they have accepted God's claim over their lives.

The unity also prevents a shallow spirituality that claims to fear God while rejecting every concrete duty. Taqwa must take form in action. Noah gives the people a specific response to the divine warning: listen to the messenger and follow the path he conveys. Inner reverence that never obeys remains only a claim.

The command could be misunderstood if separated from its first half. “Obey me” by itself might sound like a leader's demand for control. Joined to fear Allah and spoken by a trustworthy messenger, it means accept the guidance God has entrusted to me. Noah is not building a kingdom of personal loyalty.

That distinction also explains why his opponents later judge his followers by social status. They see a human community and assume a contest for influence. Noah sees a people being called to God. The authority of the call cannot be measured by wealth, rank, or the number of people who have already accepted it.

The account in 71:2–3 presents Noah's warning, the command to worship Allah, and the call to fear and obey. The parallel confirms that reverence and obedience are the basic structure of his mission. He is not offering a new social identity first; he is returning the people to their Lord.

This pattern also appears across prophetic calls. The messenger's role is to make God's claim understandable and actionable. The people remain free to accept or reject, but they cannot say that the message had no practical demand. Fear of God becomes a life ordered by obedience.

**Obedience Protects the Community From Inherited Error**

Noah's people have inherited practices that have led them away from God. Obeying the messenger creates a break with that inheritance. The break is not contempt for every ancestor or custom. It is the refusal to let tradition outrank divine guidance. Taqwa gives the courage to change when the familiar path is wrong.

A community that fears only social disapproval will preserve error. A community that fears Allah can accept correction even when correction costs status. Noah's two commands therefore form a liberation from false authority. The people are not asked to become leaderless; they are asked to follow a trustworthy messenger under the authority of God.

**The Command Still Addresses the Present Heart**

A believer can ask whether obedience is directed to God or to the desire to belong. The distinction appears whenever religious identity becomes a performance while the heart resists repentance. Noah's words call for a whole response: revere Allah inwardly and follow revealed guidance outwardly.

The command is concise because its structure is complete. Fear God, then obey the messenger who brings His call. That order preserves monotheism, gives obedience a moral purpose, and places the human leader where he belongs: as a servant pointing to the Lord of all worlds.
