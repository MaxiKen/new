## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:48

> Say, “Surely my Lord hurls the truth ˹against falsehood˺. ˹He is˺ the Knower of all unseen.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb of Force, Not of Persuasion**

*Inna rabbī yaqdhifu bi-l-ḥaqq* — my Lord hurls the truth. The verb *qadhafa* is physical and violent: to throw something with force, to fling, to cast a projectile. Arabic uses it for hurling a stone, and in a different register for casting an accusation at someone's reputation. Nothing in it suggests gentle communication. This is a surprising word to describe revelation, which the sūrah has elsewhere presented as recitation, warning and good news. The choice indicates what kind of event the verse is describing. Truth here is not being offered for consideration; it is being launched at something, and the unstated target is supplied by the Qur'an's use of the same image elsewhere. The sudden shift in register is also a response to the argument's stalemate. Persuasion has been tried for forty-seven verses. This verse describes what happens when the matter passes out of the realm of argument.

**The Image Completed in Another Sūrah**

Al-Anbiyāʾ supplies the full picture with the same verb: *"In fact, We hurl the truth against falsehood, leaving it crushed, and it quickly vanishes"* (21:18). That verse gives the projectile a target and an outcome — falsehood does not withstand the impact, and its collapse is immediate. Read against it, the compressed statement in Sabaʾ is an abbreviation of a known image rather than a bare assertion. Al-Isrāʾ adds the declaration that goes with the impact: *"The truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish"* (17:81). The three passages together describe a single doctrine about the relation between the two. Falsehood is not defeated by being out-argued; it is described as structurally unable to survive contact with truth, which means its persistence is always a function of the truth's absence rather than of its own strength.

**A Second Reading: What Is Cast Into Hearts**

The verb admits another application, and the commentators noticed it early. To hurl the truth may describe what God does to falsehood in the open, or what He does inside a person — casting the truth into a heart, where it lands without being sought and changes the one it strikes. Both senses are available in the Arabic and neither displaces the other. The second reading has considerable support from experience and from the Qur'an's accounts of sudden conversions, in which a man comes to mock a recitation and leaves having believed. Nothing in such an event resembles the conclusion of an argument; it resembles impact. This reading also explains why the verse follows the instruction to go away and reflect (34:46). A person who separates himself from the crowd and thinks honestly has put himself in the path of something.

**Knower of the Unseen, Named Here for a Reason**

The verse closes with *ʿallām al-ghuyūb*, the Knower of all hidden things — an intensive form, not merely one who knows the unseen but one whose knowledge of it is comprehensive. The sūrah has used a version of this title once before, in the oath sworn about the Hour, where it was *ʿālim al-ghayb* (34:3). Its appearance here is precisely placed. The whole dispute recorded in this chapter has been about hidden matters: whether a reckoning is coming, whether the message has a divine source, whether the beings petitioned at the sanctuary have standing. Every one of those questions is settled by information that neither party in Mecca possessed. Naming the One who does possess it, immediately after describing truth as a thrown object, identifies who is doing the throwing and why the projectile does not miss.

**A Third Sense of the Verb, Which the Sūrah Will Use**

There is a detail in the Arabic that becomes visible only five verses later, and it disappears entirely in translation. The same verb, *qadhafa*, returns at the end of this chapter to describe what the deniers did — casting or hurling at the unseen from a distant place (34:53). So the sūrah uses one word for two opposite operations. God hurls the truth, and it lands; they hurl guesses at the unseen, and nothing lands. The symmetry is deliberate and it defines the difference between the two parties in a single shared verb. Both are throwing. One is throwing something real at a target that cannot survive it; the other is throwing speculation at a target it cannot see. The Arabic reader who reaches verse 53 hears this verse again, and the echo does the work of an argument without stating one.

**Why This Follows the Disclaimer of Payment**

The sequence of these closing verses has a logic. The Prophet has just said his reward is with God and that God witnesses everything (34:47), which locates his support entirely outside his audience's control. This verse extends the same point to the message. Notice that the speaker again says *my Lord* rather than naming God in the third person, as he did when answering the boast about wealth (34:36) and again when promising replacement for what is spent (34:39). The possessive runs through all of these closing statements and keeps the argument personal: a man with no payment, no patron and no leverage, asserting that the source he is connected to does the throwing. If the truth is thrown by a Lord who knows every hidden thing, then its progress does not depend on the persuasion of any particular gathering, and the opposition of a city is not a defeat. For a community under pressure in Mecca, that was the practically important claim. The outcome of the argument had ceased to look promising by any ordinary measure, and this verse tells them the measure is wrong. The next verse states the result of the throw as an accomplished fact.
