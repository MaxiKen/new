## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:15

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Shall I inform you of what is better than ˹all of˺ this? Those mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, and pure spouses, along with Allah’s pleasure.” And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Makes the Reader Choose**

The verse opens with an invitation: *shall I inform you of what is better than all of this?* The question presupposes that the previous verse's list is already attractive — that the reader has already felt its pull — and it does not begin by attacking the list. It begins with comparison.

The rhetorical form does two things at once. It flatters the listener's attention, since a question draws a person in where a declaration leaves him at a distance, and it frames the rest of the verse as an answer. From here, everything said about the Garden stands in explicit competition with the seven attractions of the world. The reader is not being ordered to despise what he wants; he is being asked to compare it with something the speaker has information about.

**Rivers and Spouses and "To Stay There Forever"**

The description begins with the standard marks of the Garden: gardens belonging to the Lord, rivers running beneath them, permanence, and pure spouses. Each element corrects a limitation of the world it is being weighed against.

Rivers that flow under a garden are an image of abundance that does not have to be fought for; the Book's other descriptions add that they flow with rivers of water, milk, wine, and honey (47:15), and the dwellers are told that the rivers are theirs to manage and the fruits never exhausted. The spouses *made pure* are named in 2:25 and 4:57 with the promise that the dwellers will remain there always, and the purity is not only moral: it removes the weariness and disappointment that attend every human intimacy. Permanence is the last item precisely because it is the answer to the first verse's deepest problem — that everything desirable here is temporary.

The comparison is silent but total. Nothing on the earlier list is immune to loss. Nothing here can be lost.

**"Along With Allah's Pleasure" — the Item at the Top**

The verse does not end with the gardens. It adds *along with Allah's pleasure*, and the place of that clause is the point. In the sūrah of Repentance the same arrangement is stated with a decisive break in the sentence: *“and—above all— the pleasure of Allah. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph.”* (9:72).

The Book describes this contentment as reciprocal, and its language for it is intimate. At 5:119 the declaration is *“Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him”*, and the same pair of clauses closes 98:8. The fullest statement is given to the soul at the gate: *“Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺.”* (89:28). If the gardens are the answer to the world's instability, this clause answers the world's loneliness — the deepest human want, which is not to own good things but to be welcomed by the one who made them.

**Who Enters: the Mindful, Not the Ascetic**

The condition given is a single word: *those mindful*. *Taqwā* here is the same quality the Book names at the beginning of the sūrah of the Cow: *“This is the Book! There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”* (2:2). It is a watchfulness, a guardedness about one's own conduct before God.

The choice of that word rather than a list of achievements matches the previous verse. If the world's goods are to be handled rather than renounced, the virtue required is not poverty. A person with money, family, and land can be mindful, and a person with nothing can be careless. What the Book is looking for is the awareness that keeps every possession in its place, and the promise is made to whoever carries it.

**"All-Seeing of His Servants" — Why the Verse Ends There**

The verse closes with an attribute, and the attribute is chosen to fit the promise: God sees His servants. The word *baṣīr* is not about eyesight in the ordinary sense; it means seeing with knowledge, seeing what a person actually is.

The clause guards the promise from exactly the abuse a promise invites. If the reward belonged to the poor or the celibate or the landless, people would compete for the outward state. Because the entry is mindfulness, and mindfulness lives where no one can inspect it, the reward depends on a knowledge that reaches inside a person. Public standing settles nothing, and hidden effort is not lost. A servant may be invisible to everyone in the world and fully visible where the decision is made.

**What the Verse Promises Is Not the Same Kind of Thing**

The comparison only works because the two terms are not two versions of one thing. What the world offers is *matāʿ*, gear for a road; what the verse offers is a destination, a permanent home, and the contentment of the one who built it. A traveller who mistakes a full saddlebag for the end of his journey does not become a man who arrives.

That is why the Book never asks a person to stop wanting. It asks him to want the right thing at the right rank, and it supplies the argument by putting the two on one page: seven lines of goods, then three gifts that cannot be taken away. The question at the top of the verse stays open for as long as the reader looks at both.
