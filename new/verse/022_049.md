## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:49

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet Is Told What to Say About Himself**

*Qul yā ayyuhā al-nāsu innamā anā lakum nadhīrun mubīn* — Say: O humanity, I am only, for you, a clear warner.

The verse begins with the imperative *qul*, say. The Qur'an uses this formula when it wants the Prophet to announce something in the first person, and what he is told to announce here is a description of his own office rather than a doctrine.

That is itself a claim worth noticing. The message is not "say that God is one" or "say that the Hour is coming." It is "say what you are." The audience has been demanding signs, hurrying the punishment, and disputing about the messenger, and the response is a statement of job description.

**"Only" Is the Load-Bearing Word**

*Innamā* is the Arabic particle of restriction: I am *only* a warner, nothing else and nothing more.

The particle does two things at once, and both are needed. It subtracts: the Prophet is not a manager of outcomes, not a dispenser of the punishment, not a ruler over people's consciences. And it adds: what he is, he is completely. A warner without qualification is not a partial messenger.

The subtraction is the answer to the demand recorded in verse 47. The mockers asked him to bring the punishment, which is a request for a function he was never given. The verse says so plainly, and it says it as a definition rather than as an excuse.

**What a Warner Is Not**

The Qur'an is consistent about the limits of the office, and the consistency is the point. A *nadhīr* warns: he tells people what is coming and what the consequences are. He does not compel, he does not settle disputes by force, and he is not responsible for whether anyone listens.

Those limits are not a diminution of the role. They are what make the warning a moral act rather than a threat. A person who is warned retains the ability to respond; a person who is compelled does not. The whole structure of the testing period described in this sūrah — the respite granted to wrongdoing communities, the Hour that is coming rather than arrived — depends on the warning being the only thing that arrives.

**Why the Warning Must Be "Clear"**

*Mubīn* means clear, evident, manifest — the same adjective the sūrah used for the verses sent down in 22:16 and for the scripture the disputant lacked in 22:8.

Clarity is a moral requirement in the Qur'an, not a stylistic preference. A warning that could reasonably be misunderstood leaves the person warned with an excuse, and the entire edifice of judgment requires that no one has one. The messengers were sent with clear proofs and clear warnings for exactly that reason.

This is also why the clarity is attributed to the warning rather than to the warner. It is not that the Prophet is an especially lucid man; it is that what he brought is unambiguous about what is being asked and what is being threatened.

**A Refrain on Many Messengers' Lips**

The formula is not unique to Muhammad ﷺ, and the Qur'an puts it into the mouths of the messengers whose communities were just listed. Noah says it, Hūd says it, Ṣāliḥ says it, Shuʿayb says it, and the Prophet is told to say it in the same words.

The repetition across the sūrahs is a claim about the unity of the message rather than a literary device. Every messenger brought the same thing: a warning, clearly stated, to his own people. The variations were in what each community did about it.

**The Address Is the Widest One Available**

*Yā ayyuhā al-nāsu* — O humanity. The same formula with which this sūrah opened (22:1), and it is not the formula most of the Medinan passages use.

The choice is deliberate and it matches the claim being made. The Prophet is not being told to announce his office to a tribe, a city or a community of followers. He is being told to announce it to everyone, which is consistent with the sūrah's opening move of addressing the Hour to all humanity rather than to a congregation.

It is also consistent with the verse that appears earlier in this sūrah in which God is described as judging between all the communities by name (22:17). The message goes out to the same breadth as the judgment will cover.

**Where the Verse Sits**

Placed immediately after the taunt and the answer about the measure of time, verse 49 is the conclusion of that exchange. The Prophet has been asked to do something that is not his job, and he is told to say so — to the whole of humanity, in the second person, without qualification.

The next two verses state what follows from the warning for each of the two groups, which is the only remaining thing a warner has to say.

**The Verse's Restriction Is Also Its Dignity**

It is easy to read *innamā anā nadhīrun mubīn* as a demotion — I am only this, I cannot do more — and that reading gets the tone wrong.

What the particle removes is responsibility for outcomes, not significance of office. The Prophet is not accountable for whether Makkah accepts the warning; he is accountable for delivering it clearly, and the clarity is in his mandate rather than in his talents. Every messenger before him had the same brief and the same limits, and the sūrah has just spent three verses listing them.

The dignity lies in the second half of the formula. A *nadhīr* is someone sent *for you* — *lakum*, for your benefit — with a message that matters enough to be delivered in person. Being only a warner is not a reduction of the job. It is the job, and the job has never been anything else.
