## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:15

> ˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse—call four witnesses from among yourselves. If they testify, confine the offenders to their homes until they die or Allah ordains a ˹different˺ way for them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Procedure for a Grave Breach**

This verse addresses a specific crime within the family and sets a procedure before imposing a penalty. *Wa allātī yaʾtīna al-fāḥishata min nisāʾikum fa astashhidū ʿalayhinna arbaʿatan minkum* — as for those of your women who commit illegal intercourse, call four witnesses from among yourselves. The word *fāḥishah* denotes an act that is openly foul, whose shame is not limited to the actors but extends to the household. The Qur’an treats it as a harm to the family structure the sūrah is building through marriage, dowry, and inheritance. Yet even for that harm, the law demands proof of the highest level: four witnesses, not one or two, and from among your own community — people known, available for scrutiny, and answerable for testimony. The Qur’an elsewhere warns that accusing chaste women without four witnesses brings the accusers themselves to punishment (24:4). By requiring four, the verse shields households from slander and from the ease with which a sexual accusation can destroy reputation. The procedure tells the community that chastity is guarded both by prohibiting the act and by prohibiting careless accusation of it.

**Four Witnesses and Testimony**

The number four was explained as demanding direct sight, not hearsay, inference, or circumstance. Jurists detailed that the witnesses must have seen the act itself in a way that leaves no ambiguity, a standard so high that it is rarely met, which is itself the point. The law makes the crime grave, the proof grave, and the consequence of false testimony grave, so that the social fabric is protected on both sides. The witnesses must be *minkum* — from among you — which the majority understood as upright members of the Muslim community, not outsiders with unknown motives. That requirement also places the matter inside the community’s responsibility, not as a private vengeance. The sūrah will shortly turn to repentance for those who act ignorantly and then repent soon after (4:17), showing that even where proof might exist, the door to return is not closed arbitrarily. Here, the first step is to ensure that no one is confined or punished on the basis of rumour, suspicion, or a single sighting. Four concordant testimonies are needed before the next step is taken.

**Confinement Until Death or Until Another Way**

The sentence *fa in shahidū fa amsikūhunna fī al-buyūt ḥattā yatawaffāhunna al-mawtu aw yajʿala Allāhu lahunna sabīlā* — if they testify, confine the offenders to their homes until they die or Allah ordains a different way — sets a holding measure rather than a fixed corporal penalty. Early authorities understood this confinement as a stage in legislation. At this point in the Medinan period, the community was ordered to keep the women in their homes, both as protection for society and as prevention of further transgression, while awaiting what God would ordain as a way — *sabīl*. Commentators relate that the different way came later through verses on lashing for fornication (24:2) and through Prophetic clarification for adultery where marriage has occurred. The phrase *aw yajʿala Allāhu lahunna sabīlā* therefore signals that the law is not yet in its final form; it is awaiting a clarified path. That language of waiting is characteristic of the Qur’an’s gradual legislation, where a ruling is given, held, and then completed. The sūrah will soon emphasize that Allah wishes to make things clear and to guide to the ways of those before, and to turn to you in mercy (4:26), and this temporary confinement is one place where clarity arrived in stages.

**Why Confinement and Why a Way Out**

Confinement to homes — *amsikūhunna fī al-buyūt* — was read in two lights. Practically, it removed the context for repetition by limiting movement and contact, without public shaming beyond the family. Morally, it treated the home as the sphere where the act’s harm first fell, since unlawful intercourse breaches the marital bond and the lineage that inheritance law depends on. The until clause — *ḥattā yatawaffāhunna al-mawtu aw yajʿala Allāhu lahunna sabīlā* — holds two possibilities open: life-long seclusion if no new ruling comes, or a new ordained way if God provides it. The community was therefore told to act decisively and to wait expectantly at the same time. That combination keeps law from becoming either lax or hastily severe. The sūrah elsewhere commands *“do not kill yourselves”* (4:29) and describes God as ever Merciful (4:29), and even in this severe matter it leaves the future open to mercy through another way. The verse thus teaches that punishment in this faith is not the urge to harm but the search for a path that protects lineage, deters transgression, and leaves room for divine clarification.

**Temporary Legislation With Permanent Lessons**

Although the specific ruling of confinement was later superseded by detailed penalties for *zinā* in other passages, the principles inside the verse remain. The need for four witnesses remains the Qur’anic standard for proving illicit intercourse, guarding privacy while still naming the act as *fāḥishah*. The requirement that testimony be tested remains, protecting the accused from collusion and protecting the accusers from the grave sin of slander. The idea that some laws are given as an interim holding until a clearer way is ordained remains a model for how revelation unfolds: not all at once in a single form, but step by step as the community can bear and apply. The verse also mirrors the sūrah’s larger concern with entrusted wealth and entrusted persons. Just as the sūrah guarded the orphan’s property from being eaten and the woman’s dowry from being seized, it guards the household’s honour from being destroyed by unverified accusation and from being left without a remedy when the accusation is proven. Four witnesses, confinement, and an awaited way together hold those concerns in balance.

**Between Justice and Mercy**

Placed after the inheritance code and before the verses on repentance, this verse shows how the sūrah holds justice and mercy together without confusing them. The act is named without euphemism, the proof is set at the highest bar, the interim sanction is custodial rather than corporal, and the future is left explicitly to what God will ordain. That structure prevents two equal errors: treating a grave breach as if it were trivial, and treating accusation as if it were proof. The next verses will broaden the picture: both parties who commit the sin are to be disciplined and then relieved if they repent (4:16), and repentance itself has a time and a limit (4:17–18). This verse focuses on one side of the pair and on the evidential threshold that must be crossed before any sanction applies. By insisting on four and by naming the confinement as until death or until another way, the Qur’an keeps the law both firm and open — firm enough to protect the family, open enough to await the completion of its own legislation, and throughout mindful that the One who ordains the way is the One who is All-Knowing, All-Wise (4:11, 4:26).
