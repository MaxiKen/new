## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:93

> besides Allah? Can they help you or even help themselves?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Tests the Idols' Ability to Help**

The interrogation becomes specific. Can the objects worshipped besides Allah help their devotees, or can they even help themselves? The two parts expose the same failure from different angles. A claimed god must possess power for the worshipper and stability for its own condition. These idols possess neither.

The question is framed in terms of help because worship is usually tied to need. People turn to a supposed power for rescue, protection, provision, or relief. Abraham's earlier question already tested whether the idols could hear and benefit. This verse places the test at the final crisis, where the difference between appearance and power cannot be hidden.

**A Helper Must Possess What the Worshipper Lacks**

The idolaters treated created objects as sources of help, yet a carved object depends on people for its position, care, and public importance. It cannot supply itself with life or protection. A thing that needs worshippers to maintain its presence cannot be the ultimate rescuer of those worshippers.

This does not deny that people can help one another by God's permission. A doctor, parent, teacher, or neighbor may provide real assistance within created limits. The question concerns objects raised to divine status. Such objects are not simply weak helpers. They have no independent agency on which ultimate trust can rest.

**The Second Question Makes the First Ironic**

If the idols cannot help themselves, the worshippers' dependence on them becomes visibly absurd. The people had placed their own fate in the hands of objects unable to preserve their own standing. The question therefore uses the idolaters' expectation against itself. Their supposed protectors are shown to require protection.

This irony is not mockery for its own sake. It is a final exposure of a dangerous error. False worship persuades people to reverse the proper relation between Creator and creature. The dependent object is treated as lord, while the actual Lord is ignored. The judgment scene restores the order that idolatry had inverted.

**Help in the Hereafter Cannot Be Claimed by Ceremony**

Rituals can be repeated until they feel like guarantees. The verse denies that feeling. Ceremony does not create a saving power in the object toward which it is directed. The question asks what the idol can actually do, not how often people bowed before it or how impressive the shrine appeared.

This warning also applies to religious forms that have lost their inner truth. A person may collect symbols, titles, and public acts while neglecting sincere worship and repentance. The issue is not that forms are worthless, but that forms cannot help when they are severed from faith. On the final day, an empty performance cannot become a helper.

**The Lord Is Not Placed Beside the Idols as an Equal**

The phrase besides Allah identifies the central error. The question is not whether people recognize God in some distant way. It is whether they assign another object a share in the dependence that belongs to Him. By asking about help, the verse shows what practical association looks like: trust, fear, and hope are directed to a rival.

A person can say that God is supreme while behaving as though a created object controls blessing or harm. The heart's actual reliance reveals the theology beneath the words. Abraham's argument reaches that hidden level. If the object cannot help itself, it cannot deserve the trust that competes with God.

Quran 7:192 describes the idols as unable to help their worshippers or themselves. The parallel confirms that the doubled question is a recurring Qur'anic test, not a detail confined to Abraham's debate. A false god fails both outwardly and inwardly: it cannot aid its follower and cannot defend its own existence.

The recurrence also makes the argument portable. Communities change their images, names, and rituals, but the test remains. What is being treated as a lord? Can it act independently, or does it depend entirely on God and on the people who serve it? A clear answer protects the heart from confusing influence with divinity.

**The Question Places the Worshippers Before Their Own Hope**

The condemned cannot answer by saying that the idols once gave them comfort. Comfort may come from habit, group belonging, or self-deception. The question concerns help in the real sense, help that can change the servant's fate before God. Their former comfort cannot now protect them from the truth.

This distinction is important for present life. A false object may organize a community, inspire emotion, or provide a sense of identity. Those effects are real as social experiences, but they do not prove divine power. Abraham's question asks whether the object can help in the final and fullest sense. If it cannot help itself, the answer is already clear.

A created means can be respected without being worshipped. People use tools, institutions, and relationships while knowing that all benefit ultimately depends on God. The problem begins when a means is treated as a lord whose favor must be sought through worship or whose anger controls the unseen.

The verse gives a simple safeguard. Ask whether the thing can help itself and whether it has independent authority. If not, use it within its limits but do not give it the heart's ultimate allegiance. The idols fail the test completely, and the worshippers are forced to see that their dependence had been placed where no help existed. The right use of means leaves the heart grateful to God; idolatry leaves the heart answerable to an object that cannot answer. No title or ceremony can change that final incapacity. Help belongs to the Lord alone. Once that truth is clear, the human being can use created means without kneeling inwardly before them.
