## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:4

> and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Clause That Turns Outward**

A new revelation normally announces itself by displacing what preceded it. This one requires belief in both: in what has been sent down to the Prophet *and* in what was sent down before him (2:4). The second half is the surprising one, and it is stated as an article of faith rather than as a courtesy to neighbours — a Muslim is not permitted to be indifferent to earlier scripture.

The wording is pointed at a specific temptation, and the temptation had already been voiced: *When it is said to them: “Believe in what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “We only believe in what was sent down to us,” and they deny what came afterwards, though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures* (2:91). The failure there is not disbelief in revelation as such; it is the confinement of revelation to one's own community's copy. The verse before us refuses the mirror image of that failure as well. A Muslim who accepted the Quran and dismissed the Torah and the Gospel as pure fabrication would be making the same error in the opposite direction.

**The Temptation the Quran Names as Disbelief**

The strongest statement of the principle comes in another sūrah, where the act of dividing the messengers is made the definition of unbelief itself: *Surely those who deny Allah and His messengers and wish to make a distinction between Allah and His messengers, saying, “We believe in some and disbelieve in others,” desiring to forge a compromise, they are indeed the true disbelievers* (4:150–51). The verse that follows states the opposite as a description of the saved: *those who believe in Allah and His messengers—accepting all; rejecting none* (4:152).

Nothing in the Quran is more consistently repeated than this refusal to pick and choose. The believers are given a formula to say, and it is the same formula in every place it appears: *We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us; and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them* (2:136; cf. 3:84). The list is worth noticing. It names the patriarchs before it names Moses and Jesus, which means the community being instructed is not being asked to adopt someone else's canon but to recognise a single history it has now joined at the end of.

**How Much of the Earlier Scripture Is Meant**

The clause raises a question the commentators have not answered uniformly: what exactly is to be believed in? The Quran names the Torah, the Gospel, the Psalms, and the scrolls of earlier prophets, and it describes its own relation to them as confirmation: *He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel* (3:3).

Two positions follow, and the distinction between them is the useful part. The strict reading takes the object of belief to be the revelation as God sent it down, which no longer exists in an unaltered form, so that what is required is assent to the fact of those revelations and to whatever in the present books agrees with the Quran. The broader reading requires believers to affirm the present scriptures as scripture. The strict reading has the better evidence, because the Quran itself accuses some holders of earlier scripture of altering and concealing parts of what they were given, and it cannot simultaneously command belief in what it says has been changed. The clause is therefore about the source, not the surviving text — which is why the Quran can call itself a confirmer of the Torah while repeatedly correcting what its custodians do with it.

**Certainty, Not Mere Belief**

The final clause changes its verb. The first items in this list are objects of *belief*; the Hereafter is the object of *yaqīn*, certainty. The word is the strongest in the Quran's vocabulary for knowledge — the settled state in which doubt has been removed — and it is regularly set against conjecture: *and deny the Day of Judgment, until the inevitable came to us* (74:46–47); *They only speculate* (45:24). The sūrah of Competition makes the point by contrast, imagining what a person would do if he knew what he claims not to know: *Indeed, if you were to know ˹your fate˺ with certainty, ˹you would have acted differently˺* (102:5), and then the same word is used for the sight that ends the argument — *Again, you will surely see it with the eye of certainty* (102:7).

The choice of word also marks the Hereafter as the hardest item on the list to hold. The world does not look like a place with an appointment at the end of it. Its most obvious feature is that people die and are replaced: *There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time* (45:24). Certainty is demanded precisely where the evidence of the senses runs the other way, which is what makes it certainty rather than inference.

**Why the End Carries the Weight**

It is worth asking why this one item gets the stronger verb. The reason is that the others can be possessed without consequence, and this one cannot. A person can believe in God, in the unseen, and in revelation, and still treat his life as a possession with no audit — the Quran records that combination as the standard position of its opponents: *There is nothing beyond this worldly life and we will never be resurrected* (6:29). Once the Hereafter is removed, every act loses its weight, and no amount of correct belief upstream changes that.

This is also why the sūrah's opening description builds as it does. Belief in the unseen establishes that there is more than what is visible. Prayer and giving establish what a person does about it. Certainty about the end establishes that any of it matters. The three belong together: a creed with no audit attached is not the creed this sūrah describes.

**The Whole Description, Stated at Both Ends**

The sūrah does not leave these five marks behind after its opening; it returns to them at its close, in a verse that reads like a summary of everything between: *The Messenger ˹firmly˺ believes in what has been revealed to him from his Lord, and so do the believers. They ˹all˺ believe in Allah, His angels, His Books, and His messengers. ˹They proclaim,˺ “We make no distinction between any of His messengers.”* (2:285). Between the two statements lie 280 verses of law, history, argument, and instruction — all of it addressed to the people described in these lines.

The placement tells a reader how the sūrah expects to be read. Its rulings are not a code dropped from nowhere; they are addressed to people who have already accepted that there is a God they cannot see, that He has spoken more than once, and that they will answer for what they do. The sūrah states the outcome for such people in the line that follows.
