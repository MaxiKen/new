## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:4

> and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Revelation Received and Revelation Already Given**

The verse names both directions of belief in one clause. The believers accept what has come down to the Prophet ﷺ in their own time and what came down before it, and the two are treated as members of one family rather than as rivals. The Qur'an states the same position in the final verses of this sūrah: *“The Messenger ˹firmly˺ believes in what has been revealed to him from his Lord, and so do the believers. They ˹all˺ believe in Allah, His angels, His Books, and His messengers.”* (2:285)

That sentence places the Books of God among the articles of faith, alongside angels and messengers. Believing in the Qur'an while denying the scriptures that preceded it is not a stricter form of belief in the Book's own terms; it is a failure to accept what the Book says about itself. The revelation is presented as the latest instalment of a series, and the community is defined by its acceptance of the series.

**Earlier Revelation Is Not a Rival Document**

The Qur'an treats earlier scriptures as its own inheritance and its own confirmation. It describes the single way God ordained for the prophets collectively: *“He has ordained for you ˹believers˺ the Way which He decreed for Noah, and what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what We decreed for Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, ˹commanding:˺ ‘Uphold the faith, and make no divisions in it.’”* (42:13)

The verse also anticipates the objection that this privilege belongs to one community. The Qur'an praises believers among the People of the Book who accept the new revelation without abandoning the truth they already hold: *“Indeed, there are some among the People of the Book who truly believe in Allah and what has been revealed to you ˹believers˺ and what was revealed to them. They humble themselves before Allah—never trading Allah's revelations for a fleeting gain.”* (3:199). The faith described in this verse is not a new religion replacing an old one. It is the recognition of a message in an existing line.

**Certainty About the Hereafter**

The third element in the verse is not belief in the Hereafter but certainty of it. *Yūqinūn* comes from a root meaning to be settled, to have no wavering left. The Qur'an uses it for knowledge that has stopped being a hypothesis, and it places that certainty beside the two forms of revelation because the three belong to one structure: revelation given before, revelation given now, and the accounting that will settle what was done with both.

Certainty about that accounting changes how the present is valued. A person convinced that the ledger remains open after death will accept costs in this world that others will not, and the Book treats that conviction as a practical matter rather than a mood. The Qur'an's arguments for the Hereafter are arguments for the reasonableness of a life conducted with reference to it; the verse therefore ends not with a description of paradise but with the mental condition of the believer.

**Does the Verse Continue the Portrait or Start a New One?**

Commentators asked whether this clause describes the same people as the previous verse or introduces a second group. The first reading takes the conjunction as continuing: the mindful are those who believe in the unseen, pray, give, and accept both revelations, and await the Hereafter. The second reading takes this verse as describing believers from among the People of the Book who accepted the Prophet while remaining attached to what they had received.

Both readings have grounds. The first follows the grammar of a single unbroken description, and it is supported by the fact that the sūrah opens by describing one audience. The second explains why earlier revelation is mentioned at all, since it would have had little relevance to an audience of former polytheists, and it is supported by the praise given to believers among the People of the Book in 3:199. The stronger position holds the two together: the verse continues one portrait, and that portrait includes whatever believers from the earlier communities it succeeds in describing.

**"Revealed to You": An Address That Includes Everyone Else**

The verse addresses the Prophet ﷺ in the second person singular. He is the one to whom the revelation has come, and the community's belief in it is belief in what was given to him. The Book uses the same construction throughout, and its effect is to keep the community's faith attached to a messenger rather than to a text that arrived unattached.

Muslims who recite these words take part in a sentence addressed to someone else. That is the ordinary situation of the sūrah: it was given to the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah, and it is recited by everyone who comes after him. The fourth line of the Book thus establishes both the continuity of revelation and the particular channel by which the latest instalment arrived.

**Certainty and the Shape of Ordinary Life**

The verse ends with the Hereafter because conviction about it is what makes the earlier clauses portable. Belief in the unseen, prayer, giving and acceptance of revelation are all commitments that can be abandoned if death ends everything; certainty about a reckoning makes them reasonable when they are expensive. The Qur'an rests the whole moral order on that conviction.

It states the alternative without softening it: *“And say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve.’”* (18:29). The Book's believers are not described as people who have answered every question. They are described as people who have settled the question of what their lives are for, and who have arranged their prayer, their money and their expectations accordingly.
