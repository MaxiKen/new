## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:4

> and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Faith Across Many Books**

The fourth quality extends belief from the unseen in general to revelation in particular — and not to one revelation but to a chain. A Muslim is required to believe in the Torah given to Mūsā, the Gospel given to ʿĪsā, and the scriptures before them; belief in prior books is a condition of the faith, not a courtesy toward it. The Qur'an grounds this requirement in the unity of the message itself: *"He has ordained for you ˹believers˺ the Way which He decreed for Noah, and what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what We decreed for Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, ˹commanding:˺ 'Uphold the faith, and make no divisions in it'"* (42:13). One religion, delivered in installments, with each installment obliging belief in all the others. The verse therefore draws the community's boundary unusually: it excludes nobody who accepts the whole chain, and it admits nobody who accepts only their preferred link. Belief here is loyalty to a single divine project across centuries rather than subscription to a single text.

**Why the Last Book Is Named First**

The order of the clause is chronologically reversed: what was revealed *to you* is named before what was revealed *before you*. The reversal is a statement of rank. The final revelation stands at the head of the list because it functions as the guardian of the earlier ones — confirming, correcting, and completing what time and human hands had altered in their transmission. The Qur'an claims exactly this office for itself: Jibrīl *"revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah's Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers"* (2:97). A confirming book logically precedes the books it confirms in the order of belief, since the believer's access to the earlier revelations now runs through its testimony. Naming the Prophet's book first also personalizes the chain: revelation is not an abstract library but an address delivered to a man in the hearer's own generation, continuous with the deliveries before it.

**One List — or Two Groups Merged Into One**

A grammatical question with communal consequences runs through the classical commentaries. Does the "and" that opens this verse continue the description of verse 3 — one group of God-conscious, bearing all four qualities — or does it introduce a second subject: *and those who* believe in the two revelations, with verse 5's verdict covering both? Al-Ṭabarī favored the second reading and gave it historical content: verse 3 describes the believers from the Arabs, verse 4 the believers from the People of the Book who accepted the earlier revelations and now accept the Prophet ﷺ. The Qur'an attests such people exist: *"Indeed, there are some among the People of the Book who truly believe in Allah and what has been revealed to you ˹believers˺ and what was revealed to them"* (3:199). Weighing the readings, the difference matters less than it appears. Verse 5 gathers everyone described into a single "they," and the reward attaches to qualities rather than genealogy. The stronger effect of the passage is unifying: two streams of belief pouring into one guidance.

**Yaqīn: Faith That Has Stopped Negotiating**

The verse ends not with belief but with certainty — *yaqīn*. Arabic distinguishes grades of assent: conjecture (*ẓann*), knowledge acquired by report or inference, and *yaqīn*, the settled certainty that leaves no room for renegotiation. The God-conscious are described as having *sure faith* in the Hereafter specifically. The specification is meaningful. Of all the unseen realities named in verse 3, the Hereafter is the one most contested and most easily deferred — a doctrine whose consequences are all in the future and whose evidence is entirely in report. Certainty about it is therefore the hardest faith, and the verse demands it not as intellectual achievement but as a condition of character: the heart that has stopped arguing with the Last Day. Classical spirituality treated *yaqīn* as the pivot of the religious life — belief that has hardened into conviction moves the limbs, while belief that remains opinion only moves the tongue.

**The Hereafter Anchors the Whole Portrait**

Structurally, the five-verse opening of al-Baqarah closes its description of the God-conscious with a horizon. Taqwā, prayer, and donation are practices stretched across a lifetime; belief in the two revelations fixes their source; certainty in the Hereafter fixes their destination. Without the destination the earlier qualities lose their peculiar rationality — why guard, why stand at dawn, why let go of wealth, if the ledger closes at the grave? The verse answers by naming the closing of the ledger as the object of certainty. Exegetes connect this to the essay-worthy theme of the Qur'an as a whole: guidance functions as guidance only for someone going somewhere. By placing the Hereafter last in the list, the sūrah makes eschatology the frame of ethics — every act of the *muttaqīn* is read, by them, as a deposit against a Day they hold as certain.

**The Prophet Written Into the Definition**

One more feature deserves notice: the community is defined around its messenger. The verse says "what has been revealed *to you*" — the second-person address to the Prophet ﷺ inside a description of believers inserts him into their identity. In the Madinan setting this was a live boundary question: Jews and Christians accepted revelation in general while rejecting this messenger, and the verse forecloses the option. Acceptance of the chain terminates in its final link. At the same time the formulation refuses triumphalism — the same breath that names the Prophet's revelation first names the earlier revelations as objects of belief. The sūrah will later state the tension frankly: *"Never will the Jews or Christians be pleased with you, until you follow their faith. Say, 'Allah's guidance is the only ˹true˺ guidance'"* (2:120). Here at the opening, the balance stands complete: a distinct community, gathered around a final messenger, believing everything heaven ever sent.
