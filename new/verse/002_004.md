## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:4

> and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Conjunction That Asks Who Is Being Described**

The verse begins with a conjunction — *and* — and the conjunction carries an exegetical question: does *“and who believe in what was revealed to you…”* (2:4) continue the portrait of the same *muttaqīn* defined in (2:3), or does it introduce a second group whose portrait will be sealed in (2:5)? The first reading stitches (2:3) and (2:4) together as two clauses describing one people: those who believe in the unseen *and* believe in what was revealed to you. The second reading hears a pause: (2:3) describes believers among the Arabs who had no prior scripture, while (2:4) describes believers among the People of the Book who had already received a prior revelation and now affirm the present one (Ṭ; cf. 98:1-8). On that second reading the *“they who shall prosper”* of (2:5) would be the People of the Book described in (2:4) rather than the group in (2:3). Both readings are transmitted, and the choice turns on whether *alladhīna* is read as appositional or as resumptive. The more sustained reception treats (2:3) and (2:4) as one portrait: the mindful are defined first by their relation to the unseen and to worship, then by their relation to revelation and to the Last Day, so that the inner and outer marks of (2:3) are now grounded in a history.

**“What Was Revealed to You” — The Present Book Named as Descent**

*“What has been revealed to you, O Prophet”* — *mā unzila ilayka* — names the Qur'an without naming it. The verb *unzila* (sent down) is passive, with agency left for the next verse to name as “from their Lord” (2:5), but the direction is clear: the Book comes from above to a single addressee. The second-person singular *ilayka* makes the transmission personal before it is communal; the Prophet receives before the community receives through him. That singularity matters because the next clause widens the scope. The believer who is now being praised is not one who believes in the Qur'an as if prior books had been nothing; he is one whose belief in the present revelation does not require him to deny that prior descents were descents. The Qur'an insists on that continuity precisely at the moment when community identity could be formed by repudiation.

**“What Was Revealed Before You” — Continuity Without Conflation**

The phrase *“what was revealed before you”* — *mā unzila min qablik* — gathers the prior scriptures under the same verb of descent that was used for the Qur'an. Torah, Gospel, and what was given to the prophets before them are not described as human literature later superseded by a different kind of event; they are described as the same kind of event, earlier. That is why the Qur'an can later codify mutual recognition as a creedal requirement: *“Say: We believe in Allah and what was sent down to us, and what was sent down to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and the Tribes, and what was given to Moses and Jesus and the prophets from their Lord”* (2:136), and again *“The Messenger believes in what was sent down to him from his Lord, and the believers too”* (2:285). The belief demanded here is not archival expertise. It is assent that guidance has a history and that its history has a single Sender, so that to affirm the later descent while vilifying the earlier descent would be to divide what the wording has joined with a single verb.

**The Difference Made Visible in 98:1–8 and in 3:199**

The alternative reading — that (2:3) addresses one community and (2:4) another — draws its plausibility from passages where the Qur'an explicitly distinguishes believing People of the Book. *“Among the People of the Book are those who believe in Allah and in what was sent down to you and what was sent down to them”* (3:199) is the locus classicus for that category, and the sūrah of Clear Proof rehearses the same division: a people who were given the Book and then split, and within them a subset who believed and did righteous deeds as the best of creatures (98:6-8). Those who hear (2:4) as that subset take *“who believe in what was revealed before you”* as a description of prior faith retained rather than newly acquired. The more general reading does not deny that category; it simply does not restrict (2:4) to it. An early Medinan listener who had never before received a scripture would still be asked, by (2:4), to affirm that prior descents were true, even if his personal experience of them is mediated through the Qur'an’s telling.

**Certainty About the Hereafter Is Not an Extra Belief**

The verse does not say *“and who believe in the Hereafter.”* It says *“and who are certain of the Hereafter”* — *wa-bi-l-ākhirati hum yūqinūn*, with the pronoun repeated for emphasis: *they*, they are certain. The shift from *yuʾminūna* (they believe) to *yūqinūn* (they are certain) is not stylistic variety. Faith (*īmān*) can coexist with fluctuation; certainty (*yaqīn*) is faith that has become stable, resistant to erosion by delay or by competing affection. The Qur'an ties certainty precisely to this doctrine because the Hereafter is unseen in the most stringent sense — no one has returned to report it — and because its implications govern spending and prayer in the previous verse. One establishes prayer and parts with wealth *from* what was provided (2:3) only if the return is reckoned as more real than the outlay. That is why the same root *yaqīn* appears when the Qur'an contrasts those who treat the Hour as speculation with those whose hearts are fixed.

**Why the Hereafter Follows Revelation, Not Precedes It**

The sequence *unseen → prayer → spending → revelation → Hereafter* traces a movement from foundation to horizon. Unseen is the ground of theism; prayer and spending are its two immediate fruits; revelation is its historical articulation; the Hereafter is its final horizon. To believe in what was revealed before you is to accept that guidance has a past; to be certain of the Hereafter is to accept that guidance has a future beyond the present world. Without that future, the past would be nostalgia and the present would be management. With it, the past becomes precedent and the present becomes preparation. The verse therefore closes the definition of the mindful precisely where temptation to truncate it is strongest: at the point where the cost of mindfulness — the standing, the giving, the assenting to a book one did not author — must be measured against an outcome not yet seen.

**Revelation and Return Joined by a Single Waw**

Two *and*s carry the verse’s theology. The first *and* joins the present Book to the prior books under a single descent; the second *and* joins revelation to return, so that the Book that tells of the return is not separated from the return it tells of. A community could in principle have esteemed its own book while despising earlier books, or esteemed books in general while doubting that any return is sure. The verse forbids both partitions. It requires the mindful to hold descent and return together, and to hold descent as a single genus with many instances. That is the Qur'anic view of sacred history in miniature, expounded at length in its essay on history and other religions and in its essay on death and afterlife, where the continuity of prophethood and the certainty of accountability are presented not as two doctrines but as the beginning and end of one.

**They Are Certain — The Pronoun That Excludes**

*Hum yūqinūn* — “they, they are certain” — repeats the pronoun to exclude others. It is not merely that the mindful happen to be certain; it is that certainty about the Last Day distinguishes the mindful from those who process the same historical data as sociology rather than as warning. The sūrah will soon divide humanity into the mindful, the avowed rejectors, and the waverers who claim faith while deceiving (2:6-20). The doubling of the pronoun here anticipates that division: among those who intellectually grant that a book was “revealed before you,” only some live as if the last page — the Hereafter — is already written. The seal of (2:5), *“it is they”* repeated, will then make explicit what this verse has already prepared: guidance and prosperity belong not to everyone who has heard descent, but to those whose hearing has become *yaqīn*.
