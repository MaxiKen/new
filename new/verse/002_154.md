## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:154

> Never say that those martyred in the cause of Allah are dead—in fact, they are alive! But you do not perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prohibition Addressed to Speech**

The verse does not tell the community what to feel about its dead; it tells them what to say. *Lā taqūlū* — "do not say" — is aimed at the language of the household and the street, at the words spoken over a grave before the burial is even finished. The occasion the commentators connect with it belongs to the year of Uḥud, when some seventy of the community were killed in a single afternoon, Ḥamza ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib among them, and the city was left to explain to itself what had happened to its sons.

The correction is precise rather than sentimental. The Book does not deny that the bodies were buried, and it does not tell the mourners to imagine their relatives seated in the room. It denies a word — *amwāt*, dead — and replaces it with another, and then tells the speaker why he must accept the replacement: *wa-lākin lā tashʿurūn*, "but you do not perceive" (2:154). Revelation hears the difference between a fact and the whole of a fact.

**The Scope of "in the Way of God"**

The phrase *fī sabīl Allāh* is the condition that the verse attaches to the promise. Ibn Kathīr widens it to include the one killed defending his family, his property, or his community, and the community's jurists built the legal category of the martyr (*shahīd*) on that breadth: the one who dies in battle ranks first, and the reports add the one who drowns, the one who dies in a plague, and the woman who dies in childbirth.

Al-Qurṭubī notes that the phrase is not a description of the manner of dying but of the purpose of the life. A person who spent his strength in the cause of God and met his end in the middle of that work is covered by the verse; the text does not require that the end have been violent. The distinction matters to the community, because the verse's promise is attached to it.

The community's jurists took a further ruling from the same occasion. The reports record the Prophet's ﷺ instruction at Uḥud that the dead be buried where they fell, wrapped in their clothes, without the washing the funeral rites ordinarily require, and the report explains the reason given: they would be raised on the Day of Judgment with their wounds still marked, the colour of blood and the scent of musk (al-Bukhārī). The rules that separate the martyr's funeral from the funerals of others in the community's law take their point of departure from that instruction, and the verse before them supplies the doctrine the jurists were applying.

**"They Are Alive"**

The claim itself is without qualification. Al-Ṭabarī explains it as the life of *barzakh*, the interval between death and the resurrection — a state of provision and bliss for this class of the dead, distinct from the ordinary condition of the grave. Ibn Kathīr adds that the Qur'an explains its own statement elsewhere: those killed in the way of God are "alive with their Lord, receiving provision" (3:169). The two verses are read as a single doctrine.

The community's scholars took the claim literally and resisted the reduction of it to a metaphor about reputation or memory. A person whose good name survives him, al-Rāzī observes, is not described anywhere in the Book as receiving provision; the verse speaks of a state that the community cannot inspect but must affirm, and its wording is a statement about the dead rather than a comfort about the living.

**The Limits of Perception**

The closing clause is the reason the command was needed: *lā tashʿurūn*. The verb belongs to the vocabulary of sensory awareness — the same root used for the intuition that comes before reflection. The community's teachers read the choice carefully: the Book does not say "you do not know", but "you do not perceive", because the obstacle is the eye and not the intellect.

Al-Qurṭubī draws from the clause the general principle that revelation corrects the report of the senses where the senses cannot reach. The community stands in the same relation to this subject as it does to the angels and the resurrection: the evidence is the word of God, and the position is assent. The verse therefore does not console by softening a fact; it consoles by stating a larger one.

**The Reports of the Prophet ﷺ**

The most cited explanation of the verse is the report of Ibn Masʿūd in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim: the souls of the martyrs are in the bodies of green birds that move about freely in the Garden, returning to lamps hanging beneath the Throne, and when their Lord asks what they desire, they answer that they want to be returned to the world to be killed in His cause again, so great is what they have been given.

The wording of the narration differs between the transmitting companions, and the scholars treated the variations as one report in several forms. What the community took from them is the content of the verse's promise: a life that is active, provisioned, and regarded. Ibn Kathīr also records the comparison made by some of the early teachers between the souls of the martyrs and those of ordinary believers, the former described as unconfined birds.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse establishes a prohibition about language, a scope for the promise, a claim about the state of the martyr, a limit on human perception, and the reports that fill the claim in. It belongs to a passage that walks the community through patience and testing before it arrives at the reward, and it stands in the middle of it as the assurance the believers needed in the year when their graves were counted in the dozens: the dead are not dead, and the reason a person cannot see that is not that nothing is there.
