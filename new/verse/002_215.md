## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:215

> They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate. Say, “Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers. Whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question About Giving**

The verse reports a question about what the community should spend and answers it with a list of recipients, and the sentence belongs to a series of inquiries that the community brought to the Prophet ﷺ. The questions are recorded in the Book with their answers, so that the guidance is preserved for those who came after.

The answer concerns the destination of the gift rather than its amount, and the community's teachers noted the order in which the recipients are named. What the reader receives is an instruction about where generosity begins, and the sentence then states that whatever good is done is known to God.

**The Recipients Named**

The list begins with parents and relatives, then names orphans, the poor, and travellers, and the sequence moves from those who are closest to those who have no claim but need. The same list appears in the sūrah's description of the righteous earlier, and the community's scholars read the two passages together.

The order indicates that the obligations of kinship come first and that the circle widens outward, and the community's teachers used the sentence when they spoke about the arrangement of a person's giving. Readers are shown that the religion does not leave the poor without a claim, and the list's inclusion of travellers reflects the conditions of a community whose members crossed the region on business and on pilgrimage.

**The Reports About the Occasion**

The commentators report that the question was asked before the formal alms were instituted, and they read the sentence as an instruction about voluntary giving. Other passages on the subject were revealed later, and the community's scholars arranged the material in the order of its revelation.

The reading gives the sentence its place in the development of the community's practice, and the two forms of giving were distinguished by the community's jurists. Voluntary spending belongs to the whole of a person's life, and the sentence in this place supplies its framework.

**Giving From What Is Loved**

The sūrah's earlier description of the righteous used the phrase about giving from cherished wealth, and the community's teachers connected the two verses. The sentence here does not specify an amount, and the omission was read as an indication that the quality of the gift matters more than its size.

The Book's treatment of generosity elsewhere names the kinds of wealth that people value, and the reader is shown that the instruction concerns what a person is attached to. The answer given in the sentence therefore addresses the disposition as well as the recipients, and the community's teachers used it when they encouraged giving from what the giver would prefer to keep.

**The Knowledge of God**

The sentence closes with the statement that whatever good the community does is known to God, and the clause appears at the close of instructions about worship and giving throughout the sūrah. The community's scholars read it as the assurance that no act is lost, and they noted that the clause also removes the need for display.

The Book's practice of ending a command with a statement about God's knowledge gives the reader the sense that the act is performed before a witness, and the sentence's placement after the list of recipients supplies the assurance that accompanies the instruction. Readers are shown that the value of the gift does not depend on the recognition it receives, and the community's teachers drew from the clause their teaching about secrecy in giving.

**The Instruction in Its Context**

The sentence follows the account of the community's testing and the command about fighting, and it stands among the instructions that organise the community's resources. The Book has described what the community will endure and has now turned to what it is to give, so that the reader sees the two matters as parts of the same life.

The passage's concern is the support of those who cannot support themselves, and the community's teachers read the verse as one of the foundations of the community's care for its weaker members. The sentence's place among the questions and answers gives the reader a picture of a community learning its obligations through the inquiries it brought, and the guidance is preserved in the answers it received.

**The Order of the Recipients**

The answer names five groups in an order that the exegetes examined closely, and the sequence moves from the closest of kin outward to the stranger. Parents come first, then the relatives, then the orphans, the needy, and the wayfarer, so that the duty begins where the claim is strongest and extends to those whose need is discovered on the road.

The jurists built the law of maintenance on the sentence. The support of parents who have no means is an obligation upon the child, and the support of the family is placed before voluntary charity, since the report describes the spending on one's household as the most virtuous. What is given beyond the obligations belongs to the free gift that the Book commends.

The opening phrase, whatever you spend of good, places the quality of the gift within the instruction, so that the wealth given is to be lawfully acquired and honestly given. The closing clause supplies the assurance that accompanies the command, since nothing spent in the manner the sentence describes is lost with God.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the recipients of the community's giving, the order in which its obligations are arranged, and the knowledge of God that accompanies the act. The reader is given a rule about the destination of generosity, and the community's teachers used the verse when they taught about the claims that poverty and kinship place on a believer's property. The passage continues with the command about fighting, and the reader is carried from the disposal of wealth to the defence of the community.

The community's teachers used the sentence to rank the claims upon a person's property, and the ranking it gives has remained the framework of the law of maintenance. The verse also supplies the standard by which a gift is judged, since the Book asks for what is good rather than for whatever is at hand.
