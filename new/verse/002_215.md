## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:215

> They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate. Say, “Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers. Whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question and the Shape of the Answer**

The verse belongs to the sūrah's great series of question-verses — they ask you, and revelation answers — and the shape of this answer is instructive. The question concerned spending: what should be given, and to whom. The reply does not begin with amounts, percentages, or categories of wealth; it begins with people. The answer names a list — parents, kinsfolk, orphans, the indigent, the traveler — and only then, in its closing clause, returns the whole matter to God's knowledge. The structure teaches the priority of charity's legislation: before the arithmetic of giving comes the geography of need, and the giving is defined by its recipients rather than by the giver's convenience. The exegetes note the verse's period: it is said to have been revealed before the institution of the formal alms-tax, and thus to govern ordinary charitable giving — the voluntary infāq that precedes and surrounds the fixed zakāh. The list it gives is therefore the community's first curriculum of generosity, taught by naming the claimants one by one.

**The Standing Roster**

The five named recipients are not an ad hoc list but the Qur'an's standing roster of the people a righteous economy is measured by. The same names recur wherever the scripture legislates giving: the definition of righteousness counted kinsfolk, orphans, the poor, the traveler, beggars, and captives (2:177); the kindness-command of the sūrah of the Women lists parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, neighbors near and far, the companion, the wayfarer, and those your right hands possess (4:36); the war-booty legislation assigns its fifth to God and the Messenger, the near relatives, orphans, the poor, and the traveler (8:41); and the sūrah of the Exile repeats the same five with its economic principle — so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich (59:7). The recurrence is the point: the community is not given a different charity-list for each occasion but one list, installed across legislation, worship, and war alike, so that the same faces are seen in every distribution — the faces of the people the economy exists for.

**The Order of the List**

The list's sequence carries its own teaching. Parents stand first: charity begins at the root of the giver's own life, and the debt of nurture is the first claim on wealth — a priority the scripture reinforces everywhere, joining the worship of God to kindness to parents in a single breath. Kinsfolk follow: the circle of blood next, the relatives whose need the giver can see and whose claim the community's own fabric presses. Then orphans — the vulnerable whose protectors are dead, the class whose property this sūrah guards with its fiercest language. Then the indigent, the standing poor. Then the traveler — the stranger, the one cut off from his own support networks, whose claim arises from circumstance rather than relation. The movement is outward in widening circles: from the household, to the clan, to the unprotected, to the poor, to the passing stranger — and the widening is deliberate, so that generosity is trained in the order of natural attachment and then extended beyond it, until the unknown wayfarer stands inside the giver's obligation beside his own father.

**Whatever Good You Do, God Knows**

The closing clause returns the whole legislation to its unseen auditor: whatever good you do, God knows it. The same assurance stood inside the hajj-legislation — whatever good you do, God fully knows of it (2:197) — and in both places it does the same double work. It is a guarantee: the giving nobody sees, the relative supported quietly, the traveler relieved without witness — all of it is known, recorded, and answerable in reward; no act of charity is lost to the ledger for want of an audience. And it is a discipline: the knowledge of God audits the giving that *has* an audience, separating the charity performed for the eyes of people from the charity performed for the Face of God — the sūrah's recurring distinction, whose failure-mode it painted in the hypocrite who spends to be seen. The clause's placement after the list ties the two halves together: the recipients are named so the giving is directed rightly; the knower is named so the giving is motivated rightly. Direction and motive — the two coordinates of every act of charity — are fixed in one sentence.

**The Question-Verses as Classroom**

This verse is one of a dense cluster in which the community's actual questions are answered by revelation — the new moons and the doors of houses (2:189), the nearness of God to the caller (2:186), the sacred month's fighting (2:217), wine and gambling and the amount to spend (2:219), the orphans' affairs (2:220), menstruation (2:222). The cluster forms the sūrah's method of legislation: not a code delivered in the abstract but answers delivered to questions, the law growing at the points where the community's life actually presses against it. The question about spending — what should we give? — is the community asking to be directed in its daily economics, and the answer's form trains a habit: ask, and the revelation names the people. The method also honors the asking: the question is not rebuked as ignorance but answered as curriculum, and every generation inherits both the answers and the permission to bring its own cases to the text. The sūrah's legislation is thus dialogical — a community being formed through inquiry, with the roster of the needy installed at the center of the exchange.

**The Giver Between the List and the Knower**

Read whole, the verse places the giver between two presences: the visible claimants and the invisible Knower. The list makes the giving concrete — not "the poor" as abstraction but the father aging in the next room, the cousin fallen on hard years, the child whose guardian died, the beggar at the gate, the stranger whose caravan left without him. The knowledge-clause makes the giving pure — performed under an audit that sees the hand and the heart together. Between the two, charity becomes what the sūrah's whole economy intends: a transfer of wealth that is simultaneously an act of worship, directed to faces and offered to a Face. The verse that answers a simple question about spending thus carries the full moral architecture of Islamic giving — the priority of persons over amounts, the order of claims from kin outward, the surveillance that keeps the motive clean — and leaves the asker with a list to memorize and a Knower to remember. Whatever good you do, it ends; and the sentence is both the promise of reward and the standard the reward answers to.
