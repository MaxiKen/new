## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:2

> These are the verses of the clear Book.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Book Makes Its Meaning Available**

The demonstrative phrase points to what follows and to the revelation being recited as a whole. These are not private impressions or riddles reserved for a spiritual elite. They are āyāt, signs that point beyond themselves while remaining verbal statements that can be heard, remembered, examined, and acted upon. Calling the Book clear places responsibility on both sides of communication. The message has a form capable of being understood, and the hearer must not replace listening with a search for excuses.

Clear does not mean that every verse has the same level of detail or that every matter is easy for every person. It means that the Book discloses its direction. It identifies the Creator, separates worship from idolatry, describes the fate of denial, and shows the moral pattern of earlier peoples. The stories in this sūrah will not be decorative history. Their central warnings are accessible even when some details invite learned study. Clarity is therefore measured by the guidance a passage gives, not by the absence of every interpretive question.

**Mubīn Both Clarifies and Is Clear**

The Arabic adjective mubīn can describe something evident, and it can also describe something that makes another thing evident. Both senses fit this verse. The Qur'an is clear in its own wording and clarifying in its work. It brings hidden motives into view, distinguishes worship from imitation, and exposes the consequences that power tries to conceal. The same root appears in descriptions of manifest signs elsewhere in the Qur'an, including 12:1 and 43:2, so the word carries more than a compliment about literary style.

This double force changes how the Book should be read. A reader does not approach it only to collect information. The text also interprets life: Pharaoh's authority becomes a form of injustice, Abraham's questioning becomes a test of inherited worship, and a prophet's apparent defeat becomes part of a larger judgment. The Book makes things clear by giving them their true names and relations. It tells a person what he is seeing and what that sight requires of him.

**Signs Are More Than Sentences**

An āyah is a sign as well as a verse. That meaning prevents the chapter from being treated as a collection of isolated maxims. Each line belongs to an ordered revelation and points to a reality beyond its immediate wording. The sign may be a statement, an account of a prophet, a warning, or an observed feature of creation. In every case, the verse asks the audience to move from recognition to response.

The plural in this verse also matters. The Book's clarity is not confined to one proof. It will appear in the account of Moses, in Abraham's reasoning about idols, and in the repeated end of the prophetic narratives. Repetition will not be empty duplication; it will let one principle be tested in different settings. A clear Book can return to a truth without becoming confused, because each return exposes a different form of human resistance. The signs accumulate until denial is seen as a choice rather than a lack of evidence.

**Clarity Does Not Cancel Refusal**

A message can be clear and still be rejected. Rejection may arise not from an inability to understand words but from the cost of accepting what they demand. Pharaoh understands enough to threaten Moses. Abraham's people understand the question about their idols well enough to appeal to the practice of their ancestors. The coming stories will show that the obstacle is often loyalty to power, custom, or desire rather than an obscure argument.

This distinction guards against a shallow defense of faith. The believer need not claim that every doubt disappears at once, and the critic cannot claim that refusal proves the message was unintelligible. A clear sign can be resisted because it disturbs a settled life. The verse sets the standard by which the later disputes should be judged: examine whether the response answers the sign or merely avoids its consequence.

**The Chapter Opens With a Public Standard**

By naming the revelation a clear Book immediately after the mysterious opening letters, the chapter joins humility with confidence. Human beings may not possess the key to every feature of the text, yet they are not left without guidance. What must be known for repentance, worship, justice, and hope has been brought into speech. The unexplained opening does not weaken the plain message that follows; it places the reader in a proper posture before it.

That combination also answers the Prophet's distress in the verses that follow. His task is not to invent a stronger sign or to make revelation more dramatic. His task is to deliver a Book whose signs already clarify the truth. The response of his audience will reveal their character, but their response cannot erase the Book's public clarity.

**The Clear Book Is Meant to Be Carried Into Life**

Clarity reaches its goal when it changes a decision. A person may admire the Book's language, recall its accounts, and still leave its demands untouched. This verse gives no support to that separation. The signs clarify who deserves worship, how power should be judged, and why warning cannot be mocked. To call the revelation clear is to say that its path can be followed, not merely that its sentences can be admired.

The phrase also gives the Prophet a stable measure in a hostile setting. Public approval is changeable, and opponents can redefine success as popularity. The Book supplies another standard. Its truth does not rise when a crowd applauds or fall when a crowd refuses. The messenger's duty is to make the clear signs heard; the hearer's duty is to let their clarity reach conduct. Between those two duties lies the moral seriousness of the chapter.
