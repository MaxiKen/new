## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:164

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lot Denies a Financial Motive**

Lot has asked his people to fear Allah and obey him, a request that challenges a settled public practice. They could accuse him of seeking money, influence, or a position above the community. He answers that he asks no reward from them. His warning is not a transaction in which moral approval can be bought.

That independence matters because a paid advocate may be tempted to protect the interests of the person who funds him. Lot places his reward with God, so the community cannot purchase his silence or demand a softer version of the message. His concern for them is real, but it is not a strategy for personal enrichment.


Lot names God as the Lord of all worlds rather than as a tribal patron. The authority and recompense behind his mission extend beyond the people in front of him. Their approval is limited, their wealth is temporary, and their power cannot determine the value of faithful service.

This title also means that Lot's message is not an attempt to impose one family's private code on a rival group. He speaks for the Lord of every people. The community is being asked to answer to a universal Creator, not to submit to Lot because he wants to win a local dispute.

**A Messenger Can Be Compassionate Without Being Dependent**

Lot wants his people saved, and his later prayer shows how deeply their conduct troubles him. Yet love does not make him dependent on their acceptance. He can grieve their refusal without altering the command to gain their approval. His reward from God gives his compassion a stable foundation.

A leader who needs applause may confuse popularity with success. Lot's mission has another measure: faithful delivery of the truth. If the people turn back, he is grateful; if they resist, he remains responsible for warning them. The final assessment belongs to God, who knows both the messenger's motive and the hearer's response.


When a messenger asks no payment, the poor are not excluded and the wealthy cannot buy privilege. The same warning reaches the household with influence and the person with little social standing. Lot does not establish a religious market in which access to God's favor is exchanged for money.

This principle does not mean that every teacher or worker must refuse lawful support. Human services can be compensated. The messenger's declaration concerns the source of his authority and the purity of his mission. Acceptance of revelation is not a fee-based arrangement, and divine mercy is not a commodity under human control.

**The Message Cannot Be Bought Away**

A community might attempt to preserve its practice by offering Lot status, wealth, or a place within the ruling order. His statement closes that path. He cannot be bribed into silence because his true reward is not held by the people whose conduct he challenges.

This independence also exposes a common accusation. When a truthful critic refuses to flatter the powerful, the powerful may claim that he seeks influence. Lot's lack of demand makes that claim empty. The real dispute is over the authority of God's guidance, not over a benefit Lot wants to extract.


Noah, Hūd, and Ṣāliḥ made the same kind of declaration before calling their people to obedience. The repetition identifies a mark of prophetic speech: the messenger is trustworthy, independent of payment, and focused on the Lord of all worlds. Lot's mission belongs to that same line.

A community that rejects such a messenger cannot say it was confronted by a commercial scheme. The message was offered without a price, yet it still demanded a costly change in conduct. The cost was not money paid to Lot; it was pride surrendered to God.

**Reward With God Protects the Messenger's Heart**

A servant who expects the deepest reward from God can continue serving when the visible outcome is disappointing. He does not measure the worth of truth by the applause of the audience. Lot can speak with grief and firmness because his hope is not dependent on the people treating him well.

This protects against two opposite failures. The messenger does not become harsh merely because he is rejected, and he does not become silent merely because he wants to be liked. Dependence on God allows both courage and compassion to remain present.


Once Lot removes personal gain from the discussion, the people must face the warning rather than the messenger's supposed motive. They can accept God's authority or defend their practice, but they cannot fairly explain the call as a plan for Lot's enrichment.

For the reader, the verse offers a way to assess religious counsel. Ask whether the speaker directs worship to God, refuses to sell divine favor, and remains faithful when approval is withheld. Lot's reward is with the Lord of all worlds, so the community's decision cannot change the truth he was sent to convey.

The statement also protects a listener from judging guidance by the speaker's worldly circumstances. A messenger may have little wealth and no political office, yet his lack of worldly leverage can make his independence clearer. Truth is not measured by the size of the platform from which it is spoken.

Lot's refusal of payment leaves the people with a clean decision. They cannot say that he is selling them a private advantage. They must decide whether God's command is true, whether their practice can stand before Him, and whether they will exchange the safety of pride for the mercy of obedience. No payment can answer those questions for them. The messenger's lack of demand leaves the conscience face to face with revelation, without a bargain to hide behind.
