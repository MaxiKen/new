## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:2

> Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own. For this would indeed be a great sin.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Restoring What Was Never Yours**

The command is direct: give orphans their wealth. The Arabic *ātū al-yatāmā amwālahum* does not say hold it longer or invest it indefinitely; it says hand it over when the owner is ready. An orphan in Qur’anic usage is a child who has lost the father who would have guarded his property, and the community steps in as trustee, not as new owner. The verse treats that trust as real ownership by the child. The guardian is a keeper, and the wealth remains titled to the one who cannot yet defend it. That is why the same sūrah will later say *“Test the orphans until they reach a marriageable age”* (4:6) and only then return what is theirs. Between command and return lies a period of management, and this verse governs its ethics. The Qur’an elsewhere pairs justice to orphans with justice in measure and worship itself, as in *“Worship Allah and associate nothing with Him, and be good to parents, relatives, orphans”* (4:36), showing that how the strong handle the property of the weak is a measure of faith, not a footnote to it.

**Exchanging the Bad for the Good**

The prohibition *wa lā tatabaddalū al-khabīth bi al-ṭayyib* — do not exchange the worthless for the valuable — was explained by early exegetes in very concrete terms. A guardian might take a healthy animal from the orphan’s herd and replace it with a sick one from his own, or take sound coin and give back debased coin, keeping the difference. Others read it more broadly as swapping a bad practice for a good one: replacing the good trust of preservation with the bad act of consumption. Both readings point to the same habit of dressing up taking as trading. The Qur’an names that trick by calling one side *khabīth* — foul, worthless — and the other *ṭayyib* — good, wholesome. The labels stick to the act, not only the object. A transaction that looks equal on the surface is exposed by what each party actually receives. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse forbids even a supposedly balanced swap if the guardian’s portion remains mixed and self-preferential, because the power imbalance makes fair valuation impossible. The orphan cannot negotiate, so the law closes the door to negotiation entirely and commands simple restoration.

**Do Not Mix Their Wealth With Yours to Consume It**

The second prohibition *wa lā taʾkulū amwālahum ilā amwālikum* — nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own — addresses a subtler form of taking. Instead of an open exchange, the guardian merges accounts, spends freely from the combined sum, and tells himself the orphan’s share is still in there somewhere. The Qur’an calls that eating — *akl* — a word it uses elsewhere for illicit gain, as in *“Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally”* (4:29). Eating suggests both pleasure and disappearance; what is eaten does not return. The verse warns that mixing makes eating easy because boundaries blur. After this verse was revealed, some guardians became so scrupulous that they separated food, pots, and even cooking fires, fearing any mixing would count as sin. The hardship that followed was eased by a later clarification: *“If you intermingle with them, they are your brothers”* (2:220), which permits shared living with fairness, while this verse forbids shared living as a cover for consumption. The distinction is intention and accounting. Living together remains allowed; living off the orphan does not.

**Why the Warning Ends With a Great Sin**

The clause *innahu kāna ḥūban kabīrā* — for this would indeed be a great sin — uses a rare word, *ḥūb*, which the linguists connect with a grave crime that weighs heavily. By calling the act a great sin rather than merely a mistake, the verse places misappropriation of orphan wealth among major wrongs, alongside the other major sins the sūrah will later say to avoid to earn forgiveness for lesser ones (4:31). The occasion of revelation recalled by commentators sharpens the point: a boy whose parents had died was under his uncle’s care; when he grew and asked for his property, the uncle refused, and the matter was brought to the Prophet ﷺ. The verse came down to order return and to name refusal as more than stinginess. It is easy to treat a child’s property as a family buffer, especially when the child has no advocate. The verse answers that ease with a weighty noun. What feels like a small domestic adjustment is, in the ledger that matters, a heavy crime. That naming matters because guardians often reassure themselves that no one will notice; the Qur’an says the act is already noticed and already classified.

**Guardianship as a Test of Character**

The verse does more than regulate a transaction; it defines what guardianship means in this faith. A guardian is not rewarded with the orphan’s wealth for his trouble, except for the modest provision the next verses will allow to a poor guardian who is doing real work (4:6). The standard case is service without compensation, witnessed at return (4:6), and watched by God (4:1). That structure turns guardianship into a visible proof of *taqwā*, which is exactly how the sūrah opened. If humanity shares one origin and God is watchful over all, then the treatment of a child who cannot hire a lawyer becomes the clearest mirror of whether that awareness is real. The Qur’an elsewhere promises double reward for justice to the weak and severe penalty for exploiting them, as in *“Those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth consume fire into their bellies”* (4:10). This verse sets the rule; that later verse sets the consequence. Together they frame the guardianship period as a moral testing ground where the adult is the one being tested, not the child.

**A Principle Beyond Orphans**

Although the verse addresses orphans, its three prohibitions outline a broader ethic of entrusted wealth. Do not substitute the inferior for the superior, do not blur ownership to make use easier, and do not rename taking as keeping. Those moves appear in many other relationships where one party holds for another — partnerships, endowments, public treasuries, and care for those who lack legal capacity. The Qur’an’s choice to name the sin *kabīr* — great — signals that the issue is not the amount but the structure. Taking advantage of entrusted power corrupts the social fabric in a way that ordinary overspending does not. The verse therefore trains the reader to ask, whenever he holds what is not his, whose name remains on it while it is in his hand. If the answer is someone weaker, the command still stands: give it, keep it separate, and keep it whole. The next verse will extend that concern from property to marriage, showing that the same vulnerability can be exploited in a different contract, and that the same vigilance is required there.
