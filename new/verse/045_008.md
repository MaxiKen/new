## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:8

> They hear Allah’s revelations recited to them, then persist ˹in denial˺ arrogantly as if they did not hear them. So give them good news of a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hearing That Leaves No Trace**

The verse describes a physical act with no inner result. The man hears the revelations being recited — the words reach him, in his language, from a mouth he can see — and then carries on as though nothing had been said. The Qur'an returns to this picture often, and it gives it in almost the same language in another chapter: “Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they turn away in arrogance as if they did not hear them, as if there is deafness in their ears. So give them good news ˹O Prophet˺ of a painful punishment.” (31:7). The comparison in that verse is to a closed ear, and the earlier reference to those covered and deafened states the same condition (6:25). The Book is not describing a defect in the ear but a decision about the message: the sound was admitted, the claim was understood, and the will refused it. What the verse calls arrogance is that refusal after comprehension.

**Arrogance Comes Before Disbelief**

The Arabic adds a verbal form that means to swell or to hold oneself above, and the Qur'an uses it wherever status is the real subject. What stops the men of the Quraysh is not a missing argument; it is the demand that they stand level with the man reciting to them, worship as he worships, and admit that their ancestors were wrong. Pride resists that cost, and the resistance expresses itself as a claim about the text. This order — arrogance first, argument second — is the Qur'an's constant analysis of rejection, and it is why the Book so often addresses the will behind the reasoning rather than the reasoning itself. A person who refuses to be a listener has protected himself from having to answer. The verse's detail that he behaves "as if he did not hear" is exact psychology: the pose is assumed, and the pose is the point.

**"Good News" as a Deliberate Irony**

"Give them good news" is a command built on a word that normally belongs to the messengers of mercy and the announcement of paradise. Here it is attached to a painful punishment, and the effect is sarcastic without being playful: the sentence hands the man the opposite of what he thought he had earned. The Qur'an uses the same inversion elsewhere, as when those who kill the prophets are told they have glad tidings of a painful punishment (3:21), and when the mockers are given the identical sentence with the identical wording (84:24). The device does more than sting. It exposes the denier's own logic: he treats warnings as entertainment, treats reciters as performers, and treats the Hereafter as a tale. So the announcement is delivered in his own register — news, provided with a certainty he had denied, from a source he cannot sue.

**The Report Behind the Verse**

The verse is reported to have been revealed concerning Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, the same opponent whose imported tales appear in the previous verse's background. The report says he would sit within hearing of the recitation and then amuse himself by treating it with contempt, unable or unwilling to respond to what it actually said. Exegetes take the verse's address as general, whatever the occasion, and the wording supports them: nothing in the sentence is tied to a single man, no incident is named, and the behaviour it describes is available to any reader. A revelation that listed one offender and moved on would be a chronicle. This verse gives the type and its outcome, and then leaves the reader to notice how easily the type is reproduced in a different century, by people whose reasons for refusing have changed and whose behaviour has not.

**The Punishment Fitted to the Offence**

The phrase translated "painful punishment" joins a word for torment to a word for pain, and Arabic uses that pair for the kind of suffering that is meant to be felt by the one who is punished. The sūrah will return to the subject of mockery three more times, and its final statement on the matter will say that the evil consequences of what people did will unfold before them and that what they used to ridicule will close in on them. That is the shape of the sentence announced here: the very thing the man treated as a joke will be handed to him as a fact. The recitation was a serious claim and he answered it with amusement; the answer he will receive is the claim, still standing, addressed to him alone. Nothing about the punishment is arbitrary, since its form is set by the offence itself.

**The Command Is Given to the Messenger**

The verse opens with description and closes with an imperative, and the imperative is addressed to the Prophet, not to the denier. He is told to deliver the announcement and to leave the response where it belongs. That division of labour runs through the sūrah: the messenger's task is the recitation, the warning, and the refusal to soften the words for the sake of an audience that has already decided. The denier's task, if he can be said to have one, is the reckoning. Any reader tempted to soften the message in order to keep his hearers comfortable is being told here that the comfort of the hearer was never part of the assignment, and that the argument does not have to be won by the reciter in his own lifetime. The words are delivered; the judgment is not his.
