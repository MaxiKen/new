## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:53

> **Whatever blessings you have are from Allah. Then whenever hardship touches you, to Him ˹alone˺ you cry ˹for help˺.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**All Blessings From Allah**

Wa-mā bikum min niʿmatin fa-min Allāhi — and whatever blessings you have are from Allah. Mā bikum, whatever is with you, whatever you have. Min niʿmatin, of blessing, favor. Fa-min Allāhi, then it is from Allah. The particle mā is general, includes all blessings: faith, health, family, provision, cattle, rain, crops, sea, mountains, guidance. Min niʿmatin, of blessing, indicates any blessing, even small. Fa-min Allāhi, then from Allah, with fa- for consequence, then, so. Al-Ṭabarī explains that all blessings you have, whether you recognize or not, are from Allah, not from idols, not from self, not from chance. This is summary of blessings catalogue in verses 5-18: all those blessings are from Allah. The verse states it explicitly to remove any misattribution. Al-Rāzī notes that min niʿmatin is indefinite, indicating even one blessing is from Allah, let alone many, and that fa-min Allāhi with fa- emphasizes that whenever you consider a blessing, you will find it is from Allah.

**Hardship Makes You Cry To Him Alone**

Thumma idhā massakum al-ḍurru fa-ilayhi taj'arūn — then when hardship touches you, to Him alone you cry for help. Thumma, then, after blessings. Idhā massakum al-ḍurru, when hardship, harm, distress touches you, from m-s-s, to touch, and ḍ-r-r, harm, hardship. Fa-ilayhi taj'arūn, then to Him you cry loudly, from j-ʾ-r, to cry out, to moan, to supplicate loudly in distress. Al-Ṭabarī says when hardship like illness, poverty, drought, fear touches you, you cry to Allah alone for help, forgetting idols, because you know deep down that only Allah can remove hardship. This is natural disposition, fiṭrah, that emerges in distress. Al-Qurṭubī notes that even polytheists, when in distress at sea, cry to Allah alone, as 17:67 says *When harm touches you at sea, those you call upon besides Him vanish, except Him* (17:67). The verse describes human reality: in ease, you may attribute blessings to others, but in hardship, you return to Allah alone.

**Why Blessings And Hardship Are Paired**

Blessings and hardship are paired to show that both should lead to Allah alone. Blessings are from Allah, so gratitude should be to Him alone. Hardship removal is by Allah alone, so supplication should be to Him alone. In both ease and hardship, Allah is source. The verse says whatever blessings you have are from Allah, then when hardship touches you, to Him you cry. The sequence is blessings then hardship then crying to Him, showing that both states point to Him. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that fa-ilayhi taj'arūn, to Him you cry, with ilayhi, to Him, indicates exclusive direction: to Him alone, not to idols. The brackets ˹alone˺ and ˹for help˺ in translation clarify exclusivity and purpose. The verse therefore teaches tawḥīd in both gratitude and supplication.

**The Inconsistency That Follows**

Next verse will say then as soon as He removes hardship, a group associates others with Lord. The present verse sets up that inconsistency: you know blessings from Allah, you cry to Him alone in hardship, but after hardship removed, you associate others. This is inconsistent and ungrateful. The sūrah's theme of gratitude vs ingratitude appears here: to attribute blessings to other than Allah and to cry to Him alone only in hardship then associate after is ingratitude, kufr, covering favor. Al-Rāzī says this is common human pattern: in ease, heedless; in hardship, supplicating; after hardship removed, heedless again. The verse exposes pattern to make reader aware and change.

**A Verse That Summarizes Sūrat al-Niʿam**

This verse summarizes Sūrat al-Niʿam's core message: all blessings from Allah, so be grateful to Him alone; hardship removal from Allah, so cry to Him alone. It is often quoted as proof that all blessings are from Allah, and as reminder to be grateful. Al-Qurṭubī says it is also proof against those who attribute blessings to stars, idols, or self, as some Arabs said we were given rain by such-and-such star, or attributed provision to own effort. The verse says fa-min Allāhi, from Allah, to correct all misattribution. The phrase wa-mā bikum min niʿmatin, whatever blessings you have, is comprehensive, leaving no blessing outside Allah's giving.

**Linguistic Note On Niʿmah And Ḍurr And Ja'r**

Niʿmah from n-ʿ-m means blessing, favor, from same root as naʿīm, bliss. Mā bikum min niʿmatin, whatever blessing is with you. Fa-min Allāhi, then from Allah. Ḍurr from ḍ-r-r means harm, hardship, distress, from same root as ḍarar, harm. Massakum from m-s-s, to touch, indicating hardship touches, reaches. Taj'arūn from j-ʾ-r means to cry loudly, to moan, to supplicate in distress, from same root as ju'ār, loud crying. The verb taj'arūn indicates loud, desperate crying, not quiet supplication, showing intensity of hardship. The verse's structure — whatever blessings you have are from Allah, then when hardship touches you, to Him you cry — with two clauses, blessings and hardship, both leading to Allah, creates concise summary of tawḥīd in both ease and hardship, inviting gratitude in ease and supplication in hardship, both directed to Allah alone.

**How This Verse Teaches Gratitude**

Gratitude, shukr, requires recognizing that blessings are from Allah, as this verse says. If you think blessings from self or idols, you will not be grateful to Allah. The verse says wa-mā bikum min niʿmatin fa-min Allāhi, whatever blessings you have are from Allah, to teach recognition. Then it says in hardship you cry to Him alone, showing that deep down you know He is source of relief. The lesson is to make conscious what is already known in hardship: that Allah is source of blessings and relief, so gratitude and supplication should be to Him alone always, not only in hardship. The believer who lives by this verse will be grateful in ease and patient in hardship, both with Allah alone as focus.
