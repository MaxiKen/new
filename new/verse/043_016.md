## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:16

> **Has He taken ˹angels as His˺ daughters from what He created, and favoured you ˹O pagans˺ with sons?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Names the Portion**

The previous verse said the idolaters made a part of God's servants into a part of Him. This verse says what that part was: daughters. The question opens with am, "or", which in Arabic can introduce a rhetorical question of disbelief and rebuke, as if to say: is it really so that He took daughters? The verb is ittakhadha, to take or adopt for oneself, and the object is banāt, daughters, drawn mimmā yakhluqu, from what He creates. The phrasing is already an argument. If the daughters are taken from what He creates, they are created, and a created thing cannot be a portion of the Creator. The verse does not need to say this; the words say it. The second half of the question completes the picture. He took daughters and favoured you, aṣfākum, with sons. The verb aṣfā means to choose the pure or best part for someone, to single them out with something choice. The idolaters had arranged the family of heaven so that God received what they considered the lesser and they received what they considered the better. The question asks them to hear how that sounds.

**An Argument from Their Own Scale, Not God's**

The verse turns on a preference the Makkans held and the Qurʾān did not: that sons are better than daughters. Nothing here endorses that preference. The exegetes are clear that the argument is ad hominem in the proper sense, an argument from the opponent's own premises. The Quraysh despised the birth of daughters, as the next verse will describe and as 16:58–59 records in detail: the father's face darkens, he hides from his people in shame, and he debates whether to keep the child in humiliation or bury her in the dust. These were the same people who said the angels were God's daughters. The verse holds the two facts side by side. If daughters are, on your own reckoning, the inferior share, then you have assigned to God what you would be ashamed to have yourselves. The inconsistency is the point, not the ranking. The Qurʾān elsewhere states that both sexes are from a single soul (4:1) and that God gives daughters to whom He wills and sons to whom He wills (42:49), placing daughters first in that verse. The rebuke here is that the idolaters measured God by a scale they themselves used, and still gave Him the short end of it.

**The Same Argument Across the Makkan Sūrahs**

This verse belongs to a group of passages that press the identical point in nearly the same words. In 16:57 they assign daughters to God, glory be to Him, and to themselves what they desire. In 37:149–153 the Prophet ﷺ is told to ask them whether their Lord has daughters while they have sons, and whether God chose daughters over sons. In 52:39 the question is put as: does He have daughters while you have sons? In 53:21–22 it is called a division most unfair, qismatun ḍīzā. The repetition shows how central the belief was to Makkan religion and how seriously the Qurʾān took the task of dismantling it. The angels were worshipped in Makkah as intermediaries, and the three goddesses al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt were understood by some as angels or as daughters of God. The claim was not a fringe idea but the working theology of the Kaʿbah's custodians. Each of these verses attacks it from the same angle, because the angle was the one that would embarrass the Quraysh most: not that daughters are unworthy of God, but that the Quraysh thought so and assigned them anyway.

**Offspring as a Category Error**

Behind the specific charge lies the general one. The Qurʾān rejects the attribution of any child to God, male or female, human or angelic, in a long series of verses: 2:116, 6:100, 9:30, 10:68, 17:111, 18:4, 19:35, 21:26, 25:2, 39:4, and 72:3, among others, and this sūrah returns to it in verses 81 and 82. The objection is the one verse 12 prepared. A child is a portion of its parent, of the same kind, produced by pairing. God has no pair, no kind, and no parts. To say He took daughters is to place Him in the world of creatures who beget and are begotten. The phrase "from what He creates" in this verse makes the error explicit. The angels are among what He creates, and the Quraysh knew this. They did not claim the angels were uncreated. So they were claiming that God took created beings and made them His children, which is either meaningless, since a child is not adopted from one's own creation, or blasphemous, since it makes the Creator dependent on His creation for a family. Either way the claim collapses under the verse's own wording.

**What the Question Exposes**

The verse's method is to ask rather than to assert, and the method suits the subject. A direct statement, "God has no daughters", would have been true but easy to ignore. A question forces the listener to answer, and the only honest answer available to a Makkan was the one that condemned him. Did God take daughters? He would say yes. Did God favour you with sons? He would say yes, and be proud of it. Then he would have to hear what he had said: that he had given the Lord of the heavens a share he would not accept for himself. The exegetes describe this as showing the emptiness of the idolaters' position and the degree to which they belittled God's rights. The verse does not raise its voice. It lays out the two halves of the Makkans' belief in a single sentence and lets the sentence shame them. The next verse describes the face of a man told he has a daughter, and the description finishes the argument this question began.
