## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:206

> When it is said to them, “Fear Allah,” pride carries them off to sin. Hell will be their proper place. What an evil place to rest!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Sentence Every Counsel Needs**

The verse tests the portrait with the shortest possible intervention: when it is said to him, fear God. The counsel is the tradition's whole prophetic vocabulary in two words — the taqwā-command that every messenger brought his people, that the sūrah has legislated as the end of retaliation, bequest, fasting, and pilgrimage alike (2:179, 180, 183, 196–197). It is what a friend says to the eloquent impostor, what an elder says to the corrupter of the land, what the community says to its own admired speaker when the interval's report comes in. The verse's staging is precise: the admonition arrives, and what follows is the man's reaction to being told to fear God. Not to a refutation of his arguments, not to an exposure of his oath, but to the two-word counsel that names the only thing his whole performance lacks. The hypocrite's portrait is thereby completed by its response to correction — the surface was drawn in 204, the industry in 205, and here the interior's reflex, the movement of the heart when the heart is addressed directly.

**Pride That Seizes Him Into Sin**

The reaction is the verse's diagnosis: ʿizzah — pride, vainglory, the hot self-assertion — seizes him, and seizes him *into* sin. The grammar is violent in both directions: the pride is not his decision but his captor, the force that takes him; and its destination is not merely accompanied by sin but arrives at it, the carrying ending in the ithm. Al-Qurṭubī's lexicon of the word helps: ʿizzah in its rightful place is the honor that belongs to God, His Messenger, and the believers — the strength that comes from being right with the Divine; in this man it has become its counterfeit, the glory taken in the self's own standing, the prestige of the impressive speech and the invoked oath. The counsel to fear God strikes that counterfeit at its root — to fear God is to hold one's standing as borrowed — and the counterfeit defends itself by seizure: the admonished man does not consider the counsel but is carried off by his pride into the sin of rejection. The psychology is the tradition's standing account of how hypocrisy hardens: not through argument unanswerable but through glory unresigned, the ʿizzah that will not be a creature, the speaker who cannot afford to be corrected in front of an audience that admired him.

**Hell Suffices Him**

The verdict that follows uses the Qur'an's idiom of sufficiency: Hell suffices him — ḥasbuhu jahannam. The concept of sufficiency runs through the scripture in both registers, as the commentators note: God suffices the believer as guardian and helper, and in the negative register the punishment suffices the punished — it serves the purpose, it is enough, nothing need be added. The clause's economy is exact against the man's own economy: he who took so much — the admiration, the oath's credibility, the land's harvest destroyed — is paid in one sufficient thing. The sufficiency also answers his sufficiency-claim implicitly: the man whose speech said God knows what is in my heart claimed a divine endorsement; the verse returns the divine relation as the single provision of the Fire, enough for him as his portion, needing no supplement. The commentators cross-reference the formula's positive twin — if you turn away, the guidance has been delivered, and God suffices — to mark the symmetry: sufficiency is what the soul receives when it has chosen its one thing, and this man chose the self's glory, so the Fire is sufficient as its consequence.

**What an Evil Resting Place**

The verse's last clause inverts the imagery of rest: what an evil cradle, an evil place to recline. The word mihād carries the bed-sense — the cradle, the made-up couch, the place where a person settles as into their own element — and the Qur'an uses it elsewhere for Hell's furnishing, the bed of the arrogant. The inversion bites against the whole portrait: the man who impressed you with his talk of this world's life was arranging his rest in the world — the estates the Anṣār were tempted back to (2:195), the position his eloquence maintained, the comfort his corruption protected — and the verse names the resting place his choices actually furnished. Every act of the diptych's dark panel built this bed: the speech that marketed the self, the striving that rotted the land, the pride that refused the counsel — and the made bed is Hell, and the verdict on it is not merely punitive but aesthetic: what an evil resting place, spoken with the shudder of a person who has seen where the arrangement ends. The man who could not bear the two words fear God is given the sufficient and evil substitute, the couch of his own ʿizzah.

**The Circuit Completed: Speech, Act, Reaction**

The three verses now read as one closed circuit of hypocrisy. 2:204 gave the surface: the eloquent speech about worldly affairs, the oath calling God to witness the heart, the enmity beneath the impression. 2:205 gave the work: the turning away, the striving corruption, the tillage and offspring destroyed. 2:206 gives the reflex: the counsel to fear God, the pride's seizure, the sufficient Fire. Nothing is missing from the anatomy — what he says, what he does, and what he becomes when corrected — and the circuit's third stage explains why the first two are fatal rather than merely bad. A man who spoke well and corrupted badly might still be reached; the counsel to fear God is the reaching, and the verse reports it fails, not for want of clarity but by pride's reflex. The failure of correction is hypocrisy's defining difference from ordinary sin: the sinner who can still hear fear God retains the road back — the repentance-legislation the sūrah has spread through its length stands open — but the man seized by ʿizzah into sin has closed the road from inside, and the sufficiency of Hell is the verdict on a self that made itself unaddressable.

**The Portrait Against the Pilgrims**

The diptych's dark panel is now complete, and its frame is the hajj it follows. The pilgrims of 2:196–203 spent weeks in the state where the interior is legislated — the intention audited by God alone, the tongue fenced from rafath and jidāl, the boast of the fathers transferred to the remembrance of God, the petition taught to span both worlds. The man of 2:204–206 is the inversion at every point: his tongue perfected for the world's affairs, his oath profaning the divine witness, his boast reasserted in the pride that refuses counsel, his petition truncated to the world whose harvest he destroys. The passage has thus built its two citizens — the one who leaves the valley remembering God in numbered days and knowing he will be gathered to Him, and the one who leaves the encounter striving corruption in the land and being carried by glory into a sufficient Fire. The verse that follows will draw the second citizen of the diptych's bright panel — the one who sells his soul for God's pleasure — and between the sold soul and the sufficient Hell the sūrah will have completed its most compact moral map: the heart that spends itself and the heart that markets itself, the ʿizzah that seizes and the raʾfah that receives.
