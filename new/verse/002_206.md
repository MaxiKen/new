## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:206

> When it is said to them, “Fear Allah,” pride carries them off to sin. Hell will be their proper place. What an evil place to rest!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reaction Described**

The verse states that when the person is told to fear God, pride carries him off to sin, and it then names the Fire as his sufficiency. The sentence completes the portrait the passage has been drawing, and it describes the response to counsel.

The Book's account has moved from speech to conduct and now to the reaction that the conduct produces when it is questioned. What the reader receives is the picture of a person whose position hardens when it is challenged, and the sentence shows what the Book regards as the root of the refusal.

**The Counsel and the Response**

The sentence describes the advice given in a few words, and the response to it is the whole of the offence. The community's teachers noted that the counsel itself is mild and that the person's reaction is stated in a single phrase.

The Book's practice of describing an encounter between a call and a response appears throughout the sūrah, and the reader is shown the choices in their plainest form. What the sentence establishes is that the person has been given what he needs in order to amend his course, and the response is therefore his own.

The community's teachers noted that the counsel is stated in a few words and that the response is described in a single phrase, and they drew from the pairing a lesson about how a person's character is revealed by what he does when he is corrected. The Book's account of the exchange is brief and complete, and the reader is shown that the person has been given what he needs in order to amend his course. The sentence therefore places the responsibility for the refusal on the one who refuses.

**The Meaning of Pride**

The commentators explained the term as the attitude that prevents a person from accepting a correction, and they connected it with the reports in which the Prophet ﷺ described arrogance as the rejection of the truth and the contempt of others. The sentence places the attitude at the centre of the conduct the passage describes.

The Book's treatment of pride elsewhere includes the account of Satan's refusal to obey the command to prostrate, and the community's scholars connected the two passages. What the reader receives is the account of a disposition that the Book treats as the origin of a range of offences, and the sentence shows that the counsel in this verse was met by the attitude that the reports describe.

The community's reports describe arrogance as the rejection of the truth and the contempt of other people, and the sentence places that attitude at the centre of the conduct the passage describes. The Book's account of Satan's refusal to obey the command illustrates the same disposition, and the community's teachers connected the two passages when they spoke about the origin of disobedience. The reader is shown that the counsel in this verse met a resistance that had its source in the person's estimate of himself.

**"Hell Suffices Him"**

The sentence then states that the Fire is his sufficiency, and the commentators explained the term as the thing that is enough for a person whether or not he desires it. The Book uses the same vocabulary both for what a person has earned and for the punishment that awaits him.

The wording states the outcome without further discussion, and the community's teachers read the sentence as the Book's own judgment on the conduct described. The reader carries away the statement that the person reaches what his conduct has prepared for him, and the sentence leaves the matter at that point.

**"What an Evil Resting Place"**

The sentence closes with an exclamation about the resting place, and the Book uses exclamations of this kind where it describes the destination of the Fire. The phrase adds the reader's own response to the statement that preceded it.

The community's scholars noted that the Book's exclamations invite the reader to participate in the judgment, and the practice gives the passage a rhetorical force that the statement alone would not have. The sentence leaves him with the instruction to consider the destination that the sentence has named, and the passage continues with the portrait of the opposite character.

**The Contrast Prepared**

The sentence prepares the reader for the verse that follows, in which the Book describes those who give their lives for the pleasure of God. The two portraits are placed side by side, and the community's teachers read the passage as an account of the two ways in which a person deals with the counsel he receives.

The passage supplies him with the account of a refusal and, in the following sentence, of a surrender, and the passage's arrangement leaves him to see the difference for himself. The sentence's closing words complete the first portrait, and the reader is carried to the second with the contrast still before him.
