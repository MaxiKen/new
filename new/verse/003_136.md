## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:136

> Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens under which rivers flow, staying there forever. How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward Stated as a Pair**

The verse states the reward of the people described in the two verses before it, and it names two things: forgiveness from their Lord, and gardens with rivers running beneath them. The pairing matches the two orders of the passage that has just ended.

The sūrah has described the people by their conduct toward God, toward their own tempers, and toward those who wronged them. The reward now answers each side of that description. Forgiveness addresses what they did wrong, and the gardens address what they did well, and the two together make up the full settlement the Book promises to a community whose record includes both.

**The Gardens and the Rivers Beneath Them**

The phrase about rivers running below the gardens is one of the Book's most frequent descriptions of the Hereafter, and the earlier sūrahs use it in the same words. The image gathers what a garden needs in the lands from which the first audience heard the message: water that never fails.

The Book uses the picture to describe a place of continuous provision rather than an estate with fixed borders. Elsewhere the description is expanded with details of greeting and speech, as in the passage where the people of the garden are welcomed with peace and their closing words are praise of God, the Lord of all worlds (10:10). The rivers are the opening line of a description that the Book extends in many places.

**Abiding Therein**

The statement that they remain there is part of every extended description of the garden in the Book. It answers the fear that has shadowed human hopes about good things: that they end.

The Book contrasts the permanence of the next life with the passing character of this one in several places, and it uses the contrast to order its instruction about what deserves effort. A reward that could be lost would not settle the account of people who gave up what they had for it. The sentence in this verse closes that question, and the description of the garden that appears when the believers are admitted states that they stay there by the will of their Lord (14:23).

**The Word for the Workers**

The verse closes with an exclamation about the reward of the workers, and the noun describes people who do something rather than people who hold a position. The commentators noticed the choice, and some of them used the word as evidence that the reward is not only a matter of God's grace but also involves the actions people perform.

The verse before the reader supports both sides of that discussion. The people described in the previous verses act, and the reward they are given is described as forgiveness, which no action earns as a wage. The Book keeps both ideas in view, and later statements in the tradition do the same by insisting that conduct matters and that the outcome remains an act of mercy.

**The Exclamation That Closes the Verse**

The sentence that ends the verse is not a statement of fact about the gardens but a word of praise spoken about the reward itself. Its structure invites agreement rather than supplying information.

The same form appears elsewhere in the Book at the point where the reward is named, as in the declaration of the righteous in the Hereafter that God has fulfilled what He promised them and that excellent indeed is the reward of those who work (39:74). The Book puts this kind of sentence at the end of a description to leave the listener with the value of what has been described, and this verse does so in a single clause.

**Forgiveness Named Before the Gardens**

The order of the reward follows the order of the previous verse. The verse before this one ended with the request for forgiveness and the refusal to persist in sin, and the reward therefore begins where the repentance ended.

The arrangement makes the mechanics clear in the way the Book usually presents them. The garden is not entered by an unbroken record but by people whose faults have been forgiven, and the Book's description of the believers in this passage never claims that they are without faults. It claims that they do something about them, and the reward in this verse is the consequence of that turn.

**What the Two Collections Say About Deeds and Mercy**

The tradition records a statement that no one enters the garden by his deeds alone, that the most beloved conduct is what is done consistently even if it is little, and that the Prophet himself answered in the same terms when he was asked whether the rule applied to him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6464, Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2818).

Read with this verse, the report does not cancel the description of the workers; it explains the order in which the two things stand. The deeds are the road, and the mercy is what meets the traveler at the end of it. The sūrah's own sentence gives forgiveness first in the list, which is where the tradition's statement places its weight.

**The Description Completed by Its Reward**

The passage that began with a warning about the Fire and an instruction to hasten toward forgiveness ends with a portrait of the people who receive what they seek. The unit is complete: the command, the conduct, and the outcome.

The arrangement is one of the Book's recurring patterns, and the sūrah uses it again after further instructions. What makes this instance notable is the placement of the description of wrongdoing inside the portrait of the righteous. The Book's ideal community is not presented as a company of people who never fail but as a company of people who turn back when they do, and the reward stated here belongs to that company.
