## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:118

> So eat only of what is slaughtered in Allah’s Name if you truly believe in His revelations.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command About Food**

So eat only of what is slaughtered in Allah's Name if you truly believe in His revelations. The verse states a dietary rule in the form of an instruction, and it attaches the instruction to belief in the revelation. The rule concerns meat in particular, since it is over the animal that the Name is invoked at slaughter, and the Book's commentators understood the command to forbid the eating of carrion, which had not been slaughtered with the invocation at all.

The Makkan audience lived with a set of inherited customs about which animals could be eaten and which could not, and the sūrah has been dismantling those customs and replacing them with rules that rest on the Name of God. The verse before this one described the majority as followers of assumption, and the dietary command is the practical form of the difference between inherited practice and revelation.

**What the Rule Requires**

The rule requires that the Name of God be invoked over the animal, and the invocation is what marks the meat as food for the community that believes. The sūrah of the Table sets the same requirement inside a wider list of restrictions, forbidding the flesh of animals that died of themselves, blood, swine, and what has been dedicated to other than God, with an exception for the person compelled by hunger. The sūrah of the Bee gives the same instruction to eat of what God has provided and to be grateful, and it repeats the prohibition and the exception.

The commentators observed that the rule concerns the act of slaughtering rather than the kind of animal alone, since the same species becomes permitted or forbidden according to what is said over it. The Book's concern throughout this passage has been with the authority behind the rules, and the invocation of the Name is what places the meal inside that authority.

**The Condition Attached to the Command**

If you truly believe in His revelations. The verse makes the dietary command a test of belief, and the Book attaches similar conditions to other commands. The sūrah of the Cow tells the believers to eat of the good things provided and to give thanks, and it makes the instruction part of the covenant that the community has accepted. What the condition in this verse states is that the rejection of the rule is not a matter of taste but of the standing of the revelation that carries it.

The audience had argued about food on the basis of ancestral custom, and the Book's answer is that the rule is bound to belief in what has been sent down. The sūrah has already stated that the people who follow assumption do nothing but lie (6:116), and this verse makes the practical consequence of that statement plain for the table.

**The Argument in the Audience's Practice**

The sūrah's later verses describe the audience's own rules about livestock in detail: the animals they set apart, the ones they forbade to certain people, and the shares they assigned to God and to their idols (6:136–139; 6:143–144). The command in front of us stands at the head of that whole section, and it states the principle that the section applies. What the audience did with their herds and flocks was governed by claims about what God had permitted and forbidden, and those claims were inherited rather than revealed.

The Book's account of the audience's system names some of its details: the animals whose backs were declared off limits, the livestock that no one was to eat, and the shares assigned to God and to the idols (6:136; 6:139). The sūrah sets those practices beside the command to eat of what the Name of God has been mentioned over, and the contrast between the two bodies of rules is the argument the section is making.

The Book's treatment of those claims follows the pattern the sūrah has established. It states what God has actually forbidden, it exposes the invention that has grown up around it, and it leaves the responsibility for the invention with the people who made it. The verse in front of us supplies the positive rule for food, and the passages that follow describe the inventions the rule replaced.

**What the Verse Says About Gratitude and Law**

The instruction to eat of what the Name has been mentioned over connects the meal to the acknowledgement of the One who provided it. The Book's dietary rules are not concerned with purity in the abstract; they concern the recognition of God's authority over what a person consumes, and the invocation at slaughter is the moment at which that recognition is expressed. The sūrah of the Bee states the same connection when it tells the believers to eat of what God has provided and to be grateful for His favours (16:114).

The Book's language about food elsewhere attaches the act of eating to worship, and the verse in this sūrah belongs to that usage. The command is addressed to a community forming around the revelation, and the rule tells it how the most ordinary act of the day is related to its confession.

**The Verses That Continue the Section**

The verse that follows asks why the believers should not eat of what has been slaughtered in God's Name when God has already explained what He has forbidden, with the exception of what compulsion requires (6:119). The two verses state the rule and then answer the objection to it, and the answer includes the observation that many people mislead others by their desires out of ignorance.

The sūrah therefore treats the dietary question as one instance of the argument it has been conducting throughout the chapter. The subject is food, and the question is the same one the sūrah asked about the signs, the idols, and the demand for miracles: whether the community's practice will rest on the revelation or on the inherited assumptions of the majority.
