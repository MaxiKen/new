## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:66

> So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nakāl: The Punishment Designed to Be Seen**

The verse's central word is *nakāl* — rendered here *an example*, and carrying in its root the whole logic of exemplary punishment. *N-k-l* names a penalty imposed not only on its bearer but on its audience: the punishment that makes an example, that others look at and are restrained by — the same root as the ankle-shackle, the restraint worn by the one who must not move. A punishment as nakāl is therefore doubled in its economy. It pays the debt of the punished — the sentence deserved, the verdict executed — and it works on everyone who ever hears of it, converting a completed event into a standing deterrent. The Qur'an uses the word sparingly and always at its heaviest scenes: Pharaoh's punishment is nakāl for the hereafter and the first life, the double-term severity of a tyrant made into permanent exhibit. The Sabbath-town's fate receives the same instrument. The transformed people are gone — no progeny, the line ended — and what remains of them is exactly what the verse says: a case. The event has been converted from history into equipment, and the conversion is divine action: *We made it* — the nakāl is not what audiences happen to do with the story but what Allah filed the story as.

**“To What Is Before It and Behind It”**

The nakāl's range is stated in a spatial-temporal formula the exegetes read three ways: *li-mā bayna yadayhā wa-mā khalfahā* — for what is between its hands and what is behind it. The dominant reading is temporal: for the generations present at the event and the generations after — the punishment's deterrence running forward through time, every later community a beneficiary of the town's sentence. The second reading keeps the idiom spatial — *between the hands* meaning ahead, in front: for the surrounding settlements of that region and those behind them, the deterrent working geographically across the neighborhood that watched the fish-traps fill. The third reads the referent as sins rather than people: an expiation and restraint for the offenses before it and after it — the punishment settling the town's earlier trespasses and foreclosing later ones in the same stroke. The readings are compatible rather than competing, and their compatibility is the formula's point: the nakāl's jurisdiction is total in every dimension — the town's own past, its region's present, posterity's future. The temporal reading carries the verse's main weight, because verse 66 exists to explain why the story is being recited in Madina centuries later: the audience of the recital *is* the *mā khalfahā*, the behind-it, receiving the sentence as it was designed to be received — at a distance, through speech, as restraint.

**Mawʿiẓah: The Admonition and Its Addressees**

The second filing of the punishment is *mawʿiẓah* — admonition, exhortation, lesson — and its addressees are named: *li-l-muttaqīn*, for the God-fearing. The word carries both registers the classical lexicons record: counsel that draws the hearer toward good, and reprimand that pushes from evil — the single term covering the lesson's two faces, because a warning is simultaneously an invitation. The addressee-naming is the verse's most deliberate stroke. A punishment made nakāl addresses everyone through fear; the same punishment made mawʿiẓah addresses the muttaqīn through recognition. The distinction is the sūrah's own opening distinction — the Book as guidance *for the God-conscious* (2:2), a benefit that lands only where the landing-conditions exist. A lesson requires a faculty to receive it, and taqwā is that faculty: the settled carefulness about Allah's bounds that lets a person hear an old sentence as addressed to himself. Without it the same story is anecdote — entertainment at the ancestors' expense, or tribal polemic, or nothing. The verse thus splits its own audience: the nakāl restrains the outward person in whoever hears it, and the mawʿiẓah reforms the inward person in whoever fears. Both are mercy; the second requires cooperation, which is why it is restricted by name rather than by exclusion — the God-fearing are not a tribe but a condition, and the condition is open to every hearer the recital reaches.

**How a Punishment Becomes a Lesson**

The mechanism by which an event centuries dead performs this double work is the recital itself, and the verse is the recital's statement of purpose. The town's transformation is inaccessible to the senses now — no ruins of apes, no surviving witnesses — yet the nakāl operates because the event was preserved in revelation and is being read aloud in Madina. The Qur'an's own narration is the delivery system of the lesson: the same speech that files the punishment enacts its forward reach, carrying it across the generations the formula names. This is why the Qur'an insists on its stories as *aḥsan al-qaṣaṣ*, the best of narrations (12:3), and files its penalties with narrative care — the punishment of Pharaoh's body preserved explicitly so that it may become a sign for those who come after (10:92), the Sabbath-town narrated across three sūrahs. Every major penalty in the Book carries its transmission clause, and verse 66 is the clause in its purest form: the event's afterlife as lesson is part of the sentence. The reader's position in the mechanism deserves exact statement, because it defines what reading this verse is doing: to receive the mawʿiẓah is to be the *mā khalfahā* functioning as designed — the punishment completing its second purpose in the heart of someone the town never met. A lesson unlearned is a nakāl half-executed; the verse files the audience as the sentence's remaining half.

**The Saved Companies and the Lesson's Full Text**

The mawʿiẓah's content is larger than the transformation, and the fuller filing in al-Aʿrāf shows why. That sūrah's account preserves the town's three companies — the breakers, the warners, and the questioners of the warning — and preserves the outcome's sorting: the warners rescued, named by their warning (7:165). Read as one lesson, the case teaches more than *do not break the bound*. It teaches the structure of a community under test: that transgression in a town is never only the transgressors' affair, that standing in the breach with a spoken warning is itself a form of keeping the Sabbath, and that the rescue is attached by name to the warning rather than to private abstention. The questioners of al-Aʿrāf's account — why bother warning the doomed? — voice the fatigue the lesson exists to answer, and the warners' reply (discharge before Allah, hope for the target) is the lesson's positive text. Verse 66's two nouns thus divide the case's teaching cleanly: the nakāl is the negative half, the fate of the breakers restraining every hearer from the engineered breach; the mawʿiẓah is the positive half, the fate of the warners instructing every God-fearing hearer what to do in a town where the fish surface on the forbidden day. A lesson with only its first half produces fear without direction; the Qur'an files both.

**Deterrence and Mercy in One Sentence**

The verse's pairing of nakāl and mawʿiẓah states the Qur'an's whole philosophy of penalty in one sentence, and the pairing is not balanced by accident. Deterrence alone would make the punishment pure power — a display for its effect on others, using the punished as instrument. Admonition alone would make it pure speech — a lesson detachable from any consequence. Joined, they keep each other honest: the lesson is serious because it was purchased with a real sentence, and the sentence is purposeful because it was filed as teaching. The same structure runs through the recital's whole treatment of this community — severity that is always also pedagogy, from the stroke that answered the demand for sight through the covering of disgrace that answered the table-complaint. And the structure extends to the verse's position in the address to Madina: a community being audited across twenty-odd verses of its ancestors' file is receiving, in the audit's form, exactly what the Sabbath-town received — the record of penalties made into forward-facing instruction. The difference is the audience's advantage. The town learned nothing until the sentence fell; Madina is being given the sentence before its own breach, and every reader after Madina inherits the same precedence. That is the mercy inside the mechanism: the nakāl travels ahead of the sin it restrains, so the generations behind the town can keep the bound the town broke — warned by a punishment already paid for.
