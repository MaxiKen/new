## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:66

> So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Punishment Turned Into Instruction**

The verse states the purpose of the event described in the previous sentence, and it names two audiences: the people of that time and the generations that came after. The Arabic phrasing for the second group describes those who follow behind, so that the sentence covers everyone who would ever hear the story. A single community's failure was converted into material for others.

That conversion is a theme the Book states repeatedly about its narratives. The Qur'an describes its accounts of earlier peoples as reminders and warnings, and it presents history as a store of cases from which a reader can learn without paying the price himself. The verse here makes the same claim about one incident, and it does so by naming the audience before describing the use.

**The Difference Between Example and Lesson**

The sentence uses two terms, and they are not synonyms. The first names something set out to be looked at, and the Book applies it to signs and to warnings placed in view. The second names counsel or admonition addressed to a listener, and the Book uses it for the preaching of the prophets and for the content of revelation itself.

Together they describe two ways a reader might receive the account. He can study it as evidence of how a people ended, and he can hear it as advice directed at himself. The verse does not choose between the two ways; it states that the event was preserved for both purposes, and it leaves the reader to decide which use he is making of it.

**"For the God-Fearing"**

The second audience is described by the quality the sūrah has attached to the people who profit from the Book. The term comes from a root meaning to guard, and the Book's usage describes a person who keeps watch over his conduct because he is aware of what is at stake. Matthew's account of the incident would be of no use to someone who read it as a story about other people.

That limitation is stated as a fact about the audience rather than as a threat. The Book's teaching about its own narratives is that they instruct those who approach them with the intention of being corrected, and it describes the opposite effect in the passage about the parables earlier in the sūrah, where the same material produced argument in one audience and knowledge in another. The verse here names the disposition that makes the difference.

**Why the Record Was Preserved**

The Book's habit of recording the failures of favoured communities, including the community of the revelation itself, follows from the purpose stated in this verse. A scripture that reported only successes would provide nothing for its readers to avoid, and the Qur'an's honesty about Israel's record is part of what makes it usable to anyone who comes afterwards.

The sūrah applies the same standard to its own audience later, when it warns the believers about the behaviour it has been describing in others. The purpose clause in this verse explains why such material is in the Book at all: it is not there to establish a hierarchy among peoples, and it is not there to settle a controversy with one community. It is there so that a reader who fears the outcome will take the warning to himself.

**The Verse That Closes a Unit**

This sentence ends the account of the Sabbath incident and, with it, a short block of the address. The next verses begin the account of the cow, which is the longest single narrative in the sūrah's treatment of Israel and which will occupy the following six verses. The transition is abrupt, and the summary sentence makes it cleaner by stating what the preceding incident was for.

That arrangement shapes how the reader approaches the next passage. He has just been told that a punishment was recorded for the instruction of those who came later, and he now begins a narrative whose details are dominated by the community's reluctance to obey a command. The Book's handling of the two passages together is deliberate, and the connection between them is the warning stated here.

**Instruction Rather Than Condemnation**

The verse does not say that the event was preserved as evidence against the community's descendants. It says the punishment became an example and a lesson, which places the benefit with the people who read rather than with the people who suffered. The Book's addresses to Israel elsewhere make the same distinction, offering the community's own history as a reason to respond differently from their ancestors.

That orientation is what keeps the sūrah's long address from reading as an accusation. The record is detailed, the failures are named, and the mercy that followed them is stated; the purpose of placing all of it in the Book is stated here, and it is the instruction of the reader. The next narrative gives him another case, and the sūrah supplies the purpose clause before it begins rather than after it ends.

**How the Book Uses the Failures of Earlier Peoples**

The sūrah's method is to place its narratives before its warnings and let the record make the argument. The communities of the past are described in their successes and failures, and the reader is invited to see the pattern rather than to judge the people. What the Book persistently insists on is that the pattern is repeatable, and it says so directly when it warns its own community against the conduct it has just described in others.

That use of history is the reason the Qur'an narrates events that a reader might otherwise treat as closed. The material is not included to complete an archive but to make a claim about how God deals with communities, and the claim is verifiable inside the reader's own life. The verse's purpose clause states the intention, and the following narratives supply the cases.
