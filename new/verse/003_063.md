## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:63

> If they turn away, then surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the corruptors.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refusal Named Once More**

The verse follows the declaration of God's oneness with a conditional sentence: if they turn away, then God knows the corruptors. Three times in this sūrah the same refusal has been named, and each time the Book has responded differently — once by stating the duty of the messenger, once by naming the consequence in the language of divine disfavour, and here by referring the whole matter to knowledge.

The repetition traces the course of an argument that has been made and declined. The sūrah is not describing a fresh conversation; it is describing the position of people who have heard the recitation, the account of Jesus, and the creed, and have maintained their course. What the verse adds to the previous statements is a description of the refusal that is also a verdict, delivered as a fact about God rather than as a judgement passed by the messenger.

**Who Are Called Corruptors**

The Arabic word describes a person who spoils or corrupts, and the Book uses it for those who damage the order of things entrusted to them. It is applied elsewhere to people who break agreements, who spread disorder, and who ruin what has been given them to keep.

In this context the term fits the conduct the sūrah has been describing. The people addressed possess scriptures; the Book's charge against them is not that they lack revelation but that they handle it in a way that spoils it — mixing truth with falsehood, concealing what they know, and turning others away from a message they have reason to recognise. The word chosen at the end of the sentence describes the effect of that conduct on the community and on the revelation itself, and it is more exact than a simple synonym for disbelief would have been.

**Knowledge as the Final Answer to Refusal**

The sentence does not threaten punishment, promise a reckoning, or issue a further invitation. It states that God knows. The effect is to place the matter outside the reach of the dispute and to leave the disputants where they stand.

The Book uses this response at the end of several arguments, and its force lies in what it implies about the certainty of the facts. A case that has been examined and refused does not require a new argument. It requires a judge, and the verse says that the judge is already informed. The reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening declaration about what is hidden from God now meets the same knowledge applied to the particular people who have refused an account they were in a position to verify.

**The Pattern of Refusals in This Sūrah**

This is the fourth time in the sūrah that a turning away has been described, and the repetition is part of the sūrah's structure rather than a rhetorical habit. After the instruction to obey God and His messenger, the Book described those who turn away and stated that God does not like the disbelievers. After the summons to submission, it limited the messenger's duty to delivery. After the invitation to a common word, it will tell the Prophet to bear witness on behalf of the believing community.

The accumulating pattern shows what the sūrah is doing with the scriptured communities. Each refusal is recorded, each consequence is stated, and the Book continues to address them, because the invitation is not withdrawn by the earlier refusals. The verse above is the sharpest of these statements and it is not the last; the following verses continue to speak to the People of the Book directly.

**What the Verse Says About the Messenger's Position**

Read with the verses on either side, this sentence relieves the Prophet of a burden and clarifies his role. He has delivered the account, made the argument, offered the challenge, and stated the conclusion; the refusal of the other party is no longer his responsibility, and the Book has said as much in plain words earlier in the sūrah.

The verse therefore does not tell the Prophet to redouble his efforts or to modify his message in the hope of a better reception. It hands the matter over, and it does so in the form of a statement about God's knowledge rather than in the form of an instruction. The messenger's task continues, but it continues on the footing that the outcome belongs elsewhere.

**The Turn Away That Is Also a Report**

The verse's grammar is worth a moment. It is a conditional sentence whose consequence is not an instruction and not a sentence of punishment but a piece of information about God's knowledge. The Book states the refusal and states what God knows about the people who refuse, and it stops there.

The restraint is characteristic of the sūrah's handling of the People of the Book at this point in its argument. The verses that follow will continue to address them, argue with them, and invite them, so the sentence is not a closing of the door. What it establishes is that the refusal is known, that its quality is understood, and that the messenger has discharged his part. The Book's pastoral interest is in keeping the offer open, and this sentence does so by leaving the matter to a knowledge that is exact.

**A Conclusion That Opens the Next Section**

The verse closes the narrative block of the sūrah and opens the block that deals with Abraham. The subject is the same in both cases: which community has the better claim to the inheritance of a prophet.

The transition is smooth because the question has been prepared. The sūrah has just declared the truth of its account of Jesus and the oneness of God; it now turns to the ancestor whom the disputing parties shared with the Muslim community, and to the claim that he belonged to one of them. The refusers have just been described as people who spoil what they hold, and the next verses will show what they do with Abraham.
