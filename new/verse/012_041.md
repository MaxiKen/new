## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:41

> “O my fellow-prisoners! ˹The first˺ one of you will serve wine to his master, and the other will be crucified and the birds will eat from his head. The matter about which you inquired has been decided.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Interpretations Delivered in One Register**

The answer is given without preamble, without qualification and without any difference of tone between the two outcomes. One man will return to his post and pour wine for his employer; the other will be executed and left exposed. The sentence structure treats them as coordinate facts. No consolation is offered to the condemned man, no congratulation to the spared one, and no explanation is given for the difference between them. That flatness is the mark of a genuine interpretation as the tradition understands it: the interpreter reports what has been shown, not what would be kind. The commentators identify the two by their offices — the cupbearer saved and restored, the baker condemned for the plot reported against him — and the Qur'an itself never names either. Anonymity is doing work here. Neither man becomes a type of believer or of disbeliever, because neither is developed at all; both are simply the recipients of a judgement delivered in identical language. What remains for the reader is the register itself — unhedged, unhurried, and given to two people who have no way of checking it against anything they already know.

**Each Dream Returns as an Event Built From Its Own Images**

The method of interpretation is visible in the wording. The first man saw himself pressing wine; he will serve wine. The second saw bread on his head being eaten by birds; birds will eat from his head. Nothing is imported from outside the visions. The images the dreamers supplied are rearranged into the events they signify, and the rearrangement is what the interpretation consists of. That is why the discipline was called *taʾwīl*, from a root meaning to return a thing to its origin: the interpreter does not add meaning to a dream but traces it back to the reality that produced the symbols. The two cases also show that the method is not sentimental. The same procedure that reads a cup as a restoration reads a head as a corpse.

**Crucifixion in the Egyptian Setting**

The punishment named is *ṣalb*, exposure of the body after execution, which in the ancient Near East served as a public warning rather than merely as a method of killing. The detail that birds will eat from the head specifies that the body will not be buried and will remain where scavengers can reach it. The biblical account preserves the same expectation, with the baker's dream interpreted as execution and his body hung on a tree (Genesis 40:18–19). The Qur'an supplies no legal discussion of the penalty and no comment on the justice of the sentence; the crime, on the transmitted reports, was an attempt on the king's life. What the verse records is the interpretation, not an evaluation of Egyptian law. Egyptian practice is known to have used post-mortem display of an executed criminal as a public deterrent, and the wording here fits that setting without passing judgement on it. What carries weight in the chapter is the symmetry of the two sentences: both are delivered in the same voice, in the same breath, to two men who have just been told opposite fates, and neither is softened.

**A Death Sentence Stated Plainly to the Man It Concerns**

Joseph is speaking to the condemned party directly. He does not address the other man privately, does not defer the announcement, and does not frame it hypothetically. The second person is used throughout: one of *you* will serve wine, the other of *you* will be crucified. The choice is a moral one and the chapter endorses it by reporting the speech without correction. A man asked to interpret a dream has undertaken to report the interpretation, and withholding half of it would be a breach of the undertaking rather than an act of mercy. The tradition's later etiquette — that distressing dreams should not be recounted bluntly — concerns dreams a person brings privately about himself, not a case in which two men have jointly requested an answer to a question about their futures.

**"The Matter Has Been Decided" Removes Any Room for Revision**

The closing clause is *quḍiya al-amru alladhī fīhi tastaftiyān* — the affair about which you sought a ruling has been settled. The verb is the one used for a judgement issued and completed. Its function is to close the file: this is not a probable reading, an alternative possibility or a warning that may be averted by precaution. It is a decree already fixed, disclosed to the interpreter rather than produced by him. The clause answers an anxiety the two men must have felt on hearing the sentence, which is whether it could be changed. Joseph's answer is that it cannot, and the chapter treats that as a mercy of a particular kind — the mercy of certainty in a matter where hope would only prolong the waiting. A condemned man who has received a definite answer can spend what is left to him as himself rather than as a petitioner. Joseph's phrasing grants him that, and the chapter counts the granting among the services he performs in the prison, alongside the reading of dreams and the call to belief that precede it.

**Why He Answers After Preaching**

The order of verses 37 to 41 is deliberate. Joseph spends four verses on the oneness of God, on the creed of his fathers, on the comparison between many lords and one, and on the emptiness of names invented by ancestors. Only then does he interpret. Nothing in the sermon was a condition he imposed, and nothing in it was designed to embarrass his hearers into compliance. What the sequence establishes is the standing of the interpreter. A man who has just told two royal servants that their country's religion is a set of invented names is not a man whose prediction they can dismiss as the flattery of a prisoner. The creed gives the interpretation its authority, and the interpretation then confirms the creed by coming true.

**The Fulfilment Is Barely Reported**

The chapter does not dramatise the outcome. Verse 42 identifies one of the two as the man Joseph knew would survive, and verse 45 shows him back at court years later; the other is never mentioned again. No execution scene, no burial, no reaction from the king, no moral drawn. The sūrah's silence is consistent with its method throughout — the Egyptian section reports consequences rather than staging them. What the silence achieves is a certain weight. Two lives were disposed of in a single sentence spoken in a cell, and the narrative moves on, which is how the reader experiences the ordinary operation of decree. The interpretation was accurate, the men knew it was accurate, and only one of them lived to remember it. That asymmetry drives everything after it. A dead man cannot repeat a promise, so the record of Joseph's accuracy passes to the one survivor, and it stays with him, unspoken, through the whole of the years of plenty. The chapter is exact about the price of being right: it produces a witness, and witnesses endure by chance rather than by design.
