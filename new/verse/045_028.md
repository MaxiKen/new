## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:28

> And you will see every faith-community on its knees. Every community will be summoned to its record ˹of deeds˺. ˹They all will be told,˺ “This Day you will be rewarded for what you used to do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Scene Nobody Can Stand Above**

The verse puts the whole of humanity in one posture. Every community appears on its knees, and the word translated community covers religious bodies and nations, the groups into which people have divided themselves across history. The sūrah takes its name from this scene, and the image has the effect of cancelling every distinction the earlier verses have been examining — the allies who protected one another, the leaders who held their ground, the scholars who disputed, the men who followed their appetites. All of them are kneeling. The Book elsewhere pictures a different moment in which people arrive with heads raised and eyes fixed, unable to blink, their hearts empty (14:43), and the two images belong to the same day. What the verse does with a single word is to show that the audience of the judgment is comprehensive.

**Why Kneeling**

Kneeling in the presence of a sovereign is the posture of a person who has no standing to claim. The exegetes give two reasons for the picture. Some say the kneeling expresses dread at what is being read out, since the record is about to be opened in front of everyone. Others say it expresses the humiliation of being judged after a lifetime of denial, and this fits the sūrah's earlier promise that the mockers would receive a humiliating punishment. In either reading, the posture is imposed rather than chosen, and the picture has the additional effect of making every difference of rank among the assembled irrelevant. A man may have spent his life standing over others. On that day he kneels with the rest, and his record is summoned like everyone else's.

**Summoned to Its Record**

The verse moves from the posture to the procedure. Each community is called to its book, and the Book elsewhere describes the same scene with the record laid open, the guilty afraid of what is written in it, crying out that nothing, small or great, has been left out, and finding everything they did present before them (18:49). The records are mentioned in several places: an angel is said to record every soul (86:4); the books are kept by honoured scribes who know what people do (82:10–12); a person is bound to his destiny and will be handed a record that he finds spread open (17:13); and the command is given to him to read his own book, since he is sufficient that day to reckon with himself (17:14). The image is forensic, and it is built on documents rather than on testimony.

**One Ledger, or One Book for All?**

Commentators differ about whether the singular "book" names a single register or the collection of each person's individual ledger. Some take it as the record kept for each individual by the angels at God's command, gathered for the occasion so that the community appears with all its files at once. Others take it as the Preserved Tablet, the register in which everything has been inscribed and which is described in another chapter as a well-guarded writing (85:22). The first reading explains why a community can be told to appear with its book, since the books of its members would be brought together; the second explains the singular and the thoroughness of the record. The difference does not affect the doctrine, which is that nothing has been lost, and it does not affect the verse's drama, which is that the file being read is your own.

**"This Day You Will Be Rewarded for What You Used to Do"**

The address is direct and it names the standard of payment: what you used to do, in the habitual sense the imperfect tense carries. People will not be judged for their final moments or for a single gesture made under pressure; the criterion is the pattern of a life. The Book states the same rule elsewhere, saying that no soul will be wronged in the least and that people will be rewarded only for what they used to do (36:54). The wording also closes an exit. A person cannot plead the difficulty of his circumstances, since the circumstances were part of what he used to do, nor can he plead that his intentions were good while the pattern was something else. The sentence read out on that day is drawn from the ordinary texture of a lifetime.

**Every Community, Including This One**

The verse addresses all communities, and that universality is a warning to whoever reads the chapter. The Muslim community is not exempted from the scene, and the sūrah offers no assurance that a community's book will be read leniently because of its name. The same Book criticises earlier communities which confused their standing with a guarantee, including the earlier passage in this sūrah in which the Children of Israel were given every gift and then divided out of envy. The scene of kneeling is the reply to every privilege claimed by inheritance, and the sūrah places it directly after the arguments about patronage and lineage that fill the middle of the chapter. On that day the file, not the family, determines where a person stands.

**A Hearing Rather Than a Battle**

The imagery is legal because the reality is a hearing. Records are produced, communities are summoned, and the verdict is announced in the words of a judge. Nothing in the scene suggests that anyone argues his way out of the outcome, or that the assembled crowds can influence the reading of the file. The Book's descriptions of that day rarely feature a defence, and when they do, the answer given to the accused is the record itself, which speaks with an accuracy no cross-examination can shake. The verse therefore presents the judgment as the least dramatic event imaginable: a call, a file, a sentence, and an assembly of communities on their knees while the paperwork of their lives is opened and read.
