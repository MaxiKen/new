## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:80

> And his people argued with him. He responded, “Are you arguing with me about Allah, while He has guided me? I am not afraid of whatever ˹idols˺ you associate with Him—˹none can harm me,˺ unless my Lord so wills. My Lord encompasses everything in ˹His˺ knowledge. Will you not be mindful?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument His People Made**

And his people argued with him. The sūrah does not record the terms of their argument, and that silence is informative. What the Book preserves instead is Abraham's reply, and the reply shows what the dispute was about: fear. The Makkans of his day were not defending a philosophical position but a relationship they believed they had with powers that could be offended, and Abraham's answer addresses that belief directly.

The Book describes the same style of argument among the polytheists of the Prophet's own time, who said that if God had willed they would never have worshipped anything other than Him, nor forbidden anything without His command (16:35). The sūrah of the Ornament records the same claim in almost the same words, and adds that the speakers have no knowledge in support of it and do nothing but lie (43:20). The dispute with Abraham belongs to that long-running argument about human practice and divine permission.

**"While He Has Guided Me"**

Are you arguing with me about Allah, while He has guided me? The clause places guidance at the centre of the dispute, and it does so before Abraham says anything about the idols. His position rests on something he has received rather than on something he has worked out, and the sūrah has already described that gift in its account of the showing given to him (6:75).

The Book treats guidance as the decisive element in every such dispute, and it states that whoever God guides is truly guided while whoever He leaves to stray will find no guardian besides Him (7:178). The two participants in this argument were therefore not equals exchanging opinions. One had been shown the wonders of the heavens and the earth, and the other had inherited a set of names.

**"I Am Not Afraid of What You Associate With Him"**

The sentence addresses the practical stake of the controversy, and it removes the fear that the community's religion was built on. Abraham's position is that the idols cannot harm him, and the Book makes the same claim in its own voice when it challenges the idolaters to consider whether their gods could undo a harm God willed or withhold a mercy He granted (39:38).

The concession that follows is important: unless my Lord so wills. The sentence does not claim that Abraham stands outside God's decree, and it does not describe the idols as having any power that God must restrain. The Book's position throughout is that everything that happens does so by God's permission, and the verse states that principle in the same breath as the refusal of fear.

**The Knowledge That Encompasses Everything**

My Lord encompasses everything in His knowledge. The sentence gives the reason Abraham can speak with confidence about a matter that involves the unseen, and it repeats one of the sūrah's central claims. The sūrah said earlier that God knows what is on land and sea and that not a leaf falls without His knowledge (6:59), and it described the record kept of every deed (6:61).

The same phrase appears on the lips of Moses in the sūrah of Ṭā-Hā, where he tells his people that their only god is God, and that He encompasses everything in His knowledge (20:98). The sūrah of the Divorce states that God encompasses all things in knowledge (65:12), and the sūrah of the Women says that God is fully aware of everything (4:126). The claim functions in these verses as a boundary on human speculation: the audience may not know what the gods are doing, but nothing is outside the knowledge of the One Abraham serves.

**The Question at the End**

Will you not be mindful? The verse ends with a question rather than a conclusion, and the question is the same one the sūrah has asked its audience several times. It asks whether the hearers will order their lives around the awareness of God, and it takes for granted that the material for that awareness has been supplied.

Read in place, the question is the sharpest thing in the verse. The people arguing with Abraham had just heard him say that his Lord encompasses everything in knowledge, and the natural response to that sentence is not an argument about which god should be feared but a change in how a person lives. The Book lets the question stand without an answer, and the next verse continues the comparison of fears that the question has opened.

**What the Reply Reveals About the Prophet's Situation**

The Book places this story in the middle of a sūrah addressed to a Prophet whose people were making the same argument to him. They had asked him to accommodate their gods, threatened him with the displeasure of those gods, and warned him that abandoning them would cost him the protection of the city. Abraham's reply supplies the answer he was to give: the question is not which powers might be offended but who has guided whom.

The sūrah draws the parallel tightly when it puts the same claim on the Prophet's lips in the declaration that he has been forbidden to worship what they invoke and will not follow their desires (6:56). Abraham's words and the Prophet's words are the same refusal, spoken in different cities and to different generations of the same argument.

**The Refusal to Certify What They Claim**

The Book gives the Prophet a further instruction in this sūrah that answers the same pressure: he is told to ask who the best witness is, then to answer that God is witness between him and them, and then to ask the idolaters whether they testify that there are other gods besides God, with the instruction to declare that he will never testify to that and that there is only One God (6:19). The refusal to certify the audience's claim is the practical form of the position Abraham states in this verse. A person who will not say that the idols have a right to fear has already answered the argument, and the Book makes the Prophet say so in the audience's hearing.
