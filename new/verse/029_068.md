## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:68

> And who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or reject the truth after it has reached them? Is Hell not a ˹fitting˺ home for the disbelievers?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Claims the Highest Rank of Wrongdoing**

The Book states the same comparison in several chapters, and it always assigns the superlative to the same offence: fabricating lies about God or rejecting the truth after it has come (6:21; 6:93; 39:32). The form of the question is a challenge to the imagination rather than an assertion, and the answer it expects is that no one exceeds this offence in gravity. The reasoning behind that ranking is worth stating. A person who commits an ordinary wrong damages something created; a person who invents a religious claim about God damages the framework by which right and wrong are known at all, and he does it while quoting the highest authority in existence as his warrant.

The severity comes from what the offence denies rather than from the words used. A person who invents a claim about God has set himself up as a source of religious authority, and every practice built on his invention inherits the falsehood, which is why the Book keeps this offence in a category of its own.

**What Fabricating Against God Covers**

The phrase covers a wider field than deliberate forgery. The Book applies it to those who claim revelations they never received, to those who say they can produce the like of what God has sent down (6:93), and to those who attribute partners to God and then present the association as a divine arrangement. In the sūrah's own context, it applies to the men who invented a religion that promised immunity from the consequences of sin and sold it to their neighbours. The fabrication the verse names is therefore not confined to speech; it includes the system of claims by which a community is persuaded to live, and the Book treats the manufacture of such a system as a charge against its makers rather than against their victims.

The phrase also covers the subtler form of the offence. A person adds a rule, a prohibition, or a permission to the religion and attributes the addition to God, and the severity here falls on every claim to have been told something that was never told.

**"After It Has Reached Them"**

The second half of the charge removes the defence of not knowing. The Book's formulation is precise: the wrong under discussion is the rejection of a truth that has arrived, and arrival is the operative word. The sūrah has been documenting arrival throughout its length. A messenger had spoken in the city, arguments had been made, evidence had been placed in front of the audience, and the ruins of the nations that refused the same evidence were visible on their trade routes. To reject the truth after it has reached a person is not an intellectual position; it is a decision taken in the light, and the phrase locates the moral responsibility exactly where the Book has been locating it since its opening verses.

The clause also removes the defence of distance. The audience was not being asked to accept a report about a remote people or an earlier age; the warning had arrived among them in their own language, delivered by a man they could question, and the phrasing leaves them without the option of pleading that the news never came.

**The Question About Hell**

The verse ends with a rhetorical question about whether Hell is not a fitting home for the disbelievers, and the word used for dwelling carries the sense of the place where one settles and remains. The question is not a sentence pronounced in anger and it is not an argument for the punishment's severity; it asks the reader to assess the fit between the conduct described and the destination named. The Book's use of fittingness in these passages is consistent. A person who spends his life treating the truth as an obstacle is described as arriving at a place whose defining feature is exclusion from it. The question then answers itself, and the Book leaves the answer with the reader.

Mentioning the fire also keeps the passage from ending on a purely intellectual charge. The sūrah does not close with a complaint about faulty reasoning but with the consequence attached to it, and the sentence names the destination alongside the offence so that the reader sees what the dispute concerns.

**The Mercy That Was Offered First**

The sūrah has already stated, in its earlier verses, that those who believe and do good have their offenses removed and are rewarded according to the best of what they did (29:7), and that the destruction in the historical passages never came without warning. The verdict in this verse follows the same order of events: the truth arrives before it is rejected, and the offer remains open while a person lives. The Book never presents the punishment in these chapters as a first move, and the phrase about arrival is the guard against that misreading. What the reader is being shown is the end of a process whose earlier stages the chapter has described in detail, and the question about fittingness is asked at the point where the process has run its course.

The order of the two clauses also shows what the messenger's work was for. The warning had to reach a people before the sentence could describe them, and the Book treats the years of argument and recitation as the period in which that condition was being met.

**Who the Sentence Is Aimed At**

The Book's superlatives are aimed at the reader as much as at the reader's opponents, and this one is no exception. A person who hears the truth and then arranges his life to avoid acting on it is standing in the same territory as the fabricators, even if his own fault is smaller in scope. The sūrah's closing verses are addressed to a community being asked to hold a difficult position, and the sentence states what is at stake in that position by naming the alternative. The verse that follows answers the charge with the promise that the Book gives to those who struggle, and the contrast between the two is the sūrah's final word on the matter.

Both halves of the sentence have a group behind them. The inventors are the town's leaders with their inherited cult, and the deniers are the people who accepted the arrangement without examining it, and the Book names both because a community carries more than one kind of responsibility.

**The Same Sentence in Sūrah al-Zumar**

Sūrah al-Zumar carries an almost identical sentence: who then does more wrong than those who lie about God and reject the truth after it has reached them, and is Hell not a fitting home for the disbelievers (39:32)? The recurrence of the wording across chapters indicates that the Book treats this combination — invention and refusal — as the definitive form of the offence it has been describing throughout the sūrah. What the two passages share is the phrase about arrival, and its repetition across different audiences and different eras shows that the standard is not tailored to one community's circumstances. The same charge was available to be made against any people who received a messenger and answered him with a fabricated alternative.

The parallel also shows that the charge is not aimed only at the first audience. The same sentence appears wherever a community organizes itself around a claim that has no basis, and the Book expects its readers to recognize the pattern in their own surroundings as well as in the history it reports.

**Sincere Devotion Against Public Opinion**

The Book states the standard of worship in a form that anticipates resistance from the crowd: call upon God with sincere devotion, even to the dismay of the disbelievers (40:14). The phrase about the dismay of the disbelievers is the reason the verse under discussion places the fabrication of lies beside the denial of the truth. Between these two positions stands a person who has privately understood the case but will not say so publicly, and the Book's instruction that devotion be offered to God alone, whatever the audience thinks, addresses exactly that person. The sūrah has been dealing with a community that found its standing in an inherited cult; the sentence about the sincere and the deniers clears away both the originators of the doctrine and the crowd that keeps it going by not objecting.

**Why the Question Form Is Used Instead of a Verdict**

A verdict pronounced in the passive would leave the reader as an observer, and the Book's question form does the opposite. It asks the hearer to supply the answer, which means the hearer must first weigh the conduct described and then agree to the destination attached to it. The Book uses this instrument repeatedly in its closing arguments, and the effect is to make the reader a participant in the judgment rather than a spectator at it. The sūrah has already made extensive use of questions — about the creation, the rainfall, the sanctuary, and the ruin of the nations — and this last one completes the sequence by asking the reader to assess what he has seen. What he decides about the fit between the crime and the home belongs to him.
