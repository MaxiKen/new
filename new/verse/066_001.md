## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:1

> O Prophet! Why do you prohibit ˹yourself˺ from what Allah has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Functions as a Correction**

The sūrah opens by asking the Prophet ﷺ a question to which the questioner knows the answer. *“Why do you prohibit ˹yourself˺ from what Allah has made lawful to you”* (66:1) is not a request for information; it is the form a correction takes when it is addressed to someone honoured. The interrogative softens nothing about the substance, but it does place the Prophet ﷺ in the position of a man invited to examine his own reasoning rather than a defendant receiving a verdict.

That a scripture preserves the correction of its own founder is worth registering. A text composed to build the authority of a leader would not open a chapter by questioning his judgement in a domestic matter, name the cause as a wish to please his wives, and then leave the exchange in the permanent record for every generation to recite. The early community found the chapter difficult for exactly this reason, and it has been called Sūrat al-Nabī, the sūrah of the Prophet, precisely because it deals so directly with his household. Its preservation is an argument about the nature of the Book: the Messenger is a servant under instruction, and the instruction is published.

**What Was Forbidden and Why**

The occasion is reported with two main accounts. In the widely transmitted version, the Prophet ﷺ had been with Māriya, the Coptic woman who had come into his household, in the apartment of Ḥafṣa on Ḥafṣa's appointed day. His wives took their turns in rotation, and the breach of that rotation upset her. To settle the matter he asked her to keep it private and declared that Māriya was henceforth forbidden to him. The second account has him swearing off a drink of honey after two of his wives told him it had left an odour on him. Some commentators keep both, noting that the verse would apply to any case in which he barred himself from something God had permitted.

The details differ; the correction does not. In either version the Prophet ﷺ responded to friction in his household by removing something lawful from his own life, and in either version the response was disallowed. The verse's wording is careful about where the fault lies. Nothing wrong was done in the act he renounced — it is described as *“what Allah has made lawful to you”* (66:1). The error was in the renunciation itself.

**Peacemaking Is Not a Licence to Alter the Law**

*“seeking to please your wives”* (66:1) names the motive without condemning it. A husband trying to keep peace in a house where several women share a life is doing something decent, and the Qur'an nowhere criticises the impulse. What it refuses is the method. A personal oath that turns a permitted thing into a forbidden one has crossed from managing a household into legislating, and legislation is not among the things a man may offer his family as a concession.

The same principle is addressed to the whole community elsewhere, and in almost the same words: *“O believers! Do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors”* (5:87). The Qur'an also condemns those who invented prohibitions on their own livestock and food, *“falsely attributing lies to Allah”* (6:140). Read together with this verse, the policy is consistent: the boundary between lawful and forbidden belongs to God alone, and the sincerity of the person who moves it is not a defence. If the Prophet ﷺ himself is corrected for shifting that line in his own favour of domestic peace, nobody after him has standing to shift it out of piety, scrupulousness or a wish to be agreeable.

**Renunciation Is Not Automatically Piety**

Behind the ruling sits a broader argument about religious temperament. Many traditions treat self-denial as a reliable indicator of devotion, so that the person who gives up more is assumed to be nearer God. This verse checks that assumption at its source. A man denied himself something permitted, for a motive that was not selfish, and was asked why.

The correction is consistent with the Prophet's recorded impatience toward exaggerated abstinence among his Companions, and with a sūrah that will shortly define the excellent believing woman without a single reference to what she has renounced — she is described as submissive, faithful, devout, repentant, given to worship and fasting (66:5). Fasting appears there as a prescribed practice, not an invented one, and that distinction is the whole point. Discipline within the limits God set is worship; discipline that redraws the limits is presumption wearing the clothes of worship. A religion that cannot make this distinction ends by treating its most severe adherents as its most reliable ones.

**Forgiveness Named Before Any Repentance Is Reported**

The verse ends where a rebuke would not: *“And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”* (66:1). No apology has been offered yet, no expiation performed, no remorse described. The two names arrive immediately after the question, closing the correction with an assurance rather than a threat.

The placement establishes the register for the entire chapter, which will go on to correct two of the Prophet's wives, warn them about the state of their hearts, address believers about protecting their households from the Fire, and call for sincere repentance. All of that is severe, and all of it is read in the light of the two names placed here at the start. The order also carries a practical lesson about how error is handled in the Book: the fault is named precisely and without euphemism, and the door back is shown in the same breath. What the Qur'an will not do is either pretend nothing happened or leave the corrected party without a route forward. The next verse turns to that route.
