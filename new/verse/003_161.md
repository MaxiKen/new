## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:161

> It is not appropriate for a prophet to illegally withhold spoils of war. And whoever does so, it will be held against them on the Day of Judgment. Then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Statement About the Prophet**

The verse declares that it is not for any prophet to defraud, and the sentence is a defence of the Prophet's integrity in a matter where it had been questioned. The sūrah addresses the subject in the aftermath of a battle, when property had changed hands and accusations were circulating.

The commentators connect the verse with the distribution of the spoils of war and with reports that an item of value had gone missing, after which some people suggested that the Prophet himself might have taken it. Al-Ṭabarī's preferred reading is that the verse was revealed simply to inform people that such a thing does not happen with a prophet.

**What the Verb Covers**

The verb rendered as defraud is used specifically for taking part of what should be shared out, and the commentators treated the verse as dealing with the spoils above all. Some held that it concerned a valuable piece of cloth lost after one of the battles, and others that it concerned the failure to distribute spoils equitably.

The discussions show how seriously the community treated the smallest misappropriation of common property. The verse's scope was then extended by the commentators to cover every kind of embezzlement and every misuse of a trust, since the principle in a sentence forbidding a prophet to take what is not his must apply even more plainly to anyone lower in station.

**The Different Occasions Reported**

Beyond the reports about the spoils, some commentators connected the verse with the archers who left their positions and were given no share of the spoils they had been trying to secure. Others linked it to expeditions whose spoils had been distributed before the fighters returned. Al-Ṭabarī records a further view that the verse has nothing to do with spoils at all, and that it forbids the Prophet to withhold any part of the revelation entrusted to him.

The variety of these reports does not affect the verse's meaning, since each of them concerns a trust and the taking of what is not one's own. The fact that so many occasions were proposed is a sign of how directly the sentence spoke to the community's situation and of how many of its problems touched the same principle.

**What the Fraudster Brings on the Day of Judgement**

The verse states that whoever defrauds will bring what he took on the Day of Resurrection. The image is a concrete one: the misappropriated thing accompanies the person who took it into the presence of God.

The commentators quote reports that describe people arriving on that day with the objects they had wrongfully taken hanging from their necks, and the description matches the sūrah's later verse about those who were miserly, who will be collared on the Day of Resurrection by what they hoarded. The Book's imagery in such passages gives property a weight that follows its possessor, and the sentence is designed to make a person think about what he will be carrying.

**The Payment Made in Full**

The verse then states that every soul will be paid what it has earned in full and will not be wronged. The sentence widens the view from the specific offence to the general settlement.

The Book uses this formula at the close of its instructions about property, and it does so because the fear of loss and the desire for gain shape more of human conduct than most people admit. The community is told that the account will be accurate and complete, and the phrase about no one being wronged answers the anxiety that a person might lose what he deserves.

**The Reports About Public Trusts**

The commentators connect this verse with reports about officials collecting alms who returned and said that part of the property was the community's and part had been given to them as a gift. The Prophet's response, according to those reports, was to ask whether the man's gift would have come to him had he stayed in his father's or his mother's house.

The question exposes the practice as a concealed payment for a public office. The verse's principle reaches such cases directly, and the tradition has used it as the ground for its rules about gifts to officials, just as it has used it against the taking of anything that belongs to the common fund.

**The Generalization Beyond the Spoil**

Though the occasion of the verse concerned the spoils of war, the commentators generalised it, and their generalisation has governed the community's treatment of the subject ever since. Any mishandling of public property falls under the sentence, and the tradition classifies the taking of spoils before distribution among the serious offences.

The verse's placement in the sūrah supports the wider reading. It comes after a long passage about the community's conduct in battle and before verses that discuss the difference between those who seek the contentment of God and those who earn His anger. The subject of trust follows naturally from the account of a community that had just been tested in a matter involving property.

**What the Verse Teaches About Leadership**

The sentence begins with the word for any prophet, and the point is that the office itself excludes such conduct. The person who conveys God's message is entrusted with the message and with the community's affairs, and the verse removes him from any suspicion of serving his own interest.

The community that hears the sentence is being told what it should expect from its leaders and what it should refuse to believe of them without grounds. The sūrah corrects a rumour and establishes a principle at the same time, and the next verse contrasts two figures so that the principle can be seen in human terms.
