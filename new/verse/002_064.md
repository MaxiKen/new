## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:64

> Yet you turned away afterwards. Had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you, you would have certainly been of the losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Turn Described Without Qualification**

The verse reports that the community turned away after the covenant and the raised mountain, and it does not describe the distance of the turn or the time it took. The Arabic construction places the event after the pledge and states it flatly, so that the reader is left with the contrast between the seriousness of the ceremony and the speed of the departure from it.

The sūrah has reported the same pattern with the calf, with the demand to see God, and with the change of words at the gate. This verse adds a summary judgment, and the summary is offered in one sentence rather than developed, which keeps the passage moving toward the next item in the record. The Book's accounts of this community are compressed in this way throughout, with each incident stated and then left standing.

**Grace and Mercy as the Only Reason for Survival**

The sentence places the community's survival on God's grace and mercy and says explicitly that without them they would have been lost. The word for lost describes a person who has given away something valuable and has nothing to show for it, and the sūrah has used the same term for the men who traded guidance for misguidance.

What makes the sentence remarkable is the arrangement it describes. The community's conduct is stated as a fact, and then its continued existence is attributed entirely to a decision taken elsewhere. Nothing in the verse suggests that the grace was earned or that the community's position was restored because of something it did between the turning away and the mercy. The Book reports a people preserved in a state that would otherwise have ended them.

**What "the Losers" Means in This Sūrah**

The term the verse uses has been defined by use earlier in the chapter. The Book applies it to those who break God's covenant after affirming it and spread corruption in the land (2:27), and to those who trade the Hereafter for this world (2:86). The word carries a commercial sense of a loss recorded in an account, and the sūrah's opening passages built the reader's understanding of it before this verse arrives.

The conditional form of the sentence therefore carries real content. The community's record, measured against the covenant it had just accepted, would have settled its account as a loss. The verse states that a different entry was made, and it names the two attributes responsible.

**The Mercy That Precedes Repentance**

The Book's treatment of this community connects the grace in this verse to the requests for pardon it makes elsewhere, and it records the mercy as arriving before any improvement in conduct. The sūrah's account of Adam used the same order: the failure occurred, and words of repentance were supplied to him by his Lord before he had assembled any defence.

That order is a fixed feature of the Book's account of divine dealing with human failure. The Qur'an does not present mercy as the response to a successful appeal, and it does not describe a period during which the offender must prove himself. It describes mercy that meets the offender where he is, and it attributes to that mercy the survival of a people whose record would otherwise have ended the relationship.

**The Verse as a Summary of the Address to Israel**

This sentence gathers the whole of the preceding narrative into a single statement. The gifts, the failures, the punishments and the pardons have been recited at length, and this verse distils them into two clauses: they turned away, and they were spared. Everything the sūrah says to this community afterwards assumes those two facts.

The reader who reaches this point has been given the reason the address continues at all. A people whose history is a record of turning away and being shown mercy is being asked, in the sūrah's remaining verses, to accept a messenger who confirms what they already possess. The verse supplies the grounds for that request, and it does so without claiming that the community deserved what it received.

**Why the Narrative Pauses Here**

The sentence acts as a joint in the address. Everything before it described what the community received; everything after it turns to specific offences and their consequences, beginning with the Sabbath case in the next verse. By inserting a summary here, the Book separates the account of gifts from the account of failures without pretending that the two are unrelated.

The placement also affects how the following material is read. A reader who has just been told that a covenant-breaking people survived only through grace approaches the next incident with an understanding of the relationship it took place inside. The offences are real, the record is preserved, and the arrangement that allowed the community to continue was not its own achievement.

**Mercy as the Explanation for Continuity**

The Book's answer to the question of why a favoured people remains in existence after repeated failures is given in this verse and repeated elsewhere in its addresses. It does not credit the community's later conduct with repairing the breach, and it does not present the mercy as a reward for anything done in the interval. What the sentence describes is a survival that the record does not explain on its own terms.

That reading of the verse is what the sūrah's treatment of its own audience assumes. The invitations extended to the descendants of these people are issued by the same mercy, and the Book states the terms plainly when it tells the Prophet to announce that God forgives all sins for those who turn to Him. The mercy in this verse is not an isolated act of leniency; it is the arrangement on which the whole address rests.
