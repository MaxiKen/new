## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:64

> Yet you turned away afterwards. Had it not been for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you, you would have certainly been of the losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Turning Away After Everything**

Three words carry the verse's indictment: *turned away, afterwards*. The verb *tawallā* is not the glancing-off of inattention but the deliberate turn of the back — the same verb the Qur'an uses for the one who hears the call and walks the other way. Its object here is everything verse 63 assembled: the covenant taken, the mountain raised, the command to hold with strength. And the temporal marker *baʿda dhālik* — after that — is the aggravator. This was not a people who turned away in ignorance, before the demonstration; they turned away after the canopy, after the sea, after the resurrection of verse 56, after the pardon of verse 52. The recital's whole accumulation of favors stands behind the adverb, and the adverb converts the turning into the sūrah's definition of ingratitude: not failing to see, but walking away from what was seen. The verse states the turn in the barest grammar available — a verb, a pronoun, a time-word — because elaboration would soften it. Every defense a reader might reach for (we did not know, we were not shown, we were never asked) has been pre-filed in the preceding twenty verses, and *afterwards* closes the file.

**The Counterfactual of Loss**

The second sentence is a conditional about a history that did not happen: *had it not been for Allah's grace and mercy upon you, you would have certainly been of the losers*. The *lawlā* construction — were it not for — states the community's actual position by naming the alternative it escaped. The turn away deserved a consequence, and the verse identifies the consequence precisely: loss, *khusr*, the sūrah's commercial word for the soul that trades its capital for nothing. The counterfactual does two kinds of work at once. Juridically, it establishes that the turning-away merited ruin — the grace was not the payment of a debt but the interception of a verdict. Pedagogically, it teaches the community to read its own survival: everything this people still has after the turning — covenant, scripture, posterity, the standing offer of repentance — exists inside the intercepted alternative, on loan from two divine attributes. The Qur'an's counterfactuals are among its sharpest instruments, because they force gratitude to reckon with what was deserved. A community that reads only its blessings grows entitled; a community that reads the sentence suspended over its blessings grows careful. The verse is the recital's balance-sheet moment: here is what you did, here is what you earned, here is the two names standing between you and it.

**Faḍl and Raḥma: The Two Names That Intercepted**

The interception runs through a pair: *faḍl* and *raḥma* — grace and mercy. The two nouns are not synonyms stacked for emphasis; they name distinct operations, and the pair's division of labor is exact. *Faḍl* is surplus: giving beyond what is owed, beyond what is earned, beyond the measure of the deed — the word the Qur'an uses for provision granted without accounting, for reward multiplied past the act's worth. *Raḥma* is tenderness toward the miserable: care that reaches a recipient in a condition it cannot remedy itself — the womb-root's mercy, moving toward need rather than toward merit. The turning-away community stood in need of both operations at once: it had earned less than it had (so surplus was required — faḍl), and it was in a condition it could not exit unaided (so descending care was required — raḥma). The pair appears across the Qur'an as the standing formula of rescue — the believers hold their direction of prayer through Allah's faḍl and raḥma, the sūrah's own verse on the qibla test closes on Allah as Raʾūf and Raḥīm toward humanity (2:143) — and its filing here, immediately after the turning-away, makes the theological point the recital keeps proving: the covenant survives its breaches because its divine side runs on grace-and-mercy rather than on match-and-mirror. The human side turns; the divine side's response is governed by names that do not.

**What the Mountain Could Not Do**

Verse 63's canopy produced a pledge; verse 64 records that the pledge did not hold. The pair of verses states, in the Qur'an's own sequence, a limit on terror: fear compels the tongue but does not convert the heart, and a covenant signed under a hovering mountain requires a different power to keep. The sūrah had already legislated the alternative — verse 45's method clause: seek help through patience and prayer, the disciplines that hold a person on the line after the line's terror has faded. Verse 63's own purpose clause said the same thing from the other end: hold firmly, remember, *so perhaps you will become mindful* — taqwā formed by grip and memory, not by fright. Verse 64 is the demonstration of why the purpose clause was needed. The raised mountain is thus given its exact weight in the recital's theology: enough to arrest a refusal, not enough to found a fidelity. What fidelity requires is the pair of this verse — grace that gives the turning-away community a second standing, mercy that keeps working on hearts the mountain could only shake. The sequence fright → pledge → turn → grace is the sūrah's whole account of how law alone fares with human beings, and it is why the recital never relies on severity twice: every terror in the file is followed by provision, every collapse by an intercepted verdict.

**Of the Losers**

The verdict intercepted is named in the sūrah's market idiom: *you would have been of the losers* — *la-kuntum min al-khāsirīn*. Loss, in this sūrah, is never vague misfortune but failed exchange: its opening portraits buy misguidance at the price of guidance and complete the purchase without profit (2:16); its hypocrites deal in deception that returns on the dealers; its deniers trade the eternal for the fleeting. To be *of the losers* is membership in that company — the people whose ledger shows capital spent on nothing. The commercial frame sharpens the counterfactual's arithmetic. The turning-away was itself a transaction: the covenant's obligations set down, the covenant's benefits retained as far as possible, the direction of trade reversed. What grace intercepts is not a mood but a deal — the completed sale of a delivered people's standing for the price of an unkept pledge. And the *min* of membership does its habitual work: the losers are a class, and the intercepted community was one clause away from enrollment. The sūrah's readers stand inside the same grammar. Every turning-away is a small purchase in the same market, and the verse states what keeps any buyer out of the losers' roll: not the buyer's discipline, but the two names that move before the transaction settles — grace upon you, mercy upon you.

**The Mercy That Keeps the Case File Open**

The verse's placement makes it the recital's refrain in miniature. Between the mountain and this sentence, the file has already run its cycle once: sin, consequence, pardon, purpose (the calf, the stroke, the reversal, the gratitude-clauses). Verse 64 compresses the whole cycle into two sentences — the turn stated, the interception stated — and thereby publishes the rule by which the rest of the recital will be read. Nothing after this verse surprises: the gate's mockers, the table's complainers, the calf's builders are all communities turning away afterwards, and all of them are still being addressed, which means the interception is still standing. The final audience of the verse is the individual reader, and its grammar is built for that transfer: *upon you* is second-person, and the counterfactual is addressed to anyone who has ever walked away from a covenant they signed under conviction. The sūrah's answer to every such walk is this verse's answer — not a demand to un-walk it by willpower, but the naming of what has been holding the file open the whole time: faḍl that keeps giving past the deserving, raḥma that keeps descending into the condition. The turning-away is real, the loss is earned, and the two names are greater than both — which is the only reason verse 65 can go on to address the same community again.
