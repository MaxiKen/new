## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:80

> When they lost all hope in him, they spoke privately. The eldest of them said, “Do you not know that your father had taken a solemn oath by Allah from you, nor how you failed him regarding Joseph before? So I am not leaving this land until my father allows me to, or Allah decides for me. For He is the Best of Judges.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Despair of Persuasion, Not of the Situation**

The verse opens with a specific kind of hopelessness. The brothers despair *of him* — of moving the official, of obtaining a substitution, of arguing further. They do not despair of Benjamin's survival or of returning home eventually. The narrowing is important. Verse 78's appeal has been refused on legal grounds, and the refusal is final enough that no further plea is attempted. What remains is not negotiation but a decision about what each of them will do next, and the verse records the decision being taken privately among themselves rather than in front of Joseph. The privacy is significant. For twenty years these men have acted as a group with a shared story and a shared silence; here they withdraw from the officials, from the crowd and from the brother in custody to work out what comes next. The chapter marks the withdrawal because what emerges from it is not a plan of escape but the first honest sentence any of them has spoken.

**"They Conferred Privately"**

The word denotes a private consultation, a closed conversation among a group. The Qur'an uses the same noun of intimate counsel elsewhere and states that no such conversation occurs without God's knowledge: *“If three converse privately, He is their fourth. If five, He is their sixth”* (58:7). The chapter's use of it here marks a boundary — the brothers have moved out of the official's hearing and into a discussion among themselves. It is the first time in the Egyptian section that they deliberate as a group, and the deliberation produces the only morally serious statement any of them has made in front of the reader. The deliberation matters because it is deliberation. Nobody prompts them, no official accuses them and no brother confronts them; alone with the consequence of a journey they undertook freely, they reach a judgement about themselves. The chapter shows conscience working without external pressure, which is the condition that makes the confession in verse 91 possible when it finally arrives.

**The Eldest Speaks**

The commentators identify the eldest as Reuben, though the Qur'an does not name him. He is the one who speaks, and what he says is addressed to his brothers rather than to Joseph or to God. The chapter gives the floor to the eldest at the moment of crisis, which is consistent with the family structure it has described throughout: these men act as a group and the group has an order. It may also be significant that verse 10 records one of the brothers proposing that Joseph not be killed but thrown into a well instead, and the tradition often identifies that speaker with the eldest. If so, the man who once proposed a lesser harm is now the man who refuses to go home.

**He Invokes the Oath and the Earlier Failure Together**

The speech has two references and they are joined deliberately. First, the covenant their father took from them in God's name (12:66). Second, their earlier failure regarding Joseph — the pledge they gave about a son they did not return, and the report they brought their father instead. The eldest does not mention the oath without the failure or the failure without the oath. He sets them side by side so that his brothers hear the second inside the first: we are now in the position we were in before, and last time we did not keep our word. The chapter's whole account of these men turns on that juxtaposition. The juxtaposition is the whole of the chapter's moral argument about them. Two failures are named in one breath: the loss of Joseph, for which they handed their father a false account, and the loss of Benjamin, for which they have sworn before God. The first cannot be repaired and the second they refuse to compound, and that refusal is where the change in them becomes visible.

**"I Will Not Leave This Land"**

The vow is personal and open-ended. He will remain in Egypt until his father permits him to return or until God decides for him. The commentators gloss the first condition as Jacob calling him home, and the second as God's decision either to restore Benjamin or to send him back without him. Neither condition is within the eldest's control, which is the point: he has committed himself to a stay whose length is fixed by someone else. The vow is the only act of reparation any of the brothers performs before the reconciliation, and it costs him the one thing he has — his own freedom of movement. The cost is what distinguishes him from the others. Nine brothers return to Canaan with provisions and a story; the tenth stays in a foreign country without family, without freedom and without any prospect of release, because he swore an oath and will not break it. The chapter records the decision in a single sentence and lets it stand as the first reparation the family receives.

**"He Is the Best of Judges"**

The clause closes the vow and transfers the decision. The same words appear in the Qur'an's account of a prophet addressing a divided people: *“then be patient until Allah judges between us. He is the Best of Judges”* (7:87). In both cases the formula is used by a person who cannot resolve a dispute by his own action and does not intend to leave it unresolved. The eldest is not abandoning the matter; he is declining to decide it himself and declining to let his brothers decide it by going home. The chapter places the formula in the mouth of the one brother who has begun to understand what the family owes.

**The Verse Divides the Brothers**

Until this point the ten have acted and spoken as one body. Verse 80 splits them: one stays, and the remainder return to Canaan with a report their father will not believe (12:81–83). The division is the beginning of the chapter's reconciliation, because a group that cannot be held together by a lie eventually has to be held together by something else. The eldest's refusal to travel home is the first act in that direction. He will not carry a second false report to Jacob, and he will not appear before his father without the son his father extracted an oath about. The chapter marks the change without naming it. The division produces a family structure the narrative has not had before. Nine sons arrive in Canaan with grain and a report; a tenth remains in Egypt as surety for the eleventh; and the eldest, who until now has spoken only as part of the group, speaks as an individual carrying a commitment of his own. The chapter marks the change without naming it.

**The Speech Is Addressed to Men Who Know It Is True**

Nothing in the eldest's argument would persuade a stranger. He cites an oath, an earlier failure, and a decision he cannot make. His audience is nine brothers who were present at the well, who produced the bloodied shirt, who heard Jacob's refusal in verse 64, and who have just watched a second son detained under a penalty they themselves stated. The force of the speech is entirely internal to the family, and the chapter's decision to place it in a private conference rather than in front of Joseph means the reader overhears a reckoning that no outsider in Egypt is aware of. The reckoning is the real event of verse 80.
