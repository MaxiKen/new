## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:9

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Might and Mercy Complete the Sign**

The refrain joins two names that people often separate. Al-ʿAzīz, the Almighty, denotes power that cannot be overcome, while al-Raḥīm, the Most Merciful, denotes care directed toward those who receive it. The earth's sign is not governed by blind force or by kindness without authority. It comes from a Lord strong enough to create, judge, and protect, and merciful enough to warn before judgment and to open the path of return.

That pairing explains why the previous statement can speak both of disbelief and of a sign. God does not need to compel belief to prove His power. His might is already complete. He gives the hearer room to respond, and His mercy makes that room meaningful by sending reminders and prophets. When the response is refusal, mercy does not become weakness; when judgment comes, might does not become cruelty. The two names establish the moral character of the Lord who is addressing the chapter's audience.

**The Lord Is Near to the Prophet's Burden**

The use of your Lord keeps the statement relational. It is not an abstract description of a distant deity. It addresses the Prophet through the Lord who called, instructed, and sustains him. After the warning about grief and the observation that most people refuse signs, the refrain steadies the messenger. The audience's reaction does not diminish the Lord's power, and the Prophet's loneliness does not place him outside divine mercy.

This address also gives the Prophet a sound basis for continuing. He is not asked to trust in public response. He trusts in the Lord who is mighty over the outcome and merciful toward the servant who fulfills his task. The same balance will appear at the end of several prophetic accounts in this sūrah. Their communities reject, yet the refrain keeps the story from ending with rejection as the final reality.

**The Refrain Turns One Scene Into a Law**

The sentence returns after accounts of Moses, Abraham, Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, and Shuʿayb. Its repetition is not a break in the stories; it is the line that lets the stories speak to one another. Each prophet faces a distinct social problem, but the same divine names remain appropriate. The Almighty is not limited by the scale of one tyrant or tribe, and the Merciful does not abandon a people without warning.

Repeated words can deepen rather than flatten meaning. After the earth's sign, mercy explains why evidence and reminders continue. After a prophet's rescue, might explains how the arrogant power was overcome. After a community's destruction, mercy recalls that warning preceded the consequence. The refrain makes the chapter's narrative rhythm theological: history is not a series of unrelated disasters, but a recurring meeting between divine authority and human refusal.

**Mercy Includes a Warning**

People may imagine mercy as the removal of every painful consequence. The Qur'anic pairing corrects that idea. Mercy gives life, provision, guidance, time, and opportunities to repent. It also warns a person about the path he is choosing. If he persists after warning, the resulting judgment does not disprove mercy. It shows that mercy is not approval of wrongdoing or an agreement to erase moral reality.

The name al-Raḥīm is especially fitting for the prophet's mission because a messenger brings a form of care that demands a response. A warning can feel severe while protecting a person from a greater harm. The Prophet's grief arises because he understands this, while his audience treats the warning as an insult. The refrain places their dispute under a larger truth: the One who warns them is both able to judge and willing to forgive.

**Might Prevents Despair; Mercy Prevents Arrogance**

A believer who sees only divine might may become afraid without hope. A believer who imagines only mercy may become hopeful without repentance. The paired names correct both distortions. God's might prevents the world from becoming morally empty; His mercy prevents judgment from being read as a desire to destroy. Together they produce a disciplined hope: fear of wrongdoing, confidence in forgiveness, and patience with the work of obedience.

The Qur'an uses similar pairings elsewhere, including descriptions that bring forgiveness and punishment, or power and mercy, into one horizon. The point is not to balance two unrelated attributes mathematically. It is to teach that every divine act is understood within the fullness of God's names. Creation, warning, rescue, and judgment each display different aspects of the same Lord.

**A Refrain That Protects the Messenger From the Crowd**

The audience may count rejection as evidence against the Prophet. The refrain supplies the proper answer. The Lord's might is not measured by the number of people who submit today, and His mercy is not disproved by allowing them time. A messenger's task is set within a divine plan larger than one public response.

For the Prophet, this is consolation without illusion. Some will not believe, and their refusal will have consequences. Yet the story remains under the care of the Almighty, Most Merciful. The refrain closes the opening argument by turning the Prophet's attention from the crowd's posture to the Lord's character. That is where steadfastness begins.

**The Two Names Shape a Balanced Hope**

A servant who depends on mercy alone may excuse the habit that keeps him from God. A servant who thinks only of might may regard every delay as a sign that he is doomed. The refrain supplies a better path. Divine strength makes repentance urgent, because no wrong is beyond the reach of judgment. Divine mercy makes repentance possible, because the warning itself is an opening rather than a final sentence.

This balance also governs how believers speak about others. They do not promise safety to a person who persists in injustice, and they do not declare the door of return closed while life remains. They warn with seriousness and invite with compassion. Such speech reflects the two names rather than choosing one against the other. The Lord who is mighty enough to judge is merciful enough to receive the servant who turns back.
