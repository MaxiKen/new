## Sūrah an-Naml 27:48

> And there were in the city nine ˹elite˺ men who spread corruption in the land, never doing what is right.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Precise Count**

The Qur'an gives a number: nine. Not a faction, not a majority, not the people of the city — nine men, in one settlement, named by their count and their conduct.

The specificity narrows the story considerably. Thamūd had split into two parties in verse 45, and the disputing group was presumably large. Yet what follows — the conspiracy, the oath, the plan to murder Ṣāliḥ's household by night — is the work of this small number. Al-Ṭabarī identifies them as the ones who acted together to hamstring the she-camel and then conspired against the prophet. The Qur'an is describing how a nation's destruction is actually organised, and the answer is that it does not require many people. It requires nine, in the right positions, and a population that does not stop them.

**They Were Notables, Not Outcasts**

Al-Qurṭubī records that these men were among the city's leading figures, and lists among their offences corrupt lending practices and a habit of dishonouring and humiliating people. That places them at the top of the social order rather than at its margins.

The detail matters for how the corruption is to be understood. A city's nine most powerful men do not spread *fasād* by petty crime; they do it through the instruments they control — credit, contracts, and the informal machinery by which some people are made small in front of others. The lending charge is concrete and recognisable. A creditor with standing can ruin a family legally, and repeated often enough the practice reorganises a whole society around debt and obligation. Ṣāliḥ's message to them was to worship God, and the Qur'an's other accounts of Thamūd add warnings about how they treated the weak. A demand for worship and a demand for justice arrive together, and the men with most to lose from the second are the ones who organise against the first.

**Corruption and Repair Are the Qur'an's Standard Pair**

The verse defines them twice, positively and negatively: they work *fasād* in the land and do not *iṣlāḥ*. The two roots are paired throughout the Qur'an as opposites — spoiling and setting right, breaking and mending.

The negative half is not filler. It closes an excuse that would otherwise be available, since a man who causes damage can always point to something he also built. These nine are described as doing no repair at all, which turns a description of acts into a description of a settled direction. The pairing also defines corruption usefully. *Fasād* in the Qur'an is not primarily personal vice; the root refers to something going rotten, a functioning thing being spoiled. A society is a working arrangement, and corruption is what makes it stop working — which is exactly what predatory lending and routine humiliation do to the bonds that let people live together.

**The Word for City and What It Implies**

They are located *fī al-madīnah*, in the city. Thamūd's dwellings are described elsewhere in the Qur'an as carved into rock, and the ruins in northwestern Arabia — monumental tomb façades cut into sandstone outcrops — were known to the Prophet's contemporaries and are visible today.

This was a settled, wealthy, technically accomplished civilisation, not a wandering tribe. That context bears on the charges. Corrupt lending requires a monetary economy; a class of notables requires an established hierarchy; a city requires organisation that can be captured. The Qur'an's destroyed nations are consistently advanced ones, and the pattern is part of the argument it makes to the Quraysh, themselves a prosperous merchant community. The chapter has already condemned Egypt and corrected Sheba, both sophisticated states. Thamūd is the third, and its ruins were on the road north.

**Nine Men and a Silent Majority**

The verse raises a question it does not answer: where was everyone else? A city contained these nine. The believing party existed, the disputing party existed, and the plot proceeded.

The Qur'an's destruction narratives generally fall on whole peoples while identifying particular instigators, and the tension between those two facts is the uncomfortable part. What the structure implies is that a community is answerable for what its leading men are permitted to do in it. Nine notables ruining a city by lending and humiliation could be stopped by a population that declined to accept the arrangement; they are not stopped, and the arrangement holds. That is not an accusation the verse makes explicitly, and it should not be pressed further than the text allows. What the text does make explicit is that these nine were in the city and were doing this, stated as a fact everybody in the story would have known. Their private conversation is reported next, and it shows what men with that much standing thought they could get away with.

**Why the Number Is Given at All**

The Qur'an rarely counts the wicked. It names peoples, cities and courts, and it identifies Pharaoh and Hāmān; it does not usually say how many men were in a conspiracy. Here it does, and the precision has an effect.

A number makes the group finite and therefore resistible. Nine men are not a tide of history or an inevitable social force; they are nine individuals who could be identified, opposed, and refused. Stating the figure removes the excuse that the corruption was simply how things were. It also fixes responsibility with a kind of legal exactness, distinguishing the instigators from the disputing faction at large and from the city that housed them. The chapter has been careful about attribution throughout — the hoopoe blamed Satan for adorning Sheba's deeds, Ṣāliḥ blamed nothing on omens — and this verse continues the pattern by naming the actual agents rather than gesturing at a climate.
