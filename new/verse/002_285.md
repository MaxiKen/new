## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:285

> The Messenger ˹firmly˺ believes in what has been revealed to him from his Lord, and so do the believers. They ˹all˺ believe in Allah, His angels, His Books, and His messengers. ˹They proclaim,˺ "We make no distinction between any of His messengers." And they say, "We hear and obey. ˹We seek˺ Your forgiveness, our Lord! And to You ˹alone˺ is the final return."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger and the Believers in One Sentence**

The sūrah's penultimate verse opens with a subject and an addition: *The Messenger believes in what has been revealed to him from his Lord, and so do the believers*. The commentators mark the sentence's unusual construction — the Prophet's faith stated first and the community's joined to it in the same breath — and read it as the closing sequence's deliberate leveling and linking at once: the Messenger's belief and the believers' belief have one object (what was revealed to him from his Lord) and one grammar, and the community's faith is thereby defined as participation in his rather than imitation at a distance. The revelation's source is named with its full chain — *from his Lord* — and the chain continues into the verse's second sentence: the belief's objects are enumerated in their descending order, God first, then the angels, the Books, and the messengers, the same four (with the Last Day) that constitute the tradition's articles of faith. The verse is the sūrah's creed in narrative form — not a command to believe but a description of belief happening, in the Messenger and in those with him, at the moment the Book completes.

**The Articles Enumerated**

The four named objects are the tradition's standard enumeration, and the commentators locate the verse inside the Qur'an's creed-network: the same articles appear in the sūrah's own righteousness-verse — belief in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets (2:177) — and in the sūrah of Women's faith-command, where denying Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, and the Last Day is named as the far straying (4:136). The three passages together give the articles their canonical form, and this verse's list is the one that runs in the order of descent: Allah, then the angels who carry, then the Books carried, then the messengers who receive — the revelation's chain of custody stated as the belief's structure. The commentators note what the verse's position adds: the creed arrives immediately after the audit-verse's account of God's total knowledge and sovereign will (2:284), and the tradition preserves the connection — the Companions' distress at that verse was answered by the revelation of these closing verses, whose first sentence describes the response the audit sought: not evasion but belief, held by the Messenger and shared by the believers. The articles are thus not abstract theology but the distressed community's answer, revealed as the shape faith takes when the heart's contents are known and the will forgives or punishes as it wills.

**We Make No Distinction**

The creed's next clause is spoken in the believers' own voice: *We make no distinction between any of His messengers*. The formula is the sūrah's recurring confession — commanded at 2:136 (we believe in what was revealed to us and to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes; we make no distinction between any of them), at 3:84 in nearly identical words, and listed among the markers of true faith at 4:152 — and the commentators explain its double work. The first work is internal to the faith: the no-distinction clause stands in deliberate tension with the preferment-verse (2:253 — some messengers raised above others), and the tradition's resolution governs both: the ranking concerns the missions and endowments, not the persons' truthfulness or their claim on belief, so the believer ranks what God ranked and distinguishes no one God sent. The second work is polemical, and the commentators state it plainly: the confession distinguishes the believers from the communities that accepted some messengers and rejected others — the Jews who rejected Jesus and Muhammad, the Christians who rejected Muhammad. The no-distinction clause is therefore the sūrah's final word in its long argument with the selective faith of the People of the Book: the community this verse describes receives the whole chain, and its creed is the chain's unbroken acknowledgment.

**We Hear and Obey**

The believers' speech continues into its operative clause: *We hear and obey* — samiʿnā wa-aṭaʿnā, the formula whose meaning the sūrah has been building since its early covenant-scenes. The commentators' cross-reference is exact and deliberate: when the mountain was raised over the covenant-community and they were told to hold firmly and obey, they answered *we hear and disobey* (2:93) — and the calf's love was rooted in their hearts. The closing verse's samiʿnā wa-aṭaʿnā is that answer reversed: the same hearing, the same covenant-context of a book received, and the obedience where the disobedience stood. The tradition reads the reversal as the sūrah's ring-closure at the level of response: the Book that documented one community's hearing-without-obedience documents this community's hearing-with-obedience as its final confession, and the difference between the two is the difference the whole sūrah has been legislating toward — the law heard and the law kept, the ribā abandoned at the command, the debt written at the command, the heart's contents surrendered at the audit-clause. The obedience-clause is also the verse's hinge into its prayer: the community that has just said we hear and obey now asks, in the same breath, for what hearers-and-obeyers still need — forgiveness.

**Your Forgiveness, Our Lord**

The prayer-clause is brief, and the commentators note its grammatical form: *Your forgiveness, our Lord* — an entreaty with the verb implied, grant us Your forgiveness, the request so immediate it dispenses with its own asking-word. The implied imperative is the verse's theology of prayer in miniature: the forgiveness is named as the object alone, without qualification or condition, the way a drowning man names the rope. Its placement inside the creed-sentence is the tradition's point: belief, hearing, and obeying do not close the account — the audit-verse has just described the heart's total registration and the will's forgiveness-or-punishment — and the community's response to its own obedience is to ask forgiveness for how it was performed. The commentators connect the request to the sūrah's entire legislative career: two hundred eighty-some verses of commands kept, prohibitions left, contracts written, increases abandoned — and the keepers' final word is not a claim but a plea. The verse thus defines the obedience it confesses: samʿ and ṭāʿah performed by people who know their performance is audited at the heart-level, and who therefore pair the obedience with the request for its shortcomings' covering. The creed ends, as the tradition's spirituality consistently ends, at the mercy-door.

**To You Is the Final Return**

The verse's last words return the prayer to the sūrah's first theology: *And to You alone is the final return* — the masīr, the journey's end, the same return the sūrah's opening argument named (to Him you are returned, 2:28) and the Day-return verse legislated mindfulness of (2:281). The commentators mark the ring: the Book began with the return as a proof against disbelief and ends with the return as a prayer's horizon — the same destination, held at the beginning as doctrine and at the end as address. The return-clause also gathers the verse's own movement: the belief (in the Revealer), the no-distinction (among the sent), the hearing-and-obeying (toward the commanded), the forgiveness-ask (for the performance) — all four terminate at the One to whom the journey ends, and the termination is the verse's final definition of faith: belief lived as a journey with a known destination, whose travelers confess their creed, keep their obedience, beg their covering, and walk. The sūrah's last legislative word (the documentation, the audit) and its last creedal word meet here in the return: everything written is kept, everything concealed is known, everything obeyed is forgiven — and everything, the verse's closing phrase insists, comes back. The believers' first sentence said they believe in what was revealed from their Lord; their last says where they are going. Between the two stands the whole sūrah, and after the two stands only its final verse — the prayer of the capacity-bounded, which the tradition recites with this one, as the two verses the night is sufficient with.
