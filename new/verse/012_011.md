## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:11

> They said, “O our father! Why do you not trust us with Joseph, although we truly wish him well?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opening Move Attacks the Premise, Not the Request**

Nothing is asked for yet. The brothers begin by interrogating Jacob's refusal, which means the refusal already exists and they have met it before. A first request would have run differently: send him with us. Instead the sentence takes the form of a complaint about his judgement, and the complaint does the work of establishing the ground on which the real request will be made in the next verse. Anyone who has been prepared by this method will recognise it. Once the other party has been made to feel that his caution is unreasonable, the favour he grants afterwards feels like a concession to sense rather than a surrender to pressure. The strategy is deliberate, and the Qur'an reports it in the order it was executed, which allows the reader to watch a manipulation being assembled in real time.

**"What Ails You" Is a Question With an Answer Built In**

The Arabic *mā laka* — what is the matter with you, what ails you — is not a neutral inquiry. It presupposes a defect in the person addressed and invites him to explain it. The idiom is common in the Qur'an and always carries that charge; it is the form used when a speaker has decided that something is wrong with the other's perception rather than with his own. Applied here, the phrase converts a father's protective instinct into a symptom requiring diagnosis. Jacob is not told that he is mistaken about a fact. He is asked why he cannot trust his own sons, a question with no honest answer that does not accuse them. The construction traps him: to justify the refusal he must state a suspicion he cannot prove, and stating it will confirm the picture of an unreasonable old man.

**"We Truly Wish Him Well" Claims the Virtue of Sincerity**

The word behind *we truly wish him well* is from *naṣīḥah*, sincerity, the quality of wanting good for another without reserve — a term the tradition treats as one of the highest social virtues, the substance of the pledge the Prophet ﷺ took from his Companions to advise every Muslim sincerely. The brothers appropriate it. Their sentence has two emphatic particles wrapped around the claim, which in Arabic produces something close to an oath: we are, without doubt, sincere well-wishers toward him. The irony is exact. Sincerity is being asserted by men who agreed two verses earlier to throw the boy into a well, and the assertion is aimed at the one person in the family with the standing to test it. Al-Rāzī observes that the claim was designed to present love and compassion for Joseph, implying that no harm could be intended. It is the first lie of the chapter spoken directly to the father, and it is a lie about a virtue.

**Their Question Confirms That Jacob Had Already Refused**

The verse settles a question the narrative has left open. The brothers had asked before and been turned down, which is why the well plan recorded in verse 10 was probably one of several schemes rather than the first. It also explains the shape of verse 8's grievance: a father who refuses his grown sons their younger brother is a father whose preference has become visible in conduct, not merely in feeling. The refusal is evidence of the partiality they resent. Two readings of Jacob's motive are possible and both fit the text — ordinary protectiveness toward a child, or the specific fear that verse 5 records, that these sons would devise something against him. The second is more likely, since the father in this sūrah is never portrayed as merely anxious. He saw the plot coming and said so aloud.

**Credibility Is Built Before the Favour Is Asked**

The division of labour between this verse and the next is a technique. Verse 11 establishes character; verse 12 makes the request. No demand is issued until the demanders have been presented as sincere and the objector as unreasonably suspicious. Modern readers will recognise the sequence from any negotiation in which one side spends its first minutes repairing its reputation rather than stating its case. The Qur'an's decision to reproduce both stages separately, instead of compressing them into one speech, forces the audience to sit through the preparation. The result is that when the request finally comes — send him out with us tomorrow — it arrives already insulated from refusal, and Jacob's hesitation looks like stubbornness rather than insight.

Worth noticing too is what the verse does not contain: an answer. The rhetorical question is left hanging, and the father's reply comes only after the concrete request of verse 12. Jacob does not argue with the accusation. A man asked why he cannot trust his own sons has no reply that does not either insult them or expose a suspicion he cannot substantiate, and the text shows him declining the trap rather than falling into it. He moves the conversation from his character to the boy's safety, which is the only ground on which he can actually stand. That shift costs him the rhetorical exchange and saves him the appearance of accusing ten men of murder to their faces. It also means the brothers' charge goes unanswered on the record, where it will continue to look reasonable to anyone who hears only their side.

**The Father's Suspicion Was Correct, and That Is the Point**

Everything the brothers say here is false, and the verse is constructed so that the reader knows it. The chapter has already shown the private deliberation (12:8), the two proposals for disposal (12:9), and the compromise of the well (12:10). When the polished public version arrives, its falsity is not inferred; it is remembered. That arrangement produces the sūrah's central dramatic effect. Jacob will be deceived, and the audience will not be. His eventual refusal to believe their later story about the wolf will look like paranoia to anyone reading only verse 17, and like accuracy to anyone who has read this one. Distrust is being vindicated in advance, and the vindication depends on the reader holding two versions of the same family in mind at once — the one presented to the father, and the one presented to God.
