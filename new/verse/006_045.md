## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:45

> So the wrongdoers were utterly uprooted. And all praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence of Removal, Not of Battle**

The verse reports the end of the peoples described in the preceding passage with a verb of cutting off. The image is agricultural as much as military: a people is described as uprooted, removed from the ground entirely, so that nothing of it continues. The Book uses the same verb for the destruction of those who denied the signs in the time of Hūd (7:72), for the disbelievers at Badr (8:7), and for the decree given to Lot about the sinners of his city, that they would be uprooted in the morning (15:66).

The choice matters for how the audience is meant to read the passage. These communities were not defeated in a war; they were removed from the record as communities, and the uprooting is described as a consequence rather than an event. The Book describes the wind that destroyed ʿĀd in the same register, leaving its people like trunks of uprooted palm trees (69:6–7).

**Who Is Being Described**

The wrongdoers in this sentence are the peoples of the previous verses: those who were sent messengers, tested with hardship, hardened by the testing, given ease when the lesson was lost, and then seized at the height of their confidence. The verse closes their account with a single verb, and it does so without naming a single one of them, because the pattern is the subject.

The Book names communities elsewhere for the same reason and with the same effect: the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the towns of Lot. The Makkans would have known those names, and the suppression of the names here is a way of pointing forward. What had happened to the named peoples was the general law, and the general law was still in force.

**Why Praise Follows Judgment**

The most striking feature of the verse is its second half: and all praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds. The formula is the one that opens the Fātiḥah and the one the sūrah itself uses at its own opening (6:1). Placing it directly after a report of destruction is a deliberate theological statement, and the commentators explain it as praise for God's justice and for the patience He showed before the punishment fell.

Read that way, the praise is not a celebration of the destruction. The Book's own account of these episodes stresses the length of time the messengers spent among their peoples, the warnings issued, and the afflictions that preceded the end. Praise after judgment acknowledges that the judgment was right, that no one was wronged, and that the door had been held open far longer than the offenders deserved.

**The Two Vocabularies Held Together**

The sentence joins mercy and severity in a single breath, and the sūrah has been doing that from the beginning. It opened with praise for the Creator of the heavens and the earth and of darkness and light (6:1); it stated that God prescribed mercy for Himself while announcing the gathering for judgment (6:12); it described the wrongdoers as people who ruin only themselves while their Lord could have guided everyone (6:35). The final clause of this verse is the formula that holds the two together.

The Book returns to the pairing in other places where nations are destroyed and praise is given to God for His judgement, and it does so because the alternative is worse. A universe in which the oppressor is never removed is not a universe in which mercy means anything, and the praise is the recognition that the removal is part of the mercy.

**What the Verse Does for the Prophet**

The Prophet had been consoled a few verses earlier with the fact that earlier messengers were rejected and endured until help came (6:34), and with the impossibility of forcing his people to believe (6:35). The passage about the hardened communities supplies the other half of the picture: what happens when a people continues past the point at which the warning has been exhausted.

For a messenger in the middle of a hostile city, that is a difficult and necessary piece of information. It tells him what the end of the story is for a community that refuses, and it tells him that the ending is not arbitrary. The verse does not invite him to look forward to it. It tells him that the matter is in hands that handle it correctly.

**The Praise That Teaches the Reader How to Read the Passage**

Every element of the verse prepares the reader to receive the following passage, which returns to the argument about signs, evidence, and the revelation itself. The sūrah moves from the historical record of ruined communities to the specific objections of the Makkans, and the transition is the praise.

The reader is told to accept the account of the uprooted peoples as part of the goodness of God's government, not as a difficulty to be explained away. A person who can say that praise while reading about the destruction of a nation is a person who has understood the sūrah's argument about who owns the world, who sets its terms, and who is entitled to close an account when the last warning has been delivered.

The Book places the same formula at the beginning of this sūrah, where praise is given to the One who created the heavens and the earth and made darkness and light (6:1), and at the end of this section, where the wrongdoers are uprooted. Read as a frame, the repetition tells the reader that the two statements belong to the same subject. The God who is praised for the creation is the God who is praised for the judgment, and the sūrah offers no version of the first that can be held without the second. The whole of the argument that follows sits inside that frame.
