## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:55

> So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life, then their souls will depart while they are disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Visible Prosperity Is Not Divine Approval**

The verse tells the Prophet not to be impressed by wealth or children possessed by the hypocrites. These are natural blessings, but they become dangerous when observers treat them as proof that Allah approves of the owner's faith. Richness can coexist with rebellion, and a large family can coexist with a heart that refuses gratitude. Numbers and possessions do not reveal the final moral condition of a person.

This warning remains necessary because people read comfort as a verdict. They assume that the wealthy must be wise, the influential must be favored, and the struggling must have failed. Revelation breaks that shortcut. Prosperity may be a trust, a test, a means of good, or a means through which a person becomes more attached to the world. Its meaning is discovered by how it is used, not by how it looks.

**The Same Gift Can Become a Punishment**

Wealth and children are not described as evil in themselves. They become a punishment when they increase fear, distraction, pride, and resistance to Allah. The owner must protect property, manage relationships, defend status, and satisfy desires. If these concerns become the center of life, the very things that appear to give security begin to govern the soul.

Punishment through wealth can include forced separation from it, anxiety over its loss, and the burden of maintaining an identity built upon it. Punishment through children can include pride, possessiveness, and grief when love is severed from surrender to Allah. The blessing remains outwardly attractive, but its inner effect is constriction. A person may possess much and live under the rule of what he possesses.

**The Worldly Life Is the Arena of the Test**

The verse places this punishment in the worldly life, where wealth and children are held. The warning does not claim that every hardship in the world is a punishment or that every prosperous unbeliever is visibly miserable. It describes a moral process: the gifts can draw a person deeper into attachment while he still has time to repent.

This is why delay is not always kindness in the form people recognize. A person may receive more resources after ignoring earlier warnings. The increase can be a chance to become grateful, or it can give the ego more material with which to defend itself. The believer asks Allah to make blessings useful rather than assuming that increase alone is good news.

**Do Not Envy the Burden You Cannot See**

The command is addressed to the Prophet because even a righteous observer sees the surface before the hidden cost. The community may envy someone who has money, heirs, or social security without knowing the fear that controls him. Envy becomes less reasonable when the apparent advantage may be carrying a severe spiritual burden.

This does not mean the believer should refuse lawful prosperity. He may seek wealth to support family, pay debts, give charity, and protect dignity. He simply refuses to admire prosperity detached from obedience. The right prayer is not, “Give me what made that person proud,” but, “Give me what helps me remember You and spend Your gift correctly.”

**Departure Exposes What the Possession Could Not Save**

The verse ends by mentioning the departure of their souls while they remain disbelievers. Death removes the illusion that wealth and children can secure a person permanently. They may delay consequences, but they cannot accompany the soul as a substitute for faith. The final condition matters more than the impressive inventory of a life.

This ending also teaches urgency. Possessions are temporary, and relationships require care before death separates people. A parent should not only provide materially but teach gratitude and truth. A wealthy person should not only accumulate but release a share for Allah. The gift is safest when it has already been converted into worship before the owner is carried beyond the chance to choose.

**Measure Blessings by Their Effect on Worship**

The believer tests wealth and family by asking whether they increase remembrance, justice, and service. If they do, he thanks Allah and remains watchful. If they create pride, fear, or neglect, he treats the condition as a warning rather than defending it because it looks successful.

The verse does not invite contempt for the prosperous. It invites clear sight. A humble poor person may be closer to Allah than a powerful benefactor, and a wealthy servant may be among the most grateful people if he uses the gift well. Only Allah knows the final account. The believer therefore seeks beneficial provision, fears the spiritual cost of attachment, and asks that every blessing carry him toward Allah rather than away from Him. He teaches his family that possession is a trust and that gratitude has practical signs. He pays what is due, refuses dishonest gain, and accepts that children are not extensions of his ego. Wealth becomes safer when it circulates through charity, care, and useful work. Family becomes safer when love is joined to truth. The verse does not ask the servant to despise what Allah gives; it asks him to prevent the gift from becoming the master of his soul.

A household can therefore be a place of worship or a place of distraction. Wealth supports faith when it pays rightful expenses, protects dependents, and opens a path to generosity. Children support faith when they are raised with truth rather than treated as trophies. The same possessions punish when they make the owner afraid to obey, too proud to learn, or too busy to remember Allah. The believer asks not only whether a blessing has increased, but whether the blessing has increased his capacity for gratitude. That question turns admiration into responsibility and guards the heart from mistaking comfort for acceptance.
