## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:58

> We recite ˹all˺ this to you ˹O Prophet˺ as one of the signs and ˹as˺ a wise reminder.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Break in the Speech**

The verses before this one were God's address to Jesus; this one turns to the Prophet of the sūrah, who is told that the narrative he has just received is being recited to him. The change of addressee is unmistakable in the Arabic and it marks the passage off from what preceded it.

The Book uses this device to signal that a story has ended and its lesson is being handed over. What the Prophet has been given is not a legend to be repeated but an account with a purpose, and the purpose is named in the second half of the verse. The reader who has followed the dispute about Jesus and the plotting against him is now told what he has been reading and why.

**"We Recite It to You"**

The verb describes a recitation taking place in the present, and the Book uses it for the act by which revelation is delivered to the Prophet. The same verb appears at the openings of sūrahs where the revelation is being called to the reader's attention, and it places the Prophet in the position of a hearer rather than an author.

The construction has a doctrinal edge in a sūrah that has just been arguing with communities who possessed scriptures. A messenger is being told that the stories of the previous messengers are being recited to him by God, which means his knowledge of them is direct rather than inherited. Every narrative in the Book is presented as coming from the same source as the ones that came before, and the word "recite" keeps that claim alive at the level of the sentence.

**The Two Descriptions: Signs and a Wise Reminder**

The verse calls what it has recited two things. First, the material is a sign, and the Book uses that word for both the verses of revelation and the events of the natural world; both are described as things put in front of a person to be read. Second, the whole of it is a reminder that is wise, and the commentators noted that this phrase is used elsewhere as a name for the Qur'an, with some taking it to refer to the Preserved Tablet from which revelation comes down.

The two descriptions answer two purposes. A sign instructs by pointing at something beyond itself, and the disputes about Jesus, the plots, and the promises all point at the God who governs such matters. A reminder instructs by bringing back what is known, and the sūrah has been reminding its readers of truths their own traditions contained. Both descriptions apply to the passage the reader has just finished, and while the first of them requires him to think, the second requires him to remember.

**The Wisdom Claimed for the Reminder**

Calling the reminder wise is a claim about its composition as well as its content. The Book applies wisdom to the arrangement of revelation in other verses, and it rests the claim on the fit between what is said and the situation that requires it.

The passage as it stands supports the claim. A community troubled by the claims of a neighbouring religion is given a clear account of the prophet at the centre of the dispute, a statement of his own creed, the record of his supporters, the plot against him and its answer, and a statement of the two destinies. Nothing in it is extraneous, and the parts answer the questions in the order that a person's objections actually arise. The verse's closing description is thus not decoration; it is an argument that the reader can test against the material he has read.

**Where the Sūrah Goes From Here**

This verse marks the end of the section that began with the announcement to Mary, and the two verses that follow it address the Prophet directly about what belief in Jesus requires. The sūrah moves from the past to the present, and from the story to the disputants who had raised the story as an argument.

That transition is worth noticing because it explains the use of "we recite" here rather than at the start of the narrative. The sūrah has no interest in telling the story of Jesus for its own sake; the account is being delivered specifically to the man who will have to answer the community that disagrees with him about Jesus, and to the community that will have to hold its position. The verse states the source of the material at the moment the material is about to be put to work.

**"One of the Signs" — the Identity of the Narrated Event**

Calling the narrative a sign places the story of Jesus in the same category as the natural phenomena the Book calls signs, and the comparison is deliberate. A sign in the Qur'anic sense is an event arranged to be read, and its value lies in what it points to rather than in itself.

The verses the reader has just finished contain several such events: a birth announced to a woman, a speaking child, a mission with proofs, a conspiracy, and a removal that defeated the conspiracy. Each could be read as an interesting occurrence or as a claim about the God who governs them, and the verse's first description settles which reading the Book intends. What has been recited is not history for its own sake. It is evidence, and it is placed in front of the reader with that status attached.

**What the Reader Is Meant to Do**

A reminder that is wise makes a demand on the person reminded, since it is possible to hear a reminder and leave it where it was found. The verse places the reader in the position of both the Prophet and the community: the story is recited, and it will be recited again in every generation that recites the sūrah.

What follows is a statement about the truth of the whole account and a warning against doubting it, and then the challenge to the disputants. The verse above is the hinge, and its grammar says something about how the Book understands its own delivery: revelation is not a document handed over once but speech that continues to be recited, which is why the Book's vocabulary for it is dominated by words for speaking and hearing rather than for writing and reading.
