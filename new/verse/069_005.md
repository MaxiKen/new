## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:5

> As for Thamûd, they were destroyed by an overwhelming blast.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thamūd Gets Named Before ʿĀd**

Verse 4 indicted both peoples together; now the sūrah tries them separately, and Thamūd stands first. Chronology would reverse the order, since ʿĀd preceded Thamūd in time, so rhetoric must explain the choice. Thamūd's ruins at al-Ḥijr lay nearer the Prophet's audiences and fresher in their memory — carved facades still staring from cliffsides along the Syrian trade road. Prosecutors open with their strongest exhibit, and the nearer wreck made the nearer warning. The amma...fa construction ("as for...") divides judgment into cases, giving each people its own sentence after their joint charge. Such pairing-and-splitting structures the whole passage through verse 12. Each nation will receive a punishment fitted to its crime, and the fitting itself preaches. Order here serves impact rather than timeline, placing the most visible corpse at the head of the procession.

**One Word Holds Three Punishments**

"Overwhelming blast" renders al-ṭāghiyah, a noun exegetes have unpacked three ways. Most read it as the calamity's utmost severity: the Cry that seized Thamūd (11:67; 54:31), the earthquake that shook their valley (7:78), or the thunderbolt that struck them down (41:13; 51:44). Each identification draws support from a verse naming Thamūd's end, and the strongest scholarly instinct layers rather than chooses — one morning of compounded horrors, quake beneath and shriek above. A second reading hears "rebellion" itself (ṭughyān), so that Thamūd perished by its own uprising against God. A third hears "the overpowering ones," the ringleaders who stampeded the tribe into slaughtering the she-camel. These senses need not compete. The same root binds deed and doom: transgression summoned the transgressive blast. What the ear hears as thunder, the heart should hear as verdict.

**The Blast That Outdid All Bounds**

Root-letters preach here. Ṭ-gh-y means to exceed limits, overflow banks, and burst restraints — the verb of floods and tyrants. Thamūd's punishment therefore matched its sin by shared vocabulary: a people that overstepped every bound met a blast that overstepped every measure. Poetic justice of this precision runs throughout the Book, where punishments rhyme with crimes. Pharaoh drowned in the element he boasted of commanding; Qārūn sank into the earth whose treasures had inflated him; Thamūd shattered under excess answering excess. The definite article adds finality — not a blast but the Blast, the one their valley still remembers. Suddenness compounds severity, since ṭāghiyah connotes a peak reached instantly rather than suffering prolonged. One morning sufficed. Carved mountains that promised eternity became scenery for annihilation before the carvers' breakfast cooled.

**Destroyed by Their Own Rebellion**

The second reading — perishing by ṭughyān, their own rebellion — turns the punishment inward. On this view the verse names cause alongside effect: Thamūd's defiance itself became their executioner. The she-camel embodies that defiance: a living miracle pastured among them by God's mercy, hamstrung by their knives in deliberate contempt (91:13; 54:23-30). Killing the sign meant killing their last bridge to mercy, and the three days' respite that followed only confirmed the sentence. This reading carries the passage's heaviest pastoral weight. External calamities frighten, but self-destruction convicts — hearers must face that sin is not merely punished but punishing, a poison drunk as though it were wine. Both senses cohere: heaven's blast struck from without what rebellion had already hollowed within. The tribe died twice, first in spirit and then in body, three days apart.

**A Minority Can Doom a Majority**

The third reading blames "those who overwhelmed" — the powerful few who stampeded the many. Thamūd's nine ringleaders, named elsewhere as corrupters of the city, pressed the tribe toward the camel's slaughter until the whole people owned the crime (27:48-49). This assigns collective guilt without dissolving individual agency: the majority consented, and consent enrolled them in the minority's deed. Social psychology confirms the pattern — determined factions routinely drag passive crowds past lines no individual would cross alone. The verse thus warns communities, not only persons. Tolerating the loudest corrupters counts as joining them; silence at the slaughter is a hand on the knife. Makkan hearers would have felt the sting, since their own chiefs played the ringleaders' part against the Prophet. Societies choose their persuaders, and the persuaders choose their doom. Thamūd chose flatterers of defiance and harvested a single dawn of thunder.

**Mountains Carved, Then Abandoned**

Setting sharpens the pathos. Thamūd had hollowed dwellings from solid rock, engineering security against every enemy except the one that mattered. Their facades advertised permanence — stone against time, height against assault, craft against nature. Yet the blast ignored architecture entirely, striking through rock as though it were tent-cloth. What engineering promises, only obedience delivers: safety belongs to the sheltered by God, not to the sheltered by geology. Travelers on the Syrian road could verify the moral with their eyes, walking streets of empty stone houses whose builders' bones lay scattered. The Qur'an elsewhere forbids even entering such ruins except weeping, lest the same fate find new tenants. Thamūd's mountains remain; Thamūd does not. Every cliff-tomb preaches the verse's arithmetic: subtraction of a people, remainder of a warning.

**The Pattern Opens With the Nearer Ruin**

Verse 5 completes the first case and sets the template for the rest. Name the people, name their end, and let the pairing preach — that is the sūrah's method through verse 12, applied next to ʿĀd, then Pharaoh, then Noah's flood. Brevity serves memorability: one line per nation, easy for reciters to carry and impossible for hearers to dodge. The verse also escalates the overture's dread into documented fact. Verses 1-3 asked what the Hour was; verse 5 answers with a corpse, saying in effect that lesser hours have already struck and greater ones will follow. Thamūd's dawn of thunder becomes a promissory note for the Day of din. No bridge of mercy softens the transition, because none softened Thamūd's morning. With the nearer ruin displayed, the sūrah turns to the farther one, describing how ʿĀd met its wind.
