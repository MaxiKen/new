## Sūrah an-Naml 27:87

> And ˹beware of˺ the Day the Trumpet will be blown, and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will be horrified ˹to the point of death˺, except those Allah wills ˹to spare˺. And all will come before Him, fully humbled.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sound That Ends the World**

The trumpet appears in many chapters of the Qur'an as the signal that opens the final sequence. Tradition identifies the one who blows it as the angel Isrāfīl, and the Book treats the blast as the hinge between this order and what follows.

Commentators divide over how many blasts there are. Al-Ṭabarī transmits an account of three — this terrifying one, a second causing all to swoon, and a third bringing the resurrection. Al-Qurṭubī reports the view that there are only two, on the grounds that the blast described here is the same as the one in az-Zumar, which causes those in the heavens and the earth to swoon. The disagreement rests on whether two nearly identical descriptions in different chapters name one event or two, and neither side can settle it from the text alone.

**What the Trumpet Might Be**

Most take *ṣūr* to mean a horn, and the word is used that way in Arabic. Al-Rāzī records two other possibilities and both are worth hearing.

On one, the image is military: a trumpeter mustering an army, with the raising of the dead pictured as troops being called into formation. That reading fits this chapter unusually well, since the previous judgment scene described the deniers being driven in ranks using the word the sūrah applied to Sulaymān's marshalled hosts. On the other, *ṣūr* is read as *ṣuwar*, the plural of *ṣūrah*, form — so that what is blown is spirit into bodies. Al-Rāzī reports both without deciding, and the second is the more speculative, depending as it does on an alternative vocalisation.

**"Horrified" — and an Alternative Sense**

The verb is *faziʿa*, ordinarily to be terrified or startled. Al-Qurṭubī notes that it can also mean to respond to a summons with haste, which would describe the resurrected coming quickly out of the graves rather than cowering.

The two senses are not as far apart as they look. Both describe a violent reaction to a sudden sound; they differ over whether the reaction is recoil or movement toward. The verse's second half may favour the first, since those who come to God are described as humbled rather than eager. But the ambiguity is genuine, and a word that means both fright and immediate response is well chosen for an event that is simultaneously a catastrophe and a summons.

**The Exception**

*Illā man shā'a Allāh* — except whom God wills. The verse names an exempt group and does not identify it, and the commentators have filled the gap in several ways.

Al-Ṭabarī reports the view that they are those slain in God's path, whom another chapter describes as alive with their Lord and provided for, and therefore already beyond the terror that strikes the living. Al-Qurṭubī records the view that certain angels are meant, sometimes specified as Jibrīl, Mīkāʾīl, Isrāfīl and the Angel of Death; some extend the exemption to prophets and the saints. None of these is stated in the verse, and the disagreement is of a particular kind. These are not competing reports from the Prophet; they are inferences, each reasoning from some other verse to a group that ought to qualify. The martyr identification is the strongest of them because it argues from scripture — those slain in God's path are elsewhere described as already alive with their Lord, so the terror that strikes the living would not reach them. That is an argument, not a transmission, and it should be received as one. The chapter itself resolves the question differently and better two verses later, by naming a criterion instead of a list. What the text commits to is only that the terror is not universal and that the exemption is God's to grant.

**"All Will Come Before Him, Fully Humbled"**

The final clause changes the register. After the blast and the terror comes an arrival, and everyone who arrives is described by one word: *dākhirīn*, abased, humbled, small.

The Qur'an makes a related point elsewhere by saying each of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone. Both descriptions strip away what people arrive with. The chapter has been full of persons defined by their retinues — a king with hosts of jinn, men and birds; a queen with a council and an army; a Meccan aristocracy with clans behind it. The verse describes a moment in which none of that accompanies anyone. The one figure in an-Naml who arrived at this posture voluntarily was Sheba's queen, who came a great distance and said she had wronged herself.

**Terror as the Qur'an Describes It**

Other chapters fill in what this Day does to people, and the descriptions are consistently about the severing of attachments. A guilty man would ransom himself with his children. Hearts tremble. A man flees from his brother.

None of these is about physical pain; each is about the collapse of the relationships a person's life was built on. That emphasis clarifies what the terror consists of. It is not primarily fear of a penalty, which has not yet been assigned at this point in the sequence, but the exposure of a self with nothing left attached to it. An-Naml has consistently treated relation as the thing that matters — who is heard, who answers, who is spoken to. The trumpet verse describes the moment when every relation except one has ended.

**A Warning Placed After an Invitation**

Notice the arrangement. The previous verse pointed at the night and the day and called them signs. This one describes the blast. The chapter alternates between the gentle evidence and the severe outcome rather than grouping them.

That alternation runs through the whole sūrah. Verse 2 promised guidance and good news, verse 4 described those who wander blindly. Verse 73 called God bountiful, verse 74 said He knows what is concealed. Here the pattern appears one last time, and in the same order: the invitation first, the warning second. What follows in the next verses is the return, with the mountains moving and the deeds weighed, before the chapter ends on a command to recite.
