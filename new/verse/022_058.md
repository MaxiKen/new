## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:58

> As for those who emigrate in the cause of Allah and then are martyred or die, Allah will indeed grant them a good provision. Surely Allah is the Best Provider.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Addresses a Question Nobody Wanted to Ask**

The immediate context explains the wording. Some of the Emigrants had died in Madīnah of illness rather than in battle, and people were saying — within the community — that dying while fighting for God was the better death.

The verse's answer is in its grammar. *Thumma qutilū aw mātū* — then are killed *or die*. The two outcomes are joined by a conjunction that gives no preference, and both are followed by the same promise with the same verb. Whatever hierarchy existed in the community's talk, the revelation declines to ratify it.

That is a substantive ruling rather than a consolation. The emigrant's act was the leaving; whether the leaving ended in a battle or in a bed is not presented as the variable that matters.

The verbs are also worth attending to. *Qutilū* is passive: they *were killed*. It is not *qātalū*, they fought. The verse names what was done to these people rather than what they did, and it places the passive verb alongside *mātū*, they died — which is likewise something that happens to a person rather than something he does. Both branches of the sentence describe a fate, and the act for which the reward is promised sits outside the pair, in the emigration itself.

**What Emigration Was**

*Hājarū fī sabīli Allāh* — those who emigrated in the way of God. The verb shares a root with *hijrah*, the emigration, and the word carries the sense of cutting a tie and going.

The sūrah has already named this act as a crime committed against the believers: they were expelled from their homes (22:40). Emigration is the other side of that expulsion — the same departure, described from the point of view of the person who chose it rather than the people who imposed it.

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the promise attached to it in unambiguous terms: *“Whoever leaves their home, emigrating ˹in the cause of˺ Allah and His Messenger, and then dies, their reward is guaranteed by Allah”* (4:100). The guarantee is tied to the departure and the intention, and death overtaking the emigrant does not interrupt it.

The intention is what makes the act what it is, and the Prophet ﷺ said so in the report that opens al-Bukhārī's collection: actions are reckoned by their intentions, and a person has what he intended — so that whoever emigrates for God and His Messenger has emigrated for God and His Messenger, while whoever emigrates for some worldly gain or for a woman he wishes to marry has emigrated for that (Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907). That is why the verse's phrase *fī sabīli Allāh* is not decorative. Emigration is not defined by the distance travelled but by what the traveller left for.

**"God Will Provide Them a Good Provision"**

*La-yarzuqannahum Allāhu rizqan ḥasanan* — God will provide them a good provision. The verb is emphatic, with the doubled *n* of the strongest form of the future.

The provision is described as *ḥasan*, good or beautiful — a different adjective from the *karīm* provision of verse 50, and the difference deserves attention. *Karīm* described the manner of the giving; *ḥasan* describes the quality of the thing given. Both are promised here, in effect: God gives well, and what He gives is good.

The commentators differed about when this provision arrives. Many took it as the provision of the Garden, owed to the emigrant in the Hereafter. Others took it as a present provision — that the martyr is sustained now, in the interval between death and resurrection, rather than only after it. The second reading has a strong parallel in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, which uses the same root: *"Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead. In fact, they are alive with their Lord, well provided for"* (3:169). On that reading the promise of this verse is not deferred compensation but a description of the present state of the dead, and the dead are described as being provided for rather than as waiting.

**"He Is the Best of Providers"**

*Wa-inna Allāha la-huwa khayru al-rāziqīn* — and God is the best of providers.

The comparative is pointed, because human beings provide too. Families feed their members, patrons maintain their clients, states distribute. The verse does not deny that; it ranks God above every provider and thereby relativises the others.

For an emigrant that ranking was the whole issue. Leaving Makkah meant losing the economic protection of one's clan, which was the only social security a seventh-century Arab had. The people this verse addresses had given up the only provider they had ever known, and they are told that the exchange was not a loss.

The argument is generalised elsewhere, and the generalisation is directed at exactly this fear. *"How many are the creatures that cannot secure their provisions! ˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ provides for them and you ˹as well˺"* (29:60). Provision is not distributed according to who holds the means of producing it; if it were, the argument runs, the animals would starve. The same point is turned into a prohibition: *"Do not kill your children for fear of poverty. We provide for them and for you"* (17:31). The emigrant who has walked out of his clan's protection has not walked out of the arrangement described in those two verses.

**Why the Emigrants Are Named Here**

The sūrah has just stated the two destinations at the judgment (22:56–57), and it now applies the reward to the people who had done the hardest thing the community had been asked to do so far.

The application is also an argument. The permission to fight was granted in verse 39 on the ground that these people had been expelled; verses 40 and 41 described what they would do if established. Verse 58 now says what becomes of those who left and did not come back — which is, in a sūrah that has been insisting that God's help is not a matter of visible outcomes, the assurance the community needed most.

The form of the answer is as important as its content. A community that had begun ranking its dead is answered in the vocabulary of sustenance, not of rank: *rizq*, provision, rather than *darajah*, degree. The verse does not say the two deaths are equal in merit. It says both are provided for, which is the answer a grieving community can actually use, and it removes the comparison rather than settling it.

**A Report Behind the Verse, and Its Limits**

Al-Qurṭubī preserves the occasion: two of the Emigrants died in Madīnah, and the talk in the community was that dying in battle was better than dying at home. The verse is reported to have come down in response to that conversation. Whether or not that specific occasion is accepted, the verse's wording settles the question it raises. Al-Rāzī extended the meaning to everyone who leaves home to serve the religion, and the general reading is the safer one, since the wording names the act rather than the circumstance of the death.

**Death Is Not the Variable**

The last thing to draw out is the verse's treatment of the two deaths. It does not say that dying in bed is as meritorious as dying in battle; it does not rank them at all. What it does is place both under the same promise, which removes the question from the community's jurisdiction.

That is consistent with the sūrah's method throughout. Where human beings are inclined to grade one another, the revelation either supplies a different criterion or declines to grade. Here it does the second, and in doing so it protects the living from the conclusion that those who died quietly died less well.

The protection runs in both directions. The person who died in bed is not diminished, and the person who died fighting is not inflated — and the second half matters as much as the first, because a community that glorifies one kind of death makes the other kind unbearable for the people facing it.

The sūrah has been given to correcting exactly this instinct. It refused to grade the poor who ask against the poor who do not (22:36), it refused to grade the emigrant's manner of dying, and it will close by commanding a striving that is defined by its object rather than by its form (22:78).
