## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:93

> Go with this shirt of mine and cast it over my father’s face, and he will regain his sight. Then come back to me with your whole family.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Commands in One Verse**

The verse contains an instruction about an object and an instruction about a journey. Take this shirt, cast it over my father's face, and he will see; then bring me your family, all together. The first command concerns a single person and a single act, and the second concerns a whole household and a migration. The chapter places them together because they belong to a single purpose: the restoration of Jacob. Sight is restored by the shirt, and the reason for wanting the sight restored is that there will be a son to see. Verse 99 will report both commands fulfilled — the parents received, the family entering Egypt — and verse 100 will show the result.

**The Third Shirt in the Chapter**

Shirts have carried the chapter's evidence from beginning to end. The first was stained with false blood and produced to a father as proof that a wolf had devoured his son (12:18). The second was torn from behind and examined by a household witness, establishing that Joseph was fleeing rather than attacking (12:26–28). The third is this one, and its function reverses the first: a garment belonging to Joseph is carried to Jacob and restores what a garment falsely attributed to him destroyed. The chapter never comments on the pattern. It simply uses the same object three times, for a lie, for a truth and for a healing, and leaves the reader to notice the sequence. The sequence is the chapter's most sustained symbolism and it is never explained. A garment dipped in false blood establishes a death, a garment torn from behind establishes an innocence, and a garment carried to Canaan establishes a healing. Each is offered as evidence, each is trusted by whoever receives it, and the third undoes what the first asserted twenty years earlier.

**"Cast It Over My Father's Face"**

The instruction specifies the face, and the specification is not incidental. Verse 84 reported the injury in the same location: his eyes turned white from the grief he suppressed. The remedy is applied where the damage was done. The commentators do not elaborate on the mechanism, and the verse offers none — no explanation of how a garment affects sight, no statement that the shirt has a property, no claim about the material. What is given is a command, a location and a predicted result. The chapter's economy here is at its most severe: three clauses, and the middle one is a physical instruction. The physical instruction is what makes the healing concrete. Joseph does not say that his father will be restored or that God will cure him; he specifies an object, an action and a body part, and attaches the outcome to the performance. The chapter treats the recovery of sight as an event with a method, ordinary enough to be carried out by a travelling son.

**A Prediction Stated as Certain**

*He will regain his sight.* Joseph does not say perhaps, or if God wills, or that he hopes. The prediction is unconditional, and the commentators explain it as based on divine inspiration rather than on medical judgement. Nothing in Joseph's knowledge of his father would support the claim: he has not seen Jacob in decades, does not know that his eyes have failed, and has received no report of the injury. Verse 84 describes the whiteness of the eyes in Canaan, and verse 93 predicts its reversal in Egypt. The chapter therefore attributes the prediction to the same source as Joseph's other knowledge, which verse 21 described as taught by God. That source has been established repeatedly and is not restated here. The interpreter of two prison dreams, the forecaster of fifteen years of Egyptian weather and the man who told his brother not to grieve now predicts a blind father's recovery, and the chapter places the prediction in the same category as the others: knowledge taught to him, delivered without mechanism, verified by the outcome.

**The Tradition About the Shirt's Origin**

One account records that this shirt was made of the silk of Paradise and was brought to Joseph by Gabriel while he was in the well (12:15). The report is not in the Qur'an and cannot be verified from it, but it explains why the exegetes looked for something unusual about the garment. A shirt that heals blindness by being placed on a face invites an account of its nature, and the tradition supplies one. The Qur'an's own text is content to describe the shirt as Joseph's — *my shirt* — and to attach the healing to a command rather than to a property of the cloth. Attaching the healing to the command keeps the theology clean. A cloth with intrinsic power would become an object of veneration; a cloth that heals because a prophet said to cast it is an instrument, and the power stays where the chapter puts it. The wording is careful to make the shirt a means rather than a cause, which is why it is called his shirt.

**"Bring Me Your Family, All Together"**

The second command names the whole household. The brothers are to return to Canaan and bring the family in its entirety, and the phrase used denotes the people belonging to a man — his household, his kin, those who follow him. The instruction completes the movement that began with the dream in verse 4: eleven stars, a sun and a moon are to be gathered into one place. Joseph does not ask for a visit or for his father alone. He asks for the migration of a family, which is the precondition of verse 99 and verse 100 and of the settlement of the Israelites in Egypt that the rest of the Qur'an's narrative assumes. The comprehensiveness completes the reversal. Joseph was taken from his family alone, at night, by his own brothers, and sold out of the country; he now summons the whole household into it, on his own authority, described by relationship rather than by number. The well removed one boy from Canaan and this verse puts an entire family into Egypt.

**Verse 96 Reports the Fulfilment in the Same Terms**

The bearer of good news arrives, casts the shirt over Jacob's face, and he regains his sight (12:96). The chapter reports the healing in a clause as short as the prediction, and Jacob's response is to remind his children that he had told them he knew from God what they did not know. The verification is therefore complete and public: a man blind from weeping receives his sight by means of a garment sent from Egypt by a son he was told had been eaten by a wolf. Verse 93 is the instruction, verse 94 is the anticipation of its effect, and verse 96 is the report. The chapter gives the healing three verses and no explanation.

**The Shirt Travels the Route the Bloodied Shirt Travelled**

The object's journey reverses an earlier one. Decades before, a garment belonging to Joseph was carried from a well in the wilderness to Jacob in Canaan, stained with blood that was not his, and produced as evidence of his death (12:18). Now a garment belonging to Joseph is carried from Egypt to Jacob in Canaan and produced as the means of his recovery. The same route, the same object type, the same recipient, and the same sender in both cases — the brothers, who carried the first lie and now carry the second truth. The chapter does not draw the comparison. It arranges the two journeys so that the reader draws it, which is the method it has used for every structural echo in the sūrah.
