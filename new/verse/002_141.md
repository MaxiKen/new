## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:141

> That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Bracket Closed**

The verse repeats the statement that appeared seven verses earlier, and the repetition closes the passage that runs from the first occurrence to this one. The material between the two statements concerns the claims of the two communities about Abraham and the patriarchs, the confession of the believers, and the question of guidance and schism, and the whole sequence is now enclosed by the principle of individual accountability.

The Book uses this arrangement when a passage contains a long exchange of claims and counterclaims. The reader receives the principle before the argument and meets it again at the end, so that the material in between is understood as an examination rather than as a verdict on the parties discussed.

**Why the Frame Matters**

The framing keeps the reader from drawing the wrong conclusion from the dispute. The sūrah has described communities that misstated the history of their own ancestors, and a reader might carry away an impression of those communities as entirely defined by that failure. The repeated statement refocuses the passage on what each person and group will be asked about.

The Book applies the principle to the living as well as to the dead. The believers reading the passage are told that they will not be questioned about what the earlier communities did, and the corollary is that they cannot rely on the earlier communities' virtues either. What the passage leaves with the reader is his own account.

**A Community That Has Passed**

The phrase describing the community as having gone before appears in both occurrences, and it locates the ancestors in a past that is closed. The Book treats them as historical figures whose deeds are recorded and whose time for action has ended, and it presents the living as being in a different position.

The verse's wording therefore discourages the practice of arguing with the dead. What the two communities were contesting was a claim about their own standing, and the sentence states that the standing of the ancestors was settled when their lives ended. What remains open is the position of those still alive.

The verb describes a departure that is finished, and the sūrah uses it to place the ancestors beyond the reach of the argument. Their page is closed, and the discussion that follows in the next verses concerns the living. The reader is left with a question he can answer about himself, which is what he intends to do with the account he has received.

**The Same Words With a New Weight**

The sentence is identical to the earlier verse, and its placement gives it a different emphasis. The first occurrence follows a passage about the faith of the patriarchs and the blessings granted to their descendants; the second follows the record of the claims made about them and the confession of the believers. The reader meets the same words after hearing more about what is at stake in the argument.

The repetition also has a rhetorical function. When a statement is repeated after a long discussion, it functions as a conclusion, and the sūrah uses it here to indicate that the discussion of the patriarchs is finished. The passage moves on to the direction of prayer in the next verse, and the transition is marked by the closing of the bracket.

**Individual Accountability Restated**

The Book states the principle of personal accountability in several forms: no soul will bear the burden of another, each person will reap what he has sown, and every person's record will be presented to him. The sūrah uses the principle here to settle a dispute about history rather than to describe the Day of Judgment, and the application shows how far the Book extends the idea.

For the communities being addressed, the consequence is significant. The virtue of Abraham and the favour granted to his descendants could not be drawn on as a credit, and the sins of any earlier group could not be charged to the descendants who had not committed them. What the Book leaves is a relation between each person and God that cannot be mediated by ancestry.

The sentence also rules out the idea that a person could be credited with the obedience of another. The Book repeats the principle in different words across its chapters, stating that no soul carries the burden of another and that each person will be handed a record he will read. The repetition indicates that the temptation it answers is a durable one, since the desire to inherit merit is as old as the communities this sūrah has been describing.

**What the Two Verses Teach About Argument**

Read together, the two occurrences model a way of conducting religious arguments. The Book states the material, allows the reader to hear the claims and the answers, and then reminds him that the outcome for the parties involved is not the reader's business. What belongs to the reader is the truth established by the discussion and his own response to it.

The method protects the community from two errors. It prevents the reader from becoming a judge of the dead, and it prevents him from treating the merits of the dead as his own possession. The Book uses the same technique in its later discussions of the community of Israel, and the reader who has followed this passage will recognise it elsewhere.

**The Transition to the Next Subject**

The verse closes the section on the patriarchs, and the next verse takes up the questions raised about the change of the direction of prayer. The subject changes, and the principle established here still governs it: the question asked by the foolish will be answered, the community will state what it holds, and the outcome will rest with God. The framing of the patriarch passage therefore prepares the reader for the treatment of the next dispute.

The shift is abrupt and deliberate. The sūrah has spent several verses on the claims of two communities about their ancestry, and the next verse begins with the question of the foolish about the direction of prayer. The move tells the reader that the subject of the passage was never the patriarchs themselves; it was the habit of building a religious position on inherited standing, and the direction of prayer becomes the next place where that habit appears.
