## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:141

> That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Sentence, Spoken Twice**

This verse is a word-for-word repetition of 2:134 — the same community that passed, the same earnings, the same disclaimer of accountability. Verbatim repetition within a single argument sequence is rare enough in the Qur'an that its occurrence here demands an account. It is not filler and not a scribal doubling; the two placements carry different weights because they follow different material. The first occurrence came immediately after Abraham's deathbed and the injunction to die in submission (2:132–133), where it cut the nerve of genealogical reliance. The second comes immediately after the exposure of the anachronistic claim that the patriarchs were Jews or Christians, and after the charge of concealing testimony (2:140). The sentence has not changed; the case around it has. Read the first time it was a principle announced; read the second time it is a judgment pronounced.

**The Bracket That Closes the Argument**

Structurally the two occurrences form an inclusio — a bracket enclosing the densest block of sectarian polemic in the sūrah. Inside the arms of the bracket lie the rival invitations of the Jews and Christians (2:135), the community's full confession of faith (2:136), the two exits of belief and opposition (2:137), the dyeing of God (2:138), the disengagement from disputing about God (2:139), and the chronological demolition of the patriarch-annexation (2:140). Everything argued between the brackets is answered by the sentence that closes them. The ancestors whose ownership was fought over are a finished community; their earnings are settled; the living are back where they started, holding only their own accounts. The rhetorical effect is that of a courtroom: evidence heard, claims examined, and then the same ruling that opened the session restated from the bench as the verdict — no new argument required, because the principle was never in dispute.

**Ring Composition and the Second Landing**

Repetition as a structural device — saying the frame twice so that the material between is heard as enclosed — belongs to the Qur'an's own compositional habits, and here it does precise work. When the sentence first appeared, a reader could still hope that the ancestors' merits might be reachable, that lineage might be leveraged into standing. Seven verses later, after the sects have been shown claiming patriarchs who predate their very names, the identical sentence lands as confiscation. The community that passed took its earnings with it, and the claimants who fought over its name have fought over an estate that closed with its death. Nothing about the words changed; the argument between the two occurrences converted an observation into a dispossession. This is why the verse should never be read as a mere duplicate of the first — the duplication is the point, and the second instance carries the accumulated weight of everything it brackets.

**Slow Deeds and Fast Lineage**

The principle has a prophetic commentary that sharpens it into a single image. In a long report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (2699a), Abu Hurayrah narrates the Messenger of God closing a catena of teachings with this sentence: whoever is slowed by his deeds, his lineage will not speed him up. The image is of a race in which a man of noble ancestry is falling behind on merit, and his pedigree — whatever its glories — exerts no forward force. The ḥadīth states exactly what the verse's bracket states: earnings are personal, and lineage is inert. A descendant of prophets walks at his own pace; an heir of the patriarchs' name carries none of the patriarchs' speed. Placed beside 2:141, the report converts the theological rule into a warning addressed to the very audience of the polemic — and to every later reader tempted to borrow velocity from the dead.

**Two Contexts, Two Edges**

The double placement of the verse gives it two distinct applications, and both were live in the sūrah's first audience. At its first occurrence the edge is turned against genealogical complacency: the children of Abraham by blood or claim cannot cash his righteousness, because that community's account closed with its death. At its second occurrence the edge is turned against sectarian appropriation: the claim to own the patriarchs — to make them retroactive members of a party — has just been shown to be chronologically impossible and morally culpable, and the verdict repeats to seal the exposure. One sentence, two boasts, one refutation. And the verse carries a third edge that neither occurrence spells out but both imply: it applies to the Muslims' own reverence for the patriarchs. Honor them, follow their faith, recite their story — but expect no transfer of credit. The bracket encloses the believers too.

**Neither Their Merit Nor Their Guilt**

The final clause deserves its own weight: you will not be accountable for what they have done. The disclaimer runs in both directions, and the second direction is the merciful one. Not only can ancestral virtue not save a descendant; ancestral sin cannot damn him. The sons are not punished for the fathers — a principle the Hebrew scriptures state in their own idiom when Ezekiel insists that the son shall not bear the father's iniquity nor the father the son's (Ezekiel 18:20), and the Qur'an states it in the idiom of burdens elsewhere: no soul bearing a load will bear another's (53:38). Between the two directions stands the individual, unrepresented and unabsorbed: no clan behind him to claim his case, no ancestry beneath him to carry his weight. This is the anthropology the whole bracket has been defending — the human being as a personal account, opened at birth, closed at death, audited by a God who never confuses one ledger with another.
