## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:197

> ˹Commitment to˺ pilgrimage is made in appointed months. Whoever commits to ˹performing˺ pilgrimage, let them stay away from intimate relations, foul language, and arguments during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, Allah ˹fully˺ knows of it. Take ˹necessary˺ provisions ˹for the journey˺—surely the best provision is righteousness. And be mindful of Me, O people of reason!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Months Well Known**

The verse fixes the hajj's calendar: the pilgrimage belongs to appointed months — months well known, as the Arabic puts it, a phrase that presupposes a community already acquainted with them. The exegetes identify them as Shawwāl, Dhū'l-Qaʿdah, and Dhū'l-Ḥijjah, with some limiting the third month's share to its first ten days, since the sacrificial rules change after the Day of Sacrifice. The months are not arbitrary but the Arabian sacred season the previous verse's legislation had already engaged — the time in which the ʿumrah-in-hajj combination was performed, in which the pilgrim caravans moved, and whose sanctity the war-code protected (2:194). Juridically, the designation carries a ruling: according to many authorities it is disliked to enter the iḥrām for the hajj outside these months — a pilgrim should not consecrate himself early and carry the state across the intervening weeks until Dhū'l-Ḥijjah. The phrase *well known* is itself informative: the calendar of the pilgrimage was public knowledge, fixed by practice before the verse confirmed it, and the revelation legislates inside a living custom rather than inventing a season — the same method the fasting-legislation used with Ramadan's moon.

**No Lewdness**

The first prohibition of the consecrated state is *rafath* — lewdness — which the exegetes read primarily as sexual intercourse, and secondarily as speech about it. The legal consequence of the primary sense is the verse's severest: intercourse before the standing at ʿArafah invalidates the hajj entirely, obliging the pilgrim to sacrifice and repeat the pilgrimage the following year. No other infraction of iḥrām carries the pilgrimage's annulment. The secondary reading — speech directed toward, or concerning, women outside one's close kin — extends the prohibition into the tongue's territory and composes with the next two items, so that the consecrated pilgrim is guarded in body and speech at once. The prohibition's logic belongs to iḥrām's whole design: the state suspends the ordinary lawful — scent, cut hair, hunted game, marital intimacy — to concentrate the pilgrim entirely on the rite, and rafath is the strongest of those suspendings, the appetite whose suspension most completely signifies the soul's temporary exit from ordinary life. The months are for God; the body's most private claim is the first thing laid down at their threshold.

**No Iniquity, No Quarreling**

The second and third prohibitions — *fusūq* and *jidāl* — cover sin and strife. The iniquity named is wrongdoing in the general sense, the violations of God's law that iḥrām's holiness makes doubly grave; the quarreling is defined by the exegetes with precision: not intellectual disagreement, which the pilgrimage never forbade, but the interchange that ends in anger and insult — the argument as combat. Al-Qurṭubī supplies the historical instance: the Quraysh used to quarrel over the Station of Abraham (2:125), each party observing a spot different from the other Arabs', and he prefers to read the prohibition as covering disputes over the hajj's places and times — though it may equally target the boasting that accompanied the pre-Islamic pilgrimage, the claims that one's hajj was finer than another's, one's forefathers nobler. The three prohibitions together fence the pilgrim completely: the body (rafath), the deed (fusūq), the tongue (jidāl) — and the fencing is against the pilgrimage's own social conditions, the weeks of crowded travel, shared hardship, and tribal proximity in which these three exactly are the standing temptations. The tradition preserves a saying that gathers all three: an oath is reported that no work done under the sky excels striving in God's cause together with a pilgrimage kept faithfully — one free of lewdness, iniquity, and argument.

**Whatever Good You Do, God Knows**

Between the prohibitions and the provision-command stands the verse's surveillance clause: whatever good you do, God knows it. The placement is exact. The hajj is performed largely unseen — the iḥrām's inner discipline, the patience under crowding and heat, the quarrel swallowed rather than returned, the charity given anonymously on the road — and much of its goodness has no human witness at all. The clause assures the pilgrim that the rite's invisible ledger is kept: the good unrecorded by people is known to God, and the knowing is the reward's guarantee. The same clause balances the prohibitions from the other side: the lewdness, iniquity, and argument are equally known, so the verse does not merely promise but implicitly warns — the knowledge of God covers the pilgrim's whole conduct in the sacred state, the kept and the broken alike. Read with the previous verse's closing (know that God is severe in retribution), the two clauses form the hajj's moral frame: severe knowledge of the violation, complete knowledge of the good, and between them the pilgrim's freedom, exercised where no crowd and no companion can see.

**Take Provision — the Best Provision Is Taqwā**

The verse's most-quoted movement begins as practical legislation and pivots into its spiritual sense. Take provision, the pilgrims are told — and al-Qurṭubī explains the command concretely: come materially prepared for the hajj, carrying your food and needs, not relying on the locals and the other pilgrims for shelter and sustenance; and the pilgrimage, in any case, is not incumbent on those without the financial means. The historical occasion behind the command is reported: some early pilgrims set out with nothing, trusting that God would provide, and became a burden on the way — and the verse legislated the trust that carries its own bread. Then the pivot: *the best provision is taqwā* — the righteousness, the God-consciousness, that the pilgrim must also pack. Al-Qurṭubī states the spiritual reading: God-consciousness is the travel-food of the next life, mirroring the supplies packed for this one. The two provisions are not alternatives but a hierarchy: the material provision makes the journey possible, the God-consciousness makes it worth making, and the verse names the second best so that the first is never mistaken for sufficient. The pilgrim who arrives fed but unreverent has packed the lesser baggage alone.

**O People of the Kernels**

The verse ends with an address that gathers its whole argument: be mindful of Me, O people of reason — ūlū al-albāb, the possessors of the lubb, the kernel within the shell. The title is the Qur'an's honorific for the wisdom-capable, used where its deepest thinking is demanded: over the qiṣāṣ's paradox of life-in-retaliation (2:179), over wisdom's gift, over the signs of creation. Its use here is pointed. The hajj is the rite most exposed to the shell-level reading — a journey of bodies, crowds, animals, shaved heads, and stones thrown at pillars — and the verse summons exactly the readers who can crack it: those who see in the provision-command a lesson about dependence, in the quarrel-prohibition a discipline of the soul's heat, in the months a calendar of consecrated time, and in the whole rite the rehearsal of the gathering to God. The shift from the third person to the first is the verse's final intimacy: after legislating the rite in the language of law — months, prohibitions, provisions — God commands in His own name, *be mindful of Me*, so that the pilgrimage's every external act is re-anchored in the internal relation. The people of the kernels are told where the hajj's kernel lies: not in the journey completed but in the Me remembered through it.
