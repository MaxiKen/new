## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:67

> Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian; he submitted in all uprightness and was not a polytheist.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Negations Before an Affirmation**

The verse answers the question of what Abraham was, and it begins by excluding the two categories that the disputants wanted him to occupy. He was neither a Jew nor a Christian, and he was not a polytheist. Only after clearing both names does the sentence say what he was.

The order is deliberate, and it is the order of a legal finding that first removes the parties' claims and then states the position. The Book uses this technique where an argument has become a competition over labels: it denies the labels and supplies the description, which is why the verse's middle term carries the whole weight of the sentence. What is affirmed after the exclusions is not a faction but a disposition.

**Ḥanīf: Turning Away and Turning Toward**

The word rendered "in all uprightness" describes a person who inclines away from the worship of anything other than God. In the Book's usage the term is tied to Abraham specifically, and it carries the idea of a deliberate turn — away from the practices of a people who had gone astray, and toward the one to whom worship belongs.

The Book pairs the term with Abraham in several places. The sūrah of the Cattle records the Prophet's own declaration of following the way of Abraham, the upright, who was not of the polytheists (6:161); the sūrah of the Bees describes Abraham as a model of excellence, devoted to God and upright, and again denies that he was a polytheist (16:120); and the sūrah of the Cow instructs the believers to say that their way is that of Abraham, the upright (2:135). The recurrence shows that the term functions in the Book as the name of a religion that existed before the books, which is why it can serve as the shared ground in this dispute.

**The Third Negation**

The clause denying that Abraham was a polytheist is not a repetition of the first two denials, and it deserves its own attention. A person could be neither Jewish nor Christian and still be a worshipper of other gods, since those were not the only options available in the ancient world.

The Book adds the clause because the argument it is conducting concerns the oneness of God, and the sūrah has been occupied with exactly that subject for many verses. Abraham's refusal of idols is the pattern to which the sūrah keeps returning, and the verse identifies his religion with that refusal before it says anything else. The positive content of his faith is stated as a combination of turning away from false objects and facing the one God, and the Book is careful to name both halves of the movement.

**What the Verse Removes From the Dispute**

The exclusions answer the claims of both communities at once, and they do so without insult. Neither community is told that its tradition is false; the verse simply declines to trace Abraham's religion to either.

That restraint is important for what the sūrah is doing. It has just criticised the two communities for arguing about the ancestor without knowledge, and it now supplies a description that neither can claim as its own property. The effect is to remove Abraham from the argument about communal standing and to place him where the Book consistently puts the prophets: in a category defined by conduct toward God rather than by membership in a later community. What the following verse does is to say who does have a claim on him, and the answer is stated in terms of following rather than descent.

**The Prophet's Own Position in the Sentence**

The sūrah's readers would hear this verse as a description rather more than a history, since the same three clauses describe the position of the Muslim community in relation to the older traditions. It is neither Jewish nor Christian, it refuses the association of anything with God, and its founding model is the man who did the same before any of the books existed.

That is why the verse can carry the argument forward with a single sentence. Abraham is being presented not as the property of a lineage but as the first instance of a stance that the sūrah wants its readers to recognise as their own, and the identification is made twice within the sentence: once by the word standing in the middle and once by the denial at the end.

**The Shape of the Argument**

The argument of the sūrah with the scriptured communities has been building to this point. It has shown that Jesus was created as Adam was, that the communities dispute about Abraham without information, and now that Abraham belonged to neither of them.

Each step removes a claim and leaves the reader with a smaller field of disagreement. What remains after this verse is the question of who can claim to be his descendant in spirit, and the Book answers it in the next lines with a definition based on following rather than ancestry. The description of Abraham as upright and free of partnership is thus not only a correction of the past. It is the criterion the sūrah applies to everyone who reads the verse.

The three clauses also read as a portrait of a man who stood alone. Uprightness in this vocabulary is a direction taken by an individual rather than a position inherited from a group, and the denial of polytheism is a statement about a person surrounded by it. Abraham's story in the Book is exactly that: a single figure arguing with his people, breaking the images, and being thrown into a fire for it. The verse's grammar is the grammar of that life.
