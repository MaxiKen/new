## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:32

> but persisted in denial and turned away,

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Omissions to Commissions**

The previous verse named two things this person did not do: he did not affirm the truth, and he did not pray. This verse names two things he did do. The movement from omission to commission is deliberate, and it traces the ordinary progress of unbelief. Neglect is where it starts — a person simply never gets around to the matter — but neglect does not stay still. Once the reminders have been ignored often enough, ignoring them hardens into a verdict about them, and the person moves from not attending to actively rejecting. The sūrah is describing a trajectory rather than a pair of faults, and the next verse will carry it one step further. The distinction also settles a question about responsibility. Omissions can be excused by weakness, forgetfulness, or pressure; commissions cannot, because they require a person to have acted. By pairing the two, the sūrah removes the excuse before it can be offered. This man is not being condemned for failing to be attentive. He is being condemned for having reached a conclusion and then conducted himself in accordance with it.

**Takdhīb: Declaring It False**

The first verb is from the root that means to lie and, in this form, to declare something a lie. It is stronger than disbelief and different in kind. A person who does not believe may simply have never considered the claim; a person who declares it false has considered it and passed judgement. That is the significance of the root's constant appearance in the Qur'an's accounts of the messengers' audiences: what was rejected was not an unexamined proposition but a demonstrated one, and the rejection took the form of a counter-claim. *“Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, ‘Ancient fables!’”* (83:13). Calling the message old stories is a verdict on it, not an absence of information about it. The Qur'an goes further and states that the verdict can be reached against the person's own conviction: *“And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly.”* (27:14). Denial here is not the absence of belief but its suppression, and the motive named is arrogance rather than doubt. That single verse settles the sūrah's underlying claim: what the denier lacks is not evidence but the willingness to be the kind of person the evidence would make him.

**Tawallā: the Turn of the Back**

The second verb is physical. *Tawallā* means to turn away, to give something one's back, and the Qur'an uses it wherever it wants to describe a deliberate withdrawal rather than a gradual drift. The pairing of the two verbs is fixed in the Qur'an's vocabulary — *“who deny and turn away”* (92:16) — and the order is always the same: a verdict is formed, and then the person turns his back on what he has judged. The image is of a man who has decided and now physically removes himself from the place where the thing is being said. The decision comes first and the departure follows, which is why the Qur'an treats rejection as an act of the will rather than a failure of the evidence. The same point is made to the Prophet in a verse of consolation, and it identifies what was actually being rejected: *“It is not your honesty they question—it is Allah’s signs that the wrongdoers deny.”* (6:33). The objection was never to the messenger's character, which was not in dispute; it was to the claim being made on their lives. Turning away is the physical form of that refusal.

**Why Denial Is Not Ignorance**

The sūrah's whole account rests on this distinction, and it is worth stating plainly because the alternative reading is common. If unbelief were ignorance, the remedy would be instruction, and a person could plead that he had never been told. The Qur'an forecloses that plea in the strongest terms: a man who has heard the message, understood it, and declared it a lie has no such defence, and his responsibility is precisely located in the verdict he reached. The Qur'an describes the hardening that produces such verdicts as something a person does to himself over time — *“their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!”* (83:14) — and the staining is the accumulated effect of repeated refusals.

**The Escalation Across Three Verses**

The sequence the sūrah has now laid out is worth reading as a whole. Verse 31: he neither believed nor prayed — the private life, empty of what should fill it. Verse 32: he declared the message false and turned away — the verdict formed and the back turned. Verse 33 will show him going home and swaggering about it — the verdict performed in front of an audience. This is how a position becomes an identity. What began as not attending ends as a public self, and the distance between the first stage and the last is covered without any single decision that felt decisive at the time.

**What Turning Away Looks Like Now**

The description has a contemporary shape that the Qur'an itself supplies elsewhere. A person can be told the same truth repeatedly and move further from it each time: *“We have surely varied ˹the signs˺ in this Quran so perhaps they may be mindful, but it only drives them farther away.”* (17:41). The remedy is not to vary the message more cleverly, since the variation was already there. The obstruction is in the will of the hearer, and the sūrah's answer to it is the one it has been giving since the first verse: the Day is coming whether or not he turns his back on it, and turning away does not alter what waits at the end of the road.
