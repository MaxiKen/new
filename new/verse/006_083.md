## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:83

> This was the argument We gave Abraham against his people. We elevate in rank whoever We please. Surely your Lord is All-Wise, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Credited to God**

This was the argument We gave Abraham against his people. The sentence closes the pericope with the Book's own verdict on the sequence just related, and it settles the question the commentators had raised about the star, the moon, and the sun. Whatever Abraham said and however the statements are read, the discourse as a whole is described here as something given to him rather than as something he produced, and the giving is attributed to God.

The Book uses the vocabulary of proof for the mission of the messengers generally when it says that they were sent as bearers of news and warnings so that humanity would have no excuse before God after their coming (4:165). That statement explains what the argument was for: the demonstration given to Abraham removed the ground on which his people could plead ignorance of their Lord, and the Book presents it as a case so well made that no answer to it was available.

**Abraham's Case Elsewhere in the Book**

The Book records Abraham making the same kind of case in other settings and in other forms. In the sūrah of the Cow he argues with the king who disputed with him about his Lord, and he answers the claim to give life and death with a demonstration that the king cannot perform (2:258); in the sūrah of the Prophets he questions his people about the statues to which they are devoted and then acts against them (21:52–58); and in the sūrah of the Poets he recites the catalogue of what his Lord has done for him, ending with the promise of forgiveness on the day of judgment (26:69–82).

Each version of the case is a demonstration from the created order or from the speaker's own dependence. The sūrah's version, which moves through the star, the moon, and the sun, is the one the Book places in the middle of this sūrah because it matches the sūrah's argument from the heavens and the earth.

**The Elevation of Whom He Pleases**

We elevate in rank whoever We please. The sentence states the principle that explains why Abraham could see what his people could not, and it repeats a formula the Book uses whenever it accounts for differences between people. The sūrah of Joseph uses the same words at the moment when Joseph's plan succeeds, noting that God elevates in rank whoever He wills and that above everyone with knowledge is the One who knows all (12:76). The sūrah of the Gatherings states that God will elevate those who believe and those given knowledge by degrees (58:11).

The principle also answers a question the sūrah raised much earlier, in the passage about the poor believers and the wealthy notables. The sūrah said that God tests people by means of one another (6:53), and here it states the same arrangement in terms of rank. Differences of understanding and of station are part of the design, and the sūrah's closing verse will return to the subject when it describes the ranks by which people are tested with what they have been given (6:165).

**The Two Names at the End**

The verse closes with the All-Wise and the All-Knowing, the same pair that ends the sūrah's declaration about creation and the trumpet (6:73) and that appears throughout the Book in statements about God's ordering of human affairs. The names fit the sentence they follow: the elevation of some over others is the act of a being whose knowledge covers what He is arranging and whose wisdom determines where each one is placed.

The Book attaches these names to the same subject in several passages. It says that the Lord is All-Wise, All-Knowing at the end of the verse in which Abraham's case is described as God's argument, and it uses the pair in connection with the giving of scripture and the sending of messengers. The sentence therefore states the principle and then names the attributes that make it just.

**What the Verse Does for the Sūrah's Argument**

The verse is the hinge on which the sūrah turns from the dispute with the Makkans to the line of prophets. It states that the case for God's oneness which the sūrah has been building was given to Abraham as a gift, and it states that the gift was measured out by a wisdom that distributes its gifts as it will. The audience's objection to the Prophet's mission, which was that he was a man of no particular standing, is answered by the same statement.

That answer had already been given in the sūrah's discussion of the poor believers, where the Book said plainly that God knows best who is grateful (6:53). Here the same principle is applied to the greatest of the messengers, and the application shows how far the principle reaches. Neither poverty nor wealth, neither obscurity nor fame, determines who receives understanding from God.

**The Transition to the Prophetic Line**

The verses that follow list the men who inherited the same guidance: Isaac and Jacob, Noah and his descendants, David and Solomon, Job and Joseph, Moses and Aaron (6:84), and then Zachariah, John, Jesus, and Elias (6:85), and then Ishmael, Elisha, Jonah, and Lot (6:86). The sūrah has placed Abraham at the head of a chain, and the gift of guidance is described as passing through it.

That arrangement gives the audience a way to read their own history. The Prophet being addressed in Makkah stood in a line whose members had all been given what Abraham was given, and the sūrah's next verses name them one by one so that the pattern cannot be mistaken for a claim about one exceptional man.
