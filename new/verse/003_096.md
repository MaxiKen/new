## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:96

> Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A House for All People**

The verse states that the first house established for humanity was the one at Bakkah, and it describes that house with two things: blessing and guidance for all peoples. The claim is about priority and about purpose, and both are stated in a single sentence.

The word for humanity is the widest available, and it is the term that carries the verse's meaning. The House was not founded for one tribe or one lineage; the verse presents it as the first place of worship set up for the whole of the human race. That universal framing is why the sūrah can move from a dispute about ancestry to a discussion of pilgrimage, since the House belongs to a wider public than the disputants had been assuming.

**Bakkah and Makkah**

Bakkah is the name used here, and the commentators identified it with Makkah, treating the difference between the two forms as a common alternation between the sounds m and b. Some held that Bakkah designated the ground on which the House itself stands while Makkah referred to the settlement around it.

Others connected the name with a root meaning to crowd, which they explained by the gathering of pilgrims at the site and by the concentration of worshippers around it. The two explanations are not in competition. One is topographical and one is etymological, and both attach the name to the feature of the place that the verse goes on to make central, which is the assembly of people who come to it.

**Blessing and Guidance**

The verse names blessing and guidance as the House's two characteristics. The commentators noted that the word for blessing carries the senses of growth and permanence, which suits a place whose significance has outlasted the empires around it.

The guidance is stated as being for all peoples, matching the universality of the verse's opening. In the Book's usage, a place does not guide by itself; it guides by directing those who come to it toward their Lord, and the next verse names the signs that stand in it. The two words also answer the two needs of a visitor: blessing for his situation in the world and guidance for his relation to God, which is the same pairing the Book uses when it describes what the prophets brought.

**The First House and the Shrine at Jerusalem**

A well-known report gives the verse's priority a concrete shape. Abū Dharr asked the Prophet which mosque was built first on the earth, and was told it was the Sacred Mosque; asked which came next, he was told the Aqṣā Mosque; and when he asked how long separated them, he was told forty years (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3366).

The report establishes what the verse claims without requiring the reader to imagine the House as the first building of any kind. The Book elsewhere reports Abraham and Ishmael raising its foundations and reports that God assigned the site to Abraham, and the two accounts together give the House a foundation in the earliest period of worship. The narration's final instruction, that prayer should be performed wherever its time falls due, keeps the honour of the House from becoming a reason to neglect worship elsewhere, which is a useful balance in a passage that is about to lay an obligation on those who can travel.

**The House and the Debate the Sūrah Is Conducting**

The verse's position in the sūrah is deliberate. The argument before it had established that the practices attributed to Abraham did not belong to his lifetime, and that what remains of his legacy is the upright way toward God.

The House enters the discussion as the visible form of that legacy. A community arguing about which of them inherited Abraham is shown a building whose foundation the Book attributes to him and whose purpose is the worship of God alone, and the verse's description of it as a place of blessing and guidance for humanity reopens the question of who actually honours what he built. The next verse specifies the signs in it and the duty attached to it, and the reader arrives at the obligation with the house's universal purpose already established.

**Guidance That Comes From a Place**

The phrase about guidance for all peoples raises a question that the commentators treated at length, which is how a location can be a source of guidance. Their answer was that the House guides by gathering, by directing worship, and by standing as a permanent witness to the worship of one God.

In the account of al-Rāzī, the House draws people of exalted aspiration and luminous inner life, and at every hour some portion of humanity faces it in prayer, a condition that has held for thousands of years. Whatever the reader makes of the imagery, the verse's own wording keeps the House subordinate to its Lord: the guidance is attached to a place that exists for worship, and the sūrah's next verse attaches an obligation to it which the Book elsewhere ties to ability rather than to rank.

**Before the Building Stood**

The verse speaks about a house being established, and the commentators distinguished between the site and the stones. Several reported traditions tracing the Kaʿbah's foundation to the time of Adam, and on that reading the verse's priority refers to the ground plan and the place where worship was first appointed rather than to the masonry of any particular age.

Abraham's role in the Book's account is then to raise foundations that already had a location, which is how the verse that reports him and Ishmael at work reads: they raise the foundation of the House, and they pray as they do it. The distinction keeps the verse's claim about humanity and worship intact while leaving the archaeology of the site to those who studied it, and it explains why the sūrah can treat the House as older than the community that now faces it.
