## Sūrah an-Naml 27:33

> They responded, “We are a people of strength and great ˹military˺ might, but the decision is yours, so decide what you will command.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Answer in Two Movements**

The council's reply has a hinge in the middle. First a statement of capability: we possess strength and severe force. Then a withdrawal: the command is yours. They report what they can do and decline to say what should be done.

Al-Ṭabarī reads the first half as a reference to their worldly power and military strength, with the implication that they could back her in a fight. That is the natural sense of the two nouns, *quwwah* and *ba's shadīd*, the second of which the Qur'an regularly uses for martial force. What they are telling her is that resistance is available as an option. What they are not telling her is that she should take it. Al-Rāzī judged this a good answer precisely for its balance — deference to the queen combined with a display of strength — and the judgment is fair. They have answered the question she could not answer herself, which is what her forces can bear, and left her the question only she can answer.

**They Do Not Push for War**

The restraint here is easy to overlook and is genuinely unusual. A military council informing its ruler that it possesses great might, in response to a foreign demand for submission, is one sentence away from recommending that the might be used. They do not take the step.

Compare the councils elsewhere in the Qur'an. Pharaoh's chiefs press him to act against Mūsā, warning that the land will be ruined if he does not. The notables of earlier peoples consistently harden their rulers against the prophets sent to them. Sheba's *mala'* break the pattern. The phrase *al-amru ilayki* — the matter is yours — hands back a decision they could have shaped, in circumstances where the shaping would have favoured their own standing. Generals who argue for war argue for their own importance; these ones report and stand down. Whatever this court's failings in religion, its chiefs do not pursue their institutional interest at the cost of their state.

**Strength Is Stated as Fact, Not as Boast**

The Arabic construction is worth noticing: *naḥnu ulū quwwatin wa ulū ba'sin shadīd* — we are possessors of strength and possessors of severe force. The form is descriptive. They claim possession of a capacity rather than asserting they would prevail.

That distinction is the difference between an inventory and a prediction. They do not say they can defeat Sulaymān, that his forces are overrated, or that the kingdom has nothing to fear. They say what they have. This is the correct form for advice given to someone who must weigh it against information the advisors lack, and it leaves room for the queen's counter-argument two verses on, which concerns what happens when kings enter a land rather than which army is larger. Had they claimed victory, she would have had to contradict them. Because they claimed only capacity, she can accept their report in full and still reject the course it seems to point toward.

**Her Question Was Answered on Its Own Terms**

She had asked for a ruling — *aftūnī*, give me your judgment. They gave her something narrower: a capability assessment and a formal deferral. On the surface that looks like an evasion of the question she asked.

It is better read as an accurate scoping of what they were competent to answer. The letter demanded submission in religious terms, framed in the name of a God this court did not acknowledge. Military chiefs are not qualified to rule on that, and they do not pretend to be. What they can speak to is whether the kingdom has the means to refuse, and they speak to exactly that. Ibn Kathīr's suggestion that she probably knew more than they did fits this reading: she had understood the letter and they had understood the army, and she asked them anyway. A ruler who consults specialists and receives an answer bounded by their specialism has received a good answer, even if it is not the one that settles the matter.

**The Decision Returns to Where It Started**

The exchange ends with the burden back on the queen, and the narrative structure makes something clear about consultation that the chapter is quietly arguing. Asking for advice does not transfer responsibility. She consulted honestly, received an honest reply, and remains the one who must choose.

The Qur'an's picture of *shūrā* has this shape throughout. The Prophet ﷺ was commanded to consult his companions in affairs and then, in the same verse, to put his trust in God once he had resolved — so consultation feeds a decision that one person still makes and answers for. Sheba's queen is shown operating the same way three thousand years earlier. She does not hide behind her council's assessment, and she does not treat their deferral as an inconvenience. She takes the decision, and the reasoning she gives for it is the most interesting thing she says in the entire chapter.

**What the Reply Reveals About the Kingdom**

A council's manner of answering tells you how a state is run, and this one is informative. The chiefs are confident enough to state their capacity plainly, disciplined enough not to advocate, and secure enough not to fear that deferring will cost them influence.

That combination requires a ruler who has not punished honest advice in the past. Courts learn quickly what kind of speech is safe, and the safe speech in most of the Qur'an's royal narratives is flattery or incitement. Sheba's notables produce neither, which suggests a working relationship built before this crisis arrived. The detail matters for the story's outcome. A queen surrounded by men who told her what she wanted to hear would have had no external check on her thinking, and her eventual willingness to revise her position in Sulaymān's palace would be less credible. She is shown, in this exchange, to be someone who receives accurate information from the people around her, which is the precondition for changing her mind when the evidence changes.
