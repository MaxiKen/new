## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:34

> For every community We appointed a rite of sacrifice so that they may pronounce the Name of Allah over the sacrificial animals He has provided for them. For your God is only One God, so submit yourselves to Him ˹alone˺. And give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to the humble:

**Expanded Commentary**

**Every Community Got a Rite**

*Wa-li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan* — and for every community We appointed a rite. The word *mansak* refers here to sacrifice specifically, though some commentators took it as religious ritual generally and others as the place at which the ritual is performed.

Taken as sacrifice, the claim is anthropologically striking: there has never been a community without it. The Qur'an is not embarrassed by the universality of the practice it prescribes; it explains it. Sacrifice was given to every people, which means the institution is not a tribal custom the Arabs invented and not a concession to them.

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the same principle for law and way of life: *“To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life. If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given ˹each of˺ you”* (5:48). Sūrah al-Naḥl states it for messengers: *“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, ‘Worship Allah and shun false gods’”* (16:36), and Sūrah Yūnus for the warning itself: *“And for every community there is a messenger”* (10:47).

**Why the Rite Was Given: to Mention the Name**

*Li-yadhkurū isma Allāhi ʿalā mā razaqahum min bahīmati al-anʿām* — so that they mention God's name over what He provided them of the grazing livestock.

The purpose clause is the verse's heart. The rite exists so that the name is pronounced, and the name is pronounced over something described as provision. Two ideas are fused: the animal came from God, and the act of killing it is to be performed with God named over it.

This is what separates sacrifice from slaughter in the Qur'an's economy. An animal can be killed for food anywhere; what makes an act a rite is the invocation and the dedication. Every community had the institution; what the Qur'an insists on is the name spoken over it, which is why verse 30 placed the prohibition on meat dedicated to other than God at the centre of its dietary ruling.

**"Your God Is One God"**

*Fa-ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥidun* — your God is one God. The sentence is the conclusion the universality of the rite was building toward: every community was given the same institution because every community was addressing the same God.

*Wāḥid* carries both senses that the English "one" carries — single, and the same. The commentators noted both: God is one, and the God of every community is identical. That is why the diversity of rites is not evidence against unity: the plurality was ordained (5:48), and the object is not plural.

The Qur'an states the first half of this claim in its baldest form elsewhere: *“Your God is ˹only˺ One God. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”* (2:163).

**"So Submit to Him"**

*Fa-lahu aslimū* — so to Him submit. The imperative follows directly from the oneness, and the inference is tight: if the God is one and the same, the appropriate response is a single posture of surrender rather than the negotiation of rival claims.

The verb is *aslama*, to hand oneself over whole, and it is the root of the word *islam*. The religion's own name is a description of an act rather than of a tribe or a founder, which is why the Qur'an can attribute it to Abraham and to every messenger before him.

**The Good News Is Addressed to the Humble**

*Wa-bashshir al-mukhbitīna* — and give good news to the humble. The word *mukhbit* is unusual and dense: it describes someone who is quiet, lowly, settled — a person whose interior has gone still.

The choice of this word over "the believers" or "the righteous" is pointed. The verse has just said that God is one and that everyone should submit to Him; the people who actually do it are described by a quality of temperament rather than by a credential. Not the learned, not the powerful, not the scrupulous — the ones whose hearts have stopped arguing.

That is also why the good news is given *to* them through the Prophet rather than announced about them. They are the audience that can hear it, and the sūrah will describe them in four particulars.

**The Argument the Verse Settles With the Quraysh**

The claim that every community was given a rite is also an answer to the objection the Qur'an records from its opponents more than once: that they follow what they found their fathers doing.

If inherited practice settled the question, every community would be right by definition and no messenger would ever have been sent against anyone. The Qur'an's response is not to deny the inheritance but to relocate the authority. The rite is inherited; the name pronounced over it is not. What distinguishes a sacrifice from a slaughter is not its antiquity but the God it is addressed to.

That is why the verse moves from the universality of the rite straight to the oneness of God in the same sentence. The plurality of rites is granted and rendered harmless in one move: many rites, one name, and the name is what counts.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Argument**

Verse 34 is a hinge in the passage. It closes the legislation on sacrifice by widening it to all of human history, and by grounding the whole institution in the oneness of God. What follows is no longer about animals. Verses 35 to 37 will describe the people this legislation produces, and the description will begin where this verse ends — with the interior state.
