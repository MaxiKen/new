## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:280

> If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt, postpone it until a time of ease. And if you waive it as an act of charity, it will be better for you, if only you knew.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Struggling Debtor**

The ribā-block turns from the creditor's sin to the debtor's distress, and its legislation begins with recognition: *if it is difficult for someone to repay a debt* — the ʿusrah, the straitness, the hard circumstance that makes the due date arrive against an empty hand. The verse does not treat the struggling debtor as a defaulter to be pressed but as a condition to be waited out: *postpone it until a time of ease*. The commentators mark the command's addressee and its force: the creditor who holds a matured debt against a debtor in genuine straits is ordered to grant the respite — the inaẓār, the looking-along, the patience of the term extended. The jurisprudence that grew from the clause distinguished the straitened debtor from the evasive one (the mūsil, the procrastinator with means, whose delay is ẓulm in the tradition's famous saying), and assigned this verse to the former: where the difficulty is real, the postponement is not the creditor's option but his obligation until the ease arrives. The verse thus completes the block's debtor-protection: the increase was cancelled at the principal (2:279), and now the principal itself is granted patience — the debt survives, and the debtor survives it.

**The Respite as Charity**

The verse's second movement converts the obligation into an invitation and ranks the conversion: *if you waive it as an act of charity, it will be better for you*. The waiving — the ṣadaqah-word the giving-section built its fifteen verses on — is applied here to the debt itself: the creditor may release not the increase (already cancelled) but the principal, turning the loan's remainder into a gift. The tradition's arithmetic of the respite makes the invitation's gradient explicit: a ḥadīth the exegetes cite states that whoever grants delay to one in difficulty is credited an act of charity for each day of the delay — the patience itself generating the giving's reward day by day — while the outright waiving is the section's own better, the excellence beyond the duty. Al-Qurṭubī's ruling fixes the two ranks' legal relation: the respite is commanded because the debtor's difficulty is real, but the principal remains the creditor's claim — the verse encourages the waiving without requiring it, since the lender's capital is legitimate property the law protects (the neither-wronged half of 2:279). The structure is the sūrah's characteristic ethics one final time: the floor legislated (postpone), the height pointed at (waive), and the better named without being imposed.

**The Loan's Charitable Nature**

The commentators read this verse inside the tradition's wider theology of the qarḍ — the loan — and the reading explains why the waiving is natural here in a way it would not be in a trade. Loans, in the Islamic scheme, were ideally charitable in kind: money lent for a term and returned in the same amount, the lender surrendering the time-value as his gift, while the profit-seeking uses of capital ran through other instruments — the credit-sale, the lease, the partnership — where return compensates risk or exchange rather than duration alone. The ribā-block has just legislated that scheme's boundary (the increase cancelled, the principal retained), and this verse legislates its summit: the loan that, at the debtor's distress, becomes a gift outright. The tradition's sayings gather around the summit — the man who relieves a believer's worldly distress relieved by God of the distresses of the Day; the lending described in the ḥadīth-collections as a deed whose reward runs ahead of the ordinary ṣadaqah, because the loan goes to the one too dignified to ask and the gift to the one who asks. The verse's if-you-only-knew is the same ranking stated as withheld arithmetic: the better-ness the waiving carries is visible from where the Day stands, and the verse invites the creditor to borrow that vantage now.

**If Only You Knew**

The closing conditional — *if only you knew* — is the verse's rhetorical hinge, and the commentators note its unfinished grammar: the apodosis is absent, the knowing left hanging over what it would change. The tradition reads the ellipsis as deliberate: had you known what the respite earns day by day, what the waiving weighs at the reckoning, what the debtor's distress is in God's sight — the sentence breaks off because the knowledge, if it arrived, would make the choice immediately and argument would be unnecessary. The formula matches the giving-section's whole pedagogy, which has consistently relocated the arithmetic from the visible ledger to the unseen one: the reward deposited with the Lord (2:262, 2:274, 2:277), the date nurtured into a mountain, the blight and growth operating where the amounts do not show (2:276). The creditor considering his struggling debtor is asked to perform the same relocation: the principal in his ledger is a visible amount, and the better the waiving carries is an invisible one — but the invisibility is an artifact of the vantage, not of the value, and the if-only-you-knew is the verse's promise that the vantage exists and is coming. The ribā-block ends its legislation on the word knew, and hands the whole matter, like the amnesty-clause before it, to the knowledge of God.

**The Block's Human Face**

Positioned between the war-declaration and the Day-return, this verse is the block's scene of ordinary mercy, and its placement is the sequence's final argument. The verses around it operate at the scale of systems — the devourers' resurrection, the blight and growth, the pillar-community, the war with God and His Messenger, the settlement at the principal — and this verse operates at the scale of two people: one who owes and cannot pay, one who is owed and can wait. The commentators read the scale-shift as the legislation's purpose: the ribā-prohibition exists for this scene, so that the creditor standing over the straitened debtor is standing inside an economy whose rules have already cancelled the increase, retained only the principal, ordered the postponement, and invited the waiving — every structural verse terminating in the concrete encounter the verse describes. The tradition's reports of the Prophet's own practice fill the scene: the debtor granted time, the debt remitted, the creditor counselled to accept less than his due. And the verse's two ranks — the commanded respite and the better waiving — leave the encounter with its whole moral range intact: justice at the floor, charity at the height, and between them the knowledge the closing word withholds, waiting for the Day to supply it.
