## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:280

> If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt, postpone it until a time of ease. And if you waive it as an act of charity, it will be better for you, if only you knew.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instruction Stated**

The sentence instructs the creditor to grant the debtor who is in difficulty a respite until the time of ease, and it states that the release of the debt as charity is better for him. The verse follows the rule about the principal and supplies the instruction that governs the cases in which the debtor cannot pay.

The Book has treated the claims of the creditor in the previous verses, and the sentence here places the claim within the circumstances of the person who owes. The community's teachers read the two passages together, so that the law of the debt and the duty of mercy were presented as one subject.

The wording addresses the creditor with the voice of the instruction, and the community's scholars noted the form of the phrase. The passage does not require the release of the debt; it commends the respite and then names the gift that exceeds the duty.

**The Case of Difficulty**

The commentators explained the phrase as the description of the debtor whose circumstances prevent him from paying, and they discussed the state that the term covers. The community's jurists built the law of the insolvent debtor upon the sentence and the reports, and they set out the manner in which the case is established.

The teachers noted the kindness of the instruction, since the passage does not treat the inability to pay as a fault to be punished. The Book's teaching about the community's shared life runs through the passage, and the sentence here supplies the rule that protects the weaker party of the contract.

The community's scholars also observed the difference between the person who cannot pay and the person who refuses, since the sentence concerns the first. The judges of the community applied the distinction when the disputes over debts came before them.

**The Respite Named**

The sentence instructs the creditor to wait until the time of ease, and the community's teachers explained the phrase as the statement that the term of the debt is extended to the point at which the debtor is able to pay. The wording gives the creditor the duty of patience and the debtor the time that his circumstances require.

The commentators noted that the instruction is addressed to the person who holds the claim, so that the passage attends to the conduct of the one who has the power in the contract. The Book's method of naming the party whose restraint is required appears in its other instructions about dealings.

The community's scholars drew from the clause the lesson that the wealth of the creditor does not entitle him to press a person who cannot pay. The passage's concern for fairness, which the previous verse named, is carried forward in the instruction of the sentence.

**The Gift Commended**

The sentence adds that the release of the debt as charity is better for the creditor, and the community's teachers read the clause as the invitation to an act that exceeds the obligation. The Book's treatment of charity in the earlier passage supplies the framework of the statement, since the gift that is given for the sake of God is described there as a loan that is repaid.

The scholars of the community noted the phrase about the better course, since the sentence places the remission of the debt among the goods that the Book commends while leaving the claim of the creditor in place. The wording therefore presents the release as a choice rather than as a requirement.

The teachers also drew from the clause the distinction between the respite and the remission, so that the community's law preserved the right of the creditor while the gift remained with the person who chose it. The passage's instruction was applied by the judges with attention to the two matters.

**The Reports About the Duty**

The reports of the community preserve a saying of the Prophet ﷺ about the creditor who grants the delay to the person in difficulty, and the material describes the reward that is recorded for each day of the respite. The teachers used the saying when they explained the sentence and when they encouraged the believers in the conduct that the verse commends.

The community's scholars gathered the material about the conduct of the early believers in the matters of debt, and the accounts describe the generosity with which the claims were settled among them. The passage's teaching was received as a part of the community's conduct rather than as a rule for the courts alone.

The commentators also noted the connection between the sentence and the passage about charity that precedes it, since the release of a debt is presented in the Book as one of the forms that the gift takes. The two subjects were read together by the community's teachers.

**The Place in the Passage**

The sentence stands between the rule about the principal and the reminder of the day of the return, and the community's teachers read the arrangement as the passage's movement from the law to the account. The verses have treated the practice that is forbidden, the warning that accompanies it, and the claims that remain, and the sentence here supplies the conduct that the Book requires of the creditor.

The community's scholars observed that the passage attends to the person who owes as well as to the person who lends, so that the teaching of the section covers the two parties of the contract. The sentence in this place supplies the rule for the weaker of them.

The verse that follows names the day on which every soul is paid for what it has earned, and the community's teachers read the reminder as the ground of the instruction. The passage therefore closes its treatment of the dealings with the account that belongs to the life to come.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the duty of the respite for the debtor in difficulty, the commending of the release of the debt as a gift, and the difference between the obligation of the creditor and the generosity that exceeds it. The community's teachers used the verse when they set out the law of debts and when they urged the wealthy among the believers to the conduct that the passage commends, and the sūrah's next verse names the day of the return, so that the instruction is followed by the reminder that accompanies it.
