## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:78

> Strive for ˹the cause of˺ Allah in the way He deserves, for ˹it is˺ He ˹Who˺ has chosen you, and laid upon you no hardship in the religion—the way of your forefather Abraham. ˹It is Allah˺ Who named you ‘the ones who submit’ ˹in the˺ earlier ˹Scriptures˺ and in this ˹Quran˺, so that the Messenger may be a witness over you, and that you may be witnesses over humanity. So establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and hold fast to Allah. He ˹alone˺ is your Guardian. What an excellent Guardian, and what an excellent Helper!

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Strive for God With the Striving Due to Him"**

*Wa-jāhidū fī Allāhi ḥaqqa jihādih* — and strive for God with the striving that is His due.

The construction mirrors the phrase in verse 74, where the sūrah said that these people had not measured God with the measure due to Him. The same grammatical shape appears twice in five verses, and the pairing is deliberate: where the estimate of God falls short, the striving for Him falls short with it.

The commentators took the striving in several directions. Al-Qurṭubī read it as striving to do what God commands and to keep away from what He forbids, and to do both for neither worldly advantage nor reputation. Al-Ṭabarī took it as striving without fearing the blame of those who blame. Al-Rāzī read it as a call to strive as the earliest generation strove.

The readings are not in competition. What they share is the exclusion of a striving undertaken for anything else — for a tribe, a party, a reputation, or the pleasure of winning. The qualifying phrase *fī Allāh*, in God, is what does the work, and it is the same qualifier the sūrah used at verse 58 for emigration.

**The Striving Is Not Only Military**

It is worth saying this plainly, because the word has been narrowed in later usage and the sūrah has used it in more than one sense.

Three verses before the end, the sūrah used the same root for people who *strive against* the signs (22:51). In verse 58 it was used for leaving one's home. In verse 39 the permission was for fighting. The verb covers the expenditure of effort, and the object determines what kind of striving it is.

Some commentators read this verse as a call to inward effort and cited a report in which the Prophet ﷺ is said to have remarked, on returning from a campaign, that the community had returned from the lesser striving to the greater. [UNVERIFIED: that report is widely regarded by ḥadīth critics as weak or without a sound chain; I have not been able to confirm a reliable collection or number for it, and it is cited here only as an interpretive tradition, not as evidence.] The verse is sufficiently general without it: the striving due to God is whatever the situation requires, done for Him.

**"He Has Chosen You"**

*Huwa ijtibākum* — He has chosen you. The verb is from the same family as the *yaṣṭafī*, He chooses, of verse 75, which was used for the selection of messengers.

The echo is the sūrah's last structural move. God chooses messengers, and God chooses a community, and the two elections are described with the same vocabulary. What is being said to the believers here is that their standing is not an achievement they consolidated; it is a selection.

That is also why the clause precedes the command to strive. The striving is a response to being chosen rather than a bid for it.

Sūrah Fāṭir names what the choosing conveys, and it is the Book itself: *“Then We granted the Book to those We have chosen from Our servants”* (35:32). Chosen, then entrusted — the election carrying the inheritance with it, though the inheritors divide afterward into the wronging, the middling and the foremost. Verse 78's *ijtabā* stands at the same point in the sequence: the striving commanded is the inheritance being worked, and the division of the workers comes later. Election first, effort after, sorting last.

**"And Has Not Laid Upon You Any Hardship in the Religion"**

*Wa-mā jaʿala ʿalaykum fī al-dīni min ḥaraj* — and He has placed no *ḥaraj* upon you in the religion.

*Ḥaraj* is the word for constriction, for being squeezed into a narrow place. Al-Ṭabarī glossed it as confinement or straitening. What the verse denies is that the religion is a narrow passage.

The jurists filled the phrase with content: the concessions granted under difficulty, such as shortening the prayers while travelling and praying seated or lying down when ill, and the general ruling that no sin is without a way out of it. Sūrah al-Baqarah states the principle in a form that covers every obligation: *“Allah intends ease for you, not hardship”* (2:185), and *“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford”* (2:286).

The placement of this clause between the command to strive and the description of the creed is significant. A sūrah that has just demanded the striving due to God immediately removes the assumption that the demand is impossible.

**"The Creed of Your Father Abraham"**

*Millata abīkum Ibrāhīm* — the creed of your father Abraham.

This is where the sūrah closes its own circle. Abraham was commissioned at verse 26 to purify the House, commanded at verse 27 to call humanity to the pilgrimage, named at verse 31 as the model of the *ḥanīf*, and invoked at verse 43 among the messengers whose people denied them. The final verse names him as the community's father and its creed as his.

The word *millah* denotes a religion considered as a way rather than as a set of propositions, and the Qur'an attaches it to Abraham consistently: followers of his creed are commanded to follow it in sūrah after sūrah (3:95; 4:125; 6:161; 16:123). In nearly every occurrence the word *ḥanīf* appears with it, which returns the reader to verse 31 of this sūrah and to the turning that the whole chapter has been demanding.

Sūrah al-Anʿām states the creed as the Prophet's own commission, in the verse that gathers every term: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Surely my Lord has guided me to the Straight Path, a perfect way, the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists’”* (6:161). Guidance, path, Abraham, uprightness, negation of association — the whole of verse 78's middle in a single sentence. The *millah* is not one option among the sūrah's themes; it is the name the Qur'an gives, here and everywhere, to the religion Muhammad was sent with.

**"He Named You Submitters Before, and in This"**

*Huwa sammākumu al-muslimīna min qablu wa-fī hādhā* — He named you the submitters aforetime and here.

Two readings were recorded. Some took the naming as Abraham's prayer, recorded in Sūrah al-Baqarah, that God make him and his progeny a community submitting to Him (2:128) — which would make the name ancient and petitioned for. Others took the subject as God: He named you submitters in the earlier scriptures and now in this recitation.

The second reading is the stronger one grammatically, since the sentence's subject is the same pronoun that governs the choosing. But both converge on the same claim: the name is not the community's invention and the identity is not recent. What the Qur'an is describing is a standing description that the earlier revelation also used.

Abraham's prayer at the foundations asks for the name before the nation exists: *“Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You and from our descendants a nation that will submit to you”* (2:128). The submission petitioned for himself and his line together — the father asking to be made a submitter and his descendants a submitting nation. On the first reading verse 78 answers that prayer across the centuries: the name Abraham asked for is the name the community bears, in the earlier scriptures and in this one.

**"So That the Messenger May Be a Witness Over You, and You Witnesses Over Humanity"**

*Li-yakūna al-rasūlu shahīdan ʿalaykum wa-takūnū shuhadāʾa ʿalā al-nās* — so that the Messenger be a witness over you, and you be witnesses over humanity.

The purpose clause is the verse's centre of gravity, and the sūrah has been building toward it since verse 17.

Sūrah al-Baqarah states the same arrangement in the same words: *“And so We have made you ˹believers˺ an upright community so that you may be witnesses over humanity and that the Messenger may be a witness over you”* (2:143). Al-Rāzī's note on the arrangement is the one to keep: God wants the witnesses over humanity to be upright and just, so that others can come to faith through them. Being a witness is therefore a function rather than a privilege, and it can be discharged badly.

Ibn Kathīr took the witnessing in a second sense as well — that the community is *witnessed*, pointed at, held up as an example. Both senses bind the same obligation: a community that is looked at is obliged to be lookable at.

**The Closing Commands**

*Fa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātū al-zakāta wa-ʿtaṣimū bi-Llāh* — so establish the prayer, give the alms, and hold fast to God.

The three commands are the sūrah's last words of instruction, and they compress what the chapter has been arguing. The prayer is the posture of verse 77; the alms are the fourth item of verse 41 and the giving of verse 35; holding fast is the response of a person who has been told, seventy-seven verses running, that everything else he might hold is not his.

The verb *iʿtaṣimū* means to grip, to take hold of something for protection. The Qur'an uses the same image elsewhere of holding fast to a rope (3:103), and the image is of a person holding on rather than of a person standing on his own.

The holding-fast is commanded to the community as its unity, with the rope named: *“And hold firmly to the rope of Allah and do not be divided”* (3:103). *Wa-ʿtaṣimū bi-ḥabli Allāh* — grip the rope, refuse division. Verse 78's *wa-ʿtaṣimū bi-Llāh* shortens the image to its essence: the rope is God Himself, and the holding is the sūrah's last instruction after seventy-seven verses of reasons. Prayer established, alms given, and then the grip — the religion ending where it began, with a person holding on.

**The Last Words**

*Huwa mawlākum fa-niʿma al-mawlā wa-niʿma al-naṣīr* — He is your Master, and what an excellent Master, and what an excellent Helper.

The sūrah that opened with an earthquake closes with two nouns: *mawlā*, the protecting patron, and *naṣīr*, the ally who comes when called. Both are the words the sūrah used for what the idolaters thought they had — a patron (22:13) and a helper (22:71) — and both are here returned to their true owner.

The sūrah began by telling humanity to fear its Lord because the Hour is a dreadful thing. It ends by telling the believers who their Patron is. Between those two sentences lies the whole argument: the world is going to end, the dead will be raised, the idols are weaker than flies, the messenger has been chosen, and the One who holds all of it has taken this community as His own.

Sūrah al-Anfāl closes an address to the fledgling community in the identical words: *“then know that Allah is your Protector. What an excellent Protector, and what an excellent Helper!”* (8:40). *Niʿma al-mawlā wa-niʿma al-naṣīr* — the same superlatives, the same two nouns, spoken to believers facing an enemy they could not match. Verse 78 borrows the ending because the situation rhymes: a community told to strive beyond its strength is left with the Patron who is beyond every strength. The sūrah's last sentence is older than the sūrah, and it holds.
