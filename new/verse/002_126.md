## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:126

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day.” He answered, “As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham's Prayer for the City**

The verse records a prayer for two things: security for the city and provision for its people. Abraham had settled part of his family in a valley the Book describes elsewhere as uncultivated, and the request for fruits is therefore a request for something the place could not supply by itself.

The Book presents the prayer in the same terms as the earlier account of the House, so that the reader sees the sanctuary and the city as a single project. What Abraham asked for was not private welfare but conditions in which worship could be established, and the two requests address the dangers that threatened such a place: insecurity and want. The prayer also indicates what Abraham thought the sanctuary needed in order to function. Security and provision are the conditions of settled worship, and the Book presents the request for them as part of the founding of the House rather than as a separate concern.

**The Meaning of Security**

The Arabic word for secure shares its root with the word for sanctuary used in the previous verse, so that the prayer asks God to make the city a place of safety in the sense that the House itself was a sanctuary. The commentators noted that the city's inviolability was recognized before the coming of Islam, and the Book treats that status as a gift granted in answer to Abraham's prayer.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the same status on the day Makkah was conquered when he declared that the city had been made inviolable by God on the day the heavens and the earth were created, and that killing and the felling of its vegetation were not permitted in it. The report connects the community's law directly to the prayer recorded in this verse, and it gives the verse a place in the community's own legal and ritual memory.

**Provision for the People**

The second request concerns provision, and the Book's account of the valley where the family was settled describes it as unproductive. The request for fruits is answered in the city's later history, and the community addressed here isknew the markets and the pilgrimage that supplied its people from every region.

The prayer is phrased with a condition: Abraham asked provision for those among the people who believe in God and the Last Day. The Book records the divine answer immediately, and the answer widens the provision while keeping the final outcome distinct, so that the reader is shown both the generosity of the response and the limits of what worldly comfort can secure. The request and its answer together show the Book's treatment of worldly provision: it is granted widely and enjoyed for a time, and the Book never presents it as evidence of divine approval. The verse's closing words place the final measure elsewhere.

**The Divine Answer About the Disbelievers**

The answer states that the disbelievers will be granted enjoyment for a time and then be driven to the punishment of the Fire. The Book makes the same distinction in other passages, where worldly provision is described as a grant given to all people while the reward of the Hereafter belongs to those who earn it.

The phrase about being compelled toward the punishment is stronger than a mere prediction of suffering, and the commentators noted its suggestion of a forced march toward a destination the person would rather avoid. The sentence therefore answers Abraham's question about those outside the condition he had named, and the answer is a statement about the two outcomes rather than about the conditions of this life.

**"What an Evil Destination"**

The verse ends with a condemnation of the destination, and the Arabic describes the place to which a journey leads. The Book uses the image of a road and its end repeatedly, and it applies the phrase to the Fire elsewhere in the sūrah in connection with those who turn away.

The closing words belong to the divine answer rather than to Abraham's prayer, and their presence in the verse gives the reader both halves of the exchange. What began as a request for a secure and fruitful city ends with a reminder that the prosperity of a place does not determine the outcome of the people in it. The phrase also anticipates the sūrah's later descriptions of the Fire with their emphasis on the condition of those who arrive there. The Book's warnings frequently end with a statement about the destination, so that the reader weighs a course of conduct by where it leads.

**The Prayer and Its Place in the Passage**

The verse sits between the purification of the House and the raising of its foundations, and it supplies the human side of the story the sūrah is telling about the sanctuary. Abraham builds, prays, and asks for what the place needs, and the Book records the answers so that the reader sees the relation between the servant's request and the outcome in history.

The passage that follows continues the same narrative with the prayers offered during the construction of the House, and the sequence shows the community's rites being traced to Abraham's own words. The sūrah is building an argument about the religion of Abraham, and this verse supplies the material for it by connecting the city's security and provision to the man whose faith the Book upholds.

**A Sanctuary in a Barren Valley**

The Book describes the site where Abraham settled his family as an uncultivated valley, and the prayer in this verse asks for what the place could not supply on its own. The request for fruits is therefore a request for a condition rather than for luxury, since a sanctuary cannot function if its people must leave it to eat. The Book records the answer in the city's later history, where the pilgrimage brought provisions and markets from every region, and the community's own memory of Makkah before Islam included its role as a place of trade and gathering. What the verse establishes is that the sanctuary's material support was asked for and granted rather than assumed. The reader is shown a founder who prayed for the conditions of worship before he prayed for anything else, and the city's later prosperity is presented as the answer to that request.
