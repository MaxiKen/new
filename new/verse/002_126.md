## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:126

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day.” He answered, “As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Patriarch's Civic Prayer**

The verse records the second of Abraham's two prayers for the House's setting, and this one is urban: *my Lord, make this city secure and provide fruits to its people*. The petition's two terms are the two goods a settlement lives by — safety and supply — and their order is doctrinal: security first, because provision cannot be enjoyed where fear governs, and worship cannot be gathered where the roads are unsafe. The city is Makkah, the barren valley the patriarch had settled his household in with the prayer Sūrah Ibrāhīm preserves: *our Lord, I have settled some of my offspring in a barren valley, near Your Sacred House, so that they may establish prayer — so make the hearts of people incline toward them, and provide them with fruits, so perhaps they will be thankful* (14:37). The two prayers are one petition across decades: the valley without cultivation asked for fruit, the city asked for security, and both asked on the House's account — the settlement's goods requested for the sake of the worship it hosts. The same sūrah preserves the prayer's third term, the patriarch's fear for its interior: *make this city secure, and keep me and my children away from the worship of idols* (14:35) — security and provision bracketing the one danger that would void both, the idol inside the safe, fruit-bearing town. The verse's prayer is therefore a complete civic theology in one sentence: safety for the body, fruit for the body's life, and — in the clause that follows — the qualification that reveals what the body's goods are for.

**The Qualifier and the Answer That Widens It**

Abraham restricts his petition: *those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day* — the security and the fruits asked for the believers of the city. The divine answer accepts the prayer and exceeds its restriction, and the exceeding is the verse's central instruction: *as for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire*. The disbeliever is not excluded from the city's provision; he is included in it, for a while, and the while is named as a while. The amendment teaches three things at once. Provision is not a verdict: the fruit of Makkah falls on believer and disbeliever alike, so neither prosperity nor its absence reads as divine approval — the doctrine the sūrah will legislate in its own terms against every reader who mistakes supply for standing. The patriarch's intercession is honored past its own scope: Abraham asked for the believers, and the answer shows the asking was received in a generosity wider than the ask — the city's economy cannot be run on the prayer's exclusion, because the world's economy is not run that way. And the enjoyment is structured as respite: *a little while*, then the destination — the worldly provision of the disbeliever is not a share but a span, and the span's end is filed in the same sentence: *what an evil destination!* The verse thereby answers, in advance, the theology of verse 80's claimants and every successor who reads worldly inclusion as otherworldly insurance: the Fire's condemned were fed too.

**The Sanctuary Declared**

The prayer's first term — *secure* — received its historical ratification in the tradition's most documented declaration. On the day Makkah was taken, the Prophet ﷺ stood in the city and stated its status: *"Allah has made Mecca a sanctuary, so it was a sanctuary before me and will continue to be a sanctuary after me. It was made legal for me — that is, I was allowed to fight in it — for a few hours of a day. It is not allowed to uproot its shrubs or to cut its trees, or to chase or disturb its game, or to pick up its lost things except by a person who would announce that publicly"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1833) — with the tradition's single recorded exception, when al-ʿAbbās asked for the idhkhir-grass used by the goldsmiths and for graves, and the Prophet ﷺ granted it. In another formulation of the same announcement, the tradition dates the sanctity to the day the heavens and the earth were created and extends it to the Day of Resurrection, with the killing inside it permitted to no one before or after. The declaration's content is Abraham's petition made law: the city *secure* — not merely peaceful by circumstance but inviolable by statute, its safety a ruling rather than a condition. The statute's granularity in the ḥadīth — the shrub, the tree, the game, the lost article — extends the sanctuary past human blood to the city's whole living furniture, and the few-hours exception of the conquest confirms the rule by marking its only breach as unique, dated, and closed. The verse's prayer is thus answerable in the historical record: asked at the House's founding, legislated at the city's return, and the *āmin*-root binding it to verse 125's *amn* ties the city's security to the House's sanctuary as one institution.

**Fruits in the Barren Valley**

The petition's second term carries the geography's standing miracle. Makkah sits in a valley the patriarch's own prayer described as without cultivation (14:37) — no orchards, no fields, no water-command of its own — and the fruit asked for it is not agricultural produce but commercial and pilgrim provision: the goods carried in because the House draws the carriers. The prayer's mechanism is stated in 14:37 itself — *make the hearts of people incline toward them* — the fruit routed through inclination, the supply created by love of the destination, and the history the verse's audience knew ran exactly on that mechanism: the caravans of winter and summer, the market fairs of the pilgrimage season, the barren town fed by its own drawing-power. The recital's audience was also hearing the petition's purpose-clause, which 14:37 states and verse 126 assumes: *so that they may establish prayer*, *so perhaps they will be thankful*. The fruits are instrumental — provision for a worshipping settlement, supply ordered to gratitude — and the instrumental ordering is what the answer's second half enforces from the other side: the provision arrives for all, but it is received rightly only where the gratitude runs, and where it does not, the enjoyment is booked as respite and the destination as evil. The verse leaves the two receptions standing side by side in one city: the believer fed and thankful, the disbeliever fed and respite-held, the same fruit, the same security, two destinations — and the difference between them located, as the recital always locates it, in what the fed heart does with the feeding.

**The Respite and the Destination**

The answer's structure — *a little while, then* — is the Qur'an's grammar of respite, and its logic runs through the whole passage this section belongs to. The enjoyment granted the disbeliever is real and it is temporary, and both facts are load-bearing: real, because the verse does not promise the world's deprivation to the unbelieving — Makkah's fruit-fed history included its idol-decades; temporary, because the while is bounded by the *then*, and the *then* carries the destination-clause with its verdict-word — *what an evil destination*, the same exclamation-form the recital has filed at every trade's settlement (86, 90, 102). The respite-doctrine answers the question every reader brings to a secure, fruit-fed city of mixed belief: why does the provision continue where the gratitude fails? And the answer is the verse's own: because the continuation is part of the sentence, not its suspension — the enjoyment is the little while in which return remains possible, and its littleness is what makes the destination evil rather than merely unfortunate. The patriarch's prayer and the divine answer together give the recital's audience the complete civic theology: pray for your cities' safety and supply; expect the prayer to be answered past the boundaries of your asking; do not read the answer's breadth as endorsement; and hold the destination-clause over every enjoyment — the little while named little, the then named then, and the evil destination named evil, in the same breath that grants the fruit.
