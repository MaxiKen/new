## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:161

> when their brother Lot said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lot Speaks From Within the Community**

Lot is introduced as their brother before his question is heard. He is not a stranger who has arrived to mock the people from a distance. He shares their human world and addresses them as someone who knows their society from the inside. His warning therefore carries both intimacy and authority.

Brotherhood does not mean approval of every practice in the community. Lot's concern for his people requires him to challenge what is wrong rather than protect their reputation. A truthful relative can be more loving in correction than a flattering ally who leaves a people to its ruin.


Lot asks whether they will fear Allah. The question connects his story to the calls of Noah, Hūd, and Ṣāliḥ. Prophetic guidance begins with recognition that human desire is not the highest authority. Taqwa places conduct before God and prevents a society from calling every appetite a right simply because it is desired.

Fear here is not panic or hatred of the self. It is moral awareness. The people are asked to remember that God sees what is public and private, and that pleasure does not erase accountability. Lot's question invites them to pause before defending a practice that revelation has condemned.

**A Local Messenger Removes an Excuse**

The people cannot claim that Lot knows nothing of their circumstances. He belongs to them and can speak their language. The closeness makes their response more responsible: they are hearing a warning from someone who has shared their environment and understands the social pressure around the conduct he addresses.

Quran 7:80 also introduces Lot's rebuke to his people in the context of a shameful practice. The parallel confirms that the question is a moral summons, not a private dispute between a prophet and a few individuals. Lot is calling the community to examine its public direction before harm becomes normalized.


A distant rule can be discussed abstractly. Lot's question is carried by a person whose relationship to the community can be felt. He has family ties, memories, and a stake in what happens. His warning is not delivered from superiority; it comes from concern for people with whom he is connected.

That human closeness also makes rejection more revealing. The people know that Lot is not attacking them because he is an outsider who hates them. They reject a moral claim because it threatens what they have chosen to protect. Their resistance cannot be explained simply as a clash of unfamiliar cultures.


Sexual desire, social custom, and public approval can reinforce one another. A person may participate because he wants the act, because others praise it, or because he fears being excluded if he objects. Lot's question places every one of those pressures beneath God. The crowd is not allowed to turn agreement into moral truth.

A society that fears Allah must protect the ability to refuse wrongdoing. It cannot force every person to celebrate what he believes is forbidden, nor can it make public approval a substitute for conscience. Taqwa creates space for truthful correction even when the dominant mood is hostile.

**The Warning Is About Conduct, Not Mere Existence**

Lot's rebuke concerns what the people do. It does not authorize contempt for a person merely because he experiences a temptation or struggles with desire. Moral accountability concerns chosen acts, public encouragement of wrongdoing, and the harms associated with a practice. The prophet's question calls for restraint and repentance, not dehumanization.

This distinction matters because a community can condemn sin while becoming sinful in how it speaks about sinners. Fear of Allah requires justice, privacy, and refusal to turn correction into cruelty. Lot's own position, as shown by his concern for his people, keeps moral seriousness joined to human dignity.

**The Question Is an Opening Before Judgment**

Lot asks rather than announcing that the people cannot change. A question gives the hearer a moment to recognize the Lord's claim and turn back. The opportunity is real, even though the practice has become established and defended.

Mercy often begins with an interruption. A society may be moving confidently along a harmful path, and a messenger breaks the momentum by asking whether it fears God. The question does not flatter the people, but it gives them a way to stop before their habits become a public identity from which they no longer wish to depart.


They can respond with a slogan and continue unchanged, or they can let fear of Allah reshape their conduct. Taqwa is visible in restraint, repentance, protection of others, and willingness to accept revealed limits. It is not proved by saying that God is great while treating His command as optional.

For the reader, Lot's opening question is both intimate and demanding. A familiar messenger speaks to a familiar community, and the Lord's claim is clear. The right answer is not merely an emotional reaction to the story. It is a life that refuses to make desire, custom, or crowd approval more authoritative than God.

The question also guards the dignity of moral correction. Lot does not treat his people as beyond recovery, and he does not make the practice a reason to abandon every person within the community. He separates the wrong from the worth of the human being who can repent. That balance is difficult, but it belongs to prophetic compassion.

A society that hears such a question should examine the atmosphere it has created. Can a person admit wrongdoing without being mocked? Can a family seek guidance without being forced to defend the community's reputation? Taqwa grows where truth can be spoken honestly and where repentance is valued more than public pride.
