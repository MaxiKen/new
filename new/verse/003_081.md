## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:81

> ˹Remember˺ when Allah made a covenant with the prophets, ˹saying,˺ “Now that I have given you the Book and wisdom, if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him.” He added, “Do you affirm this covenant and accept this commitment?” They said, “Yes, we do.” Allah said, “Then bear witness, and I too am a Witness.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Covenant Binding Every Prophet**

The verse reports an undertaking that God made with the prophets as a class. They were given a book and wisdom, and when a messenger came to them confirming what they had, they were to believe in him and support him. The covenant is then ratified: God asks whether they agree and take up the obligation, they answer that they do, and God calls them to witness while declaring Himself a witness as well.

The scope of the sentence is what gives it weight. It is not an agreement made with one people at one time; it is a standing obligation attached to the office of prophethood itself, so that every messenger is bound by it as a condition of his mission. The Book states the same idea here in the form of a documented undertaking, complete with offer, acceptance, and witnesses.

**What the Covenant Requires**

The two verbs are believing and supporting, and the pair covers both the inner and the outer response. The messengers are not merely to acknowledge a subsequent prophet; they are to stand with him, which the commentators understood to include helping his cause and making it known.

The addition of support is important, because it explains why the covenant is stated as a mutual obligation rather than a private assent. A prophet has a community behind him, and the covenant binds the mission rather than the individual alone. The commentators took this to mean that the obligation passes to the followers of each prophet, since the prophets themselves would not fail in such a matter; what the covenant establishes is the standing rule of the religious history that revelation describes, in which each messenger's community is answerable for how it receives the message that arrives after them.

**The Confidence of the Report**

The verse reports the exchange as a completed transaction, with the givers of the covenant answering yes and God calling witnesses to the agreement. The Book uses this documentary style in several places for the covenants it records, including the covenant with the Children of Israel and the covenant taken from all of humanity in the primordial testimony that God had them give regarding themselves.

The style does work that a bare statement of obligation could not. A covenant that has been ratified and witnessed cannot be treated as a suggestion, and the parties to it are shown agreeing to its terms before any specific case arrived to test them. The Book's readers are therefore presented with a history of revelation in which the prophets of the past are committed in advance to recognising the prophets of the future, and the verse leaves no room for a reading in which the earlier missions were self-enclosed.

**The Messenger in View**

The commentators held that the messenger to whom this covenant points is the Prophet of this sūrah, and the wording of the sūrah supports the reading, since the formula it uses for a messenger confirming what his predecessors had is the formula the sūrah has applied to the Qur'an itself at its opening.

What the covenant does, in that reading, is to place the mission of the Prophet inside the obligations of all the earlier missions rather than beside them. The communities that held the earlier books are told that their own prophets had promised to acknowledge the one who fulfilled their message, and the argument the sūrah has been conducting about Abraham and about Jesus is thereby given a further dimension. The dispute is not between one community's revelation and another's; it is between a covenant and the conduct of the people who inherited its obligations.

**Why the Report Is Placed Here**

The verse stands after the passage on worship and before the verses that state the consequences of turning back. Its position answers the question a reader might carry from the discussion of Abraham and the People of the Book: on what basis can a new messenger require recognition from communities that already possess scriptures?

The answer given is that the earlier messengers themselves had been bound to this. The obligation the new message imposes is not novel, and it does not represent a demand by the newcomer upon unwilling predecessors. It is the honouring of an agreement whose terms the predecessors had accepted, and the sūrah states it before it states the consequences of refusing, so that the reader meets the obligation before he meets the warning.

**A History of Revelation That Holds Together**

The verse's picture of religious history is of a single successive mission in which each messenger knows he is not the last. That picture differs from a story in which revelation is the property of the communities that received it, and the sūrah has been arguing against the second picture from its opening verses.

The repetition of this theme through the chapter — the books confirming one another, the messengers acknowledging one another, the people of Abraham defined by following rather than by descent — gives the reader a coherent account of what he is reading. What the covenant adds is the mechanism: the confirmation is not an accident of history but a term of the commission, agreed to by every messenger before he began.

**The Last Line of the Verse**

The sentence ends with the witnesses: they bear witness, and God is a witness. The line is short and it closes the transaction, and the sūrah's choice to end on it rather than on the obligation itself is characteristic.

What the reader is left holding is not only what was promised but the fact that it was recorded. The next verse states what becomes of a community that turns back after such an agreement, and the two verses are meant to be read together.
