## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:60

> This is the truth from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Address Directed at the Prophet**

The verse changes addressee again and speaks to the Prophet of the sūrah about the account he has just received. What has been recited about Jesus, his birth, his mission, and the comparison with Adam is described as truth from his Lord, and the sentence closes with a prohibition against doubt.

The combination of a statement and a warning in one sentence is characteristic of the Book's manner with its own messenger. The Prophet is not excused from the demand the revelation makes on everyone who hears it, and the Book's treatment of him throughout contains warnings as well as commendations. A messenger who is told not to doubt is a messenger who is being held to the same standard he is delivering.

**"Truth From Your Lord"**

The phrase identifies both the content and its source. What has been recited is true, and it comes from the Lord of the person addressed, which places the Prophet in the same relation to God as the readers of the Book.

The construction recurs when the Book wants to end a discussion with a verdict rather than an argument. In the sūrah of the Cow, after the change of the direction of prayer and the disputes it produced, the verse states that what has come to them is the truth from their Lord and that they should not be among those who doubt. The sūrah here states the same formula at the close of the dispute about Jesus, and the effect is to remove the question from the plane of debate and return it to the plane of revelation.

**What the Warning Presupposes About Knowledge**

The clause assumes that a person can hold the truth and still be in danger, which is a claim about how conviction works rather than about what the evidence is worth. If certainty were produced automatically by contact with revelation, a warning of this kind would have no object.

The Book's position is that the truth is established and the person holding it is variable. Its answers to that variability take several forms elsewhere: the repeated call to remember, the instruction to renew one's understanding, and the prayers of the believers who ask that their hearts not be allowed to swerve after guidance has come to them. This verse adds another form, the direct address that names the danger while the material is still fresh. Its pastoral function is to make a reader notice that the warning has been given and to place the weight of the account's origin beside it.

**Doubt and How It Is Prevented**

The clause is usually read as an assurance that the Prophet would not entertain doubt rather than as a suspicion that he might, and the commentators understood it as a warning that also strengthens. The Book addresses its messenger with language of this kind at several points where the pressure of the surrounding denial was greatest.

What the Book treats as dangerous is not the enquiry that wants an answer; it is the drift that follows prolonged exposure to hostile argument. A person who hears the same objection made confidently, every day, for years, may begin to hold the answer less firmly without ever having been convinced by the objection. The prohibition in this verse addresses that process, and it answers it by naming the source of the material rather than by supplying a further argument. A truth that comes from one's Lord does not need to be won again in every conversation.

**Who Are "Those Who Doubt"**

The Arabic participle describes a person in a state rather than someone performing an act, and it is used in the Book both for those who waver and for those who stand outside belief. In this verse the reference is broad: the sūrah has just described a dispute in which a party refused to accept an account of Jesus that the Book presents as clear.

The phrase therefore covers a community that had knowledge of the story and chose an interpretation that suited it, as well as any reader who hears the account and hesitates. The sūrah does not distinguish between them here. What it does is describe the state it wants the Prophet kept out of, and it leaves the reader to decide how close his own position lies to it.

**The Pastoral Weight of a Single Clause**

The clause is short, and it does a great deal of work for readers who come after the Prophet. Anyone can recite the account of Jesus in this sūrah and then spend years in a setting where it is contradicted by crowds, by authorities, or by the sheer persistence of a rival account.

What the Book provides against that pressure is not a new proof in each generation but the identification of the material's origin. The believer who accepts the verse is accepting a claim about where the story comes from, and that claim is what must be held when the argument cannot be settled in the room. The Book's confidence in the persistence of its own message is expressed here in the simplest possible form: the truth stands, and the danger is only that a person might drift from it.

**What Certainty Looks Like in the Sūrah**

The sūrah has been developing a picture of what religious conviction rests on. It opened with a Book revealed in truth; it described a testimony given by God, the angels, and the learned concerning His unity; it has just recited the account of a prophet whose birth was a sign, and now it tells its own prophet that what he holds is truth from his Lord.

The sequence shows that certainty in this Book is not a feeling of confidence that a person generates. It is produced by evidence whose source is identified, and the Book's warnings against doubt are warnings against treating the evidence as though it were something else. A reader who accepts that premise will understand why the sūrah follows this verse with a challenge to the disputants rather than with further explanation: the account is settled, and the next step concerns the terms on which the disagreement will be conducted.
