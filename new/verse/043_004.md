## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:4

> **And indeed, it is—in the Master Record with Us—highly esteemed, rich in wisdom.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Book, Seen from Heaven**

The previous verse spoke about the Qurʾān as the Makkans encountered it: Arabic, recited, addressed to them. This verse turns the same object around and shows where it sits from God's side. It is in the umm al-kitāb, with Us, and there it is ʿalī and ḥakīm. The phrase "with Us" is the hinge. What the Quraysh heard as a man's voice in a Makkan street exists in God's presence in a form beyond their reach. The verse therefore does two jobs at once. It answers the charge that the Qurʾān is a human composition by locating its original in heaven, and it prepares the argument of the next verse, which asks whether God should withdraw such a thing from a people who reject it. A text that is both near enough to be understood and exalted enough to be with God is the answer to the two objections the Quraysh kept alternating between: that it was too ordinary to be divine, and too strange to be accepted.

**What the Mother of the Book Is**

Umm al-kitāb literally means the Mother of the Book, and umm in Arabic names the origin or root from which something comes; the road that other roads branch from is called an umm, and Makkah is umm al-qurā. Most exegetes, including Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī, identify the Mother of the Book here with the Preserved Tablet, al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ, named in 85:22, where the Qurʾān is described as a glorious recitation in a preserved tablet. On this view the Tablet is the source from which all revealed scripture comes, and the Qurʾān has its origin there before it was sent down in stages. The same expression appears in 13:39, where God erases and confirms what He wills and with Him is the Mother of the Book, which supports the reading of an archetype in God's keeping. A smaller group of exegetes takes the phrase here as a reference to the Qurʾān itself, drawing on 3:7, where the clear verses are called the umm al-kitāb, the foundation of the Book. The first view is stronger in this verse because of the words "with Us": the Qurʾān as recited is with the Prophet ﷺ and his people, while the Tablet is with God.

**ʿAlī: Beyond Reach and Beyond Corruption**

The first adjective is ʿalī, high or exalted. Al-Rāzī takes it to mean that the Book is transcendent, and therefore beyond the possibility of corruption or falsification. What lies with God cannot be altered by human hands, and the recited Qurʾān draws its immunity from that origin. This connects to the promise in 15:9 that God sent down the Reminder and will guard it, and to 56:77–80, where the Qurʾān is described as noble, in a guarded book, touched only by the purified, a sending-down from the Lord of the worlds. Al-Rāzī records a second sense when the adjective is read as describing the Qurʾān directly: it is higher than every other revealed book, because it is the one miracle that will remain until the end of time, whereas the miracles of earlier prophets ended with their lives. Both senses stand. The height is a height of station, not of distance from the reader; the verse before this one has already said the same Book was brought into Arabic so that it could be understood.

**Ḥakīm: Wise, Firm, or Making Wise**

The second adjective, ḥakīm, is usually rendered "wise", and the Qurʾān is called ḥakīm elsewhere (10:1, 31:2, 36:2). But the root ḥ-k-m is rich, and the exegetes draw three meanings from it here. The first is wise in the ordinary sense: the Book contains and conveys wisdom. The second is muḥkam, firm or well-made. The Qurʾān describes its own verses as uḥkimat, made firm, in 11:1, and speaks of muḥkam verses in 3:7; on this reading ḥakīm means a text whose parts are set firmly and cannot be pulled apart. The third is muḥkim, that which makes wise, so that the Book, or the Tablet it comes from, is the source of wisdom for those who study it. Arabic allows a single adjective on the pattern faʿīl to carry an active, a passive, and a stative sense, which is why these three do not exclude each other. The strongest reading holds all three together: the Book is wise in content, firm in structure, and productive of wisdom in the reader. That last sense answers the purpose clause of verse 3. A Book that makes wise is precisely a Book that people are meant to understand.

**Two Descriptions, One Text**

The two verses together, 3 and 4, describe a single text with two faces. On earth it is Arabic, comprehensible, addressed to a particular people in their own words. With God it is exalted and wise, kept in the root of all scripture. The order matters. The accessible description comes first, and the exalted one second, because the argument is being made to people who had only encountered the first face and were being told about the second. This is the reverse of how a reader might expect a claim of divine origin to be made. The Qurʾān does not begin by asserting a heavenly original and then condescend to an earthly copy. It begins with what the Makkans could hear and test, and then tells them that what they have tested has a source above them. The description also protects against two errors in later reading. One error would treat the Arabic as incidental packaging around a timeless meaning; the verse before this one refuses that. The other would treat the Arabic text as nothing more than an Arabic text; this verse refuses that. The next verse asks whether such a Book should be taken away from those who mock it.
