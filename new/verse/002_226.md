## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:226

> Those who swear not to have intercourse with their wives must wait for four months. If they change their mind, then Allah is certainly AllForgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Suspended Wife**

The verse addresses a specific pre-Islamic institution of harm, and the commentators describe it plainly: a man would stay away from his wife without divorcing her — swearing against marital relations and leaving her in a limbo that could last for years (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī). The suspended woman had neither of marriage's two goods: not its intimacy, since the oath withheld it, and not its exit, since no divorce freed her to marry another. She was a wife in law and a prisoner in fact — bound to a man who had sworn against the marriage's central act while refusing the word that would release her. The practice weaponized both the oath and the marriage: the oath's sanctity made the withholding feel irrevocable, and the marriage's bond made the woman's waiting indefinite. The verse abolishes the institution by giving its instrument a term: those who forswear their wives — who make the oath against relations — have four months. The number is not a permission for four months of suspension but a cap on it: the oath may not hold the marriage hostage beyond a bounded season, and at the season's end the swearer must be either a husband or a divorcer.

**The Four Months as Harm-Detection**

Al-Rāzī states the term's function with precision: the four months draw a line beyond which the abstention is taken as an intent to harm the wife. The reasoning reads the calendar as a diagnostic. A man angry with his wife may withdraw briefly — days, weeks — in the heat whose cooling the marriage normally provides; the withdrawal up to four months is within the range of a quarrel's natural history. A withdrawal that persists past the fourth month is no longer explainable as anger; it is a course of conduct, and its object is the wife's harm — the deliberate use of her suspended state as punishment or leverage. The verse thereby converts an invisible intention into a legible date: the community, the judge, and the wife herself do not need to read the husband's heart to know when harm has become his settled practice; the calendar reads it for them. The four months are, in effect, the law's instrument for distinguishing the marriage's weather from its poisoning — the temporary storm from the engineered drought — and the distinction is what makes the sequel's two exits just.

**If They Return**

The first exit is reconciliation: if they return — the resumption of marital relations — God is Forgiving, Merciful. The exegetes defined the return concretely: the resumption of intercourse itself; and they extended its validity — the return counts even where the husband intends it and is hindered by some obstacle, since the intent to harm has ceased; for some, bearing witness to the return suffices. The legal question the return generated concerns the oath itself: many understood the Forgiving-Merciful clause to mean that no expiation is necessary when the husband returns (al-Ṭabarī) — the reconciliation absorbs the oath without ransom; others held that some expiation, such as feeding the poor, remains due, since returning to the wife constitutes going back on the sworn word. The two readings compose a coherent policy: reconciliation is made as cheap as possible — the verse's names are forgiveness and mercy, not audit — while the oath's gravity is not entirely dissolved. The decisive point is the welcome: the swearer who comes back is met by the same names that closed the pilgrim's istighfār (2:199) and the comfort of the Emigrants (2:218), and the marriage that resumes after estrangement resumes inside pardon rather than under penalty.

**The Oath's Scope**

The jurists debated the boundaries of the īlāʾ — which oaths fall under the four-months law — and the debate maps the verse's concern. Some held the ruling applies only when the oath is made in anger with intent to harm: the man who swears against relations for another reason — protecting the well-being of a nursing child, for instance — stands outside the verse's ruling (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī). Others extended it to any oath that keeps a man from relations with his wife, regardless of motive. Others extended further still: the forswearing need not concern relations at all but may cover any withdrawal intended to harm the wife — not speaking to her, not touching her. The three positions share the harm-criterion the four-month term installed and differ only on the harm's vehicles; the nursing-child exemption shows the criterion at work, since a withdrawal serving the child's welfare is not a withdrawal against the wife. The verse's legislative achievement is visible in the debate's shape: a pre-Islamic practice with no term, no definition, and no remedy became a bounded institution whose every boundary — which oaths, which motives, which harms — the law could now argue about, because the scripture had first made it a matter of law.

**The Deadline's Enforcement**

The sharpest question the verse generated is what happens at the four months' end: is divorce effected automatically by the lapse, or must further steps be taken? The commentators record the division — some maintained that upon the period's completion, divorce could be imposed by a judge at the request of the wife (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī). The judicial reading is the verse's protective machinery made concrete: the deadline does not merely define harm but empowers its victim — the wife who has waited out the term can bring the matter before the judge, and the judge can dissolve what the husband will neither live in nor leave. The automatic reading accomplishes the same release by operation of law. Either way, the verse's architecture guarantees the exit: the suspended wife's limbo has a date, and the date has teeth. This is the īlāʾ-legislation's whole innovation against the pre-Islamic institution — the years-long suspension became a four-month process with a judicial remedy at its end, the woman's freedom secured by the same calendar that measures her husband's harm.

**The Verse in the Household's Legislation**

The īlāʾ-verse sits at the hinge of the domestic series. Behind it: the marriage's gate of faith (2:221), the body's cycle and its beloved purifications (2:222), the tilth and the meeting with God (2:223), the oaths' general legislation (2:224–225). Ahead: the second exit — the resolved divorce, heard and known (2:227) — and the whole dissolution-architecture of waiting periods, counts, ransoms, and nursing (2:228–233). The verse's office in the series is to legislate the marriage's estrangement: not its daily friction, which the oaths-verses handled, and not its ending, which the divorce-verses will handle, but the suspended state between — the estrangement that pretends to be marriage. The four months force that pretense to choose, and the two exits the pair of verses provides — return under Forgiving and Merciful, divorce under Hearing and Knowing — cover the whole field: the marriage resumes or it ends, and what may not resume or end is the wife's captivity. The harm-criterion the verse installs will govern everything that follows — the retention that harms (2:231), the concealment of the womb (2:228), the taking-back of gifts (2:229) — making this short ruling the domestic legislation's declaration of principle: in the household God governs, no person's suspense may be another person's weapon.
