## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:226

> Those who swear not to have intercourse with their wives must wait for four months. If they change their mind, then Allah is certainly AllForgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Described**

The verse states that those who forswear their wives must wait four months, and it states that if they return to them God is forgiving and merciful. The practice described is a man's oath to abstain from relations with his wife while keeping her within the marriage.

The sentence belongs to the section on marriage and divorce, and it addresses a practice that could leave a woman without the benefits of marriage and without the freedom to marry another. The Book's answer places a limit on the situation, and the community's teachers read the sentence as a measure that protects the wife from an open-ended arrangement.

**The Practice Before the Ruling**

The commentators explained that the practice could last for years, and they described the condition of the woman whose husband refused both relations and divorce. The reports show what the sentence changed, and the community's scholars noted that the harm to the wife was the concern the ruling addressed.

The Book's treatment of the divorce arrangements in the surrounding verses consistently attends to the position of the woman, and the sentence in this place belongs to that concern. Readers are shown that the legislation intervenes in an arrangement that the husband controlled entirely, and the four-month limit is described by the community's teachers as the point at which the abstention begins to be treated as harm.

**The Period and Its Meaning**

The sentence fixes the waiting period at four months, and the community's jurists discussed what follows when the period ends. Some held that the marriage is dissolved at that point, while others held that further steps are required, and the discussions reflect the different ways of reading the sentence with the verses that follow.

The community's teachers noted that the period is the longest permitted abstention, and they described it as the boundary beyond which the husband's conduct is understood as an intention to harm his wife. Readers are given the account of a ruling whose purpose is the protection of the spouse, and the sentence's statement of the period supplies the term that the community applies.

**The Return Permitted**

The sentence states that if the husband returns to his wife then God is forgiving and merciful, and the community's teachers read the clause as the permission to resume the marriage within the period. The reports differ on whether an expiation is required for the oath, and the discussion shows the community working through the question.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere describes the expiation that a broken oath requires, and the scholars connected the passages when they set out the law. The passage shows that the Book's ruling on the practice leaves room for the couple's reconciliation, and the sentence's second clause supplies the assurance that accompanies the return.

**The Reports and the Applications**

The commentaries preserve the discussions of the early teachers about the cases in which the ruling applies, and some held that it concerns an oath taken with the intention of harming the wife while others applied it to any oath that prevents the relations of marriage. The variety shows the community applying the sentence to the circumstances it met.

The reports also describe the differences about what follows the completion of the period, and the community's jurists recorded the positions. **The Names at the End**

The sentence closes with the description of God as forgiving and merciful, and the community's scholars read the pair as the ground of the permission that the sentence has granted. The attributes accompany the return to the wife, and the community's teachers used the clause when they spoke about the reconciliation of spouses.

The Book's practice of ending a ruling with a description of God appears throughout the sūrah, and the sentence here follows the pattern. The passage closes with the account of a limit that the Book has placed on a damaging practice and with the names that accompany the mercy of the arrangement.

**The Vow of Abstention**

The practice the sentence limits belonged to the customs of the Arabs, and the reports describe the woman who was left suspended between the state of a wife and the state of a stranger while her husband kept his vow. The Book answered the situation by fixing a term beyond which the arrangement could not continue.

The jurists read the sentence with the rules that follow it, and they discussed what happens when the four months expire without a return. Some held that the marriage ends at that point without a further declaration, while others required the pronouncement that the verses describe, and the reports of the companions were cited on both sides.

The verse leaves the way back open, since the return within the term remains possible and the attributes at its close accompany it. The teachers also noted that the expiation of a broken oath belongs to the matter, so that a man who returns to his wife does not treat his vow as nothing.

The rule protects the woman from a bond that had been emptied of its content, and the community's practice applied the term to the cases that came before its judges.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the four-month limit on the oath of abstention, the possibility of the husband's return, and the attributes of God that accompany the ruling. The community's teachers used the verse when they set out the law on the practice, and the reader is shown that the Book's legislation on the household protects the position of the spouse who would otherwise be left without recourse.
