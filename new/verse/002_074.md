## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:74

> Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah. And Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hardened After the Sign, Not Before It**

The verse begins with the word *then*, and its force depends on what comes before it. The community had just watched a dead man restored to life in front of them, and the sentence states what happened to their hearts afterwards. The Book is not describing people who lacked evidence; it is describing people whose evidence made no difference, and it locates the change in the organ that receives understanding rather than in the information available.

The same sequence is reported of earlier generations in the same Book: after a punishment went unheeded, the hearts of a people hardened and their conduct began to look attractive to them (6:43). The Qur'an treats hardening as a process rather than a verdict, and it presents each warning that passes unheeded as one more turn of the screw. The community in this verse had received warnings in abundance, and the hardening is stated as the outcome of the whole sequence.

**Stone as the Standard of Comparison**

The Arabic compares the hearts to stone and then raises the comparison: harder than that. The Book's comment on this construction is the sentence that follows, which supplies three examples of rocks that behave better than the hearts being described. A rock can be split by water, and a heart that has been given a sign and remains unmoved is doing less than the stone it is being compared to.

The image is a favourite of the Book's moral vocabulary. Earlier in the same chapter it warns the readers that a person diverted from God's remembrance is described in the same terms, and elsewhere the Qur'an states plainly that hardened hearts are the mark of those who have gone astray (39:22). The proof offered in this verse is not a doctrine but an observation about the physical world, which is why the comparison has such weight.

**Three Rocks, Three Ways Water Comes Out**

The sentence lists its cases in ascending order of surprise. Some rocks produce rivers. Others crack open and yield water. A third kind falls from its place out of awe of God. The first two are ordinary natural events that any traveller in Arabia had seen, and the third is a statement about the created order that the Book makes elsewhere in the passage quoted in the same sūrah: if the Qur'an had been sent down upon a mountain, it would have been humbled and torn apart in awe of God (59:21).

The progression therefore moves from what can be observed to what can only be known by revelation, and the community being addressed had access to both. The verse does not ask its hearers to accept a difficult claim before accepting an easy one; it places the familiar behaviour of stone beside the response of created things to their maker, and it lets the reader draw the conclusion about hearts that do neither.

**What Water Stands For in This Book**

The Qur'an treats water as the substance of life and uses it for the coming of revelation and mercy. It describes every living thing as made from water, the rain as a mercy that revives dead land, and the springs in the desert as a gift given without cultivation. In this verse the image does the work of contrast: rock, the hardest available material, is described as a channel for the thing that gives life, while the heart described in the same sentence holds nothing.

That contrast is sharpest against the narrative that has just ended. The community in the desert had been given water from a rock by a command, and this verse describes the same kind of people as having a heart from which nothing flows at all. The Book leaves the two pictures side by side, and the reader who has followed the passage from the springs to the murdered man's restoration sees what the comparison is measuring.

**Created Things That Answer Their Maker**

The Book repeatedly describes the physical world as responsive to God in ways human beings are not. The heavens and the earth and everything in them glorify Him, and no creature is exempt from that praise even if people cannot understand it (17:44). Mountains are commanded to echo David's hymns along with the birds (34:10). When God disclosed Himself to the mountain before Moses, the mountain crumbled (7:143).

The Prophet ﷺ supplied the same picture from ordinary experience: a palm trunk in his mosque wept when it was replaced by a pulpit, and he explained that it was grieving for the knowledge it used to hear recited beside it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3584). The verse in al-Baqarah argues from that same family of facts. What the reader is meant to notice is not that stones are remarkable but that a heart can be less responsive than they are.

**Not Unaware of What You Do**

The sentence closes with a statement about knowledge rather than about judgment. The Book does not say that God will punish the hardened hearts in this verse; it says that nothing they do escapes notice, which is a more uncomfortable claim and a more useful one. A person who has stopped being moved by evidence is not thereby unobserved.

The same attribute is stated at the end of the two charges earlier in the sūrah and at the end of the covenant violation that follows in this passage. The Book attaches it to its accounts of conduct in order to remove the possibility that a person's inner state is private. The hardening described in this verse is not a private misfortune; it is the condition of people whose conduct is being recorded, and the verse says so in its final clause.

**What the Verse Asks of Its Own Hearers**

The sūrah does not stop at reporting what happened to an earlier generation. It closes the sentence with the statement that nothing done by these people escapes God's notice, and the address is in the second person, so the community hearing the charge is included in it. The comparison with rock is therefore a warning rather than a piece of history, and the warning applies wherever the same pattern appears: a person receives evidence, is unmoved by it, and continues as though nothing had happened. The Book's answer to that condition is never to add another sign in the hope that this one will work. It states that the signs already given were sufficient, and it leaves the comparison standing. Water still comes out of stone, and the verse asks why the heart it describes yields nothing at all.
