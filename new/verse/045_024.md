## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:24

> And they argue, “There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time.” Yet they have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this ˹claim˺. They only speculate.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opponents Are Allowed to Speak**

The verse hands the argument to the deniers and lets them state their case without interruption. This is the Book's regular method, and the position is stated here with unusual clarity: nothing exists beyond this life, generations replace one another, and the only thing that ends a person is time. The logic is coherent on its own terms, and no caricature of it appears in the sentence. The Qur'an records its opponents' arguments in their own words before answering them, which is one reason its replies have force. A reader who expects scripture to defeat a straw man will find instead that the case for disbelief has been given at full strength, in a form its holders would have recognised as fair.

**"We Die, and Others Are Born"**

The first half of the claim describes the world accurately as far as it goes. Individuals die, and the species continues; a generation leaves the stage and another arrives to fill it. The same words appear in another chapter's report of the same denial, where the claim ends with the assertion that none will be resurrected (23:37). What the statement leaves out is the person. A cycle that replaces human beings as a field replaces its crops is a description of continuity, not of destiny, and it answers nothing about the fate of the particular man who is making the argument. The verse does not dispute the biology. It lets the claim stand and moves to its second half, where the real error lies.

**"Nothing Destroys Us but Time"**

The Arabic word here for time is dahr, and the phrase names a habit well known among the Arabs of the Prophet's lifetime: when hardship came, people cursed time itself, treating it as the agent that had struck them down. The practice was serious enough that the Prophet was instructed to forbid it, and the report is established in the two most authoritative collections. Abu Hurayrah narrates that the Messenger of God said: "The son of Adam hurts Me for he abuses Time though I am Time: in My Hands are all things, and I cause the revolution of day and night." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4826; the same meaning is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2246). The point of the prohibition is precise. Time is a measure of what happens, not a cause, and abuse aimed at it travels past it to the one who actually does the deeds.

**What the Deniers Have Described Correctly**

Time does swing between people, and the years do end the lives of the strong as well as the weak. The Book itself presents the alternation of day and night as one of the signs that reveal the creator, and the sūrah's own fifth verse used it that way. The deniers have taken an accurate observation and attached an agent to it that is not there. Every human system does something similar: effects are credited to the nearest visible cause, and the cause behind the cause is set aside, because acknowledging it would carry obligations. The verse's criticism is therefore not that the deniers described a world of change. It is that they stopped at the description and named the wrong author.

**"They Have No Knowledge" — an Epistemic Verdict**

The sentence that follows the quotation is a judgment about evidence rather than a denial of competence. The deniers have stated what happens after death without access to the information; they have asserted a negative about the unseen with no more support than the person who asserts a positive. The Book delivers the same verdict on the same kind of claim twice more, saying that people follow nothing but inherited assumptions and that assumption cannot replace the truth (53:28), and that those who argue this way have no knowledge to support the claim, and produce nothing but lies (43:20). The criticism is not that they are unintelligent. It is that they have pronounced on a matter on which no one can pronounce without being told, and have treated their guesswork as a settled report.

**Conjecture Is Not Humility**

A person might answer that his position is at least modest, since he asserts only what he can see. The verse denies the modesty. A negative claim about what follows death — nothing, forever — is as large as a positive one, and it carries the burden of proof that the positive claim carries. The refusal to believe is not the suspension of judgment; it is a judgment, made in the same courtroom as the position it rejects and on the same evidence. The sūrah has already shown what was available to these people: a sky, a body, weather, and a recitation, all pointing in a single direction. Their speculation is described as the product of a will that had already selected its conclusion.

**The Answer the Sūrah Will Give**

The Book's counterargument to this verse is not a philosophical demonstration but a statement about who holds life and death, which the following verses supply. Until that answer arrives, the chapter has set the debate up as clearly as possible: a group asserting that nothing survives the grave, without evidence; a revelation asserting that everything does, on the basis of the testimony of the one who made both the grave and what follows it; and a reader who is asked to weigh the two. The sūrah leaves him in no doubt about which side it considers honest, because it applies the word conjecture to one of them and reserves the word knowledge for the other.
