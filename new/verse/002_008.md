## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:8

> And there are some who say, “We believe in Allah and the Last Day,” yet they are not ˹true˺ believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third Category Introduced by a Saying**

After the mindful who are upon guidance (2:5) and those who persistently cover the truth so that warning is the same to them (2:6-7), the sūrah introduces a third human type whose mark is not refusal but utterance: *“And among people are some who say, ‘We believe in Allah and the Last Day’”* (2:8). The portrait is not yet of secret disbelief in the narrow sense of a political conspiracy in Medina; it is of a religious posture that uses the vocabulary of faith without the reality it names. The sūrah pauses on the verb *yaqūlūna* — they say — and then immediately annuls what was said: *“yet they are not believers.”* The annulment is categorical. It does not say “their faith is weak” or “their belief is incomplete”; it denies that the speech they offer counts as belief at all. That severity announces that the issue with this group is not degree but kind.

**Why “Allah and the Last Day” Is the Chosen Formula**

The formula they choose — belief in Allah and in the Last Day — repeats two of the pillars that the mindful affirmed in (2:3-4): unseen, prayer, spending, revelation, and certainty about the Hereafter. The hypocrites select precisely the two that are hardest to falsify by observation. To say “I establish prayer” can be checked by whether one stands at prayer time; to say “I spend from what I was provided” can be checked by the open hand. To say “I believe in Allah and the Last Day” is to claim an interior assent whose evidence is not immediately visible. The sūrah therefore exposes a strategy: the claim migrates to where verification is least public. That is why the refutation that follows will move inward — to hearts that are diseased (2:10) — and outward — to corruption they cause while calling it reform (2:11-12), to mockery they perform in private (2:14) — so that the interior claim is tested by its interior and exterior fruits.

**Who Are These “Some”? History and Generality**

The source notes that *al-munāfiqūn* — hypocrites — refers originally to a known circle in Medina who feigned Islam while collaborating or temporizing, and then extends to anyone who replicates the posture. The sūrah of the Hypocrites (63:1-8) will supply the fuller anatomy: they make their oaths a shield, their appearance pleases, their words are listened to, and they think every cry is against them. The Medinan occasion gives the verses a historical sharpness, but the grammar — *wa-min al-nās* — “and among people” — universalizes it from the first syllable. Every generation contains *among people* those who find it advantageous to be counted among the mindful without undertaking the mindfulness that defines the class. That is why a later report, traced to Salmān al-Fārsī, is transmitted as saying that the people described in the corruption verses “have not yet come” (Ṭ; accepted by Ṭ, IK) — not to deny the Medinan referent but to prevent the comfortable thought that hypocrisy expired with them [UNVERIFIED: Salmān al-Fārsī “have not yet come” report — transmitted in al-Ṭabarī/Ibn Kathīr as Companion/exegete comment, not as graded prophetic hadith; treated here as tafsīr report, not as hadith with chain].

**Saying and Being — Why Speech Alone Is Annulled**

The Qur'an elsewhere distinguishes saying from being with the same bluntness. *“When the hypocrites come to you they say, ‘We bear witness that you are the Messenger of Allah’ — and Allah knows you are His Messenger, and Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are liars”* (63:1-2). The sentence structure is identical: a quoted profession of faith followed by a divine repudiation that the profession counts. The point is not that speech is irrelevant — the believer’s *shahādah* is itself a saying — but that speech is not self-authenticating. It must be corroborated by the relation between heart, limb, and expenditure that (2:3) established. The hypocrite’s profession borrows the believer’s vocabulary and then detaches it from the discipline that gives that vocabulary weight. By immediately saying *“yet they are not believers,”* the verse teaches the community to listen for corroboration rather than for fluency.

**Faith in the Last Day as a Test Case**

Why is the Last Day paired with Allah in their formula? Because certainty about the Hereafter (*yūqinūn*, 2:4) was the final mark of the mindful, and the sūrah’s subsequent portraits will show that hypocrisy is most visible precisely where the Hereafter is concerned. The hypocrite’s calculation — to hedge between believers and rejectors, to keep options open, to spread corruption while calling it reform — is rational only if the final accounting is treated as uncertain or distant. A community certain that *“every soul shall be paid in full for what it earned”* will not treat the double life as costless. By having them claim the very belief whose behavioral implications would forbid their behavior, the verse exposes an inconsistency that is ethical before it is doctrinal: they claim to be certain of the very reality their actions presuppose is not there.

**“They Are Not Believers” — A Present Verdict**

The denial — *wa-mā hum bi-muʾminīn* — uses the emphatic *bi-* to strengthen the negation: they are not *in any sense* believers, not even minimally. The present tense matters. The verse does not say they *will* not be believers or that they were *never* believers; it says, of people who are now saying “We believe,” they are not. The sūrah thus refuses to allow the future possibility of their repentance to soften the present description, and it also refuses to allow their past association with the community to count as present faith. Each of those refusals will be needed later when the hypocrites protest that they are peacemakers (2:11) or that they are superior in intellect (2:13). The present verdict provides the standard against which later claims will be measured and found wanting.

**From the Two Sealed to the Many Who Say**

The triptych is now complete in outline: the mindful who are upon guidance (2:2-5), the persistent rejectors whose hearts are sealed (2:6-7), and the sayers who are not what they say (2:8). The third group is longer in treatment than the second, as the coming verses show, because pretense is more complex than refusal. Refusal can be stated in two verses; pretense requires ten. The next verses will anatomize that complexity — the attempt to deceive God that deceives only the self (2:9), the disease that is increased (2:10), the corruption relabeled as reform (2:11-12), the contempt for believers as fools (2:13), and the two-faced conference of mockery (2:14-15) — so that the simple denial of this verse is gradually filled with its evidence. The verse’s bluntness is therefore also its pedagogy: it states the conclusion first, so that the reader watches the evidence accumulate under a fixed verdict.

**A Grammar That Refuses Neutrality**

Finally, *wa-min al-nās* — “and among people” — refuses the moral neutrality that modern ears might wish to grant to private belief. The sūrah does not allow a category of people who “say” faith as a social nicety while holding no position. To say “We believe” in the context of a community that is being summoned to prayer and spending, and to do so while withholding the heart’s assent, is not a neutral social act; it is a taking of sides. The verses that follow will show that the taking of sides has public consequences — corruption on the earth — and that the failure to perceive those consequences is itself part of the disease. The denial *“they are not believers”* is therefore not an ad hominem; it is the identification of a social fact that the remainder of the opening will prove.
