## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:8

> And there are some who say, “We believe in Allah and the Last Day,” yet they are not ˹true˺ believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third Group Enters the Sūrah**

The opening of al-Baqarah now turns to a new category. The mindful were described in the second through fifth verses; those whose rejection is settled occupied the next two; here a group appears whose speech and conviction do not match. The sūrah's first twenty verses present these three portraits in order, and the third is the longest and most detailed, running from this verse through verse 20.

The historical setting explains why. In Madinah, after the migration, profession of Islam had become the price of belonging to a community that was also a political order. That situation produced a class of people whose interests lay with the community's enemies while their public position lay inside it. The Qur'an gives them the name by which the whole subsequent tradition knows them: the hypocrites.

**A Profession Quoted and Denied**

The verse does not paraphrase the hypocrite; it quotes him. The sentence *We believe in Allah and the Last Day* is placed inside quotation marks and then contradicted by the clause that follows it, which states that they are not believers. The Book uses this technique repeatedly for this group, letting them speak and answering their words, and quotation marks are the only way a text can stage an opponent.

Two features of the quoted creed are notable. It names God and the Last Day and omits the Messenger, the Book and the Hereafter's details. It is a formula general enough to survive inspection and empty enough to avoid commitment. What the Qur'an denies is not the vocabulary but the claim attached to it: people who say these words are being told that saying them has not made them true.

**Why Deception Is the Wrong Description of What They Do**

The next verse will describe their attempt to deceive God and the believers, and the sūrah's placement of the denial before the attempt is precise. A person who says the words of faith to gain a place among the believers is not deceiving God, since nothing is hidden from God; he is misrepresenting himself to a community that cannot read hearts. The Book's verdict here interrupts the project at its first step: the words are not accepted as evidence.

The Qur'an describes the same people with the limbs of a different scene, when they stand for prayer *“half-heartedly only to be seen by people—hardly remembering Allah at all”* (4:142). In both passages the criterion is identical: what is done before witnesses is measured against what is absent when the witnesses are gone. This verse states the verdict on the public side, and the following verses will describe the private one.

**The Hypocrite as a Recurring Type**

Sūrah 63 is named for the hypocrites and opens by describing their characteristic act, an oath of witness that the Book calls a lie (63:1). The category is treated throughout the Qur'an as a permanent feature of religious communities rather than as a scandal confined to the Prophet's lifetime. Wherever a group holds power, prestige or protection, people will attach themselves to it for those reasons, and where those incentives disappear, so will some of the attachments.

The Book does not answer this with a demand for proof of sincerity, which no human being could supply, and it does not dissolve the community into a group of the verified. It defines the offence, describes its costs, and leaves the judgement to the one who knows what is in the hearts. Verse 8 states a fact about God's knowledge; the community that receives it is not given the same instrument.

**ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy and the Politics of Divided Loyalty**

The reports identify ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl, a leading man of the Khazraj tribe of Madinah, as the most prominent figure of this kind. He entered Islam without conviction, negotiated on behalf of the city before the migration, and lost standing when the Prophet ﷺ arrived and the tribes gave him authority. The literature of the sūrah's setting shows a man whose position inside the community kept improving as his commitment to it stayed minimal.

His case shows what the verse describes without naming names. The problem for a community is not the presence of a disbeliever, who can be dealt with openly; it is the presence of a member whose loyalty cannot be audited and whose interest is served by every division he can create. The Qur'an's answer at the head of this sūrah is to describe the condition and its end, not to issue a list of suspects.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Opening Argument**

The sūrah is the longest in the Qur'an, 286 verses, and it spends most of its length on law, history and argument. It opens, though, with a sorting of humanity into three portraits and a definition of what each responds to: guidance accepted, guidance refused, guidance claimed without being held. Everything that follows in the sūrah is addressed to a community that contains all three, and the legislation that begins later will assume the third group's existence.

That is why the denial in this verse is stated as a clarification rather than a curse. The sentence does not expel anyone from the community, and it does not name a punishment. It corrects a description: the words have been said, and the words have not been made true. What the verse announces is an accountability that the community cannot enforce and that the speakers cannot escape.

**A Present Tense Without a Fixed Membership**

The verse says that there are some who say, not that there were some. Arabic carries the distinction in a verb that describes an ongoing situation rather than a completed event, and the choice keeps the sentence open. The reports preserve the names of specific men in Madinah whose conduct matched the description, and the Book itself declines to supply them. A reader who is given a description instead of a list must decide what to do with it.

That silence serves two purposes at once. It protects the community from turning the passage into an instrument against named neighbours, which no human being could apply reliably, and it prevents any reader from closing the file with the conclusion that the matter concerned only a generation already dead. The description is present tense because the type it names is present.
