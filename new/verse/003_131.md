## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:131

> Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Guard Oneself**

The verse commands the believers to guard themselves against the Fire, and the word it uses is the one the Book employs throughout for the awareness that keeps a person within limits. It carries the senses of fear, care, and standing watch.

The same root provides the word for the quality that the previous verses named as the condition for the garden, so that the command and the promise use a single vocabulary. The Book's instructions are built this way: the safeguard it recommends is the awareness of God, and the reward it promises is attached to the same awareness. What the verse adds here is the object of the guarding, which is a specific destination.

**The Fire Described as Prepared**

The sentence states that the Fire has been prepared and names those for whom the preparation is made. The Book uses the same image in its earlier description of the Fire, which it calls a blaze fuelled by people and stones and states to be prepared for the disbelievers (2:24).

Another passage addresses the believers and tells them to protect themselves and their families from a fire whose fuel is people and stones, guarded by severe angels who carry out every command they are given (66:6). The image of preparation suggests something made ready in advance, and the Book uses it to remove the sense that the destination is a metaphor for consequences in general.

**Prepared for the Disbelievers, Addressed to the Believers**

The apparent tension in the verse is that a warning about the disbelievers is issued to the believers, and the sūrah's method explains it. The community is told what the destination of a certain course of conduct is, and it is told in time to avoid taking that course.

The passage immediately before this verse concerned the prohibition of interest, and the passage immediately after concerns obedience to God and the Messenger. The warning is placed between two instructions, which is where the Book usually puts it, and the community reading it is given the choice the passage is designed to present.

**Fire and Fuel in the Book's Descriptions**

The Book describes the Fire through its fuel in several passages, and the descriptions are consistently concrete. The earlier reference in the sūrah of the Cow states that the fuel is people and stones; the passage in the sūrah of the Prohibition uses the same words.

These descriptions do not function as information about the physical construction of the Hereafter. They function as moral images. What fuels a fire is what is put into it, and the Book's use of the image tells the reader that the destination is not arbitrary: it is fed by the conduct that leads to it, and the same image reappears in the passage about those who hoard wealth and do not spend it in the way of God.

**What Guarding Consists Of**

The command to guard does not specify a technique, and the surrounding verses supply the content. The Book's usage of the root is consistently about conduct and about the awareness that governs conduct.

The sūrah has already supplied several of the practices it means: giving what a person values, calling others to what is good, holding together as a community, obeying God and His Messenger. The warning in this verse is not a separate instruction to be carried out on its own but a reminder of the stakes attached to the instructions that surround it.


The verse stands at the point where the sūrah turns from the battle narrative and the promises connected with it toward a series of moral instructions about wealth and conduct. Its placement gives those instructions their frame.

A community that has been told about the fate of its enemies and about the losses of its own day is now told what kind of conduct leads where. The warning is short because the material around it is not: the verses that follow describe the character of the people for whom the garden is prepared, and this sentence provides the contrast against which that description is read.

**A Warning the Community Recites About Itself**

The verse has been recited by Muslims in every generation as part of the sūrah, which means it has always functioned as a warning addressed to the community that reads it rather than as a piece of information about someone else.

That is how the Book intends such sentences to work. The community that hears itself described as the recipient of a warning about a fire prepared for the disbelievers is being told that the distinction between the two is not fixed by the label a person carries, and the sūrah's next sentences describe what the distinction consists of in practice.

**The Book's Refusal to Soften the Warning**

The Book describes the Fire in plain terms and does not present it as a figure of speech for unpleasant consequences. It names its fuel, its preparation, and its guardians, and it places the descriptions in passages addressed to the believers as often as to anyone else.

The tradition follows the Book in this. The warnings are repeated in the community's instruction, its recitation, and its preaching, and the practical effect is the one the sūrah intends here: a person who has the destination in mind orders his conduct accordingly. Nothing in the passage suggests that the warning is meant to frighten people into despair; the verses that follow immediately describe the alternative and the character of those who take it, and the two destinations are set out with the same directness.
