## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:59

> So it was. And We awarded it ˹all˺ to the Children of Israel.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rulers' Former Glory Changes Hands**

The verse states the reversal simply: what the tyrants left was awarded to the Children of Israel. The people whom Pharaoh treated as laborers become heirs to the worldly goods associated with the ruling class. This is not presented as a human coup planned by the Israelites. It is God's judgment on a system that believed its possessions and ranks were permanent.

The word all gathers the earlier list—gardens, springs, treasures, and residences—into one reversal. The meaning is not that every object was immediately carried away by the Israelites or that every detail of later settlement is supplied here. The point is that Pharaoh's people lost the status and resources they treated as proof of superiority, while Israel was raised from subjection into a new position.

**Inheritance Here Is a Divine Transfer of Status**

Commentators differed over how literally to understand the inheritance. Some took it to mean that the Israelites came to possess the Egyptians' actual wealth. Others emphasized a transfer of worldly station and the reversal of power, while some reports connected the wealth with what the Israelites took by God's command when they left. The wording supports a real reversal, but the larger Qur'anic account cautions against forcing every detail into a single material scene.

The strongest conclusion holds together possession and status. God stripped the tyrants of what made them appear secure and gave the oppressed a share in the dignity and resources from which they had been excluded. Whether every residence was occupied immediately is a secondary question. The central truth is that Pharaoh's order was not the final owner of Egypt's good things.

**The Transfer Is Judgment, Not Mere Reward**

The Israelites are not described here as receiving wealth because wealth itself proves their virtue. The transfer occurs within judgment on Pharaoh. The tyrants lose what they used to support domination. The Israelites receive what God's decree assigns them. This is a moral reversal in history, not a doctrine that material possession always marks divine favor.

That distinction matters because the Israelites will later face their own tests in the wilderness. Inheriting goods does not make a community incapable of disobedience, and losing goods does not prove that a people is rejected. The verse describes one act of justice in one historical setting. It should not be turned into a simple formula equating wealth with righteousness.

**God's Gifts Can Expose the Fragility of Ownership**

Pharaoh's court treated ownership as an extension of power. The verse shows that ownership can change when God changes the order. Possession is therefore contingent. A ruler may build residences, control water, and store treasure, but none of these becomes his by an eternal right. God can remove him and award the same goods to people he regarded as beneath him.

This is a warning against pride in property. The good things of the world are real, but they remain gifts. The proper response to receiving them is gratitude and justice, not the construction of a hierarchy that denies other people's humanity. The transfer from tyrants to Israelites exposes how foolish it is to treat a temporary holding as proof of permanent superiority.

**The Verse Uses Succession to Correct Historical Memory**

Pharaoh's narrative presented the Israelites as outcasts who had no rightful place in the land. God's action tells history differently. The people who were excluded from full dignity are now named as recipients. The ruler's story of who belongs is overturned by the Lord who owns the land and its resources.

This does not mean that every political claim made in the name of inheritance is automatically sacred. The verse is about a divine act in a specific prophetic history. It teaches that human rulers cannot write the final account of belonging. A community's imposed low status can be reversed by God, and the possessions that seemed to confirm the ruler's story can become evidence against it.

**A Scholarly Difference Does Not Change the Main Reversal**

The report that the Israelites later returned to Egypt and took possession of the goods has been attributed to some commentators, but that reading sits uneasily with the Qur'an's account of their later journey and the land they were commanded to seek. A more careful reading stays with what the verse plainly establishes: God transferred the former tyrants' worldly standing and possessions to Israel in the course of judgment.

The disagreement is useful because it shows how interpretation should work. One should distinguish the text's central assertion from a later narrative detail that may not fit the wider scriptural sequence. The main point remains firm even when the exact manner of inheritance is discussed: no tyrant can guarantee that his wealth will remain with his house, and no oppressed people is forgotten by the Lord who can reverse the order.

**The Awarded Goods Carry a Responsibility**

Receiving what once belonged to an oppressive order creates a new test. The Israelites must not merely enjoy the reversal; they must learn how to use power and provision without reproducing Pharaoh's injustice. The verse does not develop that duty here, but the structure of the story makes it clear. Deliverance from domination is incomplete if the rescued community adopts domination as its own ideal.

God's award is therefore both relief and responsibility. It gives the Israelites visible evidence that Pharaoh was not sovereign. It also places goods in their hands that can be used well or badly. The true inheritance is not possession alone. It is the chance to live under God's lordship rather than under the rule of a man who called his own control permanent.
