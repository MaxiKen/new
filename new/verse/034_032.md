## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:32

> The arrogant will respond to the lowly, “Did we ever hinder you from guidance after it came to you? In fact, you were wicked ˹on your own˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Defence Built on a Technicality That Happens to Be True**

The leaders answer with a question rather than a denial of fact, and the question is precisely framed: *a-naḥnu ṣadadnākum ʿani al-hudā baʿda idh jāʾakum* — did we turn you away from the guidance **after it had come to you**? The final clause is the whole defence. They are not claiming innocence of influence; they are pointing out that the guidance reached their followers directly, in the same words and the same streets, and that nothing they did prevented it from arriving. Once a message has been delivered to a man's own ears, the leaders argue, what happens next is inside him. This is a stronger case than the followers' and it is uncomfortable for exactly that reason. The Qur'an does not put a foolish argument in the mouths of the arrogant. It puts the best one available, and lets its strength stand.

**The Verb They Choose for What They Did Not Do**

*Ṣadda* means to block, to turn back, to divert something from its course — the word used for barring people from the sacred mosque and for turning others from the path of God. The leaders select it because it names a physical obstruction, and physical obstruction is the charge they can most safely deny. They did not seize anyone's ears. But the verb's narrowness is what exposes them, since the Qur'an uses *ṣadd* for more than blockade: the sūrah has already described people who *strive to discredit Our revelations* (34:5), which is obstruction by other means. What the leaders have done is answer a wide accusation with a narrow denial and hope the difference passes unnoticed. Anyone who has watched a powerful man respond to a charge of corruption by denying a specific transaction will recognise the technique.

**"In Fact, You Were Wicked"**

Having deflected the charge, they attack: *bal kuntum mujrimīn* — rather, you were criminals. The particle *bal* is corrective, and the noun is *mujrim*, from *jurm*, a crime — the same root the believers used of themselves in the disclaimer a few verses earlier (34:25). Notice the tense. *Kuntum* is the past continuous, describing a settled state rather than an act: you **were** criminals, all along, independently of us. The counter-charge is therefore not that the followers made a bad decision but that they were the kind of people who would. This is the arrogant man's characteristic move at the moment his authority stops protecting him. He does not defend his leadership; he disowns the people who accepted it, and reclassifies them as defective material that would have failed under any management.

**What the Qur'an Concedes to This Argument**

The Book does not treat the leaders' point as worthless, and the honest reading has to say so. It insists repeatedly that no one carries another's burden (6:164; 17:15; 39:7), which is the principle the leaders are invoking. It records that the followers chose whom to follow, and the choice is described elsewhere as a preference for wealth and influence over the messenger who came with proofs. Al-Aḥzāb has the followers make the same complaint in the next world — *"Our Lord! We obeyed our leaders and elite, but they led us astray"* (33:67) — and the Qur'an's answer there is not to cancel their responsibility but to add to the leaders': *"Our Lord! Give them double ˹our˺ punishment"* (33:68). So the accounting the Qur'an operates is not zero-sum. Guilt assigned to a leader is not subtracted from a follower, which means both sides of this quarrel can be substantially right and still both be convicted.

**Why Neither Party Mentions the Evidence**

There is something absent from this exchange that ought to be present. The dispute is about who caused a failure of belief, and neither party discusses whether the message was true. The leaders do not argue that the Qur'an was implausible; the followers do not say they examined it and were unconvinced. Both treat belief as a social outcome produced by pressure, influence and class, and they argue only about the distribution of the pressure. That shared assumption is the deeper failure the scene exposes, and it explains why the Qur'an lets the quarrel run at length instead of interrupting it. Two parties arguing about who is responsible for a decision that neither of them ever made on the merits have convicted themselves of something neither is accusing the other of. The sūrah will shortly prescribe the opposite procedure, telling people to stand apart from the crowd, singly and in pairs, and think (34:46).

**The Sound of Authority Withdrawing**

Read as speech rather than as doctrine, the leaders' reply has a recognisable tone: the cool, technically accurate self-defence of a man whose subordinates have become a liability. The relationship that held in the world does not survive the hearing. In Mecca these men had offered protection, standing and belonging, and their followers had traded compliance for it; here the protection evaporates in a sentence, and the followers are informed that they were criminals on their own account and always had been. The Qur'an makes this desertion a fixed feature of the next world's scenes, where alliances built on worldly advantage are shown collapsing precisely when they would be needed.

**A Question Asked by People Who Know the Answer**

One further feature of the reply deserves notice: it is phrased as a question rather than a statement. *Did we turn you away?* A man confident of his innocence generally asserts it; a man who puts it to his accuser as a question is inviting the accuser to produce proof, which is the move of someone who believes the proof is unavailable rather than someone who believes the charge is false. The distinction is exactly the one the followers will exploit in their next answer, when they stop describing a general atmosphere of discouragement and name a specific instruction they were given. Until that point the leaders' question stands unanswered, and it is worth registering how effective it is. Vagueness on one side and precision on the other decides most arguments, and at this stage in the exchange the precision belongs entirely to the arrogant.
