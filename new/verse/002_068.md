## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:68

> They said, “Call upon your Lord to clarify for us what type ˹of cow˺ it should be!” He replied, “Allah says, ‘The cow should neither be old nor young but in between. So do as you are commanded!’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**“Your Lord”: The Pronoun of Distance**

The question that opens the negotiation carries its diagnosis inside its grammar: *call upon your Lord* — *rabbika*, your Lord, not our Lord. The pronoun will stand unchanged through the whole exchange — *your Lord* in every question, three times running — and its consistency rules out accident. A community inside its covenant says *our Lord*; a community conducting business with its prophet about a command it has not accepted says *your Lord*, locating the obligation on the messenger's side of a line it has not yet crossed. The Qur'an's dialogue preserves distances exactly this way, and the exegetes hear the distance here: the questioners stand at arm's length from the command even while asking about it, treating the order as Moses's property to explain rather than Allah's claim on themselves. The distance is also the mockery-question's continuation in polite dress. *Are you joking?* became unsayable once Moses took refuge; the same refusal now wears the vocabulary of inquiry — *call upon your Lord, ask Him for us, let Him clarify* — deference in form, deferral in function. Every word of the question is technically proper; the pronoun discloses the posture. The verse thus teaches a reading skill the whole recital rewards: in divine dialogue, the grammar of address tells you where the speaker actually stands.

**A Question or a Delay?**

The commentators divide on the questioners' intent, and the division matters because the two readings produce different lessons. The first takes the question at face value: a command to sacrifice *a cow* left genuine ambiguity — which cow, whose cow, from what herd — and the questioners sought the specification that lawful performance required. The second reads the question as stalling: the command was perfectly executable (any cow answers to an indefinite noun), so the question sought not clarity but time, each round of specification delaying the knife. The grounds for the second are stronger, and they are internal to the text. The preceding question was a mockery-accusation, which establishes the heart's direction; the exchange ends with the narration that they slaughtered it *but almost did not* — reluctance confirmed at the finish; and the whole arc's cost-structure (the specifications narrowing to a single expensive animal) shows the questions adding difficulty rather than removing it. Yet the Qur'an's handling of the delay is the verse's real instruction, because Allah answers. Each question receives a specification — the stalling tactic is met not with refusal but with accommodation, and the accommodation is itself the penalty: every answer narrows the field and raises the price. A reader who delays a command by interrogating it should expect the interrogation to be answered — precisely, cumulatively, and at his expense.

**Neither Old Nor Young: The Specification of the Middle**

The answer arrives with the divine formula attached — *Allah says* — and specifies age: *neither ˹too˺ old nor ˹too˺ young, but in between*. The two excluded terms are *fāriḍ* — the over-aged animal, past work and past milk, tough and spent — and *bikr* — the uncalved heifer, too young for the service. The commanded animal is *ʿawānun bayna dhālik*, a mature one between the two: in its full strength, neither declining nor unformed. The specification of the middle is the Qur'an's recurring signature of excellence, and the sūrah itself supplies the doctrinal parallel: the community of this revelation is made *an upright community* — *ummatan wasaṭan*, a middle nation — set between extremes to stand as witnesses over humanity (2:143). The Arabic *wasaṭ* carries both meanings at once: the midmost position and the excellent one, since the middle of a thing is its balance-point and its strength. Applied to the cow, the rule produces the best available animal — full of life, fit for sacrifice, neither a worn-out beast offered cheaply nor a calf offered prematurely. Applied to the soul, which the esoteric commentators do immediately, it produces the law of spiritual timing: Ibn ʿAjībah reads the specified age as the season of self-correction — the soul neither too old, its habits set and change grown costly, nor too young, feeling itself immortal and seeing no need. The command's first specification is thus also its most transferable: whatever is offered to Allah is offered in its middle strength, and the middle strength of a life is now.

**“So Do As You Are Commanded”**

The answer closes with a clause that is not specification but rebuke: *fa-fʿalū mā tuʾmarūn* — so do what you are commanded. Its placement is the verse's sharpest stroke. Allah has answered the question fully, and in the same breath redirects the questioners from asking to doing — the answer given, and the command to act on it restated, so that the clarification cannot become the ground of a further clarification. The clause is the divine version of the prophetic rule the ḥadīth legislated: leave me as I have left you; when I command, do as you are able (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1337). And the sūrah's wider legislation names the danger the clause fences: believers are warned not to ask about matters which, once made clear, may distress them — questions whose answers convert latitude into obligation (5:101). The cow's questioners are living inside exactly that mechanism in reverse: they ask, and each answer obligates. The rebuke-clause is mercy offered at the first round — the door back to the cheap obedience still open, the original command still standing in full force under the new specification. That the questioners will pass through it and ask again is what turns the episode from instruction into case law: the verse records the last moment when *do as you are commanded* was the whole remaining task.

**Each Answer Raises the Price**

The economics of the exchange deserve exact statement, because they are the verse's permanent warning. An indefinite command is a cheap command: any cow, taken from the nearest herd, slaughtered today, discharges *sacrifice a cow* in full. Each specification the questioners extract removes some of the field — age first, then further marks in the rounds that follow — and each removal transfers cost from the command to the commanded. By the end of the negotiation the field has narrowed to a single animal owned, the reports say, by a person who knows its uniqueness and prices it accordingly; the obedience that began free ends purchased at the market's maximum. The dynamic generalizes beyond cows. A law received with *how much must I do?* rather than *let me do it* is a law whose minimums will be discovered, defined, and paid for — the interrogator ending up owing more than the doer, having converted his own questions into obligations. The tradition's verdict on the episode states the counterfactual: had they slaughtered the nearest cow, it would have sufficed. The sufficiency was there at the first word, and every word after it was a price increase the questioners ordered for themselves. The verse is thus the sūrah's instruction on the reception of commands, delivered in the form its first hearers needed — a negotiation they recognized, whose invoice they inherited.

**The Soul in Its Middle Season**

The esoteric reading the verse invites is not decoration but application, and its structure follows the specification exactly. The cow to be sacrificed is the egotistical soul, and the age-rule is the rule of the work's timing. Too old: the soul whose habits have calcified, whose appetites have become character, for whom the knife of discipline arrives after the flesh has set — the change possible still, but at the cost the over-aged animal names. Too young: the soul that has not yet tasted consequence, that feels its days inexhaustible and its reform always postponable — the calf of a life not yet formed enough to have anything to give up. Between them stands the *ʿawān*: the soul in its working strength, old enough to know what it carries and young enough to set it down — and the sūrah's own middle-nation verse (2:143) confirms that the between-position is where Allah stations the communities He intends to keep. The reading also preserves the historical event's primacy, which is the rule of every sound esoteric reading of this recital: the cow was a cow, slaughtered in a wilderness camp, at the end of a negotiation that cost its conductors dearly — and the soul-rule rides on that event because the event is true. What the verse commands its first hearers to slaughter, it commands every later reader to identify: the animal in between, the season that is now, the obedience that grows expensive by the hour. *So do as you are commanded* — the clause spoken over a cow, standing over every middle season since.
