## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:55

> And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We will never believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes,” so a thunderbolt struck you while you were looking on.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Demand Made to a Man in Front of Them**

The community's words are addressed to Moses, and the demand is not for a sign from God but for a sight of God himself. The phrasing is confrontational: they will not accept him until they see, and the object of the sight is the one who sent him. The Book records their sentence as spoken, so that the reader hears the claim in the form in which it was made.

The request belongs to a pattern in this community's history that the sūrah has been documenting from the beginning of the address. Deliverance, the sea, the appointment, the calf, the pardon, and now a demand for proof of a kind that no messenger can supply. The Book presents the demand as the next step after a pardon, which is why it makes no attempt to explain its motive.

**What They Had Seen Already**

The sentence is spoken by people who had watched the sea open and close, been fed with bread from the sky, and been forgiven after an idol. Nothing in their situation lacked evidence, and the Book's account of this moment makes the abundance of prior signs plain enough that the demand reads as something other than a search for information. What was being asked for was a change in the terms of the relationship: not more proof, but a different kind of access.

The Book has already had Moses answer an earlier iteration of the same request, and it tells the reader plainly that the sight of God is not available: in the same chapter God tells His prophet that no human being can see Him and live, and Moses himself asks to see and is answered by the destruction of the mountain he stands behind (7:143). The demand in this verse is answered before it is made, and the answer has already been spoken to Moses himself.

**Moses' Own Request and the Difference Between Them**

The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf records the same desire in a different mouth. Moses asks to see God, is told that he cannot, and is shown a demonstration at the mountain which leaves him unconscious when he recovers. He then says of himself: *“Glory be to You! I turn to You in repentance, and I am the first of the believers.”* (7:143).

That comparison is what separates the two incidents. One man asked out of longing and repented when he was taught the limits; a crowd demanded a sight as a condition of its acceptance, and it was destroyed by a thunderbolt while it watched. The Book presents the same wish in two contexts and draws the distinction between a creature learning its place and a community demanding terms.

**The Punishment That Struck Them Where They Stood**

The Arabic word rendered thunderbolt describes the blast that destroys, and the Book uses the same term for the destruction of earlier communities. Its detail is important: the strike came while they were looking on, which means it was not a gradual disaster but an event that happened in their presence, in front of the man whose authority they were refusing.

The Book's judgments on defiant communities are regularly described in this immediate way. The demand for a direct sight was answered by a direct blow, and the sentence ends with the people on the ground. The next verse reports their restoration, which means the event is narrated in the sūrah as a lesson rather than as a verdict, and the surrounding passage treats it as one item in a series that includes the community's subsequent choices.

**Why the Demand Was Not Satisfied**

The Book does not consider a demonstration of the kind demanded to be useful even if it were possible. Its treatment of evidence throughout the sūrah is that signs confirm people who are already prepared to accept them and harden people who are not, and the same principle appears in the story of the calf, where an object that made a sound was enough to hold a crowd's loyalty after the sea had divided.

The Book states the principle in the general form elsewhere: the deflection of the eyes and hearts of those who refused at first, with the result that they are left wandering (6:110). The demand in this verse belongs to that category. What the community asked for would not have settled their position, and the answer they received was not the sight they requested but the consequence of having asked for it.

**The Frame of the Demand: "We Will Not Believe You Unless"**

The Book places the community's own preposition at the centre of the verse: their belief was conditional, and the condition was set by them. That is the grammar of the sentence, and the remainder of the passage continues in the same register, with requests, complaints and stipulations being made to a prophet who is at that moment interceding for them.

The sūrah's later verses will record them making conditions about food, about a cow, and about access to the land. Read together, the passage describes a community that treats revelation as a party in a negotiation rather than as an address from its Lord, and this verse is where the pattern is first stated in the plainest terms. It ends with the thunderbolt because the negotiation had reached its limit.

**What the Book Does With Demands for Proof**

The request refused in this verse is one the Book declines elsewhere on different grounds. When the Quraysh asked the Prophet ﷺ for particular signs, the reply was that signs belong to God and that a messenger is only a warner; the Qur'an treats the demand as a request for a different relationship rather than for more evidence. The community at Sinai had already been given more than most, so the demand in this verse was not a request for information, and the punishment that answered it was not the withholding of evidence.

What the Book consistently offers instead of proof on demand is the ordinary material of a life: the created world, the record of earlier peoples, and whatever guidance has already arrived. The demand in this verse belongs to a family of requests the Book reports and refuses, and the thunderbolt that followed it shows what kind of answer such demands receive when they are pressed as conditions of acceptance.
