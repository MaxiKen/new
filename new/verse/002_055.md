## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:55

> And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We will never believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes,” so a thunderbolt struck you while you were looking on.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith Offered a Condition**

The verse records a sentence with a structure worth holding still: *we will never believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes*. This is not a request for evidence — the speakers had the sea, the manna, the mountain — but a condition attached to faith itself, a demand that belief suspend until the unseen becomes visible. The grammar is emphatic at every joint: *lan*, the particle of never; *ḥattā*, until; and the object not a sign but Allah. The sūrah's opening had defined the God-conscious as believers *in the unseen* (2:3); this sentence is that definition refused. And the refusal targets Moses in his office: *we will never believe you* — not "we doubt this sign" but "your word, as such, is not creditable until our terms are met." The verse thus names a posture the Qur'an returns to constantly — faith negotiated from the position of the skeptic, with the terms set by the skeptic — and answers it not with argument but with event. The negotiation collapses in the same verse that reports it: the condition was seeing, and what arrived instead was a stroke that ended sight's bargaining.

**Jahratan: The Demand to See Openly**

The demand's manner-word is *jahratan* — openly, plainly, manifestly. The same word qualifies speech elsewhere in the Qur'an, describing what is said aloud rather than in secret, and applied to seeing it carries both senses the commentators draw out: seeing with the eye in plain sight, and seeing publicly — a theophany witnessed, undisputed, available to the whole camp. Either sense convicts the demand. If *jahratan* means with our own eyes, the speakers are asking the invisible to submit to the organ of a creature — sight, the faculty that grasps surfaces and finite things, set as judge over the One whom, as the Qur'an states, vision does not grasp while He grasps all vision (6:103). If it means publicly, they are asking God to perform — to appear on demand, before an audience, on their schedule — which converts the Lord of the covenant into a supplier of spectacles. The two readings share one root fault: the demand reverses the direction of disclosure. In the covenant, Allah shows what He wills, when He wills, through signs He chooses; *jahratan* asks that choice to be handed over. The thunderbolt's arrival inside the same sentence is the answer to a demand framed as ultimatum.

**Ṣāʿiqah: A Word Wider Than Lightning**

The instrument is *ṣāʿiqah* — rendered thunderbolt, but the word's range in Arabic and in Qur'anic usage is wider: a stroke of destruction, a deafening cry, fire from the sky, an earthquake's shock, a swoon-inducing blast. The Qur'an sends it upon ʿĀd and Thamūd as the punishment that overtook them (41:13, 51:44), and it names with the same root the collapse of Moses at the mountain — when his Lord manifested to the mountain and levelled it to dust, *"Moses collapsed unconscious"* (7:143), *ṣaʿiqa*, struck down by what the mountain could not stand. The same root describes creation's swoon at the Trumpet on the Day of Judgment (39:68). The breadth matters for reading this verse: the *ṣāʿiqah* here is whatever it needs to be — fire, blast, shock — to accomplish one stated effect, the death of the speakers, reported flatly in the next verse's premise. The word choice also ties the scene to its own precedent. Moses asked to see, and the seeing was refused with a mountain's demolition and his own unconsciousness (7:143); the people here demand to see, and the demand is answered with the same family of stroke. One prophet asked once, in private, and was answered with a lesson and a repentance; a community demanded as ultimatum, and the answer arrived without the lesson's softening.

**Seventy Men, or a Separate Scene**

The exegetes divide on whether this verse and the scene in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf are one event. Al-Aʿrāf reports that *"Moses chose seventy men from among his people for Our appointment and, when they were seized by an earthquake, he cried"* — and there pleads for them, attributing the seizure to the foolish among them and asking pardon (7:155). One side identifies the two: after the calf's destruction, Moses took seventy of the choicest — including calf-worshippers seeking reconciliation — and their demand to see Allah drew the stroke of this verse. The other side keeps them separate, noting that al-Aʿrāf's instrument is *rajfah*, an earthquake-shock, while this verse's is *ṣāʿiqah*, and that the contexts differ: seventy chosen men at an appointment, against a communal demand filed to Moses in the camp. The identification reading has to explain the word-difference; the separation reading has to accept two stroke-scenes in one wilderness. Neither is decisive, and the Qur'an's habit of filing one event twice with varied detail — the sea, the mountain, the manna all appear in multiple sūrahs with different instruments named — makes identification entirely plausible. What both readings agree on is the event's substance: a demand to see, a stroke from the unseen, witnesses present. The identification question changes the cast list, not the case.

**Struck While Watching**

The verse's final clause — *while you were looking on* — is the same circumstantial phrase that closed the sea's drowning at verse 50, and its reuse is exact in function. There, a people watched their pursuers drown; here, the demanders watched each other fall. The clause removes every buffer between the sign and its witnesses: the stroke did not happen in the distance, in the dark, or to a generation that could be mythologized later — it landed inside the field of vision of the people it is now being recited to. The Qur'an's epistemology of signs treats sight as the highest-bandwidth evidence and charges accordingly: what is seen binds absolutely, and a community struck down *while watching* has no epistemic escape left. The clause also does a quiet work of self-implication. The speakers said *until we see* — and they saw. They saw the thing they demanded the impossible version of: not Allah disclosed, but Allah's judgment disclosed, visibly, immediately, at the moment of the ultimatum. The demand for sight was answered with sight, and the sight was of the demand's own consequence.

**Why the Sight Was Refused**

The refusal behind the stroke is doctrine, and the Qur'an states it in the scene this verse echoes. Moses asked — reverently, once, as a prophet at the appointed meeting: *My Lord! Reveal Yourself to me so I may see You* — and the answer was a demonstration: *"You cannot see Me! But look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, only then will you see Me"* (7:143). The mountain, levelled to dust by a manifestation it could not bear, published the reason: creaturely substance does not survive direct disclosure in this life, and the demand of verse 55 was therefore a demand for the impossible dressed as a condition for faith. The sūrah itself files the same impossibility as eschatology: *"Are they waiting for Allah ˹Himself˺ to come to them in the shade of clouds, along with the angels? ˹If He did˺, then the matter would be settled ˹at once˺"* (2:210) — full disclosure ends the test, and the test is the point of the descent. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ confirms the verdict on this very demand: the people who asked for a book from heaven had asked greater of Moses — make Allah visible to us — and the thunderbolt seized them for their wrongdoing (4:153). The stroke of verse 55 is thus not anger interrupting negotiation but the covenant's boundary marked in the only medium an ultimatum understands: the unseen staying unseen, visibly.
