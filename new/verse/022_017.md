## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:17

> Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magi, and the polytheists—Allah will judge between them ˹all˺ on Judgment Day. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A List of Six, and the Last Name on It**

The verse names six groups: those who believe, the Jews, the Sabians, the Christians, the Magians, and those who associate others with God. The first four appear together elsewhere in the Qur'an in a formula of promise. The addition here is the last two, and their addition changes the sentence's function entirely.

The familiar four-name list is a list of communities with scriptures, or with claims to one. By placing *al-mushrikīn* — the associators, the polytheists — at the end of the same grammatical series, the verse does two things at once. It refuses to grant the associators a standing the earlier list would have implied, and it refuses to settle the question of who among the named groups is right. Everyone on the list is on the same footing before the thing that matters, which is not membership but judgment.

That flattening is the verse's most striking feature. A Jew, a Christian, a Zoroastrian, and a Makkan idolater are enumerated with the same conjunction between them, and the sentence that governs them all is that God will decide.

**The Sabians: an Identity the Commentators Could Not Settle**

*Al-ṣābiʾūn* appears in the Qur'an alongside the Jews and Christians as a community with a claim to revelation, and the commentators disagreed sharply about who they were — a baptismal sect near the marshes of southern Iraq, a star-worshipping group in Harrān, or people who had left one religion without entering another.

The disagreement matters less than what it shows. The Qur'an names a community whose precise identity was already uncertain to its earliest readers, and the uncertainty has never been resolved to everyone's satisfaction. What the text cares about is not the taxonomy but the principle: a group that stands outside the familiar scriptural categories is still named, still counted, and still within the scope of the divine judgment rather than outside it.

The legal consequence drawn by the jurists from the parallel verse in Sūrah al-Māʾidah was that membership in a named community is not what saves. *“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians and Christians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”* (5:69). The condition inside that sentence is belief, the Last Day, and sound action — not the label.

**Majūs: a Word That Occurs Once**

*Al-majūs* appears nowhere else in the Qur'an. The word is generally understood to derive from the Greek *magos*, which came from Old Persian *magu*, referring originally to a hereditary priestly line among the Persians. In later usage it was applied to Zoroastrians generally, and that is how Muslim writers have almost always understood it.

The history of the term is worth a moment, because it explains a legal argument that ran for centuries. If the Magians were a priestly class rather than a religion, they could not be People of the Book. If they were a scripture-owning community, the rules governing marriage, slaughtered meat, and the poll tax would follow. The jurists split: many, including al-Shāfiʿī, treated Zoroastrians as People of the Book; others did not; and the practical rulings diverged accordingly — some authorities forbade intermarriage with Zoroastrians while permitting the purchase of their meat, and others ruled the reverse.

The Qur'an itself does not settle that question here. What it does is place the Magians inside the list of communities whose differences will be adjudicated, which is a different and prior claim: they are not outside the reckoning.

**Judgment Deferred Is Not Judgment Denied**

The verse's main assertion is that God will judge *between* them on the Day of Resurrection. The preposition is *bayna*, between — a dispute with parties on both sides, and a decision that has not yet been issued.

This is the Qur'an's standard posture on inter-religious disagreement, and it is worth stating plainly because it is so often misrepresented. The Qur'an does not say that all communities are equally correct. It says where the adjudication will take place. The communities themselves make counter-claims that cannot both be true — *“The Jews say, ‘The Christians have nothing to stand on’ and the Christians say, ‘The Jews have nothing to stand on,’ although both recite the Scriptures”* (2:113) — and the text's response is not to declare a winner in the abstract but to name the court.

Deferral here is not evasion. It is the claim that the dispute is real, that its resolution requires knowledge no party possesses, and that the resolution will actually happen.

**"God Is Witness Over All Things"**

The verse closes with *inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd* — God is, over everything, a witness.

The attribute is what makes the deferred judgment coherent. A court needs evidence, and the evidence in a dispute about what people believed and why is their interior life, which no human tribunal can examine. The claim that God is *shahīd* — present, observing, able to testify — is the claim that the record exists, that it is complete, and that nothing in any of these communities' histories is missing from it.

It is also, quietly, a warning to the reader. The same sentence that says the religious disputes of history will be settled also says that everything has been seen. Nobody in the list of six is being told that his case has gone unnoticed in the interval.

**The Verse's Political Consequence**

A text that defers adjudication to the Day of Resurrection is also a text that declines to authorize coercion in the meantime, and the early community read it that way. Sūrah al-Bayyinah describes the People of the Book and the associators as waiting for *“the clear proof”* to come to them (98:1), and the proof's arrival is what divided them — not the sword.

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the principle in its most explicit form: *“To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life. If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given ˹each of˺ you. So compete with one another in doing good”* (5:48). Plurality is described as the test, and the competition is in good works rather than in verdicts.

That is the temper of this verse too. The six names are listed, the dispute is acknowledged as genuine, and the only thing asserted is that it will be settled — by the Witness, on the appointed day.
