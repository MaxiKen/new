## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:30

> That is so. And whoever honours the rituals of Allah, it is best for them in the sight of their Lord. The ˹meat of˺ cattle has been made lawful for you, except what has ˹already˺ been recited to you. So shun the impurity of idolatry, and shun words of falsehood.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"That Is So" — a Seal on the Passage**

*Dhālika* — that is how it is. The demonstrative closes everything from verse 26 onward: the assignment of the site to Abraham, the purification of the House, the call to the pilgrimage, the arrival on foot and on lean camels, the sacrifice, the release and the circuit.

The same word closed verse 1's argument in verse 6 and will close the passage on the symbols of God in verse 32. Each time it does the same work: it declares the matter settled. What has just been described is not a proposal or a preference; it is how things are, and the reader's relationship to it is one of acceptance rather than assessment.

Placed here, the word also separates the rites from what follows. Verses 31 onward are general instructions to the community; verses 26 to 29 were the pilgrimage's founding charter. *That* is how it is; now for you.

**"Whoever Magnifies the Sacred Things of God"**

*Wa-man yuʿaẓẓim ḥurumāti Allāhi* — and whoever magnifies the inviolable things of God. The word *ḥurumāt* is the plural of *ḥurmah*, and it carries two senses that are both in play: things that are sacred, and things that are forbidden — since what is sacred is precisely what must not be violated.

Al-Ṭabarī's definition gathers both: the sacred things are the pilgrimage, the lesser pilgrimage, Makkah and its precincts, and by extension everything that is prohibited in those places and times. The verse that follows two verses later speaks of the *shaʿāʾir*, the rituals or emblems, in almost the same sentence, and the two are best read together as one idea: outward markers whose inviolability is owed to God.

The verb is *yuʿaẓẓim*, to make great, to treat as great. Reverence here is an act performed by the worshipper rather than a property of the object. The sacred months, the precinct, the sacrificial animal — none of these is impressive in itself. What makes them *ḥurumāt* is that a person has been told to treat them as such.

**"It Is Better for Him With His Lord"**

*Fa-huwa khayrun lahu ʿinda rabbihi* — it is better for him, with his Lord.

The prepositional phrase carries the weight. The benefit is located *ʿinda rabbihi*, in God's keeping, not in the visible outcome. That is an honest qualification, because magnifying the sacred things often costs something: a pilgrimage costs money and months, a scruple about the sacred months costs advantage, a refusal to violate the precinct costs a tactical opportunity.

The verse does not pretend otherwise. It says the return is better and places the return where it cannot be audited in this life. A religion that promised immediate and visible profit for every observance would be making a claim that experience refutes daily. This one puts the ledger somewhere else.

**Cattle Permitted, With the Recited Exceptions**

*Uḥillat lakum al-anʿāmu illā mā yutlā ʿalaykum* — the grazing livestock have been made lawful to you except what is recited to you.

The exception points backward to revelation already given, and the prohibitions are listed in full in Sūrah al-Māʾidah: *“Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, and swine; what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah; what is killed by strangling, beating, a fall, or by being gored to death; what is partly eaten by a predator unless you slaughter it; and what is sacrificed on altars”* (5:3). The shorter statement in Sūrah al-Baqarah names the core: *“He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah”* (2:173).

The placement of this permission directly after the rites of sacrifice is not incidental. Having just commanded slaughter, the verse clarifies what may be slaughtered and eaten, and the emphasis falls on the name pronounced over the animal: *“Do not eat of what is not slaughtered in Allah’s Name. For that would certainly be ˹an act of˺ disobedience”* (6:121). The meat is lawful; the invocation is what makes it so.

**"Shun the Filth of Idols"**

*Fa-jtanibū al-rijsa mina al-awthāni* — so avoid the filth of the idols. The word *rijs* is the term used for filth that defiles, and it is applied here to the objects themselves rather than to the act of worshipping them.

Sūrah al-Tawbah draws the legal consequence from the same vocabulary: *“Indeed, the polytheists are ˹spiritually˺ impure, so they should not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year”* (9:28). The impurity is not physical dirt — idols are often well-kept objects — but a defilement that attaches to the person who has contact with them, and the verb *ijtanibū*, keep away from, prescribes distance rather than careful handling.

**"And Shun False Speech"**

*Wa-jtanibū qawla al-zūri* — and avoid the speech that is false. The same verb governs both objects, which places the two offences on the same footing.

That pairing is the verse's most striking move. Idolatry is the gravest sin in the Qur'an; lying about God would seem to belong to a different scale entirely. The verse puts them together, and the reason is that the two are the same act in different registers. Idolatry is a false claim about God rendered in stone; false speech is the same claim rendered in words, and the words are what keep the stones standing.

The Qur'an gives specimens of the speech it means: *“those who take other lords besides Him, ˹saying,˺ ‘We worship them only so they may bring us closer to Allah’”* (39:3), and *“And they attribute ˹angels as˺ daughters to Allah—glory be to Him!— the opposite of what they desire for themselves”* (16:57). Neither is a lie about a neighbour. Both are lies about God, told to justify an arrangement.

A verse that commands purity in worship and truthfulness in speech in a single breath is describing a religion with two non-negotiable sides: the direction of the worship, and the honesty of the account given of it.
