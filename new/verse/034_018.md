## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:18

> We had also placed between them and the cities We showered with blessings ˹many small˺ towns within sight of one another. And We set moderate travel distances in between, ˹saying,˺ “Travel between them by day and night safely.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Blessing: A Road**

The account of Sheba's favours returns, and the new item is not agricultural. Between their homeland and the blessed territories to the north lay a chain of settlements, evenly spaced, each within sight of the next. This describes the incense road, the caravan route running up the western side of Arabia from Yemen to Syria, which carried frankincense, myrrh and transit goods and which made the southern kingdoms rich. The Qur'an's phrase for the northern end is *al-qurā allatī bāraknā fīhā*, the towns We had blessed — the same description the Book applies to the Syrian lands elsewhere, the well-watered country of trees and rivers that was the destination of the trade. So the sign in the previous verses was what they grew; the sign here is what they carried. Taken together the two favours cover a civilisation's whole economic base, production and distribution, and the sūrah is careful to attribute both to the same source before reporting what became of them.

**Towns Within Sight of One Another**

*Qurān ẓāhirah* is the phrase, and *ẓāhirah* means visible, apparent, out in the open. The reading that fits the context best takes it as visibility from one stage to the next: a traveller leaving one settlement could already see the next on the horizon. Anyone who has crossed open country understands what that does to a journey. A road whose next waypoint is always in view cannot be lost, cannot be missed in bad light, and removes the single greatest danger of desert travel, which is not distance but navigational error. The detail also has an economic dimension. Way-stations spaced within sight of each other can be small, because no one needs to carry supplies for a long leg, which in turn means more of them and a denser population along the route. The Qur'an is describing infrastructure again, as it did with the two irrigated plains, and again it presents the infrastructure as a gift rather than an achievement.

**Distances Measured Out**

*Wa qaddarnā fīhā al-sayr* — and We apportioned the travel in them. The verb is *qaddara*, to measure, proportion, determine a quantity, and it is the same verb used when David was told to get the gauge of his armour links right (34:11). Here the measurement applies to journey stages: the intervals were set so that a traveller finished each leg at a place where water and rest were waiting, without either exhausting himself or wasting a day. This is the unglamorous heart of what made the route profitable. Caravan economics turn on the ratio between what an animal must carry for itself and what it can carry for sale, and short, reliable stages shift that ratio decisively. A merchant on such a road travels light because resupply is near. The gift being described, in plain terms, is a low cost of trade — and it is described as something arranged rather than something the market produced.

**Safety Named as the Point of the Whole Arrangement**

The instruction attached is a single sentence: *sīrū fīhā layāliya wa ayyāman āminīn* — travel in them, nights and days, in security. Three things are worth noticing. The order puts nights first, and night travel is the test case: a road safe after dark is safe in the only sense that matters to a caravan. The phrasing covers both halves of the cycle, so the security is continuous rather than seasonal or daylight-only. And the operative word is *āminīn*, secure — the same root as *amn*, safety, and *amānah*, a trust. This is the favour the Qur'an singles out when it addresses the Prophet's own tribe, reminding Quraysh of their habitual caravans and of the Lord *"Who has fed them against hunger and made them secure against fear"* (106:4). The parallel is exact and cannot have been lost on the audience. Mecca's two annual caravans, north in summer and south in winter, ran on the same road Sheba had been given, and the sūrah is describing the southern end of Quraysh's own commercial life.

**Two Favours, One Instruction, Restated**

Set the two gifts side by side and the pattern of this passage becomes clear. The orchards came with *eat and be grateful* (34:15); the road comes with *travel in safety*. Both instructions are permissive. Neither demands a sacrifice, a levy, a pilgrimage or an abstention; each simply authorises the enjoyment of what has been provided and, in the first case, names the one condition. This is a deliberate portrait of an easy covenant. The Qur'an is not describing a people crushed under obligations who finally broke; it is describing a people asked for almost nothing, which makes the refusal recorded next harder to explain and more damning. There is also a structural echo of David and Solomon here. Each of those figures received a capacity and an instruction in the same breath — make armour and work righteously, build and be grateful. Sheba's version of that pairing is the gentlest in the sūrah.

**Why the Road Is Mentioned After the Ruin of the Orchards**

The chronology in these verses is not strictly sequential, and the arrangement is doing work. The flood and the bitter fruit were reported in verse 16, the verdict in verse 17, and only now does the sūrah return to describe another thing they had been given. The effect is to reopen the account after it appeared closed. A reader who thought the case complete — a people lost their farms for ingratitude — is shown that the farms were not the whole of what they had, and that the destruction described was therefore not the whole of what they lost. It also sets up the specific form their complaint will take. Having been given short stages and constant safety, they will ask for something else, and the request will come out of their own mouths in the next verse.
