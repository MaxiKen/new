## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:91

> Indeed, if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world, it would never be accepted from them. It is they who will suffer a painful punishment, and they will have no helpers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Ransom the Size of the World**

The verse describes people who disbelieve and die in that state, and it states that if one of them were to offer, as a ransom, gold enough to fill the whole earth, it would not be accepted from him. The image is built to be impossible, and its function is to state the finality of the matter in terms the reader can picture.

The Book uses ransom language at several points in its descriptions of the Day, and each time it denies the transaction. The sūrah of the Cow states that no ransom will be accepted from those who disbelieve, and the same denial recurs in the accounts of the last gathering. What this verse adds is the measure of the offer: not a sum beyond the reach of most people but a quantity that would suffice to fill the world, which removes the possibility of imagining an amount that would be enough.

**Why the Ransom Cannot Work**

The refusal is not a matter of the price being too low. The Book's account of the final accounting has no mechanism by which a person's standing before God is transferred or purchased, because the relation that would make a purchase meaningful has already lapsed.

The verse's own wording makes the point: those it describes are people who disbelieved and remained in that condition until death. By then the subject of the transaction is gone. The Book's language about the Hereafter consistently places the accounting of a person's life in the life itself, and the descriptions of the Day are accounts of what has already been determined rather than negotiations. The image of the world's gold serves not to set a maximum price but to close the discussion about whether a price exists.

**Dying in the Condition**

The sentence specifies death in the state of disbelief, and the specification limits the verse's scope. The passage has just stated the acceptance of those who repent and make amends, and it stated the case of those who went further in the refusal.

What this verse describes is the third case, which is the end of the trajectory. The Book attaches its statements of final severity to the condition in which a person dies rather than to acts considered in isolation, which is why these verses sit so naturally after the offer of the previous lines. The point of naming the death is to fix the moment at which the offer of return is no longer pending, and the Book's readers are reminded that the moment is not under their control.

**The Painful Punishment and the Absence of Helpers**

The verse ends with two statements: those described will receive a painful punishment, and they will have no helpers. The second statement is the one that deepens the isolation the passage has been building since the cursing was described.

The Book's accounts of the Day repeatedly strip away the supports a person might rely on. Near kindred are named as unable to help, intercession is denied for the cases described, and the entreaties of the condemned are reported as unanswered. The verse above puts the matter in its shortest form: no helpers. From the refusal of a ransom the sentence moves to the absence of anyone who might assist, and the movement is from the resources a person owns to the relationships he has, which are the two things people count on when they imagine their own future.

**The Progression Across the Three Verses**

Read together, the last three verses describe three conditions, and their order is instructive. The first offers acceptance to those who return; the second states that no return is accepted from those who believed, left, and went further; the third describes those who die in the condition.

Each verse mentions something to be offered — repentance, a return, a ransom of gold — and the sequence shows what happens to the offer at each stage. The Book's arrangement is designed for a reader who is still alive, and its logic is the logic of time rather than of rank: what is available now will not be available later, and the verses are placed so that the reader encounters the last one while the first still applies to him.

**What the Book Does Not Say About the Fire**

The verse states that the punishment is painful, and it does not elaborate. The Book's treatments of the final condition vary in detail from passages that describe scenes to passages such as this, which state a fact and stop.

The restraint has a purpose in a passage about an argument with communities who claimed religious standing. An elaborate description would invite the reader to think about the mechanics of the penalty; a single clause about pain and a sentence about the absence of help direct his attention to the seriousness of the state itself. The sūrah turns immediately after this verse to a completely different subject, and the transition is part of the same design: the discussion of the Hereafter has reached its term, and the reader is returned to the world of his conduct.

**The Transition That Follows**

The next verse speaks about the spending of what a person holds dear as the road to righteousness, and the change of subject is the Book's usual way of moving from warning to instruction.

The two halves of the sūrah's method are here in sequence. A passage about the consequences of refusal ends, and a passage about the practice of the believers begins, so that the reader is not left with a warning unaccompanied by a command. What the Book's readers take from the three verses about the Hereafter is a reason to act; what the following passage gives them is what to do.
