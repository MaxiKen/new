## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:203

> Then they will cry, “Can we be allowed more time?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Request for Time Comes After Time Was Given**

The people cry for more time only after the punishment has become real. Their request reveals what they did not value while opportunity remained. They had time to hear Shuʿayb, time to stop defrauding people, and time to abandon the demand for a spectacle. When the consequence arrives, they discover that time was the very mercy they had treated as worthless.

A request for respite can sound humble because it admits weakness, but the verse places it after the decisive moment. The question is no longer whether they understand the warning. They understand enough to want the punishment delayed. What is missing is not information; it is the ability to return to the world and use time differently.

**Late Regret Cannot Reopen the Earlier Choice**

Quran 23:99–100 describes people asking to return when death approaches so that they can do good with what they left behind, then presents the request as an appeal that will not be granted. Quran 39:58 also pictures a person wishing for a second chance after seeing the torment. These parallels show that regret may be sincere as an emotion while still arriving too late to count as the repentance that should have shaped life.

The lesson is not that Allah rejects a sincere return during earthly life. The entire point of warning is to invite that return before the barrier is reached. The refusal concerns the person who wants the conditions of responsibility removed and the consequences postponed after the appointed opportunity has ended.

**Time Is Valuable Because It Can Produce Change**

A year is not automatically a blessing, and a long life is not automatically a good. Time has value when it gives the servant room to recognize wrong, repair damage, and build obedience. If a person uses it only to strengthen denial, additional days become evidence of persistence rather than proof of safety.

The people ask for more time as though time itself could save them. It cannot. Only a changed response can give time its saving meaning. The request exposes the difference between wanting delay and wanting reform. A person may want the punishment to stop without wanting to become the kind of person who no longer deserves the warning.

**The Question Warns the Living**

The cry is preserved so that later people do not repeat it. Do not make tomorrow the place where honesty begins, apology is offered, prayer is repaired, or another person's property is returned. Tomorrow may come, but it does not belong to the servant as a guaranteed possession.

The wise response to this verse is immediate seriousness without despair. If life remains, the door of responsible action remains. Use the available time before it becomes the object of a desperate request. A person who begins turning to Allah now does not need to ask for the past to be returned when the account is opened.

The cry for more time also exposes a mistaken view of repentance. Repentance is not merely wanting the punishment to stop. It includes recognizing the wrong, leaving it, and repairing the harm as far as possible. The people ask for the clock to be extended, but the verse makes the reader ask what they would do with the extension. More days without a changed heart would only repeat the pattern that brought them to the request.

A second chance is meaningful when it produces a different direction. Someone who returns property but keeps the methods of deception has not completed the return. Someone who regrets public corruption but continues to protect its beneficiaries has not yet accepted the truth of the warning. Time is valuable because it allows these changes to be made in the world of action, before the account is sealed.

The verse also gives a sober view of regret. Regret can be a sign that the heart has finally seen the truth, but it cannot by itself restore a lost opportunity. A person may feel sorry for a missed prayer, a broken promise, or an injured neighbor and still need to take the steps that repair what can be repaired. The living can turn emotion into action; the people in the verse can only ask for a return that will not be granted.

The best answer to their question is not to wait for a dramatic crisis before becoming serious. It is to treat the present hour as the extra time people in the story wished they had. Use it to settle accounts, forgive where forgiveness is right, seek forgiveness from Allah, and stop calling a postponed consequence an absent consequence. Time becomes mercy when it is spent in a changed response.

The request for more time can be heard in smaller forms during life. A person says he will repay after the next payment arrives, apologize after his anger has cooled, or begin worship after his work becomes easier. Some delays are unavoidable, but a repeated postponement can become a quiet refusal. The verse asks the servant to distinguish a genuine plan from an excuse that protects the present habit.

A responsible use of time has visible marks. It settles what can be settled, seeks counsel where harm has been done, and does not ask the injured person to carry the whole cost of the wrong. It also accepts that repair may take longer than the original act. What matters is beginning the return while the chance exists, not speaking beautifully about the return after it has become impossible.

The final cry of the deniers therefore contains a lesson about gratitude. Every unspent day is a gift, but it is not a blank cheque. Allah gives time so that a person can choose, and the choices give the time its meaning. The one who turns now does not need to demand a different past; he can let the present become evidence of a sincere change.
