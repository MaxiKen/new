## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:17

> Their example is that of someone who kindles a fire, but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness—unable to see.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shape of the Parable**

The scene is four beats long and moves fast. A man kindles a fire. The fire catches and lights everything around him. The light is taken away. He is left in darkness, unable to see. Nothing is said about how he feels, or what he does next, or whether he tries again. The parable ends where the loss ends.

The compression is part of the effect. A fire in the desert at night is the difference between safety and catastrophe: it keeps off animals, it signals, it lets a man see the ground he is standing on and the people beside him. To have it, and then not to have it, is not the same as never having had it, and the parable's four beats exist to make a reader feel the difference.

The second beat is the one a careless reader skips, and it is the one that matters. The fire *lit up all around him*. Before anything is lost, there is a moment of genuine illumination — the man can see, and what he sees is real. The parable therefore cannot be read as a story about a fire that never caught. It is a story about a man who had the light working exactly as light is supposed to work, and who then lost it while it was working.

**What the Fire Is Taken to Be**

The most common reading among the commentators is that the fire is the declaration of faith itself. By saying *we believe*, the hypocrite acquires the light of membership: protection, legal standing, the right to marry within the community, the safety of belonging. It is a real light, and it shows him real things — which is why the parable can say that it *lit up all around him*.

On this reading the removal is at death, when the profession is unmasked and the protection it purchased expires. The reading has the merit of taking the parable's own sequence seriously: the light is genuinely possessed before it is genuinely lost. Whatever else the fire is, it is not an illusion.

**Loss Is Not the Same as Absence**

The parable's structure is its argument. Had it said that these people never had a light, the description would be one of deprivation and the appropriate response would be pity. Instead it says they had it, used it, saw by it, and then lost it — which makes the condition one of squandering, and the appropriate response is alarm.

The Quran makes the same distinction elsewhere in its vocabulary. There is blindness, and there is the blindness that follows sight; there is going astray, and there is going astray *after* guidance. The second is always treated as the worse state, because what is lost in it is not merely the good but the knowledge of what the good was. A person who has seen and then cannot see knows exactly what he is missing, and that knowledge is itself part of the darkness.

**Light in This Book's Vocabulary**

The Quran's use of light is too consistent to be incidental. Guidance is light, revelation is light, and God is described in a verse that has generated a library of commentary: *Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth. His light is like a niche in which there is a lamp… Light upon light! Allah guides whoever He wills to His light* (24:35).

That verse ends by naming the genre the sūrah is using here — *Allah sets forth parables for humanity*. A parable is not decoration and it is not a puzzle. It is a claim that the spiritual state being described has a shape, that the shape can be shown, and that a reader who has once seen a fire go out in the dark knows more about this condition than a definition would have told him.

The comparison also runs the other way, and the sūrah will exploit it. If guidance is light, then the removal of guidance is not the removal of an object but of a capacity. A man in the dark still has the ground, the people beside him, and the materials for a fire; what he lacks is the ability to use any of them. That is why the parable ends where it does — not with the man injured, but with the man unable to see what is right in front of him.

**Why a Parable Instead of a Statement**

The sūrah could have written: they had faith and lost it. It wrote a scene instead, and the difference in effect is large. A statement asks to be believed; a parable asks to be seen, and once seen it cannot be un-seen or argued with. A reader who has followed the four beats has already granted the conclusion, because the conclusion is not a claim about doctrine but a claim about what happens to a man standing in the dark.

This is also why the parables in this passage are the most quoted part of the sūrah. Abstract descriptions of hypocrisy can be applied to someone else. A picture of a man striking a light and losing it applies to anyone who recognises himself in it, which is the sūrah's intention throughout.

**The Darkness That Follows**

Nothing in the parable suggests that the man kindles again. That is the detail the sūrah leaves for the reader to supply, and the next verse supplies it in a different register. What this parable establishes is the sequence: light, benefit, removal, and a darkness in which the person cannot even find the materials to start another fire. The sūrah continues with a second scene built on the same elements.
