## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:17

> Their example is that of someone who kindles a fire, but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness—unable to see.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable That Follows a Ledger Entry**

The verse that traded guidance for error closed with a ledger — *their commerce has not profited, and they are not guided* (2:16). The parable that follows does not add a new charge; it shows what the unprofitable trade feels like from inside. *“Their example is that of one who kindles a fire”* (2:17) takes the hypocrite’s purchase and translates it into light. The fire is the hypocrite’s outward profession — the *shahādah* that provides, as Ṭabarī notes, a social light by which to eat, marry, and be protected within the community, since membership grants legal and social rights. The light that the fire throws around him is the temporary illumination that profession affords: people make room, contracts are honored, the hypocrite moves as if guided. The verse then says *Allah takes away their light* — not the fire, but the light — and leaves them in darkness, unseeing. The distinction matters. The fire that was kindled may still smolder; the benefit it was kindled for is withdrawn.

**Who Kindled the Fire? One Who Kindles for Himself**

*Mathalu-hum ka-mathali lladhī istawqada nārā* — “their example is like one who kindled a fire.” The singular *alladhī* (the one who) after the plural “their example” has been noted by grammarians as a shift from plural to singular that universalizes: each hypocrite is like a solitary fire-kindler, isolated in his own scene. The verb *istawqada* — to seek to kindle, to try to light — adds volition. It is not a fire that fell from the sky; it is a fire sought, gathered, struck. That volition links the parable to the commerce of (2:16): the fire is the light the hypocrite *purchased* with the guidance he sold. He kindled because he calculated that light without guidance would be sufficient, that profession without standing would be enough to see by. The parable grants the calculation its moment — *when it lit up what was around him* — before revoking it.

**When It Lit Up What Was Around Him — A Moment of Usefulness**

*Fa-lammā aḍāʾat mā ḥawlahu* — “when it lit up what was around him.” The clause concedes that the hypocrite’s profession does produce a temporary, circumscribed illumination. Around him — *mā ḥawlahu* — the immediate circle is lit: his household, his market dealings, his place in the ranks. That is the worldly benefit Ṭabarī identifies: the light by which the hypocrite eats, marries, and is protected. The sūrah does not deny that hypocrisy pays for a moment; it says the payment is taken away. The phrase “when it lit up” thus carries both a concession and a limit: the light works, but only around him, and only until it is taken.

**Allah Takes Away Their Light — Not the Fire, But Its Benefit**

*Dhahaba Allāhu bi-nūrihim* — “Allah takes away their light.” The possessive *nūrihim* — *their* light — preserves the irony. It was their light insofar as they kindled for it, but it was never theirs as a possession that could be retained against the One who gave the capacity to kindle at all. The verb *dhahaba bi-* — to take away with — suggests removal that carries the light off, not merely dimming. That removal is ascribed directly to Allah, as the sealing (2:7) and the increase in disease (2:10) were. The parable therefore extends the same moral physics: a light that is sought without guidance is granted as a loan, and the loan is recalled. What remains is not the fire’s absence but its uselessness — darkness that is now complete because the contrast has been tasted.

**Leaving Them in Darknesses, Unseeing**

*Wa-tarakahum fī ẓulumātin lā yubṣirūn* — “and leaves them in darknesses, unseeing.” The plural *ẓulumāt* — darknesses — gathers the earlier privations: the sealing that closed hearing and heart (2:7), the disease that dulled perception (2:10), the failure to perceive corruption as corruption (2:12) and folly as folly (2:13), the wandering that is prolonged (2:15). Darknesses plural is the sensory sum of those moral privations. The ending — *lā yubṣirūn* — “unseeing” — returns to sight, which in (2:7) was merely “covered” (*ghishāwah*) but is now wholly unable. The hypocrite who kindled for light ends unseeing, as the one who traded guidance for error ended unguided (2:16). The parable thus does not merely illustrate; it judges the trade with the trader’s own currency: he sought light without guidance and lost both.

**Why the Light Is Taken After It Has Lit**

The sequence — kindling, lighting, removal — prevents the parable from being read as predestination without human agency. The hypocrite is not left in darkness without having had light; he is left there *after* the light has lit what was around him. The interval matters because it establishes responsibility. The light that profession afforded was enough to show the path that the mindful walk (2:5), and the choice to use that light only around oneself — to light one’s own circle rather than to walk the path — is what is judged. That is why some commentators hear in the parable a specific eschatological image: the light is taken away at death, when hypocrisy is unmasked (Ṭ). The social light that worked in the market fails at the threshold where membership is no longer conferred by saying “We believe” (2:8) but by being what was said.

**The Spiritual Light Behind the Social Light**

The source rightly points to 24:35 — the Light Verse — as the spiritual dimension behind the social reading. There light is not a metaphor for advantage but for guidance itself: *“Allah is the light of the heavens and the earth, the likeness of His light is as a niche in which is a lamp…”* (24:35). The parable in al-Baqarah uses the lower, social light to make the higher, spiritual light intelligible. The hypocrite sought the lower light — the benefits of belonging — without the higher light — the guidance that makes belonging true. The verse says the lower light, because it was severed from the higher, cannot be retained. In that way the two lights, worldly and spiritual, are shown to be one economy: to trade guidance for error (2:16) is to trade the niche for the glare, and the glare, because it has no niche to hold it, is taken away.

**A Singular Fire-Kindler for a Plural “Their”**

Finally, the singular *alladhī* for a plural “their” (*mathalu-hum*) teaches that hypocrisy, though it creates a class (2:8-15), is experienced alone. Each hypocrite kindles his own fire, lights his own circle, and is left in his own darkness. The plural that began the portrait — *they purchase, their commerce, they are not guided* — contracts to the solitary figure crouched over a flame that has lit only what is around him. The next portrait will widen again — a rainstorm that terrifies a crowd (2:19-20) — but the fire parable insists on the loneliness of the trade: the hypocrite sought to be counted among people by saying “We believe as people believe,” yet his light, when taken, leaves him not among people but in a darkness that is his alone, unseeing.
