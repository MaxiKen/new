## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:17

> Their example is that of someone who kindles a fire, but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness—unable to see.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable Stated as Their Own Description**

The sentence opens with the possessive: *their* example. The Book is not offering an illustration that happens to resemble these men; it is naming the shape of their situation. Parables in the Qur'an work this way throughout the sūrah, and the next verses carry two of them, so that the portrait of the hypocrites ends in images rather than in verdicts. The images are meant to be remembered, and the men described in them are meant to recognise themselves.

What makes a parable useful here is the limit of plain description. The preceding verses have told the reader what these people say in two different rooms and what they lost in the exchange. What they cannot be told directly is what their condition feels like from the inside, and the two likenesses in this passage supply that. The first describes what happens to light a man has just made.

**One Kindles the Fire, and Then It Is Not His**

The grammar shifts inside the sentence: a single person lights the flame, and then the light that is removed belongs to a plural. The kindling was one act, and the loss is shared, which fits a passage addressed to a group whose members behave identically. Each man in the story is the one who struck the match, and every one of them loses the same thing.

That structure also fixes the asymmetry the sūrah has been building. The human being in this parable has real power to act; he can bring a fire into being. What he cannot do is retain the light it produces, because the ownership of light in this Book belongs somewhere else. The Book's account of human capacity repeatedly draws the same line: action is granted, results are not, and the gap between the two is where its moral teaching lives.

**What the Fire Was Lit For**

Al-Ṭabarī records the reading that the light here is the declaration of faith and the protection it brings, since a person's public profession of Islam in the new community of Madinah granted him the standing to eat, marry, inherit and travel under the community's safeguard. That account fits the men described in the previous verses precisely. They had recited the profession, and they had collected everything that came with it.

On that reading the parable is exact about what was lost. The fire was not imaginary and the light was not fake; the man enjoyed both while they lasted, and the hypocrisy consisted in pretending the flame was his. Which is why the loss arrives at a point he cannot anticipate or postpone. The Book makes the same point about the value of that social light elsewhere, in the scene where the hypocrites discover that their illumination has failed: *“On that Day hypocrite men and women will beg the believers, ‘Wait for us so that we may have some of your light.’”* (57:13).

**Light as a Category the Book Returns To**

The Qur'an uses light for guidance, revelation, life and the presence of God, and it never treats illumination as a metaphor with no content. The most concentrated statement is the verse of light: *“Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth. His light is like a niche in which there is a lamp, the lamp is in a crystal, the crystal is like a shining star”* (24:35), which ends by saying that God guides whom He wills to His light and sets forth parables for humanity.

Against that passage the Book sets the darkness that follows its removal: *“Or ˹their deeds are˺ like the darkness in a deep sea, covered by waves upon waves, topped by ˹dark˺ clouds. Darkness upon darkness! If one stretches out their hand, they can hardly see it. And whoever Allah does not bless with light will have no light!”* (24:40). The image in this verse belongs to that family of passages, and it ends with the same finality. The last sentence of the parable gives no indication that the man has a second match.

**Complete Darkness, and the Loss of Bearings**

The verse ends with the phrase *unable to see*, and it has already said that the darkness is complete. The two clauses work together: nothing in the surroundings is lit, and the faculty that could have compensated for the lack of light has also gone dark. A person in that condition has not merely lost a convenience; he has lost the means of deciding which direction is forward.

That is the second time in this opening passage that the Book has described people as unable to locate the road. The sentence passed on the rejecters earlier left them wandering blindly in their excess, and the sentence here leaves these men standing in the dark. The vocabulary is the Book's ordinary way of describing a spiritual condition: not a stated punishment that arrives from outside, but a capacity quietly removed, with the person still moving.

**Kindling Without Ownership**

The parable states a relation between effort and outcome that runs through the whole sūrah. The man had initiative, and he used it; what he lacked was the authority to keep what his initiative produced. Later in the same chapter the Book will put a sharper edge on the same lesson when it tells the believers that the light they will carry on the day of accounting is theirs to ask for but not to manufacture: *“Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us.”* (66:8).

Read that way, the fire is a warning to anyone who believes his standing rests on a light he himself kindled. The flame in the parable is real, and it is not enough, because the thing that matters in this Book is not whether a person can make light but whether he is among those to whom light is given and kept.
