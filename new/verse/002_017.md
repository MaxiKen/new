## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:17

> Their example is that of someone who kindles a fire, but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness—unable to see.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Passage Turns From Description to Image**

After seven verses of direct characterization — they say, they deceive, they are sick, they mock — the sūrah changes instruments and begins to draw. *Mathal*, example or parable, is the Qur'an's method of making a moral condition visible: the type is not re-described but staged inside a scene the body can picture. Two scenes follow, the kindled fire and the storm, and the first is domestic in scale — one person, one flame, one night. The switch to image is itself a claim: the hypocrite's condition resists definition and yields to depiction, because its essence is not a proposition but an experience — the feel of light that is not yours, and of its withdrawal. The parable also transfers the diagnosis from courtroom to campfire. Every listener has sat by a flame that guttered; the verse recruits that memory as evidence, and the memory convicts without argument.

**A Fire That Lit the Surroundings, Not the Self**

The verbs are exact. *Istawqaʿada nāran* — he sought, kindled, sat down by a fire: the effort is real, and so is the settling-in beside it. *Falammā aḍāʾat mā ḥawlahu* — when it illuminated *what was around him*. The light's reach is specified as the surroundings, and the specification is the parable's first cut: the fire lit his situation — the ground, the faces, the immediate world — but nothing inside him; the man himself stands in the scene unchanged by its brightness. Commentators map the correspondence: the fire is the hypocrite's professed faith, the *shahādah* he recited, which lit up his legal and social surroundings — membership granted him protection, inheritance, marriage, standing in the community — while his heart stayed where it was. Borrowed light behaves exactly this way. It makes everything visible except its source, and it flatters the borrower with the sense of sight while the eye itself learns nothing.

**Allah Removes the Light, Not the Fire**

The extinction clause chooses its noun with care: *dhahaba Allāhu bi-nūrihim* — Allah took away *their light*. Not the fire — the light. The distinction matters because fire and light are separable in experience: the flame can remain while its illumination goes, as the hypocrites' formal membership remained while its meaning drained. The removal is attributed directly to Allah, and the attribution continues the theology of verse 7 — the sealing there, the extinguishing here — where divine action confirms a state the subject built. The timing clause completes the picture: the light goes *when it has lit up all around them*, at the moment of maximum usefulness and maximum confidence. Classical exegesis places that moment at death, when the mask comes off and the communal light of the profession no longer reaches the soul. The sūrah al-Ḥadīd stages the sequel: *"On that Day hypocrite men and women will beg the believers, 'Wait for us so that we may have some of your light'… Then a ˹separating˺ wall with a gate will be erected between them"* (57:13).

**Darkness in the Plural, Sight Intact**

The aftermath is described in two clauses that do different work. *Tarakahum fī ẓulumāt* — He left them in darknesses, plural: not one absence of light but layered darkness, the grammatical plural suggesting darkness folded over darkness, the stacked condition of a night that has no single source to fix. Then *lā yubṣirūn* — unseeing. The verb form keeps their sight: the faculty is intact and functioning; there is simply nothing for it to find. This is a sharper punishment than the sealed organs of verse 7. The man whose heart was sealed could not process evidence; the men of this parable can see perfectly and are surrounded by night. They retain the capacity to know that they cannot see — awareness of the loss preserved inside the loss. Every human darkness is tolerable until the eyes work, and the verse leaves them working.

**Light Is Allah's to Give, and Fire Is Man's to Strike**

The parable sits inside a larger grammar of light the Qur'an develops, whose center is the verse of Light itself: *"Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth"* (24:35). In that grammar, light in the true sense is not a substance but a relation — illumination that belongs to Allah and is extended, guided to whomever He wills. Against it, the kindled fire of verse 17 is what a creature can produce unaided: a local, fuel-fed flame, genuine as warmth and useless as a sky. The hypocrites' error, in the parable's terms, was mistaking a struck fire for given light — assuming that a profession they manufactured would illuminate like a grace they received. That is why the removal is attributed to Allah rather than to wind or fuel: only the Giver can withdraw the given, and what He withdrew had never been theirs. The fire was their work; the light was always a loan.

**Why the First Parable Is Small**

Of the two images this passage deploys, the fire comes first and is deliberately modest — one man, one flame, one dark plain. The smallness serves the diagnosis. Hypocrisy begins domestic: a creed recited in a room, a prayer performed for an audience, a lamp kept burning because the neighbors see it. The parable refuses the type any grandeur — no storm yet, no thunder, just a guttering flame and a man standing in his own extinguished evening. Read in sequence with the verses before it, the image gathers their content: the lie of verse 8 was the striking of the flame; the deception of verse 9 was sitting down beside it; the mockery of verse 14 was boasting of how well it lit the room. The extinction of verse 17 is not a new event in the passage but the same events seen from the end. And the sūrah is not finished: a second parable follows, widening the scene from one flame to a whole sky, because the condition has more than one weather.
