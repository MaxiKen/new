## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:52

> And We inspired Moses, ˹saying,˺ “Leave with My servants at night, for you will surely be pursued.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exodus Begins With a Timed Divine Command**

After the public confrontation and Pharaoh's response, God directs Moses to leave with His servants at night. The command is precise about both the companions and the time. Moses is not asked to improvise a departure from a powerful state. He receives guidance that turns a dangerous escape into an act of obedience.

Night gives the movement concealment and protects the vulnerable community as it leaves. It does not mean that the Israelites are ashamed or that their liberation is morally hidden. It is a practical provision within a plan that knows Pharaoh will pursue them. God does not require the oppressed to expose themselves unnecessarily in order to prove courage. The departure uses wise means while remaining openly ordered by the Lord.

**My Servants Restores the Israelites' True Identity**

Pharaoh has treated the Children of Israel as labor, property, and a source of political risk. God calls them My servants. That name overturns the ruler's classification. Their deepest identity is not the status assigned by the palace. They belong to God, and the journey out of Egypt begins when that belonging is acted upon.

The phrase also defines the purpose of the departure. They are not leaving merely to exchange one earthly authority for another. They are being led out as servants of God, people whose lives and worship are no longer to be controlled by Pharaoh's claim. Freedom has a direction. It moves them from a false ownership toward the rightful Lord, whose service is not enslavement but care, guidance, and moral responsibility.

**Inspiration Gives the Messenger the Next Step**

The wording We inspired Moses shows that the plan comes from God. Earlier Moses faced Pharaoh in public; now he receives a command suited to the changing situation. Revelation is not limited to broad principles. It can give a messenger the exact action needed at a particular time. Moses must know when to remain, when to speak, and when to move.

This prevents two errors about trust. One error treats careful planning as a lack of faith. The other assumes that a person may act on private courage without seeking divine direction. Moses does neither. He has confronted Pharaoh, and now he leaves when God commands. The timing of obedience matters as much as the courage of confrontation.

**Pursuit Is Foreknown Without Making the Journey Futile**

God tells Moses that they will be pursued. The warning does not promise a safe route free from danger. It reveals that Pharaoh will not release the Israelites inwardly even when they have begun to leave outwardly. The ruler's claim is so strong that freedom itself provokes pursuit.

Foreknowledge of pursuit does not make the departure pointless. It prepares Moses and the Israelites for fear without allowing surprise to define their response. They will not be able to say that the danger proves God abandoned them. The pursuit is part of the path through which Pharaoh's claim will be exposed and the Israelites' deliverance will become unmistakable. A difficult route can still be the route God commands.

**Night Travel Combines Prudence With Trust**

The instruction to leave at night is a model of means joined to reliance. Moses is not told to wait for a public announcement or to challenge Pharaoh at every stage. He is told to move when the conditions provide protection. Prudence is not surrender to fear. It is the use of available means under divine guidance.

That principle matters for oppressed communities. Liberation requires courage, but it also requires attention to timing, routes, resources, and the vulnerability of families. The command is not a celebration of reckless exposure. It honors the lives of the servants being led out. Their freedom is pursued through an orderly movement rather than a dramatic gesture designed for an audience.

**The Command Follows a Public Proof**

The timing of this verse also shows that signs do not remove the need for action. Pharaoh and the magicians have seen the staff's victory, yet the Israelites still must leave. A miracle authenticates the command, but the servants must obey it. Deliverance involves both divine power and human movement under instruction.

The public contest has exposed Pharaoh's false interpretation, but the political system remains dangerous. The Israelites cannot remain in Egypt waiting for the ruler to admit defeat. God's command turns recognition into departure. Faith sees the sign, hears the order, and takes the next step even before the whole route is visible.

**The Pursuer Cannot Own the Destination**

Pharaoh's pursuit will make the journey frightening, but his ability to follow does not give him the right to reclaim the people. A slaveholder may chase those who leave, yet the chase proves the holder's desire for control, not the legitimacy of control. God's wording already calls the Israelites His servants, placing Pharaoh's pursuit under judgment.

The verse thus begins the movement from court debate to historical deliverance. Moses has answered the ruler with signs, and God now answers the ruler's refusal with a commanded departure. The night is not the end of the story. It is the hour in which God's servants leave the house of oppression while the oppressor prepares to prove that he will not surrender them willingly.

The order also teaches that deliverance is not measured by the absence of pursuit. A person can be on the right path and still be followed by the power he has left. What confirms the path is not immediate ease but God's command and presence within it. Moses is told the danger in advance so that fear will not be mistaken for a sign to return.
