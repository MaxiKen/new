## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:2

> ˹This is˺ a Book sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺— do not let anxiety into your heart regarding it—so with it you may warn ˹the disbelievers˺, and as a reminder to the believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Book Sent Down to the Prophet**

The verse names the thing the letters introduced: a Book sent down to the Prophet with a purpose attached. The phrase sent down carries the Book's account of its own origin, and it is used of the whole Quran as well as of particular passages. The sūrah of the Cow describes the Book as sent down to the Prophet and states that it contains no doubt (2:2), and the sūrah of the Female Ant states that the Book was sent down from the Wise, the Praiseworthy.

The address is personal, and it places the messenger at the receiving end of the revelation. The Book is not presented as a body of teaching that the Prophet composed, and the sūrah's later passages return to the same point when they describe the revelation as what has been sent down to him from his Lord (7:3). The verse in front of us states the fact on which the whole chapter's argument rests.

**The Constriction in the Breast**

The verse tells the Prophet that he is to carry no constriction in his breast because of the Book. The phrase describes the tightness that accompanies anxiety, and the Book uses it of the messengers in their hardest moments. The sūrah of Hūd records the instruction given to the Prophet not to let his breast be straitened by the task of conveying what has been revealed to him (11:12), and the sūrah of the Poets asks whether he will destroy himself with grief because the people do not believe (26:3). The sūrah of the Cave asks whether he would kill himself with sorrow over their refusal. The verse in front of us addresses the same condition directly.

The Book's answer to the messenger's distress is not an assurance that his people will accept the message. It is the reminder that the Book has been sent down and that his work is to convey it. The constriction in the breast comes from concern about the response, and the verse treats that concern by naming the revelation and its purpose.

**So That You May Warn**

The purpose clause names the first function of the Book: warning. The Book places the warning of the messengers at the centre of their mission, and it states in several places that the duty of the Prophet is to deliver the message and that the response of the people is beyond his control. The sūrah of the Family of Imran states that the messenger's only duty is to convey the message (3:20), the sūrah of the Table repeats the statement twice in a single passage (5:92; 5:99), and the sūrah of the Thunder says that the messenger's duty is only to convey (13:40). The verse in front of us names the warning as the reason the Book was sent down.

What is warned against is the consequence of rejecting the message, and the sūrah develops that theme at length. The audience is warned by the account of the destroyed communities, by the description of the reckoning, and by the signs that are laid out in the created order. The warning is therefore not a threat in the abstract; it is the statement of what has happened to those who refused in the past and of what awaits those who refuse now.

**A Reminder for the Believers**

The verse's second description of the Book is that it is a reminder for the believers. The word used denotes a recalling to mind, and the Book applies it to itself in several passages. The sūrah of Ṭā Hā describes the revelation as sent down as a reminder for the one who fears God (20:3), the sūrah of Yā Sīn states that the Book is none other than a reminder and a clear recitation (36:69), and the sūrah of the Forgiver calls the scripture given to Moses a guidance and a reminder for people of understanding (40:54). Sūrat al-Ḥijr Tract states that God has sent down the Reminder and that He will preserve it (15:9).

The two descriptions in the verse divide the Book's audience according to their response. The warning is directed at those who turn away, and the reminder is for those who accept and remember. The Book's presentation of itself in the sūrah of the Cow uses the same division, since it describes the Book as guidance for the mindful at its opening and turns to the warning of the disbelievers in its following verses (2:2; 2:6). This verse gathers both functions into a single sentence.

**The Two Audiences Named Together**

The verse places the warning and the reminder in one breath, and that arrangement is characteristic of the sūrah's approach. The introduction to the chapter describes its method as subtler and less polemical than the approach of the sūrah of the Cattle, and it argues through the history of the prophets and their peoples rather than through direct confrontation. The presentation of the Book as a warning and a reminder matches that method: the message is offered to both audiences on the same terms.

The Book's address to the community of believers appears throughout the sūrah, and the reminder is for them from its opening words. The sūrah's later passages describe the community of the Prophet's followers as the people who receive the Book and act on it, and the verse in front of us names them at the head of the chapter.

**The Anxiety of the Messenger**

The Book's accounts of the Prophet's distress over his people recur, and the verse in front of us answers it with the statement of the Book's purpose. The chapter of the Cave describes the Prophet's grief over the refusal of his people and tells him that if God willed, He could have gathered them all to guidance (6:35). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ asks whether he will destroy himself with grief. The instruction to carry no constriction is therefore part of a pattern of reassurance that runs through the revelation.

The reassurance is tied to the fact that the messenger is not responsible for the outcome. The sūrahs cited above state the same principle in their descriptions of the prophets' duty, and the verse in front of us applies it to the Prophet at the beginning of a chapter that will describe the destruction of the peoples who refused their messengers. The messenger delivers, and the audience answers for itself.

**Where the Verse Sits in the Sūrah's Opening**

The verse is the second element of the chapter's opening section, and it follows the letters of the first verse with the mention of the Book. The verses that follow state the command to follow what has been sent down, the warning against protectors other than God, the fate of the destroyed communities, the questioning of those who received the message, and the weighing of deeds. The passage in front of us supplies the ground on which all of those statements rest.

The Book is introduced, its messenger is addressed, its purposes are named, and its audience is divided. The reader who continues into the sūrah finds the warning carried out in the accounts of the prophets and the reminder carried out in the verses that instruct the community.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse turns from the Prophet to the people addressed by the message and commands them to follow what has been sent down from their Lord and not to take protectors besides Him, adding the rebuke that they are seldom mindful (7:3). The two verses form a pair: the message is sent down, and the community is told what to do with it.

The Book's warning and reminder are thus given a human response in the verse that follows. The messenger is freed from anxiety about the outcome, and the audience is given the command that the Book brings. The chapter's opening moves from the page to the people who hear it.
