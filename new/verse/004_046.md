## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:46

> Some Jews take words out of context and say, “We listen and we disobey,” “Hear! May you never hear,” and “Râ’ina!” [Herd us!]—playing with words and discrediting the faith. Had they said ˹courteously˺, “We hear and obey,” “Listen to us,” and “Unẓurna,” [Tend to us!] it would have been better for them and more proper. Allah has condemned them for their disbelief, so they do not believe except for a few.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Distorting Words After Their Places**

The verse describes *mina alladhīna hādū yuḥarrifūna al-kalima ʿan mawāḍiʿihi* — some Jews take words out of context, distorting wording after its proper place. *Taḥrīf* — to tilt, to turn aside — was explained as altering pronouncement or meaning: changing a word’s sound slightly, shifting its context, or reinterpreting it to mean what it does not. The sūrah has just said that God knows best who enemies are and is sufficient as Guardian and Helper (4:45), and that a portion of the Book was traded for misguidance with the wish to make believers deviate (4:44). Here the trade becomes audible. Three phrases are quoted as examples: *samīʿnā wa ʿaṣaynā* — we listen and we disobey — said where *we hear and obey* was due; *ismaʿ ghayra musmaʿin* — hear, may you never hear — a curse disguised as greeting; and *rāʿinā* — herd us, tend us — twisted from *unẓurnā* — look upon us, tend to us — by turning *rāʿinā* into an insult through a slight vocal shift, playing on the word’s sound to mean shepherd our affairs with contempt. The verse names the devices — *layyan bi alsinatihim wa ṭaʿnan fī al-dīn* — playing with tongues, twisting with words, and stabbing at the faith — showing that distortion is both diction and intent.

**Three Sayings That Twist Courtesy Into Contempt**

*We listen and we disobey* was heard as a public declaration that replaces obedience with defiance while claiming to have listened, preserving the form of reception and emptying it. *Hear, may you never hear* was read as a curse to the Prophet ﷺ delivered as though it were a greeting, a double tongue that could be denied as mishearing. *Rāʿinā* was the most subtle, because the call *rāʿinā* — look at us, tend to us — sounds like *unẓurnā* but carries a different root that can be stretched to mean *rāʿi* — foolish, shepherd to be herded — and was used with a drawl and wink to insult while seeming to ask for attention. The Qur’an elsewhere tells believers *“do not say rāʿinā but say unẓurnā and listen”* (2:104), replacing the ambiguous with the clear to close the opening for mockery. The sūrah’s concern for clarity — *Allah wills to make things clear* (4:26) — is therefore protected at the level of vocabulary. A single word’s tilt can tilt the community’s hearing, which is why the verse details the tilt rather than summarizing it.

**What They Could Have Said**

The verse offers the straight alternative: *wa law annahum qālū samīʿnā wa aṭaʿnā wa asmaʿ wa anẓurnā la kāna khayran lahum wa aqwam* — had they said we hear and obey, listen to us, and tend to us, it would have been better for them and more proper. *Samīʿnā wa aṭaʿnā* — we hear and obey — was the believers’ reply at *“we hear and we obey”* elsewhere, a phrase that gathers hearing and action together. *Ismaʿ* — listen — without the curse, and *unẓurnā* — tend to us — with the unambiguous root, would have secured the request without the twist. The pair *khayran lahum wa aqwam* — better for them and more proper, more upright — shows that straight speech is both more beneficial and more correctly aimed — *aqwam* — straighter, like an arrow that flies true. The sūrah has praised *qawlan maʿrūfā* — kind speech at distribution (4:5, 4:8) — and *qawlan sadīdā* — straight, true speech (4:9), and here it shows what crooked speech sounds like and what straight speech would have been. The difference is not eloquence but sincerity. A community that learns to say *we hear and obey* will also learn to handle deposits, shares, and dowries as heard and obeyed limits; a community that says *we hear and disobey* will treat limits as sounds to be played with.

**Condemned for Disbelief — Faith Except for a Few**

The closing *wa lākin laʿanahumu Allāhu bi kufrihim fa lā yuʾminūna illā qalīlā* — Allah has condemned them for their disbelief, so they do not believe except for a few — ties distortion to its root. *Laʿn* — to expel from mercy, to condemn — was read as the consequence of covering the bounty and trading it (4:37, 4:44). Because they distorted, they were condemned, and because they were condemned for persistent disbelief, the habit of faith left them, except for a few who turned, such as those who accepted the Prophet ﷺ and whose belief the sūrah elsewhere acknowledges. The clause *illā qalīlan* — except a little, except a few — keeps the door ajar for the individual. The condemnation is not ethnic fate but response to a chosen practice of turning words after their places; the few who said *we hear and obey* were not condemned, which is why belief remained possible for them. The sūrah has already said that God forgives anything less than association with Him for whomever He wills (4:48) and that a painful punishment is prepared for those who persist until death says now I repent (4:18); here the same structure applies in the domain of speech. Persistent twisting hardens into disbelief that makes belief rare, while a straight word at the start — *unẓurnā* instead of *rāʿinā* — would have been better and more proper and would have kept the way open.

**Words, Faith, and the Guarded Path**

Placed after the verse that entrusts enemies to God as Guardian and Helper (4:45), this verse shows how enmity actually sounded in the streets of Madinah and how believers are to answer it. The answer is not to mimic the twist but to replace it with clarity: do not use the ambiguous *rāʿinā*; say *unẓurnā*. The sūrah’s larger purpose — to make things clear, guide to noble ways, turn in mercy, and lighten because humankind was created weak (4:26–28) — is undone by those who play with tongues to discredit faith; it is restored when the community chooses words that are better and more upright. The three twisted phrases and the three straight ones taught the believers to listen for tilt and to correct it, not with harshness alone but with a better phrasing they themselves would use. A community that guards its words in that way will also guard the other trusts the sūrah has named — the orphan’s wealth with witnesses, the wife’s dowry as *niḥlah*, the estate’s shares after debts — because each of those trusts is also kept or broken first by what people say.
