## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:143

> ˹Allah has created˺ four pairs: a pair of sheep and a pair of goats—ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Has He forbidden ˹to you˺ the two males or the two females or what is in the wombs of the two females? Tell me with knowledge, if what you say is true.”—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pairs and the Question**

The verse opens the sūrah's final treatment of the dietary rules by taking up the claim that certain animals had been forbidden, and it puts the question to the audience through the Prophet's instruction. The created animals come in male and female, and whatever is carried in the womb belongs to the female of the pair, so the categories of the created order are only three. If a prohibition had been given, it would have attached to one of them, and the verse asks the audience to identify which. The passage begins a two-part argument in which the claim that God had forbidden certain animals is tested by a question the audience could not answer.

The Book's word for the pairs describes a male and a female of each kind, and the four pairs named across this verse and the next are the sheep, the goats, the camels, and the oxen. The sūrah of the Troops states that God produced four pairs of cattle for humanity and that He creates people in the wombs of their mothers in stages, and the verse in front of us belongs to the same account of creation as provision (39:6).

**The Structure of the Question**

The question offers three possibilities and excludes the rest. If God had forbidden some of these animals, the prohibition would have to attach either to the males, to the females, or to what was carried in the wombs. The audience's rules did not correspond to any of the three, since they distinguished among animals by the kinds of distinctions the sūrah has already described: which animal was set free, which was reserved, and which was shared.

The sentence therefore asks the audience to state a rule in terms of the categories they themselves used, and the categories do not produce the rule they claimed. The Book's method is to accept the framework of the audience's practice for the sake of argument and then to show that the practice cannot be expressed within it.

**The Demand for Knowledge**

Tell me with knowledge, if what you say is true. The clause demands evidence, and the demand is the same one the sūrah has made throughout its treatment of the audience's claims. The sūrah has described the majority as followers of assumption who do nothing but lie (6:116), and it has stated that assumption cannot replace the truth (10:36). The sentence in front of us applies that standard to the audience's dietary rules.

The Book's use of the demand for knowledge in its arguments with the audience is consistent. It asks the people who claim that God has forbidden something to produce the revelation or the reasoning on which the claim rests, and it leaves them without either. The clause in this verse states the challenge in its shortest form.

**What the Argument Assumes About God's Commands**

The question assumes that a command of God would be knowable, and the assumption runs through the Book's treatment of the subject. The sūrah has stated that God has explained what He has forbidden (6:119), and it has recorded the revelation of the rules that the community actually received. A prohibition that could not be traced to the revelation, could not be derived from the categories of the created animals, and could not be stated in the terms of the audience's own practice had no claim to be a command of God.

The Book's positive statement of the forbidden things appears later in this sūrah, where the Prophet is told to declare that he finds nothing forbidden to eat except carrion, running blood, swine, and what has been offered in the name of other than God, with the exception of necessity (6:145). The verse in front of us prepares the reader for that declaration by removing the invented prohibitions that had surrounded it.

**What the Verse Says About the Audience's Authority**

The verse addresses the audience as people who claimed to know what God had commanded, and it asks them to account for the claim. The sūrah has examined the shares they assigned to God and their partners (6:136), the killing of their children (6:137), and the rules that restricted their herds and crops (6:138–139), and it now asks them to state the basis of the system as a whole.

The Book's treatment of false attribution to God runs through the chapter, and the sūrah has described the claim to have received revelation as one of the gravest offences (6:93). The claim that God had forbidden certain animals belongs to the same family of assertions, and the question in front of us exposes it by requiring the claimant to produce the foundation of his claim.

**The Verse's Place in the Argument**

The verse forms a pair with the one that follows, which names the camels and the oxen and repeats the question with the addition of a further challenge: were the audience present when God gave them this commandment (6:144)? The two verses together complete the argument, and the second closes with the declaration that those who fabricate lies against God to mislead others will not be guided.

The sūrah's treatment of the dietary system therefore ends with the audience's claim left unsupported and the Book's declaration of the actual prohibition. What the verse in front of us provides is the first half of the challenge, and it does so by taking the audience's own categories and showing that they cannot carry the weight placed on them.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse names the second pair of animals and repeats the question about males, females, and wombs, before asking whether the audience was present when God gave the commandment and declaring that no one does greater wrong than the person who fabricates lies against God to mislead others without knowledge (6:144). The two verses are a single argument, and the second supplies the conclusion.

The passage that follows records the Prophet's declaration of what is actually forbidden and the rules that apply to the person compelled by necessity (6:145), and it then turns to the dietary practices of the Jewish communities and the guidance that the earlier scriptures contained. The verse in front of us stands at the head of that final treatment of food, and the challenge it issues governs everything that follows.
