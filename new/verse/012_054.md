## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:54

> The King said, “Bring him to me. I will employ him exclusively in my service.” And when Joseph spoke to him, the King said, “Today you are highly esteemed and fully trusted by us.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Order, Given a Second Time**

Verse 50 records the king's first command — bring him to me — and Joseph's refusal to comply until the enquiry was held. Verse 54 repeats the command in identical words, and this time it is obeyed. The repetition is not careless. It marks the difference between a summons issued on the strength of an interpretation and one issued after a public exoneration. The first brought a useful man out of a prison; the second brings a cleared man into a government. The chapter keeps the two summonses separate so that the reader can see what changed between them, and what changed is not the king's need but Joseph's standing. That distinction is the hinge of the episode. The first summons was met with a refusal and an enquiry; the second follows the enquiry and its result. Egypt's crisis has not altered by a single word and the grain is no nearer the storehouses than it was — the only difference is that the man asked to manage it has now been publicly cleared.

**"I Will Make Him Exclusively My Own"**

The phrase *akhtassuhu li-nafsī* uses a verb of specialisation: to single out, to reserve for oneself, to attach exclusively. The king is not offering a post but a relationship. The commentators gloss it as the intention to keep Joseph's abilities for the crown alone, and the wording supports a stronger sense — the man himself is to belong to the king's own circle rather than to any household or department. The offer reverses the terms on which Joseph entered Egypt. He arrived as property, sold for a handful of counted dirhams to a household that used him and then discarded him (12:19–20, 12:35). He leaves the prison as the personal attaché of the ruler of the country.

**"When He Spoke With Him" Is the Whole of the Interview**

The verse reports that a conversation took place and gives none of it. *Fa-lammā kallamahu* — when he had spoken with him. The commentators supply the content from the sequel: the king recognised his intelligence, his wisdom and his virtue. The Qur'an's method is to record the effect rather than the exchange, which is a decision about what matters. The interview is the moment a ruler forms a judgement of a man he has never met, on the basis of a forecast delivered second-hand and a scandal resolved in public. That judgement then issues in the appointment. The chapter does not need the words of the conversation to establish its quality, since the sentence that follows states the conclusion.

**"Esteemed and Trusted" Names Two Different Qualities**

*Innaka al-yawma ladaynā makīnun amīn* — today you have, with us, a place of standing and are trusted. The two adjectives are distinct and both are needed. *Makīn* describes rank, position, a settled place of influence; *amīn* describes reliability, the quality of a person who can be given charge of something. A man may have standing without being trusted with resources, and may be trusted without having the standing to act. The king grants both in one sentence, and the pairing is what makes the request in verse 55 possible: Joseph can ask for the storehouses because he has just been declared both powerful enough to hold them and honest enough to be given them. Neither term alone would justify what follows. Power without trust makes a minister dangerous; trust without power makes him useless in a famine. The pairing is what converts a compliment into a job description, and it explains why the very next verse is not an expression of thanks but a demand for authority over the granaries of the country.

**The Words Echo the Self-Description That Follows**

Joseph's answer in the next verse describes himself as *ḥafīẓ ʿalīm* — a careful keeper, one who knows. The two terms correspond to the king's two: the keeper answers to the trusted man, the knower to the one with standing. The correspondence is not accidental. A ruler has declared a judgement about a man's character, and the man responds by describing the same character in the vocabulary of the office he wants. Neither party is flattering the other. The exchange is a negotiation conducted in the language of competence, and the sūrah records both halves so that the appointment can be seen as matched rather than bestowed. The matching is exact in its terms. Knowledge answers the reliable keeper; power answers the one who knows how to administer. Nothing in the king's sentence exceeds what Joseph has already claimed for himself, and nothing Joseph claims is left unrecognised. The chapter builds the appointment out of two statements that fit one another, and it lets the fit carry the whole argument.

**From Twenty Dirhams to the King's Own Service**

The distance covered between verse 20 and verse 54 is the chapter's central reversal, and the sūrah measures it in the same currency both times. A boy was sold for a few counted coins by people who set no store by him (12:20). A man is now reserved exclusively by the ruler of Egypt, declared esteemed and trusted on the day of their first meeting. No intervening verse explains the change by reference to luck, patronage or political calculation. The mechanisms named are the ones Joseph himself possessed and used: knowledge taught by God (12:21, 12:37), conduct that earned the description of a doer of good (12:22, 12:36, 12:56), and a refusal to leave prison until the record was corrected (12:50). The elevation is presented as the outcome of those three things and nothing else.

**The King's Own Belief Is Not Reported**

The verse records the king's esteem and says nothing about his faith. He honours a prophet, employs him, trusts him, and is never described as believing what Joseph preached to two prisoners in verses 37 to 40. The transmitted reports differ: some hold that the king accepted Joseph's religion, others describe him only as a just ruler who recognised a just man. The Qur'an's silence is the safer ground, and it is consistent with the chapter's treatment of every non-Israelite in the story — the ʿAzīz, his wife, the women of the city, the cupbearer — none of whom is given a stated creed except by their own words. What the text does establish is that an upright ruler can recognise and employ prophetic competence without the narrative needing to settle his ultimate standing.

The word *al-yawm*, today, deserves a final note. The king dates his own judgement. Today you have standing with us, today you are trusted — the decree is given a day, and the day is the same one on which the interview took place. Egypt's highest office is not filled by deliberation, by consultation of advisers, or by any waiting period; a ruler forms a view of a man and acts on it immediately. The chapter records that speed without criticising it, and the next verse confirms the outcome as God's own establishment. What makes the haste defensible is that the judgement was not formed from nothing. The king had already heard an interpretation, a fifteen-year forecast, and a public exoneration delivered by the accused man's accuser.
