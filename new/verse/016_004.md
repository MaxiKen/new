## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:4

> **He created humans from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Him˺.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Cosmos To The Smallest Beginning**

After heavens and earth, the Qur'an turns to the human. Khalaqa al-insāna min nuṭfah — He created the human from a nuṭfah, a drop. The word nuṭfah in Arabic denotes a small amount of liquid, a drop that clings. It appears in the Qur'an to describe the initial stage of human formation, often paired with language of lowliness and weakness. The shift from vast heavens to a single drop is intentional. If the previous verse established Allah's power through magnitude, this verse establishes it through minuteness. The One who holds galaxies also originates a speaking, arguing, planning human from a fluid that cannot be seen without closeness. Al-Rāzī observes that this is a stronger proof of power than creating heavens, because heavens are great from great, while a human is great from insignificant. The same argument appears in 36:77: *Does man not see that We created him from a drop, and behold, he is a clear adversary?* (36:77). The wording there is almost identical to this verse, showing that the Qur'an returns to this image when confronting denial of resurrection.

**The Suddenness Of Fa-idhā**

Fa-idhā huwa khaṣīmun mubīn — then behold, he is an open challenger. The particle fa-idhā conveys surprise and suddenness. One moment a drop, the next a disputant who argues against his own Creator. The Qur'an uses this construction to highlight ingratitude and forgetfulness of origin. Al-Zamakhsharī notes the rhetorical force: the origin is weak, the outcome is bold. The human forgets the first and exhibits the second. Khaṣīm comes from kh-ṣ-m, to contend, to be party to a dispute. Mubīn means clear, manifest, open. Together they describe not quiet doubt but open argumentation, the kind that confronts prophets and denies resurrection. Ibn Kathīr connects this to a reported occasion: a Makkan, identified in some reports as Ubayy ibn Khalaf, came with a decayed bone, crumbled it, and said, who will give life to this after it has rotted? The Qur'an answers such challenges elsewhere: *Say, He will give it life who produced it the first time* (36:79). The point is not that questioning is forbidden, but that this particular questioning is rooted in arrogance that forgets its own beginning.

**What The Drop Teaches About Resurrection**

The verse is placed in Sūrat al-Niʿam among blessings, yet it sounds like rebuke. That placement is precise. The ability to argue, to reason, to speak clearly is itself a blessing, but when turned against the Giver it becomes evidence against the arguer. Al-Ṭabarī explains that nuṭfah is mentioned to remind the human of how easily Allah can recreate. If He could create from a drop the first time, with no prior example, then recreation after death is easier. The Qur'an makes this explicit in 30:27: *It is He who originates creation, then will repeat it, and that is even easier for Him* (30:27). The Makkan deniers did not doubt that Allah created them; they doubted that He would bring them back. The verse answers by pointing to their own origin: you already were brought from nothing like this, and you already know it, because you see children born. To deny the second creation while affirming the first is inconsistent. The word insān is generic, but the lesson is personal. Each reader knows his origin was a drop; each reader knows he now argues.

**The Psychology Of The Open Challenger**

Khaṣīmun mubīn describes a character type that appears throughout al-Naḥl. Later verses will mention those who argue that Allah forced them into shirk (16:35), those who say revelation is fables of ancients (16:24), those who attribute daughters to Allah while disliking daughters for themselves (16:57-59). All are forms of khaṣm, argument that defends desire rather than seeks truth. Al-Qurṭubī notes that khaṣīm here does not mean a skilled debater in a praiseworthy sense, but a contentious opponent who uses speech to push away truth. The Qur'an distinguishes between jidāl that is good, as in *argue with them in the best way* (16:125), and jidāl that is blameworthy, which is argument for falsehood. The same tongue can do either. The drop that became a tongue is tested by how it uses speech. This is why gratitude in this sūrah includes speech: acknowledging blessings, not arguing them away. The human who was created to be ʿabd, servant, becomes khaṣīm, adversary, when he forgets.

**A Link To The Blessings That Follow**

At first glance verse 4 interrupts the flow from heavens and earth to cattle and rain. In fact it prepares for it. By reminding the human of his lowly origin and contentious nature, it makes the coming catalogue of blessings an act of forbearance. Allah continues to provide warmth, food, beauty, transport, rain, crops, and guidance to a creature who openly challenges Him. Al-Rāzī counts this as part of divine generosity: despite the human's argumentativeness, Allah does not withhold provision. Instead He lists provision in detail, as if to say, see how you are treated despite your contention. This also explains the seal in verse 7: *Surely your Lord is Ever Gracious, Most Merciful* after mentioning cattle bearing loads. The grace is highlighted because the recipient is described in verse 4 as undeserving by argument. The sūrah thus moves from power to ingratitude to mercy, a pattern that will recur.

**Language And Translation Note**

Nuṭfah is translated as sperm-drop, though classical lexicons define it more broadly as a small drop of any fluid, including the combined fluids from both parents. The addition ˹Him˺ in brackets clarifies the object of challenge; the Arabic khaṣīmun mubīn is absolute, but context shows the challenge is directed against Allah, His messenger, and His promise of resurrection. The particle idhā after fa- gives vividness: it is as if the Qur'an says, look, suddenly. Translators render it as behold to capture that immediacy. The verse's brevity is part of its force. In seven Arabic words it moves from non-existence to existence to argument, compressing a lifetime into a single sentence, and thereby making the reader feel the speed with which favor is forgotten.
