## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:88

> the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day Exposes False Security**

Abraham explains the Day of resurrection by naming two of the strongest forms of worldly security: wealth and children. Neither will be of any benefit then. The statement does not deny that money and family can be blessings in this life. It denies that either can purchase acceptance, carry a person through judgment, or replace a heart turned toward God.

People often measure safety by what they possess and whom they can call upon. Wealth supplies options, while children supply affection, continuity, and social strength. On the final day, those supports reach their limit. The servant stands before God not as the owner of a protective network but as a morally responsible person.

**Wealth Cannot Be Taken Into the Final Account**

Money can solve some worldly problems, but it cannot buy forgiveness or alter the truth of a deed. It may pay for comfort, education, medicine, or influence, yet its power ends where God's judgment begins. Wealth has no independent value before the Lord if it was used to deny Him or to injure His creation.

This does not make wealth evil. Abraham's words concern its inability to benefit on the Day of resurrection, not its lawful use in the world. Wealth can be a trust used for charity, family care, justice, and worship. Its limitation is precisely what makes responsible use important: it is a means for a short life, not a shield against the final meeting.

**Children Cannot Carry Another Person's Faith**

Children are among the deepest sources of hope and pride. Parents may imagine that a respected family name, a large household, or a child's achievement will secure their own standing. The verse separates love from this illusion. Children can be loved, taught, and supported, but they cannot take responsibility for a parent's heart or substitute for the parent's obedience.

A family can help a person toward righteousness, but assistance in the world is not the same as intercession before God. Each person remains accountable. This gives children dignity as moral agents and gives parents a serious duty: family affection should guide people toward truth, not turn them into objects used for status.

**The Verse Does Not Denigrate Family or Provision**

Because the line speaks of wealth and children as unable to benefit, it must not be read as contempt for ordinary blessings. Abraham himself prays for family, and the Qur'anic account presents provision as a mercy. The problem is not possession. The problem is relying on possession as though it could decide the soul's final fate.

A believer may enjoy wealth and raise children with gratitude while remembering that both belong to God. The heart should hold blessings without making them idols. Wealth is a trust, and children are an entrusted responsibility. Neither should become the object of ultimate fear, hope, or obedience.

**The Final Day Reorders Social Rank**

Worldly societies rank people by money, family, connections, and influence. The Day of resurrection removes the protective force of those rankings. The rich person cannot make wealth answer for him, and the parent cannot present children as proof of righteousness. The categories that organize public honor are exposed as temporary.

This reordering is not arbitrary. It reveals what those categories could never prove. Wealth may show opportunity or fortune; children may show family continuity. Neither demonstrates a sound heart. God's judgment reaches the person behind the social role and measures what the person did with every trust.

**A Checked Parallel States the Same Limit**

The parallel warning in 3:10 expressly joins wealth and children with the inability to benefit those who reject God. The repetition across the Qur'an makes the point a stable principle rather than a detail limited to Abraham's prayer. Worldly security becomes dangerous when it persuades a person that no higher account will be required.

The parallel also keeps the verse balanced. It does not tell the poor that lack automatically saves them or the childless that they possess automatic spiritual advantage. Wealth and children are not the cause of ruin by themselves. The decisive issue is whether the servant uses blessings in submission or lets blessings replace submission.

**The Verse Protects the Vulnerable From Envy**

A person who hears that wealth and children will not benefit on the final day might be tempted to envy those who possess them or to glorify deprivation. Abraham's statement gives no basis for either response. The poor still need justice, and the wealthy still carry responsibility. The verse removes the illusion of ultimate security, not the need to treat worldly needs seriously.

Knowing that possessions cannot save also frees the heart from measuring its worth by comparison. A person with little can seek God without believing he is spiritually inferior. A person with much can give without imagining that generosity makes him owner of salvation. Both stand in the same need for a sound heart.

**The Heart Is the Decisive Possession**

The verse prepares the reader to see that what matters on the Day is not the size of a person's estate or household but the state of the person before God. Wealth and children are external supports. They can surround a person without changing the direction of his heart. The final account reaches the inner allegiance that possessions often conceal.

This is why Abraham's prayer for protection from disgrace is connected to the failure of wealth and children. If external supports cannot help, then only God's mercy and a faithful heart remain. The servant should use worldly blessings responsibly, but he must bring to the final day something wealth cannot buy and family cannot lend: sincere submission to the Lord.

**Worldly Blessings Become Tests of Gratitude**

Once wealth and children are seen as unable to save, their proper role becomes clearer. They are tests of gratitude, patience, justice, and care. The wealthy person is tested by how he earns and spends. The parent is tested by how he raises, protects, and releases children into moral responsibility. The blessings matter because of the choices they make possible.

Abraham's sentence therefore does not empty worldly life of meaning. It prevents worldly life from claiming final meaning. Wealth and children can serve obedience, but they cannot replace it. On the Day of resurrection, the servant will not be asked how impressive the gifts looked. He will be asked what relation he formed with the Giver.
