## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:1

> Whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Allah, for He is the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole Created Order Gives Praise**

The sūrah begins with a universal subject: “whatever is in the heavens and the earth” (57:1). This sweeping phrase is deliberate. The wording leaves no corner of creation outside the declaration. Stars, planets, living creatures, physical forces, visible societies, and realities hidden from human sight all belong to the same created order and all, in their proper manner, glorify Allah. The verse does not say that only the morally aware praise Him. It presents glorification as a condition woven into existence itself.

This does not require every created thing to use human speech. The Qur’an elsewhere teaches that every thing praises its Lord, while human beings do not understand the manner of that praise (17:44). The point is not that stones and stars imitate human prayer. Their existence, order, and submission testify in a mode suited to them. Human ignorance of that mode limits our perception, not the reality of their worship. A thing praises its Lord by obeying the measure placed within it, displaying the wisdom of its Maker, and fulfilling the role for which it was created. A star does not need a human tongue to testify. Its order, dependence, and obedience are already a form of testimony. The verse therefore places human worship inside a larger chorus rather than treating it as an isolated religious activity.

**Tasbīḥ Removes False Ideas About God**

The verb *yusabbiḥu* comes from the root *s-b-ḥ*, which carries the sense of moving away or declaring transcendence. To glorify Allah is to affirm that He is far above every defect, limitation, injustice, and likeness to created things. Praise and transcendence meet in the word. Creation does not merely say that Allah possesses power; it declares that His power is not crude or lawless, and that His wisdom is not mixed with ignorance.

This matters because human beings often project created weakness onto the Creator. A ruler may be powerful but uninformed. A scholar may be wise but unable to act. A wealthy person may possess resources but lack mercy. The verse refuses such divided expectations when it ends with Allah’s might and wisdom. His strength is not blind force, and His wisdom is not helpless reflection. The universe praises the One whose power achieves what His wisdom decrees and whose wisdom gives power a just direction.

**Power and Wisdom Complete One Another**

The two Divine descriptions at the end of the verse are carefully joined. *Al-ʿAzīz*, the Almighty, indicates irresistible strength and dignity. *Al-Ḥakīm*, the All-Wise, indicates that every act is placed with right measure and purpose. If the verse named power alone, a reader might imagine domination without moral order. If it named wisdom alone, a reader might imagine knowledge without the ability to bring judgment or mercy into effect. Together the Names describe perfect sovereignty.

The pairing also prepares for the themes of the sūrah. The chapter will speak about ownership, spending, justice, struggle, revelation, hypocrisy, and the fate of communities. None of these subjects can be understood rightly if Allah’s power is separated from His wisdom. Wealth is not ultimate because it belongs to Him. Justice is not optional because His rule is wise. Sacrifice is not wasted because His judgment reaches what human observers cannot see. The opening verse gives the theological foundation before the commands begin.

**Human Rebellion Is an Exception, Not the Pattern**

The universal scope of the verse exposes the strange position of the rebel. The heavens do not resist the order assigned to them. The earth does not claim ownership of itself. Created things do not turn their dependence into an argument against their Creator. Human beings, however, can receive every breath and still behave as though independence were possible. The verse does not flatter this posture. It places the human being within a cosmos already glorifying Allah, so refusal becomes an interruption in a larger act of worship.

That comparison should produce humility, not contempt for other people. The first person challenged by the verse is the one who reads it. Does the body obey while the heart objects? Do a person’s actions acknowledge dependence even when his words deny it? Human freedom gives worship a special moral character, but it also makes ingratitude possible. The stars praise without moral struggle; a person who praises Allah while resisting selfish desire offers a chosen obedience. The verse calls him to make his freedom agree with the testimony already carried by creation.

**Praise Must Become Conduct**

Glorification is not limited to repeated words. A tongue can say “Allahu akbar” while a hand cheats, a mind boasts, or a heart treats another creature as the source of what only Allah controls. True *tasbīḥ* reshapes conduct because it removes false rivals from the center of life. If Allah is Almighty and All-Wise, then fear of people cannot justify injustice, and love of possessions cannot excuse miserliness. Worship becomes visible in how power is used, how knowledge is handled, and how the vulnerable are treated.

The verse therefore opens the sūrah with both consolation and demand. Consolation comes from knowing that existence is not abandoned or meaningless; it is held within divine power and wisdom. The demand is to stop living as though one’s private will were the highest authority. A believer joins the universal glorification by accepting Allah’s guidance and giving each thing its due. The chapter’s later call to spend from what has been entrusted to people follows naturally from this beginning: the servant who knows the Owner can release the loan without imagining that he has lost what was never finally his.
