## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:57

> He is the One Who sends the winds ushering in His mercy. When they bear heavy clouds, We drive them to a lifeless land and then cause rain to fall, producing every type of fruit. Similarly, We will bring the dead to life, so perhaps you will be mindful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Winds Before the Mercy**

The verse names God as the One who sends the winds as bearers of good news before His mercy, and the Book uses the same description in several passages. The Ant's account states that God sends the winds as glad tidings before His mercy (27:63), the sūrah of the Romans states the same (30:46), and the sūrah of the Sorting states that God sends the winds as heralds of good tidings. The verse in front of us places the winds at the front of the rain that follows them, and the phrase before His mercy describes the rain itself as the mercy.

The Book's use of the word mercy for rain appears throughout, and the sūrah of the Romans states that God sends down the rain after people have despaired and spreads His mercy (30:24). The verse in front of us names the sequence of the wind and the cloud and the rain, and the passage that follows draws the argument about the resurrection from the same sequence.

**The Clouds and the Dead Land**

The verse states that when the winds carry the heavy clouds, God drives them to a land that is dead and sends down water upon it, and the Book uses the image of the revived land in its arguments for the raising of the dead. The account of the Romans states that God revives the earth after its death, and that He will revive the dead in the same way. The sūrah of Fāṭir and the sūrah of the Ornament state that God sends down water from the sky and revives the earth with it after its death.

The land in front of us is described as dead, and the commentary tradition treats the image as a deliberate selection of the least promising material. The rain does not restore a garden that was already growing; it brings produce out of ground that had produced nothing, and the Book's argument rests on the difference between the condition before and the condition after. The image returns when the sūrah distinguishes the good land from the poor one.

**Every Kind of Fruit**

The verse states that the rain brings forth every kind of fruit, and the Book's descriptions of the provision of the earth include the variety of the produce as a sign. The Cattle's account lists the produce of the earth, the palm, the olive, and the fruit of every kind in its accounts of the signs, and the sūrah of the Bee describes the rain that brings out crops of every kind. The verse in front of us names the result of the rain as the abundance that the audience ate from every day.

The abundance is presented as the ordinary provision of the world rather than as an exception, and the Book's argument depends on the familiarity of what it describes. The audience observed the sequence of the wind, the cloud, the rain, and the harvest every year, and the verse states that the same power that performs the sequence will perform the raising of the dead.

**Thus We Bring Forth the Dead**

The verse states the conclusion that the image is designed to establish, and the Book repeats it in several passages. The account of the Romans states that God will bring forth the dead in the same manner as He revives the earth, and the sūrah of Fāṭir uses the same comparison. The argument in front of us does not depend on the possibility of the event in the abstract but on the evidence of the world's own cycles.

The audience's objection to the resurrection was an objection about the raising of the scattered body, and the Book answers it with the production of a harvest from soil whose condition was indistinguishable from death. The commentary tradition notes that the argument is presented to people whose livelihood depended on the sequence, since the sūrah's audience lived by the rain and the pasture.

**The Call to Remember**

The verse closes with the statement that these signs are given so that people may remember, and the Book attaches the formula to the created order in several passages. The sūrah of the Cattle closes its account of the signs with the statement that God makes them clear for people who remember, and the sūrah of the Bee uses the same closing formula. This verse states the purpose of the evidence, and the passage that follows states the difference between the land that responds and the land that does not.

The remembrance in front of us is the recognition of the One who sends the winds and the rain, and the Book presents reflection on the created order as the route to that recognition. The sūrah's audience watched the sky for its livelihood, and the verse states that the same observation supplies the evidence for the world's end.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands fifty-seventh in the chapter, and it follows the command against corruption in the land and the instruction to call upon God with hope and fear (7:56). The verses that follow use the image of the good land and the poor land to describe the two responses to the revelation (7:58), and the passage then begins the accounts of the prophets and their peoples. The verse here supplies the natural sign that introduces that sequence.

The reader who has been told to call upon his Lord with hope and fear is now shown the rain that answers the call in the world's own order. The winds precede the mercy, the dead land receives the water, and the produce that follows is offered as evidence for the raising of the dead; and the sūrah's next verse distinguishes the land that keeps what it receives from the land that does not.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that the good land brings forth its produce by the permission of its Lord while the poor land brings forth little, and it states that the signs are varied for people who give thanks (7:58). The account of the rain in front of us is followed by the account of the two kinds of soil, and the image is then carried into the history of the prophets and their peoples.

The two verses together form a parable about revelation. The rain falls on both kinds of ground, and the difference in the harvest comes from the ground, not from the water; and the sūrah then presents the communities that received the warning as the land that returned little.
