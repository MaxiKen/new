## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:221

> Do not marry polytheistic women until they believe; for a believing slave-woman is better than a free polytheist, even though she may look pleasant to you. And do not marry your women to polytheistic men until they believe, for a believing slave-man is better than a free polytheist, even though he may look pleasant to you. They invite ˹you˺ to the Fire while Allah invites ˹you˺ to Paradise and forgiveness by His grace. He makes His revelations clear to the people so perhaps they will be mindful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gate of the Household**

The verse installs faith as the condition of marriage, and in doing so it opens the sūrah's great domestic legislation — the rulings on intimacy, oaths, divorce, waiting periods, and nursing that fill the pages ahead. Before the household's interior is legislated, its gate is defined: do not marry the polytheistic women until they believe, and do not give your women to polytheistic men until they believe. The double prohibition is symmetrical in its condition — belief required of the man and of the woman alike before the marriage may proceed — and its placement is architectural. The community that has just been taught to weigh wine against its benefits, to spare from its wealth for the needy, and to treat the orphan as a brother is now taught that the most intimate of all human contracts is governed by the same faith that governs the rest: the household is not a private province exempt from the creed but the creed's primary site, and its founding act passes through the gate of belief.

**The Slave Better Than the Free**

Each prohibition carries its comparison, and the comparison dismantles the Arabian marriage market's two great currencies: status and beauty. A believing slave-woman is better than a free polytheist, though the free woman pleases you; a believing slave-man is better than a free polytheist, though he impresses you. The ranking is deliberate provocation: in a society that measured persons by freedom and lineage, the verse ranks the enslaved believer above the free idolater — not in legal rights but in the worth that matters for marriage. And the concession clauses are honest about the desire they overrule: *though she pleases you, though he impresses* — attraction is acknowledged as real, as felt, as insufficient. The verse does not ask the believer to stop finding the polytheist beautiful; it asks him to know that beauty does not carry a household and belief does. The comparison thereby legislates the marriage decision's true scale: against the market's axes of freedom and attractiveness, the verse sets the single axis of faith — and the slave on that axis outweighs the notable on the other.

**The Legal Development: The People of the Book**

The verse's ruling generated one of the passage's most discussed legal questions, because the sūrah of the Table permits what appears broader: the chaste women of those given the Scripture before you are lawful to you in marriage (5:5). The exegetes record the positions. Some held that 5:5 partially abrogated this verse's ruling, opening marriage with Jewish and Christian women while the prohibition against idolaters stands — a reading that arises only if the People of the Book were classed among the polytheists the verse names. Many maintained the simpler harmonization: the prohibition was never directed at the People of the Book at all, whose scripture and monotheism place them outside the verse's category. A minority held the reverse abrogation — that this verse cancels 5:5, forbidding marriage with any non-Muslim. The operative ruling in Islamic law follows the permissive readings: Muslim men may marry Jewish and Christian women, with some jurists extending the category to Zoroastrians and, in the Indian context, to Hindus. And the asymmetry the verse's second clause established became the law's settled position: the command not to give Muslim women to polytheistic men is understood consistently as barring marriage between a Muslim woman and any non-Muslim man. The verse thus stands as the household-gate's foundation, with 5:5 as its specified opening — faith the rule, the People of the Book the exception the scripture itself legislated.

**They Call to the Fire**

The verse's theological clause explains the gate's necessity: they call to the Fire, and God calls to the Garden and to forgiveness, by His leave. The calling is not imagined as preaching — the polytheist spouse rarely sets out to damn the household — but as the direction of a shared life: the values a marriage runs on, the gods a home honors, the ends a family's daily choices accumulate toward. Marriage is the verse's deep institution of direction, the arrangement in which two people call each other, day by day, toward whatever they severally serve; and a household founded across the divide of belief is founded across the divide of destinations. The clause's parallel structure states the two calls in their full scope: the Fire, against the Garden *and forgiveness* — God's call carries both the destination and the pardon for the road. The phrase *by His leave* keeps the calling gracious: the invitation to the Garden is not a transaction but a permission-granted gift, the same gratuity the previous verse's guidance and the trial-verse's help carried. The marriage-gate thus opens onto the whole eschatological geography the sūrah has been mapping — and the spouse is revealed as, in the deepest sense, a fellow-traveler toward one of the two destinations.

**The Signs Made Clear, That They May Remember**

The verse closes with the formula that has punctuated the whole passage: God makes His signs clear to people, that haply they may be mindful. The clarity-legislation and the taqwā-end are the sūrah's fixed pair — the same closing served the fast (2:185–187) and will serve the divorce-limitations ahead (2:230) — and its presence here marks the marriage-ruling as sign rather than mere rule: the gate of belief is legible as part of a coherent design, visible to the mind that reflects upon the two worlds the previous verse named. The mindfulness the verse aims at is precisely the faculty the domestic legislation will repeatedly demand: the guardian's intention audited by God (2:220), the husband's retention-or-release conducted honorably (2:229), the parent's weaning decided by mutual counsel (2:233). All of it requires people who have learned to see the revealed order as an order — clear signs read for their meaning, not mere commands obeyed without comprehension. The marriage-gate is the first sign of the household series, and its clarity is its mercy: the believer chooses his household's foundation with the design visible, choosing faith at the gate because the signs have shown him where each road's calling leads.

**The Verse in the Community's Formation**

Read within the movement, the verse completes the community's social architecture begun at the trial-verse. The believers were told their faith would be tested as the faiths before were tested (2:214); taught the roster of their charity (2:215); commanded in what they dislike (2:216); weighed in the scales of the sacred month (2:217); comforted with the hope of mercy (2:218); trained in the weighing of wine and gambling (2:219); instructed in the orphan's brotherhood (2:220). Now the household's gate: the intimate contract that will produce the next generation of the tested, the charitable, the obedient, the weighed, the hoping, the reflecting, the guardian-brothers. The verse's two comparisons — slave above free, belief above beauty — train the choosing heart in the community's inverted scale, the scale on which 2:212 had already ranked the tattered unknown above a world of the finely dressed. The marriage-gate is that ranking applied where it costs most: the desire of the eye overruled by the knowledge of the destination, the market's prices overruled by the Fire's and the Garden's callings — and the signs made clear so that the overruling is not mere submission but mindfulness, the household founded in the same reflective taqwā the whole passage exists to form.
