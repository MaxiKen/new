## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:59

> He will certainly admit them into a place they will be pleased with. For Allah is truly All-Knowing, Most Forbearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"He Will Admit Them" — the Verb of Grace Returns**

*La-yudkhilannahum mudkhalan yarḍawnahu* — He will surely admit them an entrance they are pleased with.

The verb is the same one the sūrah used in verses 14 and 23 for the believers' entry into the Gardens, and the emphatic form is the strongest available. Entry is caused by God, and the sūrah has never described it any other way. The emigrants' leaving was their act; their arriving is not.

That asymmetry runs through the whole passage. Verse 58 promised provision from the best of providers; verse 59 promises entry from the One who admits. Both halves of the emigrant's future are described as done to him rather than achieved by him, which is consistent with a sūrah that has insisted since verse 16 that guidance belongs to God.

The emphatic form is worth pausing on: the prefixed *la-* and the doubled *n* are the strongest construction Arabic has for a future act, and the sūrah uses it for entry in exactly the places where it wants the outcome removed from human achievement. The believers *are admitted*; they do not arrive. Everything the emigrants did was on the leaving side of this promise, and everything on the arriving side is done to them.

**"An Entrance They Are Pleased With"**

*Mudkhalan yarḍawnahu* — an entering-place that they are content with.

The object of the verb is not a place but an *entering*, a verbal noun. What is described is the manner and the experience of arriving rather than the coordinates of a destination. The sūrah does not say where they go; it says that the going-in will be such that they are pleased with it.

The relative clause is the substance of the promise. Contentment is internal and cannot be conferred by the quality of a place alone — a person can be in a garden and be wretched. What is promised here is the removal of that possibility.

Contentment is the Qur'an's own term for the furthest point of the reward, and it is used reciprocally — the servant is pleased and the Lord is pleased. The close of Sūrah al-Bayyinah gives the same double: *“Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him”* (98:8). What is promised here is one half of that exchange, and it is stated as an internal state rather than as an inventory, because a person can be given everything and still be out of place.

**Why Contentment Is the Right Promise for Emigrants**

The people addressed had left their homes, their property, their clan and, in many cases, their families. What an emigrant loses is not primarily comfort; it is the sense of being where one belongs.

That is why the promise is phrased as it is. A description of palaces and rivers would not have answered the specific deprivation. What is promised is that the arriving will feel right — that the person who spent his life being out of place will be put somewhere he does not want to leave.

Sūrah al-Fajr states the same promise in the direct address to the soul at peace: *“Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺. So join My servants, and enter My Paradise”* (89:27–30). The contentment and the entry are the same event.

The deprivation was also a specific one: an emigrant has no standing where he has arrived and no home to return to. Promises of rank and of rivers do not address that; the promise of being put somewhere one does not wish to leave does. The sūrah's precision here is the same precision it showed in verse 58, where the provision was described by its quality rather than by its quantity.

**"God Is Knowing and Forbearing"**

*Wa-inna Allāha la-ʿalīmun ḥalīm* — and God is Knowing, Forbearing.

The two names are worth taking together, because their combination is what makes the verse work.

*ʿAlīm*, Knowing: He knows what each of these people left, what it cost them, and what they would have done had they stayed. The emigrants' inner histories are not interchangeable, and none of them is lost. That is the attribute that makes a promise of *contentment* credible — a generic reward could be given by a distributor, but a reward each person is pleased with requires knowledge of each person.

*Ḥalīm*, Forbearing: the word describes restraint in the face of provocation, the quality of not hastening. It is the attribute the sūrah has been illustrating since verse 44: communities are given rein and then seized. Forbearance here means that the delay did not cost these people anything. The emigrants who died before the victory are not disadvantaged by having died early.

**"Them" — Who Is Covered**

The pronoun refers back to verse 58's emigrants, and the two verses are a single promise. But the wording of verse 58 — *those who emigrate in the way of God and then are killed or die* — is general enough to cover more than the first generation.

That generality was noticed by the commentators, who extended the promise to anyone who leaves home for the sake of the religion. The extension is sound, because the verse names an act and a consequence rather than a group of named people.

Notice what the verse does *not* require. It does not require that the emigration succeed, that the person arrive anywhere in particular, or that he live to see any result. The promise is attached to the leaving and to the death or killing that follows it, whatever form either takes.

The generality also settles a question the first community would have asked about itself. The promise is not attached to reaching Madīnah, to surviving, or to seeing the outcome; it is attached to leaving for God and then dying or being killed. An emigrant who died on the road is as fully inside the sentence as one who lived to see the conquest.

**Forbearance Is the Sūrah's Own Attribute**

*Ḥalīm* deserves one more word, because it is the attribute this sūrah has been illustrating rather than merely naming.

The chapter's entire middle section is an argument about delay: communities given rein before being seized (22:44, 22:48), a punishment whose arrival is measured on a different clock (22:47), a warning delivered by a messenger who is not responsible for the outcome (22:49). Forbearance is the divine name for all of that.

Naming it here, at the close of the promise to the emigrants, is the sūrah's way of saying that the delay cost them nothing. Those who died before the victory are described as entering a place they are pleased with, and the One who admits them is the One who was never in a hurry.

**The Pairing in the Sūrah's Closing Movement**

Verses 58 and 59 form one promise, and the two names at the end of verse 59 are the seal on it. What is promised to people who gave up everything is that the One they gave it up for knows what it was and was never in a hurry about it.

For a community that had waited years for permission to fight, and that had lost people while waiting, that is the relevant assurance. The forbearance is not a delay in the reward; it is the character of the One who gives it.

The two names also close a circle the sūrah opened early. Knowing and Forbearing is what a community needs God to be when the wicked appear to be prospering and the faithful appear to be losing. Verse 44 named the pattern — rein given, then seizure — and verse 59 names the attribute behind it, and then applies it to the people who waited.
