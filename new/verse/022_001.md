## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:1

> O humanity! Fear your Lord, for the ˹violent˺ quaking at the Hour is surely a dreadful thing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Pilgrimage Sūrah That Opens With an Earthquake**

The sūrah carries the name of the pilgrimage because the rite is proclaimed in it (22:27), yet its first sentence is not about a rite at all. Before anyone is told how to walk around a House, the ground itself refuses to hold still. That ordering makes a claim about worship. A ritual performed against a backdrop of permanent, indifferent nature is one thing; a ritual performed inside a world with an expiry date is another. Whoever reaches the pilgrimage verses of this sūrah has already been told, in its opening line, that the stage on which the rites are performed will be shaken apart.

The address is *yā ayyuhā al-nāsu* — O humanity — the widest form of address in the Qur'an's vocabulary. It is not *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū*, the formula that opens most Medinan passages and that appears near the end of this sūrah (22:77). The terrors of the Hour are not announced to a community that already accepts them; they are announced to everyone, and the announcement is built to produce the fear it calls for.

**Taqwā Is a Shield, and Here You Can See What It Guards Against**

*Ittaqū* comes from the root *w-q-y*, to guard or to shield. Its noun, *taqwā*, names the state of having put something between yourself and a danger. Most occurrences in the Qur'an leave the danger general: reverence God, and the object of the reverence is God's own claim on you. This verse does something rarer. It names the danger in the same breath — reverence your Lord, because the quaking at the Hour is a dreadful thing.

The pairing is deliberate. What is commanded is not panic at a natural catastrophe; it is reverence aimed at the Lord who sends it. The earthquake is not a rival power to be appeased or bribed. Placing the two clauses side by side keeps the terror and the theology in the right order: the event is horrifying, and the event is not in charge.

**The Hour Arrives in the Text Already Familiar**

*Al-sāʿah* comes with the definite article. The term is not introduced, defined, or established by argument; it is used as something the audience already possesses. The same is true across the Qur'an, and that is why the word functions almost as a proper name for the end.

The ordinary sense of *sāʿah* is a short stretch of time — an hour, a moment. Pressing the word for a brief interval into service as the name for the end of history is a compression worth pausing on. The end arrives the way an hour arrives: not gradually, not negotiated, and not late. Suddenness belongs to the word before any verse attaches it to the thing.

**"The Quaking of the Hour": a Construct That Carries Two Readings**

The Arabic is *zalzalata al-sāʿati*, the quaking of the Hour. Two readings are available to this annexation, and the grammarians recorded both. One takes it as temporal: the quaking that occurs when the Hour comes. The other takes it as descriptive: the quaking that *is* the Hour's opening act. The difference is small on the page and large in implication. On the first reading the shaking is a herald; on the second it is the thing itself beginning.

Both readings survive because the verse declines to choose between them, and the refusal is part of the rhetoric. A reader who wants to fix the quaking on a timeline is given no peg to hang it on.

**"A Dreadful Thing" Refuses to Specify**

The closing predicate is indefinite: *shayʾun ʿaẓīmun*, a dreadful thing, a mighty thing. *Shayʾ* is among the vaguest nouns in Arabic — a thing, anything at all — and *ʿaẓīm* among the widest adjectives. The sentence could have said how long the quaking lasts, how many it kills, or which cities go down. It says none of these.

The vagueness works. Specific terror can be measured, and what can be measured can be planned around. An unmeasured *shayʾ* leaves the imagination nowhere to rest, which is precisely what a sūrah needs from its first line.

**A Word Built for Repetition**

*Zalzalah* comes from the doubled root *z-l-z-l*. In Arabic, doubling a root often signals repetition or intensity, and the pattern here is the same one found in *waṣwaṣa* (to whisper repeatedly) or *qalqala* (to shake about). The quaking is not a single jolt. The form of the word implies a shaking that keeps shaking.

The same word is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for a shaking that is not geological at all — the shaking of people under pressure: *“Then and there the believers were put to the test, and were violently shaken”* (33:11). That parallel matters for reading this sūrah. The word describes what happens to a community when its certainties are tested, as well as what happens to the ground. A sūrah that opens with *zalzalah* is announcing that the world will be shaken and that the people in it will be shaken with it — the outer convulsion and the inner one are named with one root.

**Why the Sūrah Starts Here Instead of at the Kaʿbah**

A religion can be practised as inheritance. The rites of verses 26–37 — the circuit, the standing, the sacrifice — could in principle be performed by a man who believes nothing about the end of the world, simply because his father performed them. The sūrah closes that option in its first verse. The worship it goes on to command is worship carried out inside a world that is going to be dismantled, and the dismantling is not a threat bolted onto the religion; it is the frame the religion is set in.

That is also why the sūrah ends where it does, with a summons to strive for God as He deserves (22:78) and a description of a community chosen and witnessed over. Between the earthquake of verse 1 and that summons, the sūrah builds a people who know what time they are living in.
