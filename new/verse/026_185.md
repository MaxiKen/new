## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:185

> They replied, “You are simply bewitched!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply Attacks the Messenger Instead of the Claim**

Shuʿayb has spoken about God, property, and public corruption. The people's first recorded reply does not examine any of those points. It assigns a label to the speaker: bewitched. The accusation attempts to make his words seem unworthy of serious consideration before the community has to answer them.

That movement is familiar in disputes about moral reform. When a message threatens a profitable habit, it can be easier to question the speaker's mind than to inspect the habit. The label may produce laughter, fear, or social distance. None of those responses establishes that Shuʿayb's account of justice is false.

**Bewitchment Is Used as a Social Shortcut**

To call someone bewitched can mean that his speech has been affected by an outside force and therefore should not be trusted. It is a shortcut around evidence. The people do not show a defect in his reasoning or answer the demand for honest measure. They try to settle the matter by changing the community's picture of him.

Quran 26:153 records the same accusation against Shuʿayb in this very narrative pattern, and 17:47 preserves a similar charge against the Prophet Muhammad when people heard the Quran. The repetition reveals a recurring defense of denial: make revelation look like confusion, and the hearer will feel permitted to ignore its demands.

Even if a person were ill, distressed, or unusual, that would not make a false measure just. The truth of the command must be tested by its evidence and content, not by an insult directed at the messenger. A community that cares about justice should be especially careful not to use mental or spiritual language as a weapon against a difficult critic.

The accusation also reveals the people's priorities. They are more concerned to discredit Shuʿayb than to deny that they owe others their property. When a society spends its energy policing the reformer's reputation while refusing to inspect its own conduct, the response becomes evidence of moral resistance.

**The Messenger Is Made Strange to the Crowd**

Calling a reformer bewitched creates a boundary between sensible people and the one who warns them. The crowd can then protect its identity by treating obedience as normal and correction as madness. This is how a harmful custom survives: not because no one sees its cost, but because anyone who names the cost is made socially unsafe.

Shuʿayb's message does not become less clear when the people call him strange. Their reaction shows the price of confronting a settled interest. The servant of God must refuse both temptations: he must not change truth to regain approval, and he must not answer insult by inventing knowledge of hearts.

**Serious Questions Must Be Met Seriously**

A fair response would ask whether Allah alone deserves worship, whether property may be reduced through deception, and whether corruption harms the land. It would compare Shuʿayb's conduct with his claim to trustworthiness. Instead, the people begin with a dismissal that requires no self-examination.

The verse therefore warns the listener about a habit of denial. Before repeating a charge against a teacher, examine whether the charge answers the teaching. Before laughing at a person who challenges a profitable practice, ask whether the practice can survive an honest accounting. A label may end a conversation, but it cannot settle the account before Allah.

The charge of bewitchment can also protect a listener from the discomfort of being corrected. If Shuʿayb is treated as mentally or spiritually compromised, his warning no longer has to be weighed. That relief is temporary. The community still has to live with the consequences of its measures, its treatment of property, and its response to a messenger who asks no reward.

A responsible listener distinguishes between a claim about a message and a claim about the messenger's condition. The first can be examined: is the command coherent, is it just, and does it agree with the known call to worship Allah? The second often relies on rumor and social pressure. It may say more about the accuser's fear than about the person accused.

Modern speech has its own versions of this shortcut. A reformer may be called unstable because he refuses a profitable custom, disloyal because he exposes corruption, or dangerous because he insists on equal rights. The particular label changes, but the method remains the same: make the speaker unacceptable so that the practice escapes examination. Shuʿayb's story teaches resistance to that method without asking us to accept every speaker uncritically.

The right response is neither blind loyalty nor reflexive dismissal. Listen, verify, and judge the argument by truth. If the warning is sound, changing the speaker's reputation will not make it false. If the speaker is wrong, evidence can show the error without resorting to humiliation. That standard protects both honest messengers and the community's moral intelligence.

The accusation also reveals how a group can make an insult sound like a conclusion. They speak together, and the shared voice gives the label an appearance of certainty. A repeated charge is still not evidence. It may only show that people have agreed to defend one another from an uncomfortable truth.

The believer should be careful with such language in every age. Rejecting a claim is sometimes necessary, but rejection should identify the error rather than invent a defect in the person. If a critic exposes unlawful gain, answering with ridicule leaves the gain unexamined. If a teacher makes a mistake, showing the mistake is stronger than attaching a permanent name to him.

Shuʿayb's people wanted the audience to look at his supposed condition instead of their own conduct. The verse breaks that spell by preserving their words as part of the evidence against them. Their accusation did not erase the command about justice; it showed how unwilling they were to hear it.
