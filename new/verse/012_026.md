## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:26

> Joseph responded, “It was she who tried to seduce me.” And a witness from her own family testified: “If his shirt is torn from the front, then she has told the truth and he is a liar.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Clause of Defence, Using Her Own Verb**

Joseph's entire answer is *hiya rāwadatnī ʿan nafsī* — she sought to seduce me. He does not narrate the locked doors, does not mention the pursuit, does not explain the tear in his garment and does not appeal to his own character. He takes the verb the Qur'an used for her action in verse 23 and gives it back with her as its subject. The economy is deliberate. A slave contradicting the wife of the house has one resource, which is to state the fact in the shortest possible form and let the evidence do the rest. The commentators gloss the sentence as: she wanted indecency with me and I refused and fled. The flight is contained in the clause, because a man who refuses and runs is a man whose garment is torn from behind.

**The Witness Comes From Her Side of the Family**

*A witness from her own people* is the verse's decisive intervention, and its force lies entirely in its origin. A witness from Joseph's side would have been dismissed; a witness from the household staff would have been suspect. Someone belonging to her family has no interest in her disgrace and every interest in protecting it. The transmitted reports identify him in two ways, and both go back to named authorities. ʿAbd al-Razzāq recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās that the witness was a bearded man — an adult male — and Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah, al-Suddī and Ibn Isḥāq held the same reading. Al-ʿAwfī reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that he was a babe in the cradle, and a young boy belonging to the ʿAzīz's household is likewise reported from Abū Hurayrah, Hilāl ibn Yasāf, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and al-Ḍaḥḥāk — the reading al-Ṭabarī preferred. What both preserve is the same structural point: the testimony that saves Joseph is supplied by the party least inclined to supply it.

**The Test Proposed Is Physical Rather Than Testimonial**

Nobody is asked what they saw. The witness proposes an examination of an object, and the object is the one thing in the room that cannot take a side. That is a remarkable method for an ancient narrative, and the Qur'an sets it out as a rule with two branches and a determinate outcome for each. The commentators explain the mechanics: a tear at the front indicates a struggle in which she was repelling an approaching man, while a tear at the back indicates that she seized him as he left. The rule is offered before the inspection, which means the husband cannot adapt his finding to his preference. He must accept whichever branch the garment satisfies. The verse thus builds a procedure that removes discretion from the person with the most power in the room.

**The First Branch Favors Her, and It Is Stated First**

The witness begins with the condition under which the woman is right: if the shirt is torn from the front, she has spoken the truth and he is a liar. Opening with the branch favourable to one's own relative is what makes the proposal credible. A witness who began with the incriminating condition would look partisan; this one states the exculpatory case first and the incriminating case second, in the same grammatical form, with the same evenness. The arrangement converts a family member into a judge. It also produces the verse's irony, since the branch he states first is the branch that will not apply, and he knows the garment before he speaks.

**Why the Household Needed an Outside Procedure**

The scene presents a problem no domestic authority can solve. Two people in a locked room, no third witness, one of them the wife of the master and the other his property. The husband has no means of deciding except by status, and status favours her automatically. The witness's proposal is therefore not merely clever; it is the only route by which a verdict in Joseph's favour could be reached at all inside that household. This is why the chapter gives the method such prominence and reproduces it at length across two verses. A community that decides cases by rank cannot acquit a slave, and the sūrah shows the alternative being invented on the spot by a man who had every reason not to invent it.

**The Shirt Testifies Twice in This Chapter**

The garment is the chapter's most reliable witness and it appears at both of the story's great deceptions. In verse 18 a shirt smeared with false blood was produced to convince a father that his son had been eaten, and it failed, because a garment that a wolf had supposedly torn was intact. Here a shirt torn in the wrong place destroys a wife's account of what happened in a locked room. The two cases are mirror images: in the first, the absence of damage disproved the story; in the second, the position of the damage disproves it. The sūrah is making an argument about evidence through the repetition of a single object, and it is an argument directed at the reader rather than at the characters. Things leave marks. Marks can be read. A well-constructed lie is still required to account for them.

The two episodes also differ in who was deceived. Jacob, a prophet, examined the shirt and refused the story. The ʿAzīz's household examined the shirt and accepted it. The difference is not intelligence but interest: the father had no motive to disbelieve his sons beyond the evidence, while the husband had every motive to avoid a scandal and still arrived at the correct finding. That is the strength of a physical test. It produces the same answer for a man who wants one result as for a man who wants another, and it does so quickly enough that the parties cannot reorganise their accounts around it. The chapter gives its most decisive verdict to a piece of cloth for exactly that reason.
