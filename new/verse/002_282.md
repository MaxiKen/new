## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:282

> O believers! When you contract a loan for a fixed period of time, commit it to writing. Let the scribe maintain justice between the parties. The scribe should not refuse to write as Allah has taught them to write. They will write what the debtor dictates, bearing Allah in mind and not defrauding the debt. If the debtor is incompetent, weak, or unable to dictate, let their guardian dictate for them with justice. Call upon two of your men to witness. If two men cannot be found, then one man and two women of your choice will witness—so if one of the women forgets the other may remind her. The witnesses must not refuse when they are summoned. You must not be against writing ˹contracts˺ for a fixed period—whether the sum is small or great. This is more just ˹for you˺ in the sight of Allah, and more convenient to establish evidence and remove doubts. However, if you conduct an immediate transaction among yourselves, then there is no need for you to record it, but call upon witnesses when a deal is finalized. Let no harm come to the scribe or witnesses. If you do, then you have gravely exceeded ˹your limits˺. Be mindful of Allah, for Allah ˹is the One Who˺ teaches you. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Longest Verse**

This is the longest single verse in the Qur'an — a fact the tradition states with the gravity of a marker: the Book that runs from the Fātiḥah's seven short āyāt to its own final page reserves its maximum extension for the documentation of a debt. The commentators give the verse its occasion and its range together: it is said to have been revealed in connection with the agricultural forward sales practiced in Medina — payment now for harvests deliverable one, two, or three years out — but it applies generally to every deferred agreement, loans included: wherever payment and delivery do not meet in the same moment, the verse's machinery engages. The Prophetic rule the tradition attaches to the forward sale states the verse's minimum content: whoever contracts a forward sale should fix a known amount, a known measure, and a known term. The forward sale itself is the verse's exhibited exception to the wider prohibition on selling what one does not yet possess — permitted, al-Qurṭubī explains, because the farmer genuinely needs the funds for seed and care while his wealth stands in the ground in a yearly cycle, and because the social benefit outweighs the transaction's risk. The longest verse legislates, in other words, the longest transactions: the ones that stretch across years and therefore across the failures of memory, good faith, and life itself.

**Write It Down**

The command at the verse's head — *commit it to writing* — carries a precision the commentators draw out: the writing does not replace the living witness but serves the memory. Al-Qurṭubī states the legal hierarchy: in Islamic law the testimony is primarily oral and only secondarily written, and the document's function is to anchor the terms against the drift of recollection across the deferred period. The writing-command then generates the verse's first office: *let the scribe maintain justice between the parties* — the kātib positioned between the two as a third party with a virtue assigned, the bi'l-ʿadl justice being neither the creditor's amount nor the debtor's version but the transaction's own truth. And the office carries a duty and a dignity: *the scribe should not refuse to write as Allah has taught them to write*. The commentators read the refusal-ban as establishing contract-writing among the community's obligations — the jurists' classification is the communal duty, farḍ kifāyah: someone in the society must hold the skill and exercise it when called, though it is not every person's individual farḍ ʿayn. The clause's theology is the verse's quietest gift: the scribe writes *as Allah has taught him* — literacy itself named as a divine instruction (the same teaching-verb the verse's closing will reclaim), which makes the refused contract an ingratitude and the written one an act of the taught.

**The Debtor Dictates**

The verse's dictate-clause assigns the pen's source: *they will write what the debtor dictates, bearing Allah in mind and not defrauding the debt*. The choice of dictator is deliberate and the commentators explain it as protective arithmetic: the debtor — the party who owes, including the forward-sale's farmer-seller — speaks the terms because the creditor is more liable to describe a higher amount, and because the seller in these transactions is typically the weaker party, the one whose assets are non-liquid and whose need generated the contract. The dictation is bound to two restraints: the fear of God as Lord, and the fraud-ban — nothing diminished from what is actually owed. Al-Qurṭubī reads the assignment as the verse's balance-of-power device: the document's content comes from the mouth of the party with the incentive to understate, under an oath-like charge, in the presence of witnesses who will hear any understatement — the two possible frauds (creditor's inflation, debtor's diminution) checked by giving the pen to one and the ears to all. The incompetence-clause extends the protection to the dictator who cannot dictate: the feeble-minded, the minor, the one incapacitated by illness (the exegetes' three named cases) receives a guardian to dictate justly in his place — the verse's care reaching the parties the transaction would otherwise leave voiceless.

**The Witnesses**

The witnessing-legislation is the verse's most discussed clause, and the discussion repays care. Two men are called as witnesses; *if two men cannot be found, then one man and two women of your choice* — the doubling attached to its rationale in the verse's own words: *so if one of the women forgets, the other may remind her*. The commentators establish first what the exegetes agree on: the provision is peculiar to the commercial testimony this verse governs and differs from the Qur'an's treatment of testimony elsewhere — the sūrah of Light explicitly equates husband and wife as testifiers in the gravest of accusations (24:6–9), and the classical tradition, while generally excluding women from the corporal-punishment and qiṣāṣ cases, treated even there as preference rather than incapacity, admitting women's testimony when no male witness existed. The erring named is forgetting — a lapse of memory in the contract's particulars, not a character deficiency — and the classical readings of the reminder's effect ran in two directions: the reminded woman becomes legally equal to the man, or the two women together stand equal to him, the latter the more widely held. The modern and classical commentators converge in refusing the intrinsic-inferiority reading, and their structural argument is the verse's own: nothing here excludes a transaction between two women with two men witnessing; women in Islamic law transact sales and loans independently, without witnesses at all, across the whole range of ordinary commerce. The provision addresses, rather, the specific transaction's expertise-demand — the from-among-those-whom-you-approve phrasing signals competence in a domain, and complex multi-year forward contracts were, in the verse's society, a domain women rarely worked in. Al-Qurṭubī adds the social reading: participation of this kind was itself a major change for the women of the time, and the doubled presence functions as protection against bullying or manipulation in a long-term financial proceeding — while Abū Bakr ibn al-ʿArabī's recorded puzzlement (why could not one man remind one woman if she erred? he could not answer it) testifies that the tradition itself never settled the clause into a verdict on women's minds. The witnesses' duty-clause closes the paragraph symmetrically with the scribe's: *the witnesses must not refuse when they are summoned*.

**Small or Great, Written**

The verse's next movement kills the objection the documentation-command invites — the merchant's instinct that small deals do not merit the machinery: *You must not be against writing contracts for a fixed period, whether the sum is small or great*. The commentators read the small-or-great clause as a discipline of memory rather than of amount: it is the small deferred transaction whose terms are least likely to be remembered and most likely to be disputed, and the aversion-to-writing the verse forbids is precisely the false economy that skips the record where the record is cheapest and the loss most corrosive. The three benefits the verse then names are its own cost-benefit analysis: *more just in the sight of Allah, more convenient to establish evidence, and more likely to remove doubts* — the divine register first (the document as an act of the justice God sees), then the evidentiary (the record as testimony's anchor), then the psychological (the doubt removed from both parties' future). The spot-transaction exception follows and confirms the rule's logic: where the trade is hand-to-hand, delivered and paid in one moment, no writing is required — there is no deferred term for memory to fail across — though the verse still calls for witnesses at the deal's conclusion. The whole clause is a jurisprudence of the interval: documentation scales with time, because time is what corrupts the unwritten term.

**No Harm to Scribe or Witness**

The verse's penultimate legislation protects its own machinery: *Let no harm come to the scribe or witnesses. If you do, then you have gravely exceeded your limits*. The third parties the contract requires — the literate one summoned to write, the upright ones summoned to attest — are placed under the parties' protection, and the harm-ban is backed by the transgression-verdict: harming them is the verse's word for crossing into wrong. The commentators read the clause against the social realities the machinery creates: the scribe who writes a term one party dislikes, the witness whose testimony may decide a dispute — both are exposed to the losers' pressure, and both must answer the summons without refusal. The protection makes the refusal-bans operable: the scribe and witnesses can be commanded to serve only where serving is safe. The verse's closing movement then gathers everything into the theology that has governed it from the first clause: *Be mindful of Allah, for Allah teaches you. And Allah has perfect knowledge of all things.* The teaching-verb returns — the scribe wrote as God taught him, and now the whole community is addressed as God's taught: the documentation, the justice, the witnessing, the protection are a curriculum, and the taqwā is its disposition. The Knower of all things seals the verse that legislated against the failures of human knowing: the document exists because memory fails and doubt arises, and it is sufficient because the knowledge it approximates is, at its source, perfect.
