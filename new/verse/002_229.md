## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:229

> Divorce may be retracted twice, then the husband must retain ˹his wife˺ with honour or separate ˹from her˺ with grace. It is not lawful for husbands to take back anything of the dowry given to their wives, unless the couple fears not being able to keep within the limits of Allah. So if you fear they will not be able to keep within the limits of Allah, there is no blame if the wife compensates the husband to obtain divorce. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not transgress them. And whoever transgresses the limits of Allah, they are the ˹true˺ wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Count That Ends the Cycle**

The verse's first clause installs the divorce-code's fundamental number: the divorce is twice. A couple can reconcile after a first declarative divorce and after a second; the third ends the marriage past recall — that third declaration being referred to either by this verse's release-in-grace or by the next verse's if he divorces her (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī). The count's target is the pre-Islamic abuse the surrounding verses keep circling: the man who divorced and revoked, revoked and divorced, holding his wife in serial waiting-periods — married in law, abandoned in fact, freed never. By numbering the declarations, the verse converts divorce from an instrument of indefinite control into a process with a terminus: each declaration costs the husband one of two revocable chances, and the third is the door that does not reopen. The legislation's deterrent logic is arithmetic: a man contemplating casual divorce now knows that every casual declaration spends an irreplaceable opportunity, and the husband who would use the ṭalāq as repeated punishment exhausts his power to punish precisely where the verse intends — at the third word.

**Retention in Honor, Release in Grace**

Between the counts the verse states the marriage's only two lawful postures: retain honorably — in maʿrūf — or release virtuously — in iḥsān. The formula is the divorce-code's ethical constitution. Retention in honor is the marriage maintained: the wife kept not as captive or servant but in the honorable exchange the previous verse legislated — the reciprocal rights and duties, the adornment Ibn ʿAbbās described as mutual, the closeness and peace al-Rāzī named. Release in grace is the marriage ended well: the divorce declared without spite, the rights rendered, the departure dignified, the ex-wife sent out with what the law assigns her rather than stripped of it. And the formula excludes the third posture — the retention in harm, the release in vengeance, the suspended state between marriage and divorce — which is exactly what the īlāʾ-verses abolished and 2:231 will legislate against by name. The two options together make every moment of a marriage's life and death a choice between two graces: how it is kept, or how it is released. The verse leaves no middle term because the middle is where the harm lives — the neither-kept-nor-released existence that the whole code exists to make impossible.

**The Bridewealth Not Taken Back**

The third clause legislates the divorce's economics in the wife's favor: it is not lawful for you to take anything of what you have given them. The bridewealth — the mahr stipulated in the marriage contract — is the wife's property absolutely, and the husband-initiated divorce leaves it untouched: in the ṭalāq, the wife everything the wife owns stays hers. The clause's exception is narrow and specific: a marriage not consummated allows the return of half the stipulated amount (as 2:237 will legislate). And the clause carries an anti-abuse rule the commentators make explicit: the husband may not abuse his wife into purchasing her own release — refunding, through the manufactured "request," the mahr he had given — the pressure-divorce, in which cruelty manufactures the wife's "request" and converts it into a refund, is forbidden at its root. The economics complete the count's work: the man contemplating serial divorce now spends not only his revocable chances but nothing of his wife's wealth in the process — the declarations cost him opportunity, never her. The protection is total in its domain: what he gave at the marriage's founding remains hers at its ending, and the verse surrounds that remainder with the anti-coercion rule that keeps it real.

**The Khulʿ: The Wife's Door**

The exception-clause opens the code's other door: except where both parties fear the marriage cannot stay inside divine bounds — then no blame attaches to what she ransoms herself with. The khulʿ is the wife-initiated divorce, and its mechanism is the ransom: she returns some or all of the bridewealth to obtain her release. The commentators state the symmetry with unmatched clarity: the wife's property is material to the khulʿ procedure, while the husband's ṭalāq moves no property at all — the inverse of the marriage itself, where the wife collects the bridewealth as part of the contract and carries no financial responsibility within it. Put in the tradition's own formula, rephrased: the mahr is the price at which a man negotiates entry into marriage; the ransom is the price at which a woman negotiates her way out. The condition of the door is the fear named in the dual — that the two will not keep God's limits: the marriage's obligations of companionship and intimacy unsustainable, its prohibitions against abuse and adultery at risk. The jurists debated the fear's distribution: some require both spouses' fear; others hold the wife's fear sufficient while the husband's alone is not — if she wishes to remain and he does not, no khulʿ is permissible and he can take nothing of her property; others read the you-fear as the community's assessment of the pair, with some opinions granting the arbiters of 4:35 power to divorce or recommend divorce where reconciliation is impossible.

**The First Khulʿ**

The tradition preserves the door's first opening with documentary precision. The case is described as the first khulʿ in Islam: a woman brought her case to the Prophet, complaining of her husband — the reports carried by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī describe her aversion to his physical appearance, while Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī preserves her words in another register (5273, and Ibn ʿAbbās's account at 5275–5276): the wife of Thābit ibn Qays said — O Messenger of God, I do not blame Thābit for any defect in his character or his religion, but I, being a Muslim, dislike to behave in an un-Islamic manner if I remain with him; in one account, I cannot endure to live with him; in another, I fear I may become ungrateful for God's blessings. Her complaint was not of abuse but of aversion — the fear that a marriage without love would drive her to contempt, and contempt to sin. The Prophet's ruling established the mechanism: will you return the garden he gave you as your mahr? She said yes — in one account, yes, and more. His answer fixed the ransom's measure: no, the garden alone — and to Thābit: accept your garden, and divorce her once. The case legislated the door's whole character: the wife's aversion received as sufficient ground, her property as the ransom, the excess refused, the release commanded — the marriage dissolved without blame on either, in the precise economy the verse had laid down.

**The Limits and Their Transgression**

The verse closes with the ḥudūd-formula that governs the whole code: these are the limits of God — do not approach transgression; whoever transgresses the limits of God, those are the wrongdoers. The limits are the rules the verse has just legislated — the count of two, the retention and release, the bridewealth's inviolability, the khulʿ's conditions and ransom — and the formula's force is the same as the fasting-legislation's (2:187): the limits are established, named, and fenced with the divine ownership. The commentary tradition filled in the limits' rationale with social wisdom: a bad marriage, al-Rāzī notes, can lead to further evils — adultery, social instability — and a couple starved of companionship slide toward bitterness, then toward sin; the discord the khulʿ answers (the nushūz of the marital-strife passages) is read from the verse's dual form as coming from both sides, or as originating in the wife's expressed hatred and aversion — the two readings complementary, since her aversion, even without ill-treatment by the husband, produces his resentment in turn. And the ransom's ceiling was debated: some jurists limit it to the bridewealth's amount; others allow any figure, since the verse names none — though even these consider it shameful and reprehensible to accept more. The closing verdict gathers everything: the transgressor of these limits — the serial divorcer, the coercer of khulʿs, the taker-back of gifts, the ransomer beyond honor — is named a wrongdoer in the same breath as the limits are named God's, and the household's law stands, like the fast's and the war's, under the single sovereignty the sūrah legislates everywhere: limits revealed, transgression defined, and the wrongdoer known to the Knower of the wombs and the Hearer of the divorce-words.
