## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:79

> So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, “This is from Allah”—seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they have earned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence of Wayl**

The recital's diagnosis ends and its verdict begins, in the heaviest single word the Qur'an deploys against a crime of the pen. *Wayl* — woe — is an ordinary Arabic cry of ruin, the exclamation of one who has seen the bottom drop out, and the Qur'an uses it as a standing formula of condemnation across dozens of verses. Here it falls three times in two sentences: woe to those who write; woe for what their hands have written; woe for what they earn. The triple structure is itself the ruling — the crime is cursed in its commission, its product, and its proceeds, with no remainder left unjudged. Some commentators, drawing on a report the tradition itself flags as disputed — Ibn Kathīr among those who contested it — read *wayl* as a proper name: a valley in Hell so deep that a falling denier descends forty autumns before reaching its bottom, or a mountain of blood and pus, or the very cry the punished will utter when the word becomes their only language. Whether curse or toponym, the term's function is fixed: this is the recital's maximum sentence, and it is filed not against violence or disbelief in the open field but against forgery — the manufacture of false scripture for money.

**With Their Own Hands**

The phrase the verse builds on is physical: the Scripture distorted *with their own hands* — and the Arabic beneath the translation is more literal still, naming those who write the book with their hands. The emphasis is deliberate and it narrows the indictment to an act of production. Verse 75 charged distortion of a word heard; this verse charges authorship — hands putting ink where Allah put nothing. The phrase *with their own hands* strips the crime of every mediating excuse: not inherited error, not drift in transmission, not the entropy of copying, but original composition by identifiable people at identifiable desks. The exegetes preserve the historical forms the charge took: accounts of writings composed and pawned off on the unlettered for small profit, and the graver account — the alteration of the prophecies describing the coming Prophet, the very portraits lodged in the Torah (7:157) reshaped so that the descriptions would fit something else. The mechanism is the fullest form of the slant the sūrah has been tracking: first the meaning is rotated inside the text, then, when rotation is not enough, the hand writes a new page and the new page enters circulation wearing the old warranty. The verse's specificity is its universality: it is addressed to every age's forgers, and every age has produced candidates, inside every tradition that keeps its faith in books.

**“Then They Say: This Is from Allah”**

The writing alone is not the crime's summit; the attribution is. *Then they say, "This is from Allah"* — the manufactured page is issued with the divine warrant attached, and the lie acquires its full structure: not merely falsehood but falsehood sold as revelation. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the same act in the same terms and adds the aggravation: *"They say, 'It is from Allah'—but it is not from Allah. And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly"* (3:78). The theological gravity is in the direction of the lie. To deceive a person is to wrong a person; to speak in Allah's name without Allah is to enlist the divine authority itself as the instrument of the wrong, making God — in the eyes of the deceived — the sponsor of the fabrication. The buyer of the forged page does not merely hold an error; he holds an error he believes Allah guaranteed, which immunizes it against every future correction: to question the text becomes, for him, to question God. This is why the recital ranks the crime above the distortions before it. Distortion slants a truth still present; authored attribution replaces the truth with a counterfeit wearing omnipotence as its seal — and the counterfeit's victims defend it with the sincerity real faith deserves.

**The Fleeting Gain**

The clause of commerce supplies the motive and completes the indictment: the distortion is done *seeking a fleeting gain*. The forgery is an industry — composition, warranty, sale — and the Qur'an files it under the sūrah's running economics of the sacred, which began at verse 41 with signs exchanged for a cheap price and will recur wherever the recital weighs spiritual capital against worldly cash. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān joins the two crimes in one sentence: *"Allah took the covenant of those who were given the Scripture to make it known to people and not hide it, yet they cast it behind their backs and traded it for a fleeting gain"* (3:187) — the same custodians, the same covenant to disclose, the same small money on the other side of the scale. The phrase *fleeting gain* is the verse's own commentary on the transaction's arithmetic: an infinite authority, borrowed without right, liquidated for cash that will not outlive the seller. The tradition's historical gloss — writings sold to the unlettered of Arabia at small profit, the altered prophecies preserving a position and its revenues — keeps the motive concrete: this is not error seeking truth but truth's costume seeking income. And the price being paltry does not soften the verdict; it sharpens it. The recital's repeated astonishment is that the sacred is sold cheap — that hands which have touched the warranty of heaven settle for so little.

**Capital and Proceeds, Both Cursed**

The second sentence divides the ruin into its two accounts: woe for *what their hands have written*, and woe for *what they have earned*. The writing and the earning are cursed separately, and the separation carries a ruling. The product bears guilt even where the sale fails — the forged page is wayl's object before anyone buys it; the proceeds bear guilt even where the writing went unchallenged — income from false revelation corrupts what it touches, feeding households, purchasing standing, seeding influence from a poisoned root. The doubling also closes a circuit with the verse before it: the illiterate of verse 78 knew nothing of the Book but hearsay, and this verse shows what the hearsay was made of — pages written by hands, warranted by tongues, sold to people who could not check. Producer and market, capital and demand, in one economy of fabrication, and the woe falls on the supply side where the intelligence lives. The economic precision is the verse's enduring edge for its own community: religious income is audited by its source. What is earned by carrying a true revelation is provision; what is earned by writing a false one is wayl — and no volume of sincere buyers converts the second into the first.

**The Standing Indictment**

The verse was revealed about forgers in Madina's Scripture-market and has never since lacked addressees. Every tradition that keeps its faith in texts breeds the verse's crime: fabricated reports composed for patrons, rulings purchased, histories written to order and warranted with the sacred name — and the Islamic tradition's own response to verse 79 is its proudest institutional achievement. The sciences built to police attribution — the criticism of transmission chains, the grading of narrators, the discipline that made a report's warrant a researchable object rather than an asserted one — exist because this verse exists: a revealed immune system against the forgery of the sacred, developed by a community that took the woe personally and spent centuries engineering the checks the illiterate of verse 78 never had. The verse thereby reads in three registers at once: a historical indictment of specific hands, a permanent law of religious economics in which the counterfeit's price is always paltry and its woe never partial, and a standing instruction to every generation to keep the question verse 76's conspirators feared alive in their own houses — what Allah has actually revealed, distinguished by evidence from what hands have written and tongues have warranted. The organs named in the two sentences, the hand and the tongue, remain the only instruments of the crime, and the audit of both remains open.
