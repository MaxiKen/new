## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:79

> So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, “This is from Allah”—seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they have earned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Woes in One Sentence**

The verse repeats its word of warning twice, once for the writing and once for the profit, and it names no punishment beyond the word itself. The Arabic term behind it appears throughout the Book as a cry of ruin reserved for conduct that has passed beyond ordinary correction, and its doubling inside a single sentence is emphatic enough that the commentators paused over it.

The sentence's shape repays attention. It states the act, then the attribution, then the motive, and then returns to the act and the motive in reverse order. The Book uses this symmetry when it wants the reader to hold two things at once: the distortion and the reason it was undertaken.

**Distortion by Hand**

The offence is described physically. A person works the scripture over with his own hands and then presents the result as revelation. The Book does not accuse this community of losing its book or of failing to transmit it; the charge is that material was handled, altered, and then labelled, and the labelling is the crime.

The verse from the chapter of the women speaks of those who "distort words from their proper places" and then quotes their own speech back at them, and the chapter of the table states that a party of them twisted the words of their scripture and forgot part of what they had been commanded to uphold (5:13). The Book's vocabulary for this offence is consistent, and it always places the distortion in the hands of people who had access to the text.

**"This Is from Allah"**

The phrase the verse puts in quotation marks is short, and its brevity is what makes it damning. The forgery is not published as a commentary or a tradition; it is stamped with the highest attribution available in that community. To say of one's own work that God said it is the offence the sūrah will name again when it warns that a person who invents a lie about God will not succeed (10:69).

The Book treats this offence with particular severity because of what it does to everyone downstream. A lie about God does not stay with its author; it is believed, repeated, acted upon, and eventually defended as inherited truth. The illiterate community of the previous verse, knowing nothing except what it had been told, is the standing proof of the damage.

**The Fleeting Gain**

The motive named is a passing return, and the Book uses the same description wherever a divine trust is exchanged for worldly advantage: those who conceal what has been revealed for a price (2:174), those who trade the covenant of God for a fraction of a price (3:77), and those who sell the signs of God cheaply (9:9). The word describing the gain is the key to the verse's judgment. The Book does not say the profit was worthless in the market; it says the transaction was absurd, because the commodity being sold was a trust whose value cannot be expressed in the currency being accepted for it.

That is why the sentence reads as a failure of proportion as much as a failure of honesty, and why the second cry of woe attaches to what they earned. The Book consistently measures conduct by what it produces in the end rather than by what it delivers at the moment, and no example makes the contrast plainer than this one.

**The Two Woes and How Readers Handled Them**

Commentators discussed whether the doubled cry refers to a single punishment or two, and a number of reports attach the word to a place or a state rather than to a duration. The safest reading is the one the Book's own usage supports: the term is a threat of ruin, its repetition matches the two clauses it stands over, and speculation about the geography of the hereafter does not help a reader understand the verse.

What the repetition achieves is emphasis without detail. The Book often names a sin and leaves the sentencing to a later passage, and the readers of this sūrah meet the full account of the weighing of deeds two hundred verses further on. The verse's task is to fix the offence in the memory, and the double cry performs that task better than a description would.

**Where Responsibility Is Placed in This Passage**

The previous verse described the unlettered who never knew their book. This one describes the people who supplied them with material and called it revelation. Read together, the two sentences describe a system: a small group controls the text, a large group receives summaries, and the summaries are then presented as the word of God.

The sūrah's habit in such passages is to hold the authors more accountable than the misled, and the following verses continue with a community that was given a book and a covenant. The order in which the charges are arranged, from the ignorance of the many to the industry of the few, is itself an argument about where the corruption of a community begins.

**The Question the Verse Leaves Open**

Nothing in the sentence says the entire scripture of this community was fabricated, and the Book affirms elsewhere that the Torah and the Gospel were given by God and contain guidance and light (5:44–46). What is condemned here is a practice carried out by identifiable people for an identifiable return, and the sūrah continues to address the scripture given to this people as something to be upheld and confirmed.

That distinction governs how the passage should be read. A reader who concludes that the offence described here condemns everyone born into that community has read more than the verse says, and the sūrah declares in the surrounding passages that some of them are upright (3:113). The verdict belongs to the forgers, and the verse names them precisely.
