## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:125

> And ˹remember˺ when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people ˹saying˺, “˹You may˺ take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer.” And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, who meditate in it, and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Place of Return and a Place of Safety**

The verse states the House's dual institution, and the two terms are its whole social theology. *A centre* — *mathāba*, the place of return, the point people come back to again and again: the word names not a location but a gravitational function, the station that draws repeated approach, that no visit exhausts and no pilgrim leaves for good. *A sanctuary* — *amn*, safety: the precinct where the hunted is safe, where the vendetta stops at the boundary. Al-Ṭabarī records the sanctuary's pre-Islamic operation: even a killer stood secure within its bounds, a practice the tradition reads as the continuation, in Arabian custom, of the status the House received at its institution. The two functions answer the two needs the House serves. The return-function serves worship's repeatability — a faith needs a point its body can revisit, a direction its face can find, a journey its calendar can schedule; the safety-function serves worship's precondition — the house of God must be a place where God's creatures are beyond reach of each other's violence. The pairing also reads against the verse two stations back: the masājid barred and ruined (114) were houses whose return-function was blocked and whose sanctuary-function was violated, and verse 125 files the original pattern those crimes inverted — the House made for return, made safe, owned by the One whose property verse 114 defended.

**The Standing-Place of Abraham**

Between the two institutions stands a specific commandment: *take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer*. The maqām — the standing-place — is identified in the tradition first as the physical station: the stone near the Kaʿbah said to bear Abraham's footprint, held in its enclosure beside the House to this day. The exegetes record wider readings — the maqām as the ḥajj-rites in their entirety, or as the pilgrimage's other stations, ʿArafāt, Minā, Muzdalifah — and the readings share a claim: whatever Abraham stood on or stood for in the House's service is consecrated as prayer-ground. The tradition preserves the commandment's occasion in two accounts. In one, the Prophet ﷺ took ʿUmar by the hand at the stone and said, *this is the standing-place of Abraham*; ʿUmar asked, *shall we take it as a place of prayer?*; the Prophet ﷺ answered that he had not been commanded to — and before the day's sunset the verse descended with the command. In the account ʿUmar himself narrated, preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, he wished aloud to the Prophet ﷺ, *I wish we took the station of Abraham as our praying place* — and the revelation came: *and take the station of Abraham as a place of prayer* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 402), one of the three matters in which ʿUmar reported his Lord agreed with him. The two accounts differ in who spoke first, and the difference is immaterial to the verse's fact: the prayer-site at the patriarch's stone is legislated here, in a form — *you may take* — that some exegetes read as imperative and others as declarative, the community's practice settling the reading. The stone's function completes the verse's Abrahamic architecture: the imām of verse 124 leaves a footprint, and the footprint's place becomes the community's prayer-ground — leadership followed literally, at the point of standing.

**“Purify My House”**

The verse's second half records the charge laid on the builders: *We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House*. The covenant-verb — *ʿahidnā* — makes the purification a commissioned trust, and al-Ṭabarī specifies the content: cleansing the House from idolatry and from doubt. The two objects of purification define the masjid's negative and positive conditions — no false object of worship inside it, no false posture of heart toward it — and the charge's tense carries its history: for Abraham, purification meant maintaining the House in the state it was instituted in, a standing custodianship; the tradition then records the custody's long erosion, the idols' arrival, and the restoration — the Prophet ﷺ returning the House to its Abrahamic condition at the conquest, the charge fulfilled a second time by the descendant of its first trustees. The purification-clause also binds this verse to verse 114 with a knot the recital ties deliberately: the House's existence is for the circlers, the meditators, the bowing and prostrating — and whoever fills it with anything else, or bars anyone from these, is charged with the ruin the earlier verse sentenced. *My House* — the possessive is the clause's warrant: the purification standard is not the trustees' preference but the Owner's purpose, and the purpose is named in the three classes the charge serves.

**The Three Classes of Worshipper**

The House's beneficiaries are listed in three participles, and the list covers the body's whole liturgical range. *Those who circle it* — the circumambulators, the ṭawāf whose walking orbit makes the House the fixed center of a moving devotion, the rite that is the pilgrimage's signature. *Those who meditate in it* — the *ʿākifūn*, from a root meaning both devoted-to and resident-in: the retreaters, whose worship is stationary presence. The tradition built an institution on the word: *iʿtikāf*, the practice of spending set days inside the mosque in prayer and remembrance, established by the Prophet's ﷺ own retreats in the last ten nights of Ramaḍān, and behind it the Sufi discipline of seclusion — the *khalwah* whose genealogy the exegetes trace to this participle. *Those who bow and prostrate* — the prayer's two defining movements, the liturgy in its daily form. The three classes are the House's complete population: the circling movement, the abiding stillness, the rhythmic prayer — procession, retreat, and worship gathered in one precinct, each class served by the purification, each class's exclusion a violation of the charge. The list also quietly legislates the House's schedule: it is not a monument visited but a masjid inhabited, and its trustees' duty is measured against the three forms of occupancy the Owner names.

**Father and Son, Jointly Entrusted**

The charge names two trustees, and the second name is the verse's argument to its audience: *Abraham and Ishmael*. The patriarch of verse 124 and the son whose line the Arabian hearers claimed are joined in one custodianship — the House built and purified by both, its covenant held by both, its prayer-ground marked by one and legislated for all. The joint trusteeship does precise polemical work in the recital's Madinan moment. The Israelite claim ran through Isaac's line exclusively; the Arabian claim ran through Ishmael's; the verse files the House as the two brothers' common work, under their common Father's God, which makes the House older than the division and superior to both claims. The purification-charge addressed to both also states the recital's covenant-law from verse 124 in operative form: the office descends through righteousness held jointly, not through lineage held exclusively — and the wrongdoer's exclusion the patriarch heard applies to every trustee of every generation, of either line. For the Muslims hearing the sūrah — a community praying toward this House within months of the verse's revelation, their qibla legislation about to arrive — the verse installed their direction's full pedigree: the House they faced was not a Makkah landmark but a divine institution, made center and sanctuary, charged to two prophets, purified for three classes of worshipper, its patriarch's footprint legislated as their prayer-site. The community's newest practice was given the oldest title-deed in the recital's possession.
