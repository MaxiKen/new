## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:125

> And ˹remember˺ when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people ˹saying˺, “˹You may˺ take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer.” And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, who meditate in it, and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The House as a Centre**

The verse describes the Sacred House as a place to which people return, and the Arabic term describes both the return and the gathering, so that the House is a meeting point for the community as well as a destination for pilgrims. The Book calls it the first house established for humanity, and it places the site in a valley which the sūrah's later verses describe as barren when Abraham settled his family there.

The security of the place is stated in the same sentence, and the commentators noted that the Kaʿbah served as a sanctuary in the pre-Islamic period itself, where a person who had committed a killing could find safety. The Book presents the honour granted to the site as something that preceded the community's own traditions and that continued them.

**The Standing-Place of Abraham**

The instruction that the standing-place of Abraham may be taken as a site of prayer refers to a location near the Kaʿbah marked by a stone that tradition associates with Abraham's footprints. Commentators also discussed whether the phrase refers to the pilgrimage rites as a whole or to another location, and the difference of opinion shows that the identification was not fixed for every reader.

A report recorded in the commentaries describes the Prophet ﷺ taking ʿUmar by the hand and identifying the spot, and ʿUmar asking whether it should be taken as a place of prayer, after which the verse is said to have come down. The reports matter for the way the community received the instruction, and the verse itself states the permission in a form that the Muslim rites use to this day.

**Purifying the House**

The command given to Abraham and Ishmael is to purify the House, and the commentators explained purification as the removal of idols, images, and the practices of polytheism. That reading gives the verse a specific historical content, since the House in later centuries was filled with images, and an account in the sūrah's early verses describes the community's obstruction of worship there.

The task entrusted to the two men therefore included both building and cleansing, and the Book presents them as the founders of a place of pure worship which later generations were to restore to its original purpose. The verse states the intention of the House rather than its furniture: what is dedicated to God is to be kept free of what does not belong to Him. The commission also places both men in the role of custodians rather than owners. The House is described as belonging to God in the same sentence, and what the two were asked to do was keep it free of what would compromise that dedication, which is the task the sūrah's later community inherits.

**Those Who Circle, Meditate, and Bow**

The sentence names three groups for whom the House is purified, and the descriptions correspond to the rites performed there. Circling the House is the practice of walking around the Kaʿbah, and bowing and prostration are the movements of the prayer.

The middle term describes those who remain in place, devoted to the House without leaving, and the practice of retreat in the mosque during the last nights of Ramadan is derived from the same root. The Book's mention of the practice in connection with Abraham suggests that the community's devotional life there was continuous, and the commentators noted that the three categories cover the range of worship offered in the sanctuary. The three descriptions also show the range of worship the House was purified to receive. Circling belongs to the pilgrimage, prostration to the prayer, and the middle term to the devotion that keeps a person in place, and together they cover what the Book presents as the sanctuary's purpose.

**The Practice of Retreat**

The Prophet ﷺ spent the final ten days of Ramadan in the mosque, and the practice established from his example became a recognized act of worship in Islamic law. Later readers also connected the same term with the spiritual retreat practised by some Sufi orders, and both uses derive from the sense of remaining in a place for the sake of devotion.

The verse's mention of the practice in the context of Abraham's commission indicates that the Book treats the House as a place of settled devotion as well as of pilgrimage. The reader is therefore given a picture of the sanctuary's intended use rather than only its construction. The Book's mention of those who remain in the House also indicates that the sanctuary was intended for settled devotion and not only for the rites of pilgrimage. The practice that grew out of the Prophet's example during Ramadan belongs to that understanding, and the verse's list places it alongside the circling and the prayer.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence links three things: the honour of the House, the prayer offered at the standing-place of Abraham, and the purity of the worship conducted there. It also places the Kaʿbah at the centre of the argument the sūrah is conducting about the direction of prayer, since the verses that follow describe Abraham's prayers for the city and its people.

The Book's use of Abraham in this passage is careful. He is presented not as the founder of a national shrine but as a servant who built, purified, and prayed, and both he and his son are described as asking God to accept the work. The next verses give the content of their prayers, and they supply the community's own words about what they hoped the House would become.

**The House and the Argument About Direction**

The passage about the House stands at the centre of the sūrah's discussion of prayer, and the two subjects are connected in the argument rather than only in geography. The Book has established that the sanctuary was built and purified by Abraham, that its security and provision were granted in answer to his prayer, and that its rites were appointed in response to his request. When the sūrah later addresses the direction of prayer and the objections raised about it, the reader already knows who founded the place and on what basis. What the verse supplies is therefore not merely a historical note but the ground of the claim that the House belongs to the religion of Abraham. The communities that disputed the direction were disputing about a building whose origin the Book has just traced, and the account of the two builders and their prayers is intended to settle the matter before the objection is stated.
