## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:63

> Do you find it astonishing that a reminder should come to you from your Lord through one of your own, warning you, so you may beware and perhaps be shown mercy?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Astonishment at the Reminder**

The verse records the appeal of Noah to his people's sense of wonder, and the wonder it addresses is that a reminder should reach them through a man of their own community. The Book reports the same astonishment in several passages, and the sūrah of Qāf states that the deniers are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves (50:2), while the sūrah of Ṣād reports the same reaction and the accusation that follows it (38:4). The verse in front of us places the astonishment inside the narrative and answers it.

The phrasing of the appeal presents the objection as unreasonable rather than as a difficulty to be resolved. The messenger does not argue for his own fitness; he asks why the community should be surprised at the arrangement. The Book's treatment of the objection elsewhere connects it to the desire for a messenger of higher worldly standing, and the sūrah of the Cattle reports the demand that the message should have come to a notable of one of the two towns.

**A Man From Among Yourselves**

The phrase names the feature of the arrangement that the people found hardest to accept, and the Book presents the mission of human messengers as a deliberate choice. The sūrah of the Prophets states that the messengers sent before the Prophet were men to whom revelation came and that they ate food and walked in the markets, and the sūrah of the Cattle reports the objection of the notables that the messenger was a man like themselves. The verse in front of us states the same fact in the form of an appeal.

The choice of a messenger from the community serves the delivery of the message, and the Book states that every messenger was sent in the language of his people (14:4). The messenger's share in the life of his community means that the warning arrives without the barrier of a foreign tongue or a foreign custom, and the appeal in front of us treats that as a mercy rather than as a defect. The Book's later passages state the principle that the messengers were drawn from their own peoples.

**The Warning and Its Purpose**

The verse states that the messenger comes to warn, and the Book attaches the same purpose to the sending of the messengers throughout. The sūrah's opening verses described the Book as sent down so that the Prophet might warn by it (7:2), and the sūrah of the Family of Imran states that the messenger's duty is to deliver and to warn. The verse in front of us states the warning as the content of the mission, and the two clauses that follow describe its purpose.

The first purpose is that the people may beware, and the second is that they may receive mercy. The two are stated in sequence, and the Book presents the warning as the route to the mercy rather than as its opposite. The sūrah of the Ant reports the appeal of Ṣāliḥ to his people asking why they seek to hasten the torment rather than the grace that is before them (27:46), and the verse in front of us states the two outcomes in the order in which the messenger offers them.

**The Repetition of the Appeal in the Sūrah**

The appeal in front of us is repeated in the sūrah's later narratives, and Hūd's words to his people use almost the same form (7:69). The repetition is one of the features that ties the accounts together, and it presents the messengers as making the same offer to different communities. The Book's use of the same phrasing in its accounts of the prophets shows that the offer was not tailored to the people who received it.

The sūrah's arrangement makes the repetition part of the argument. The communities that were destroyed had heard the warning in the same terms as the audience in Makkah, and the sūrah's reader is placed in the position of a community that is being offered what the earlier peoples refused. The appeal in front of us supplies the offer in the mouth of the first messenger.

**The Mercy at the End of the Warning**

The verse closes with the possibility of mercy, and the Book attaches mercy to the response of the communities throughout its accounts. The sūrah's earlier verse on the response of the believers states that those who mend their conduct and are mindful have nothing to fear and will not grieve (7:35), and the sūrah's later narratives show what became of the communities that refused. The mercy in front of us is stated as a possibility rather than as a certainty, and the wording leaves the outcome with the people who hear the warning.

The Book's treatment of mercy as the purpose of the sending appears in the sūrah of the Prophets, which states that the Prophet was sent as a mercy to the worlds (21:107), and in the sūrah of the Stories, which describes the mission of Moses as a mercy for the people. This verse states the same purpose in the appeal of the first messenger, and the account that follows shows the community's response.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands sixty-third in the chapter, and it completes the speech of Noah before the report of the denial. The verse that follows states that the people rejected him and that God saved him and those with him in the ark while drowning the deniers (7:64), and the first of the sūrah's punishment accounts reaches its end. The verse here states the last appeal that the community refused.

The reader who has followed the narrative from the call through the charge and the reply now hears the final invitation. The reminder has come through one of the people, the warning has been given for their benefit, and the mercy has been offered; and the verse that follows records what the community did with all three.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that the people rejected the messenger, that God saved him and those with him in the ark, and that He drowned those who denied the signs, describing them as a blind people (7:64). The appeal of the messenger in front of us is followed by the community's refusal and by the outcome that the sūrah's introduction has promised, and the next account begins after that.

The two verses together close the first narrative of the series. The messenger offers the warning and the mercy, the community rejects both, and the destruction follows; and the verse that follows states the rescue of the believers and the blindness of the people who would not see.
