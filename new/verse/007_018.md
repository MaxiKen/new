## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:18

> Allah said, “Get out of Paradise! You are disgraced and rejected! I will certainly fill up Hell with you and your followers all together.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Sentence**

The verse reports the answer given to the vow, and the answer repeats the expulsion in stronger terms and adds the destination of those who follow the tempter. The Book's other accounts of the scene contain the same elements: the sūrah of the Rocky Tract reports the command to leave in disgrace and the curse that rests on Iblīs until the day of judgment (15:34–35), and the sūrah of Ṣād reports the same sentence with the further statement that Hell will be filled with him and with those who follow him among the condemned (38:77–78; 38:85).

The narrative therefore answers the plan of the previous verse with the statement of its outcome. The adversary who has announced his campaign is told where the campaign ends, and the verse names both him and his followers in the destination. The sūrah's warnings about the protectors taken in place of God are given their final term in this sentence.

**Disgrace and Rejection**

The two terms of the sentence describe the condition of the one who is driven out. Disgrace covers the loss of standing, and rejection describes the separation from mercy and from the company of the obedient. The Book uses the language of expulsion and curse for the adversary in each of its accounts of the scene, and the sūrah of the Rocky Tract states that the curse will remain on him until the day of judgment (15:35).

The contrast with the honour that Adam received in the same narrative is the point of the arrangement. The angels were commanded to prostrate before the first human being, and the one who refused to bow was driven out. The text places the two outcomes side by side, and the sūrah's account of the temptation that follows shows the expelled one at work within the Garden.

**The Filling of Hell**

The verse states that Hell will be filled with Iblīs and with those who follow him, and the Book uses the same declaration in the accounts of the scene and in its descriptions of the judgment. The sūrah of Ṣād gives the statement as the conclusion of the account of Adam's creation (38:85), the sūrah of Hūd reports the word that Hell will be filled with the jinn and with humanity together (11:119), and the sūrah of the Prostration states that the word will be fulfilled and that Hell will be filled with the jinn and with people.

The declaration establishes the scale of the outcome, and it places the followers of the adversary inside the sentence that was passed on him. The Book's descriptions of the day include the gathering of the deniers with their leaders into the Fire, and the sūrah of the Heights will describe the entry of the communities into it one after another in its later verses.

**Following the Adversary**

The verse names those who follow Iblīs as the companions of his destination, and the Book's treatment of following is one of the sūrah's central themes. The command of the third verse is to follow what has been sent down and not to take protectors besides God, and the sūrah's later passages describe the people who followed their ancestors, their leaders, and their own desires. The verse in front of us states the end of one such following at the head of the chapter.

The Book's contrast between the two paths is developed throughout the sūrah's narratives. The prophets call their peoples to follow the revelation, and the peoples answer that they follow what they found their fathers upon. The verse in front of us names the consequence of that answer, and it places the consequence in the sentence passed on the one whom they followed.

**The Mercy That Remains**

The sūrah's narrative of the first human pair will end with their repentance and with the statement that God is forgiving and merciful (7:23), and the contrast between the two sentences is one of the chapter's lessons. The one who argued and asked for time is expelled; the pair who admit their wrong and ask forgiveness are received. The sūrah's later passages apply the same distinction to the communities and to the audience.

The Book's treatment of the adversary's despair appears in its accounts of the condemned who ask for another chance at the moment of punishment, and the sūrah of the Heights itself will describe that request in its later verses. The verse in front of us states the sentence as it was passed on the one who made no such request at the time when a request would have been of use.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands eighteenth in the chapter, and it concludes the exchange that began with the prostration. The verses that follow begin the account of Adam and his wife in the Garden, their temptation by the adversary who has just been sentenced, and the exposure of their nakedness. The sentence in front of us states the position of the tempter at the moment the temptation begins.

The reader of the sūrah has been given the creation of the first human being, the honour conferred on him, the refusal of the adversary, his argument, his sentence, his petition, his vow, and his destination. The narrative then turns to the human pair and to the test that the sūrah's instruction to the children of Adam is designed to prepare them for.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the command given to Adam to dwell with his wife in the Garden, to eat from wherever they wish, and to avoid the tree, with the warning that approaching it would make them wrongdoers (7:19). The narrative in front of us has stated the enemy and his intention, and the verse that follows states the liberty and the single prohibition that the first pair were given.

The two verses together set the scene for the temptation. The adversary is sentenced and remains at work, and the human pair are given a wide permission with one boundary. The sūrah then recounts the temptation, the fall, and the repentance in sequence, and the chapter's teaching about the children of Adam begins from that account.
