## Sūrah an-Naml 27:43

> But she had been hindered by what she used to worship instead of Allah, for she was indeed from a disbelieving people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What Was Blocking Her**

The verse names the obstacle precisely: *mā kānat taʿbudu min dūni Allāh* — what she used to worship instead of God. Not her pride, not her throne, not her advisors. The sun.

Al-Ṭabarī's reading is the plain one: her worship of the sun prevented her from worshipping God properly. The verb *ṣaddahā*, she was barred, is the same verb the hoopoe used in verse 24 when he said Satan had barred them from the way. The chapter is consistent about the mechanism. An existing object of worship does not leave a vacancy; it occupies the position, and the occupation is what has to be cleared before anything else can be placed there. This is why Sulaymān's letter demanded submission rather than argument, and why the hoopoe's speech began by denying before it affirmed.

**A Past Tense That Matters**

The Arabic construction *mā kānat taʿbudu* describes a habitual practice in the past — what she used to worship. By the time this sentence is spoken, the worship is being described as something behind her.

That tense is doing quiet work in the narrative. The verse is not saying she is currently blocked; it is explaining why she had not arrived sooner. Read alongside the previous verse, where knowledge is said to have come before the miracle, the two statements fit together into an account of a slow approach: she had reasoned her way toward something, and an inherited religion had held her short of it. The Qur'an is describing a delay rather than a refusal, which is precisely what distinguishes her from Pharaoh's court. They knew and refused. She was hindered, and the hindrance is named as an external inheritance rather than an act of will.

**"From a Disbelieving People"**

The verse's final clause locates the problem in her origin: she was of a people who did not believe. This is the only place in her narrative where the Qur'an offers an explanation of her condition, and the explanation is social rather than personal.

Nobody chooses the religion they are born into. A queen of Sheba inherited sun worship the way she inherited the throne, as the established fact of her world, and the verse says so without softening and without excusing. The Qur'an holds people responsible for what they do with what reaches them, and it is consistently willing to name the starting position as a starting position. The same Book records the Quraysh objecting that they found their fathers on a path and were following it — there the plea is rejected, because a messenger had come and been refused. Here the circumstance is stated as the cause of a delay that ends the moment the case is properly put to her.

**Her Failing Is Named Without Contempt**

Compare how the chapter has spoken of its two failing courts. Pharaoh's people were *corruptors* who denied wrongfully and arrogantly, having known the truth. She is described as hindered, and the thing that hindered her is given the active verb while she takes the passive.

That grammatical distribution is not accidental. Nothing in this verse calls her arrogant, deceitful or corrupt, and the chapter has just spent four verses displaying her judgment, her consultation and her honesty under questioning. What it faults is one thing, stated once, in a single clause. The Qur'an is capable of severity toward its characters and applies it where it applies; the restraint here is a judgment of its own. She had one error, it was serious, and it was the only one — which is the precondition for a narrative that ends in her own voice rather than in her destruction.

**Two Ways to Read This Verse's Speaker**

If the previous verse's second half was Sulaymān speaking, this verse continues him, and al-Ṭabarī reads it that way. If the queen was speaking there, then this verse is the Qur'an's own narration, breaking in to explain her position.

The second option is the more natural for this particular sentence. The description is in the third person, it assesses her from outside, and it supplies a causal account of her past that a speaker would not ordinarily produce about herself in a foreign court. Al-Zamakhsharī's minority reading of the previous verse and this narratorial reading of the present one fit together into a coherent whole: she states what she has come to know, and the text explains what had kept her from it. Either way the content is the same, and the narrative is now positioned for its final move. The obstacle has been named. What remains is the moment that removes it, and it arrives in the next verse through something as ordinary as a floor.

**Worship as an Occupied Position**

The image behind *ṣadda* is physical obstruction — something standing in a road. Applied to worship, it produces a claim about how belief actually works that the chapter has been making since its fourth verse.

A person is not a blank surface waiting for a true doctrine to be written on it. Whatever place in the self is reserved for ultimate loyalty is already filled, by the sun, by ancestry, by wealth, by the state, or by the self. This is why the Qur'an's creedal formula begins with a denial and why Sulaymān's letter demanded submission rather than assent to a proposition. Nothing can be installed until the previous occupant is removed. Her case is the clean illustration: an intelligent, fair-minded ruler who had reasoned her way to the edge of the truth and could not cross, because the position was taken. What finally clears it is not an argument at all.
