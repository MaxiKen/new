## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:61

> If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon ˹for your benefit˺, they will certainly say, “Allah!” How can they then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question the Opponents Answer Themselves**

The sentence instructs the Prophet to put a question to the very people who were disputing with him, and the answer is supplied in advance because it was never in doubt. The idolaters of Makkah did not deny a supreme creator; they acknowledged that the heavens and the earth belonged to God and treated the idols as intermediaries or subordinates rather than as rivals to the maker. The Book records the same admission in several chapters, where the disbelievers confess under questioning that the earth and those on it belong to God and that He is the Lord of the seven heavens (23:84–86), and where they are asked who provides from the sky and the earth, who owns hearing and sight, and who conducts every affair, with the answer again being God (10:31). The sūrah builds its case on ground the other side has already conceded.

**What Subjecting the Sun and Moon Adds**

The phrase about the sun and the moon introduces a further element: not merely creation but arrangement for human benefit. The two great bodies move on courses that make agriculture, travel, and the marking of time possible, and the Book treats that serviceability as evidence of an intention toward the people who benefit from it. Elsewhere it lists the subjects of this arrangement — the night wrapped around the day, the sun and moon running to a fixed term — and treats each as a mark of the One who set them going (39:5). An audience that had already accepted the creator was therefore being asked to notice that the same agent had also organized the world for their use. The confession being sought was, in other words, already larger than the question that produced it.

**The Contradiction Inside the Confession**

The verse's astonishment is aimed at a specific inconsistency. The people it describes affirmed God as creator and provider, and then worshiped objects they knew had produced nothing. The Book repeats this diagnosis in several places and always with the same puzzled tone, asking the idolaters why they are not mindful after giving the right answer (23:85), why they are so deluded (23:89), and whether they will not then fear Him (10:31). The charge is not that the Meccans were atheists. It is that they held a belief whose consequences they refused, and the sūrah treats the refusal as the real subject of the dispute.

The contradiction is also a matter of timing. The confession is made in this life while the benefit is being enjoyed, and the complaint is that the acknowledgement arrives at the point where it costs nothing, since the same people recognize their lord in difficulty and forget him once the danger has passed.

**The Word Behind "Deluded"**

The Arabic verb rendered here as being deluded comes from a root meaning to turn something from its proper course, and it is used for turning a person away from a road and for the fabrication of a lie that turns people aside from the truth. The passive form in this verse therefore describes people who have been turned rather than people who wandered. The same verb recurs in the Book's similar interrogations of the idolaters, and the repetition establishes it as a technical term for the specific condition of holding a true premise and acting on a false one. What is turned out of place in these people is not their knowledge of God but their conduct toward Him.

The word for delusion also names a mechanism rather than a result. The people described are being turned aside from a position they had already understood, and the passive form keeps the sentence from sounding like an accident, since being diverted implies something doing the diverting.

**The Tone of the Question**

The final clause is a question of wonder rather than a request for information, and the Book uses this construction when the inconsistency being described is too stark to be explained. The sentence does not ask what evidence the opponents would require, and it does not offer a new argument; it draws attention to the gap between what they say and what they do, and leaves the reader to feel its size. The sūrah's placement of the verse near its close matters. After sixty verses about trial, ruin, provision, and reward, the Book returns to the simplest possible inconsistency, and it holds that up as the thing requiring an explanation.

The question form also requires the audience to answer for itself. A statement can be accepted in silence, while a question leaves the hearers with a response they must either offer or refuse, and the Book's habit of closing arguments this way gives its warnings the shape of an examination rather than a report.

**A Pattern the Book Returns To Constantly**

The theme of believing in God without obeying Him runs through the Qur'an, and the sūrah has already touched it in its portrait of the wavering man who accepted the community's faith and abandoned it under pressure. The verses about the sea in the next passage take up the same subject from another angle, describing people who call upon God alone in a moment of danger and associate others with Him the moment they reach shore. What the interrogations in these chapters establish together is that the evidence for God's sovereignty was never the stumbling block for the people of Makkah. Their difficulty lay in following a conclusion they had already reached, and the verse's question about delusion names that difficulty precisely.

The pattern also explains why the sūrah can treat the audience's own admission as decisive evidence. A community that answers correctly in its own words has supplied the premise of the case against it, and the Book's arguments with Makkah frequently proceed that way, letting the opponents state the fact and then drawing the consequence.

**Gratitude as an Exchange God Has Announced**

The Book does not leave gratitude without a stated return. Believers are told that if they remember God, He will remember them, and are urged to be thankful rather than ungrateful (2:152), and a proclamation is reported in which God declares that gratitude will bring increase while ingratitude brings severe punishment (14:7). The argument of this verse closes on that arrangement. The question put to the audience about who sends down water and controls hearing and sight is not a quiz; it is the preamble to a claim about what is owed, and the Book's wider statements show what the owing looks like in practice. What the benefactor asks for is acknowledgement, and the punishment for withholding it falls on the one who withheld it.

**Why the Sūrah Asks Rather Than Asserts**

The choice of an interrogative form in this verse and the one that follows is a deliberate rhetorical strategy. An assertion can be rejected as a claim made by an interested party; a question that the opponent answers with his own words becomes part of the record. The Book uses this technique repeatedly against the idolaters, and it takes care to report the answers they give, so that the conclusion is drawn in their own voice. The effect on the reader is the same as the effect on the original audience: the argument does not depend on the messenger's authority but on the consistency of the opponent's own position. A community that acknowledges the creator of the heavens and the earth has already surrendered the premise on which its worship of other beings depends, and the verse's question simply places that surrender in view.
