## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:205

> And when they leave ˹you˺, they strive throughout the land to spread mischief in it and destroy crops and cattle. Allah does not like mischief.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conduct Described**

The verse states that when the person described in the previous sentence turns away, he strives in the land to spread corruption and to destroy the crops and the livestock. The sentence supplies the conduct that corresponds to the portrait, and the two verses are read together by the community's teachers.

The Book's method of describing a person's private disposition and then his public conduct gives the passage its shape, and the reader is shown what the earlier professions amount to. What the sentence establishes is that the words of the previous verse were a cover for activity of a different kind.

**The Meaning of Turning Away**

The commentators discussed the phrase, and they explained it as describing the person's departure from the presence of those he has addressed as well as the anger and the authority that follow from it. The conduct described therefore begins when the audience is no longer present.

The Book's attention to the moment at which a person's conduct changes gives the sentence its force, and the community's scholars read it as a statement about the difference between public and private behaviour. What the reader receives is the account of a person whose real activity begins when he is no longer being observed by those he has sought to impress.

The commentators also noted that the phrase describes a person who leaves the company of those he has sought to impress, and the community's teachers read the sentence as a statement about the difference between a person's public manner and his private conduct. The reader is shown that the professions of the previous verse are contradicted by what follows the departure, and the passage's method is to let the conduct expose the character. The Book's account of the hypocrites elsewhere uses the same pattern.

**The Corruption Named**

The sentence states that the person strives to spread corruption in the land, and the commentators explained the term as covering decay and disorder in the broadest sense, including injustice and immorality as well as damage to the creation. Some connected the sentence with the passages in the sūrah that describe the spreading of corruption by those who claim to set matters right.

The Book's use of the term gives the sentence a wide application, and the community's teachers noted that the destruction of a community's means of life is one form of the disorder the word describes. What the reader receives is a statement about the effects of conduct that goes beyond the harm done to individuals, and the sentence shows that the Book treats the ruin of a society's resources as a matter of the first order.

**The Crops and the Livestock**

The sentence names the crops and the livestock as the objects of the destruction, and the commentators explained the terms as referring to the produce of the earth and the animals that provide for people. Some extended the second term to offspring of any kind, including the human.

The Book's account of the corruption in the land elsewhere names similar objects, and the community's scholars read the pair as a description of what a community depends on for its survival. The reader carries away a picture of damage that extends beyond the person's own dealings, and the sentence shows that the harm described is done to the community as a whole.

**The Reports About the Occasion**

The commentaries mention reports in which a particular person is said to have burned the fields of a group and hamstrung their donkey, and they connect the incident with the sentence. The reports are given as illustrations, and the community's teachers read the verse as a description of a kind of conduct rather than of a single act.

The material shows how the early community applied the text, and the reader is told that the passage's concern is not the incident but the disposition it reveals. The sentence leaves him with the account of a warning whose scope extends to every form of the corruption the verse names.

**"God Does Not Like Corruption"**

The sentence closes with the statement that God does not like corruption, and the community's scholars read the clause as the statement of the standard by which the conduct described is judged. The Book's use of the language of love and aversion shows that the judgment concerns the conduct itself rather than its consequences alone.

The clause also belongs to the passage's account of the hypocrites, since the person described has presented himself as a well-wisher of the community. The passage supplies him with the statement that the profession of the previous verse is contradicted by the conduct of this one, and the sentence leaves the reader with the standard that the Book applies to both.
