## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:205

> And when they leave ˹you˺, they strive throughout the land to spread mischief in it and destroy crops and cattle. Allah does not like mischief.

**Expanded Commentary**

**When He Turns Away**

The verse follows the impostor out of the room. The portrait of 2:204 was drawn inside the encounter — the impressive speech, the oath to the heart, the listener's admiration — and this verse tracks what happens when the encounter ends: when he turns away, he strives in the land. The turning was read by the exegetes at two levels: the physical departure, his leaving the presence of those he has been deceiving, and the interior turn, the anger that shows the mask's underside. Both readings carry the same disclosure: the eloquence was a costume worn for an audience, and the audience's absence reveals the wearer. The verse thus completes the diagnostic pattern begun in the previous verse — the disjunction between the speech in your presence and the conduct out of it is the hypocrite's identifying mark. The community is taught where to look: not at the performance, which is flawless while watched, but at the interval, the going-away, the hours in which no admirer is present to be impressed. What a man does when he turns away is the report on what he was while facing you.

**The Striving That Spreads Corruption**

The verb of the verse is emphatic: he *strives* — yasʿā — in the land, and the object of his striving is fasād, corruption. The energy is the first surprise: the deceiver is not passive, not merely false, but industrious in his falsehood, working with the same diligence the believer owes to good. The word fasād carries the sense the exegetes fix — literally rot, and in Quranic usage corruption in the broadest sense: decadence, immorality, injustice, the undoing of what holds a land together. The verse's own illustration is concrete: some commentators describe a particular hypocrite who burned people's fields and hamstrung their donkey — the arson and the maiming as the portrait's case study. The striving against a whole land, not against individuals, marks the scale: this is not private malice but public sabotage, the deliberate rotting of a community's fabric. And the striving answers the previous verse's oath: the man who called God to witness the goodness of his heart spends his absent hours manufacturing corruption in the earth God made — the witness he invoked is witness to the interval.

**Tillage and Offspring Destroyed**

The verse names the corruption's two targets: he destroys the tillage and the offspring. The tillage — the harth — is agriculture, the crops, the planted fields; the offspring — the nasl — is livestock on the primary reading, and on some commentators' extension any progeny including the human. The two named things are the Arabian world's entire wealth: what the land grows and what the herds bear, the standing crop and the multiplying stock, the food of this year and the food of all years to come. To destroy them is not to injure a competitor but to attack the conditions of life itself — the scorched field does not belong to the enemy alone but to the future, and the hamstrung animal ends a lineage of provision. The modern readings the verse has generated follow the same logic outward: Muslims have interpreted such corruption-verses as condemning injustice in society and the destruction of the natural environment, citing the Qur'an's own summary — corruption has spread on land and sea through what people's hands have done (30:41). The hypocrite of this verse is the type of every agent whose striving leaves the land less fertile, the herds less numerous, the air and water and social trust less whole: fasād as the anti-cultivation, the unmaking of what God's provision grows.

**God Loves Not Corruption**

The verse's final clause states the divine disposition: God does not love the fasād. The formula — the negation of divine love — is the Qur'an's characteristic verdict-language, marking not merely prohibition but divine aversion: God does not love the transgressor (2:190), the waster, the arrogant boaster, the treacherous. Its placement here is strategic, because the hypocrite's whole performance is built on claiming God's favor — the oath that God knows his heart, the religious language of his impressive speech. The clause answers the claim from the side of God: whatever the man asserts about the divine knowledge of his interior, the divine love does not attend his work, and the work is what the verse has just described — striving corruption, destroyed tillage, ruined offspring. The negation also disciplines the community's discernment: you cannot audit his heart, but you can audit his harvest — the fields he leaves burned, the herds he leaves hamstrung, the trust he leaves rotted — and the divine non-love is pronounced over exactly that audit. The verse thereby hands the deceived a criterion that does not depend on eloquence: look at what his striving leaves growing, and what it leaves dead.

**The Anti-Economy of the Diptych**

Within the passage's architecture, this verse is the diptych's dark economy in full operation. The sūrah has just legislated an economy of cultivation and expenditure: spend in God's cause, the hands that withhold cast themselves into destruction (2:195); the pilgrim takes provision and seeks bounty without blame (2:197–198); the portion is from what is earned (2:202). Against that economy stands the hypocrite's: he spends nothing, earns nothing, and his one industry is the destruction of what others spend, earn, and grow. The tillage he burns is somebody's provision-command obeyed; the offspring he maims is somebody's bounty sought lawfully; the land he corrupts is the same land the verse of 30:41 describes as tasting the consequences of such hands. The diptych's second panel — the one who sells his soul seeking God's pleasure (2:207) — will complete the contrast from the other side: the believer's economy culminates in spending the self, the hypocrite's in destroying the harvest. Between the two, the verse's criterion of love divides them: the seller of the soul sells to a God who is kind to His servants; the spreader of corruption strives under a God who does not love corruption — and both dispositions are already, in this life, visible in the fields.

**The Verse's Standing Warning**

The warning this verse installs runs through the rest of the Qur'an's social teaching. The fasād it names — corruption pursued with striving energy, aimed at the land's fertility and the community's stock — becomes a category the scripture legislates against repeatedly: the punishment-verses of highway robbery and public disorder use the same word; the Pharaoh's administration is described as making corruption in the land; the sūrah's own opening portraits return to it at every recurrence of the hypocrites' type. And the ecological readings the tradition draws from it — injustice in society and destruction of the natural environment both named as fasād — extend the category to every scale of the modern world's corruption: the burned field is now also the cleared forest, the hamstrung donkey the collapsed fishery, the rotted trust the poisoned commons. The verse survives as the Qur'an's sharpest single indictment of productive-seeming destruction: the striving that looks like work, the eloquence that looks like leadership, the departure that leaves the land less alive. God does not love corruption — and the verse leaves the community the test by which to see it: whatever his speech in your presence, count the tillage and the offspring he leaves behind.
