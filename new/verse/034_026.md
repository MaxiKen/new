## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:26

> Say, “Our Lord will gather us together, then He will judge between us with the truth. For He is the All-Knowing Judge.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Dispute Referred Upward**

The previous verse separated the two parties' accounts; this one names the court where the separation will be enforced. The sentence has three movements in strict order: gathering, judgment, and the qualification of the judge. *Yajmaʿu baynanā rabbunā* — our Lord will bring us together. The verb is *jamaʿa*, to collect, assemble, bring into one place, and the Qur'an makes the gathering a proper name for the Day: al-Shūrā and al-Taghābun both call it *yawm al-jamʿ*, the Day of Assembly (42:7; 64:9). What is striking about the phrasing is the pronoun. It does not say He will gather them, or gather you; it says He will gather **us** — both parties, the speaker included. The Prophet is told to place himself on the same list as his opponents, subject to the same summons. An argument referred to a judge is an argument in which the referring party accepts jurisdiction over himself.

**Judgment "With the Truth" Rather Than by Power**

*Thumma yaftaḥu baynanā bi-l-ḥaqq* — then He will decide between us with the truth. The instrumental phrase is not decorative. A judgment can be rendered by authority alone, and a sufficiently powerful judge needs no further warrant; the verse specifies that this one will be rendered according to what is actually the case. That specification is what makes the referral meaningful to both sides. A party that expects to lose on the merits gains nothing from a fair court, but a party confident of the facts wants nothing else. The Qur'an holds to this standard across its descriptions of the reckoning, promising scales set up *"so no soul will be wronged in the least"* (21:47) and stating that whoever does an evil deed *"will only be paid back with its equivalent"* (40:40). The Prophet is not being told to threaten his opponents with a verdict. He is being told to submit the case.

**The Verb the Qur'an Uses for Judging Here**

The word translated *judge* is not the usual *ḥakama* but *fataḥa*, whose primary meaning is to open. Arabic uses it for opening a door, opening a city to conquest, and opening a matter that has been shut — hence its use for adjudication, since a judge opens a case that the parties have sealed by their disagreement. The image is exact for a dispute of this kind. Two sides have argued to a standstill, each holding a position the other cannot enter, and the deadlock is a closure. What the Day supplies is not merely a verdict but an opening: the concealed facts become visible, and the question that could not be settled by argument is settled by disclosure. This is also why the Qur'an says the differences will be made clear rather than merely decided. A judgment that only assigned outcomes would leave the losing party still convinced.

**al-Fattāḥ: A Divine Name Built From That Verb**

The verse ends by naming the judge *al-Fattāḥ al-ʿAlīm*, and the first of these is an uncommon name formed from the same root as the verb just used. *Al-Fattāḥ* can be rendered the Judge or, keeping the root sense, the Opener — the One who opens what is locked, whether that is a case, a door, a difficulty or a source of provision. Both senses are live, and the Arabic does not force a choice. The pairing with *al-ʿAlīm*, the All-Knowing, supplies what the first name alone would leave open, because opening a case is only useful if the one opening it knows what is inside. This is the specific qualification a human court lacks. Judges are limited by evidence, by testimony that can be bought, by motives that cannot be inspected. The verse names a judge for whom the evidentiary problem does not arise, which reaches back to the sūrah's opening claim that nothing of an atom's weight escapes Him and everything is written down (34:3).

**Why the Argument Is Left Unfinished on Purpose**

Read in sequence with what precedes it, this verse completes a deliberate refusal. The sūrah has been arguing hard — challenging the opponents to call their deities (34:22), removing the intercession they relied on (34:23), extracting their own admission about provision (34:24) — and at the point where a debater would press for surrender, it stops and refers the matter upward. This is not weakness in the argument. It is a claim about what arguments can accomplish. Positions of this kind are not abandoned because a debating point lands; they are abandoned when the facts become undeniable, and the verse says when that will be. There is also a discipline being imposed on the believing side. A community told that its dispute will be settled by God at the gathering is a community told not to settle it by other means in the meantime.

**Confidence Without Coercion**

The tone of this verse repays attention, because it is not the tone of a threat. Nothing here describes the fate of the losing party, and the sentence could be spoken without hostility to a man one hopes to persuade. Its confidence lies entirely in the referral: a speaker who invites a knowing judge to open the case is a speaker who believes the file favours him. That is a stronger move in a dispute than asserting the conclusion, and it puts the opponent in an awkward position, since to reject the referral he must deny either the gathering or the judge's competence — which is exactly the denial the sūrah opened by quoting (34:3). The chain of *say* verses continues, and the next one stops referring and starts demanding.
