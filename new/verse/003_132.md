## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:132

> Obey Allah and the Messenger, so you may be shown mercy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Obediences Named in One Command**

The verse commands obedience to God and to the Messenger, and it attaches a promise of mercy to the command. The pairing of the two objects of obedience is one of the Book's most frequent constructions.

The Book's logic in pairing them is that the messenger conveys what God has commanded, and obedience to the message is therefore obedience to the sender. The sūrah elsewhere states the connection from the other side, telling the Prophet to say that those who love God should follow him, and that God will then love them and forgive their sins (3:31). The two verses together describe a single relationship with two faces: love toward God and obedience toward the one He sent.

**Obedience as the Mark of Following the Messenger**

The Book returns to the command to obey the Messenger in many passages, and the variety of contexts shows what it covers: settling disputes, submitting to judgments, participating in the community's undertakings. The command is not restricted to a single area of life.

The battle narrative in this sūrah supplies an illustration of what disobedience costs. The archers who left their position contrary to the orders they had received left the army's rear exposed, and the losses of the day followed. The Book records the episode because it demonstrates in a single day what the command in this verse states in principle, and the community that lived through the events understood the connection.

**"So That You May Be Shown Mercy"**

The promise attached to the command is mercy, and the word appears in this sūrah at points where the community's failures are being addressed. The verse before the reader links obedience to the hope of mercy rather than to a reward that has been earned.

That choice of terms is significant. A command accompanied by a promise of wages invites a calculation; a command accompanied by the hope of mercy places the result in God's hands. The Book uses the second form when it addresses communities whose record includes failures, which is the situation of the community throughout this passage.

**Where the Obligation of Obedience Falls**

The command is addressed to the community, and its practical content concerns the way the community organises itself under the Prophet's authority. The sūrah's earlier verses describe the consultation before the battle and the decision that followed it, and the community's obligation was to carry out the decision once it was made.

The Book states the principle in another passage, where it tells the Prophet to consult his companions in matters and then to put his trust in God once he has decided (3:159). The two verses describe the same structure: consultation, decision, and an obedience that no longer reopens the question. The community's health depended on that structure, and the losses of Uḥud were the price of the break in it.

**Mercy as the Goal of the Instruction**

The sentence's aim is stated at its end, and the aim is not the community's victory or its standing but the mercy of God. The Book uses the same hope in its commands about the prayer, about patience, and about the treatment of the weak.

What the verse therefore teaches is that the community's obedience serves its own interest, not God's. The Book states the point elsewhere in a form that leaves no doubt, telling the believers that God does not need their obedience and that it is they who are in need. The promise of mercy in this verse is the same idea in a softer key.

**The Verse's Place Between Warning and Promise**

The verse stands between the command to guard against the Fire and the command to hasten toward forgiveness and the garden. The arrangement is the Book's standard sequence of warning, instruction, and hope.

The reader is shown the danger, told what to do about it, and then shown the destination of those who comply. This verse contributes the command, and its brevity is deliberate: the community has already been given the material and the reminder, and what remains is the response. The instruction is short, the promise attached to it is large, and the verses that follow describe the character of those who receive it.

**Obedience in the Passages About the Messenger**

The command to obey the Prophet appears throughout the Book, and it covers the ordinary business of the community's life: settling disputes, taking part in its undertakings, accepting its judgments. The Book states in one place that whoever obeys the messenger has obeyed God, which is the ground of the command in this verse.

The sūrah's own narrative provides the illustration. The Prophet's orders on the day of Uḥud were specific, and the men who held the hill had been told to remain there under conditions they could not have imagined when the order was given. Their failure to obey cost the community dear, and the Book records the episode for exactly that reason. The verse before the reader states the rule that the day demonstrated, and the two belong to the same instruction.

**The Place of This Verse in the Sequence**

The verse stands between the warning about the Fire and the command to hasten toward forgiveness. It is the middle term, and its position shows how the passage is built: danger, instruction, and hope.

That sequence is one of the Book's standard patterns, and the brevity of this verse is part of it. The reader is not given a discourse on obedience; he is given a command with a promise attached, and the material around it supplies everything else. The communities that recite the passage meet the same sequence in the same order, which is why the sūrah's arrangement has been preserved rather than flattened into a list of rules.
