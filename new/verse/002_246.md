## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:246

> Have you not seen those chiefs of the Children of Israel after Moses? They said to one of their prophets, "Appoint for us a king, ˹and˺ we will fight in the cause of Allah." He said, "Are you not going to cower if ordered to fight?" They replied, "How could we refuse to fight in the cause of Allah, while we were driven out of our homes and ˹separated from˺ our children?" But when they were ordered to fight, they fled, except for a few of them. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Summons to See**

*Have you not seen* — the formula that opened the fleeing-thousands scene (2:243) opens this one, and the repetition binds the two stories into a single exhibit. What the eye is summoned to now is not a multitude dying but a council deliberating: the chiefs — the malaʾ, the leading men — of the Children of Israel in the generation after Moses, bringing a political request to a prophet. The tradition identifies the prophet as Samuel, the last of the judges, and the period as the era before the monarchy: the tribes under Philistine pressure, their shrines violated, their leadership failing. The verse compresses the whole situation into its opening exchange, and the compression follows the pattern the previous scene established — the narrative will move from a human proposal to a divine demonstration, and the demonstration will concern not the enemy but the proposers.

**The Request and Its Biblical Twin**

*Appoint for us a king, and we will fight in the cause of Allah.* The request has a documented parallel the commentators cite: the elders' demand to Samuel in the first book of Samuel — a king to *go out before us and fight our battles*, so that Israel may be like the nations (1 Samuel 8:19–20). The two accounts share the structure — monarchy requested as a military solution — and differ in emphasis instructively. The biblical elders want to be like the nations, a king visible at their head; the Qur'anic chiefs frame the request entirely in terms of the divine cause: the king's function is to make their fighting possible, and their fighting's definition is fī sabīl Allāh. The Qur'an's version thus sharpens the coming collapse: these are not men asking for pomp but men pledging a holy war conditional on a commander — and the condition, once granted, will reveal what the pledge was worth.

**The Prophet's Counter-Question**

The prophet does not accept the pledge at face value: *Are you not going to cower if ordered to fight?* The question — hal ʿasaytum, might it be that you would not — is the tradition's anatomy of a promise made in comfort. He is not refusing the request; he is testing its grammar. The chiefs have said *we will fight* in the indicative, describing a future they imagine; the prophet translates the situation into its actual form — an order will come, and the question will be obedience, not inclination. The exegetes note the precision of his verb: to cower, to shrink back — the failure he names is not defeat in battle but flinching at the command. The counter-question functions in the narrative the way the river will function two verses later: a device for separating the said from the done before the doing begins. The chiefs answer it — and their answer, magnificent in its logic, will be the measure of their collapse.

**The Answer and the Collapse**

Their reply is the verse's rhetorical high point: *How could we refuse to fight in the cause of Allah, while we were driven out of our homes and separated from our children?* The argument is a fortiori and it is airtight — the double injury (exile and separation, homeland and family) supplies every motive a people could need, and the divine cause supplies the justification; what possible combination of reasons could produce refusal? The verse's next sentence is the whole point of the story: *But when they were ordered to fight, they fled, except for a few of them.* Between the answer and the order nothing changed except the arrival of the moment the answer was about. The commentators read the collapse as the counter-question vindicated in the smallest number of words the narrative could use: the prophet had asked whether they would cower; they had answered with their grievances; the order came; and the grievances proved insufficient — *save a few*. The exception-clause is the story's only hope and its recurring structure: the remnant that keeps the promise becomes the community that keeps the faith.

**What the Story Diagnoses**

The verse is a study in the difference between a people's reasons and a people's readiness. The chiefs' reasons were real — the expulsions had happened, the children had been taken — and real reasons, the narrative shows, do not automatically produce obedient men. Something between the grievance and the order converts resolve into flight, and the Qur'an's diagnosis, embedded in the story's structure, is that a pledge whose condition is external (give us a king) has located its power outside the pledge-makers: when the king comes and the order is issued, the men discover that they had promised a situation, not themselves. The few who do not flee are the counter-diagnosis: readiness that survives the command's arrival is an interior property, and the narrative sequence that follows — the tested river-crossing, the small company's maxim — will be entirely about producing and identifying such interiority in the community's fighters. The chiefs asked for a king to make them fight; the story gives them a king who finds out which of them can.

**God Knows the Wrongdoers**

The verse closes with the knowledge-clause: *And Allah has perfect knowledge of the wrongdoers*. The wrongdoers named are precise — not the enemy, not the fleeing crowd in general, but those whose flight constituted ẓulm: the pledge-breakers, who had invoked their expulsed homes and separated children as the guarantee of their fighting and then withheld the fighting the guarantee promised. The commentators connect the clause to the story's function for its first hearers: the community receiving this sūrah had its own chiefs, its own pledges, and its own approaching orders, and the knowledge-clause stands over all of them — the audit that caught the fleeing thousands (2:243) and will audit the fighters of the following verses begins here, at the council. The clause also does narrative work: God's knowledge of the wrongdoers is the story's quiet statement that the flight was seen, recorded, and named — and that the few who stood were seen too. Between the two sightings the verse leaves its reader with the question the prophet asked: what will you do when the order comes?
