## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:75

> Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you, though a group of them would hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Addressed to the Believers**

The verse turns its face. After seventy-four verses in which Allah speaks to or about the Children of Israel, the address here is to the Muslim community — *do you expect them to be true to you?* — and the recital's purpose discloses itself. The long history was not filed for its own sake but to calibrate an expectation: the young community in Madina, watching its scholars debate and its preachers hope, is asked whether it has priced the audience correctly. The verb *taṭmaʿūn* carries hope with appetite in it — hoping for, counting on — and the question's force is corrective rather than crushing: the believers are not told to stop calling or to abandon hope in persons, but to stop expecting the corporate response their own sincerity keeps projecting. The grounds for the correction are historical and are stated in the verse's second half: this is a community with a documented record on precisely the point at issue — reception of Allah's word. The verse thus functions as the recital's hinge: everything before it built the case file; this sentence hands the file to the Muslims and tells them what it is for. Conversion is Allah's business; expectation-management is the believers', and the sūrah supplies the data.

**Taḥrīf: The Word for Corruption**

The charge names its crime with a technical term: the verse's *knowingly corrupt it* renders *yuḥarrifūnah* — they distort — from a root meaning to slant, to twist, to make oblique. *Taḥrīf* is the Qur'an's standing word for the mishandling of revelation by its custodians, and it runs as a thread through the Book: the changed word at the gate (2:59), the distortion with tongues that makes listeners think the twisting is from the Book (3:78), words taken out of context with *we listen and we disobey* substituted for *we hear and obey* (4:46), hearts hardened and words distorted as one paired consequence (5:13). The verse's specification is the aggravator within the family: the distortion happens *after understanding* and *knowingly* — this is not drift, mistranslation, or the entropy of copies, but informed misdirection by people who comprehended exactly what they were changing. The lexical root reinforces the picture: a slant is not a break. Taḥrīf keeps the text's body and moves its direction — the words survive, the meaning is rotated — which is why the crime is invisible to outsiders and requires the diagnosis only revelation can supply. The verse indicts a specific professional failure: custodians converting custody into manipulation, and it does so to an audience that includes every later custodian of any text.

**Which Group? The Seventy or the Contemporaries**

The exegetes divide on the identity of the *group* — *farīq* — that heard Allah's word directly and distorted it. One reading identifies them with the seventy chosen men of the mount: the party that accompanied Moses and, on the strength of hearing the divine speech itself, returned and misrepresented it — saying, when Moses transmitted a command, that Allah had commanded otherwise, or telling the people the obligations were optional: do them if you wish, leave them if you wish, there is no harm. The reading's argument is grammatical: the verse says they *heard the word of Allah*, and the Qur'an elsewhere reserves direct hearing of Allah for the mountain's company, distinguishing it from mediated hearing (9:6). The second reading takes the group as the Jewish contemporaries of the Prophet ﷺ — scholars of Madina distorting not a voice from the cloud but the Torah in their custody, the hearing being their study and recitation of it. Both readings have ground, and the stronger conclusion holds them together: the verse's grammar fits the seventy, its occasion fits Madina, and the charge — informed corruption of received revelation — describes a pattern rather than a single generation. The farīq is a type with a founding instance, and the type is what the believers are being warned to expect.

**“After They Understood”: Knowledge as the Aggravator**

The circumstantial clause — *knowingly*, after they had understood — is the verse's legal center, and it repeats the sūrah's standing aggravation. Verse 42 forbade mixing truth with falsehood *while you know*; verse 44 charged the preacher who exempts himself *although you read the Scripture*; verse 59 filed the changed word at the gate. The consistent principle: the weight of a distortion is proportional to the understanding of the distorter. An ignorant error is corrigible by teaching; an informed corruption is a decision, and decisions are what the audit judges. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the full form of the crime: *"They say, 'It is from Allah'—but it is not from Allah. And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly"* (3:78) — the distortion completed by its attribution, the twisted meaning sold with the divine warranty attached. Verse 75's clause thereby excludes every softening reading. This is not a text that grew obscure over centuries, not a tradition that drifted in transmission, not scholars honestly divided — it is comprehension followed by contradiction of the comprehended, which the Qur'an treats as the specific sin of custodians, and which is why the believers' expectation is being corrected: a party that corrupted the word knowingly will not be moved by hearing it again correctly. The argument they lost was never informational.

**Text or Meaning: The Tradition's Own Debate**

Islamic scholarship debated for centuries what taḥrīf historically was — alteration of the transmitted text or corruption of its interpretation — and the debate belongs to any honest reading of this verse. The maximalist position, epitomized by Ibn Ḥazm in the fifth Islamic century, held that the Jews and Christians actually changed the text of their scriptures, excising and inserting. The earlier commentators were notably less eager to claim this, and al-Rāzī's reasoning is the tradition at its sharpest: the prophecies of the coming Prophet could not have been physically removed from a text as widely distributed as the Torah, since excision at that scale would require a collusion of copyists across communities too large to conceal — so what shifted, he argues, was interpretation: texts requiring careful thought acquired, over time, a normative reading drifted from their intent. Al-Rāzī himself concedes the asymmetry — a less widely distributed text could be altered without detection. The verse's own wording inclines the balance toward his reading: distortion *after understanding* describes minds working on a comprehended text, and the companion verses specify tongues and context-shifting (3:78, 4:46) rather than erasure. What both sides agree on is the operative fact for the verse's purpose: by the Prophet's ﷺ time the received interpretation stood against the received text, and the custodians knew it.

**Do Not Grieve Yourself to Death**

The question the verse puts to the believers — do you still expect? — is one the Qur'an puts to its Prophet ﷺ in the same terms, and the parallels mark this verse as pastoral instruction before it is polemical record. To a messenger whose hope for his people was consuming him, the Book says that Allah leaves astray and guides whom He wills, *so do not grieve yourself to death over them* (35:8); and again: *perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message* (18:6). The grief named in those verses is the same appetite verse 75 questions — the sincere person's investment in an outcome that is not his to produce. The correction runs in both directions at once. Against despair: the believers are not told the audience is worthless, only that expectation must be rebuilt on evidence rather than on the heat of the caller's own sincerity; the call continues, since guidance sometimes arrives where history says it will not. Against naiveté: the case file exists so that each refusal is met with recognition rather than shock — the distorters of verse 75 have heirs in every negotiation, and the believer who knows the type is neither surprised nor broken by it. The recital's long history closes its first movement here, in a single calibrated question, and the question is equipment: hope honestly priced is the only hope that survives contact with the record.
