## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:75

> Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you, though a group of them would hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Put to the Believers**

The sentence changes addressee. The sūrah has been speaking to the People of the Book about their own history, and it now turns to the Muslims and asks about their expectations. The question concerns hope rather than doctrine, and it assumes that the community was waiting for a group of people to act in good faith after being given the truth.

The Book's method here is realistic rather than disappointed. It does not tell the believers to abandon their efforts or to treat every member of the other community as an enemy; the very next passages will name those among the People of the Book who are upright (3:113–115) and those whose eyes overflow with tears when they hear the revelation (5:83). What the verse does is remove a specific expectation that had already been falsified.

**The Word for the Offence**

The Arabic verb behind the charge describes slanting or twisting something out of its shape, and the Book uses it for the treatment of scripture in several places. It appears in the account of those who distort words from their proper places in their greetings and their readings (4:46), in the warning that this people distorted the words of the Scripture and neglected part of what they were commanded to uphold (5:13), and in the description of people who write a book with their own hands and then attribute it to God.

The vocabulary matters for how the dispute has been understood. The word describes an act of bending rather than of removing, and the classical commentators differed over whether the distortion in question involved the text itself or its interpretation. Al-Rāzī argued that mass alteration of a widely transmitted text would have required collusion on a scale that is hard to credit, and he preferred the reading that the meaning had been worked away from its original intent over time.

**Hearing, Understanding, and Then Changing**

The sentence builds its charge in three stages, and the middle one is the heaviest. The group heard the word of God, understood it, and then corrupted it knowingly. Each clause removes a possible excuse: the hearing rules out ignorance of the text, the understanding rules out misunderstanding, and the adverb rules out accident.

That construction is what makes the verse a rebuke rather than a complaint. The Book's harsher passages are consistently reserved for people whose knowledge exceeded their conduct, and later in this sūrah it addresses the same problem to the believers themselves when it commands them to hold firmly to the scripture they have been given (2:63). The offence described in this verse is available to any community that holds a text.

**Who the Group Was**

Commentators identified the party in two ways. One reading takes them as the seventy chosen men who accompanied Moses to the mountain and heard the word of God directly, returning to misreport what they had heard. The other takes them as the Jewish contemporaries of the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah whose conduct the surrounding verses describe.

The verse itself does not settle the question, and the difference does not change its function. What the Book is establishing is a category of person rather than a list of names: people who were in a position to know what a revelation said and who turned it to another purpose. The same category appears later in the sūrah among the believers' own ranks, and the sūrah's warnings to its own community use the same vocabulary.

**Why the Believers Were Still Hoping**

The question assumes that the Muslims expected good faith from people whose scriptures had been given to them and whose ancestors had been favoured. That hope was not unreasonable in itself; the Book commands the community to invite people to God with wisdom and kind advice and to debate in the best manner (16:125), which is not the language of a community expecting deceit.

What the verse adjusts is the object of the hope. A believer may hope for another person's guidance and must treat him well regardless; what he cannot do is assume that possession of a scripture will by itself produce honest dealing. The Book states the principle in a later verse addressed to the same situation: some of the People of the Book are faithful, but most are rebellious (3:110), and the sentence distinguishes the two rather than merging them.

**What the Believers Are Asked to Change**

The verse does not tell the Muslims to stop speaking. The address to the People of the Book continues for many more verses, and the invitation to believe what confirms their own scriptures is repeated throughout the passage. What the question asks the believers to abandon is a substitute for judgment: the assumption that a shared text guarantees a shared standard of conduct.

That adjustment is what the rest of the passage supplies, since the sūrah goes on to name the specific failures of those who distorted their book and to describe the criteria by which a person's standing will be settled. The verse is therefore the beginning of an argument rather than a conclusion, and the question at its head opens a section in which the community is taught what to expect and what to do about it.

**A Question That Teaches Caution, Not Contempt**

The question at the head of this verse could be misread as an instruction to distrust an entire people, and the surrounding passage rules that reading out. The sūrah names trustworthy members of the same community, praises those of them who recognise the truth, and continues to invite them to accept what confirms their own scripture. What the verse removes is an assumption rather than a group. A believer who expects good faith from everyone who holds a text will be repeatedly disappointed, and the disappointment will eventually turn into contempt for a whole community. The question prevents both errors by directing judgment at conduct rather than lineage, and it does so in the same sentence that describes the offence it is warning against.
