## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:9

> turning away ˹in pride˺ to lead ˹others˺ astray from Allah’s Way. They will suffer disgrace in this world, and on the Day of Judgment We will make them taste the torment of burning.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Body Language of Arrogance**

*Thāniya ʿiṭfihi* — turning his side. The phrase is a physical description before it is a moral one: a man bending his neck, presenting his side, turning his shoulder toward something he will not face. The Qur'an uses the gesture for contempt because contempt has a posture, and everyone who has watched a person dismiss an argument knows it.

The translators render it as pride, and that is right, but the vocabulary carries more than an internal attitude. What is described is a body turned away while a text is being recited. The man is not weighing the claim and rejecting it; he has physically arranged himself so that it cannot reach him, and he has done it in public.

**From Error to Mission**

Verses 3 and 8 described a man who disputes. Verse 9 says what the disputation becomes: *li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīli Allāh* — to lead others astray from God's path. The little particle *li-* marks purpose. He is not mistaken and therefore, incidentally, misleading. Leading astray is what he is doing it for.

The shift is theologically graded, and the Qur'an grades it consistently. Holding a wrong view is one thing; working to make others hold it is another, and the second carries what the first does not: the corrosion is now being transmitted. A private error dies with the man who made it. A propagated one keeps working after he is in his grave, which is part of why the verse's punishment is doubled.

**Disgrace in This World, Then Burning in the Next**

*Khizyun fī al-dunyā* — disgrace in the present life — followed by the torment of burning on the Day of Resurrection. The two are not alternatives and not a sequence of coincidence. The Qur'an pairs them as a standard form wherever it describes organized opposition to the revelation.

Those who prevent God's name from being mentioned in His places of worship are told: *“For them is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter”* (2:114). The same pairing is given to those who wage war against God and His messenger (5:33), to those whose tongues believe while their hearts do not (5:41), and in the plain statement of Sūrah al-Zumar: *“So Allah made them taste humiliation in this worldly life, but far worse is the punishment of the Hereafter, if only they knew”* (39:26).

The regularity of the formula answers the question a reader might raise. Is the disgrace in this world the real punishment, with the Hereafter an afterthought? No: the pattern is always that the first is partial and the second is the thing itself.

**Disgrace Here Can Be Lifted; the Other Cannot**

That said, disgrace in this life is not fixed in the way the punishment of the next is fixed, and the Qur'an says so. Sūrah Yūnus singles out the one community in history whose belated belief changed their earthly sentence: *“If only there had been a society which believed ˹before seeing the torment˺ and, therefore, benefited from its belief, like the people of Jonah. When they believed, We lifted from them the torment of disgrace in this world and allowed them enjoyment for a while”* (10:98).

The exception proves the rule and explains the logic. Humiliation in this world is a warning delivered inside a period when warnings still work; the punishment of the Hereafter is delivered after that period has closed. This is the same principle the sūrah established four verses earlier with the Hour that is *coming* (22:7): there is an interval, and the interval is the point.

**"We Will Make Them Taste"**

The verb is *nudhīquhu*, We will make them taste. The Qur'an's vocabulary for punishment is overwhelmingly sensory, and tasting is among its most frequent choices.

Taste is the sense that cannot be refused at a distance. You can decline to look at something and you can stop your ears, but a taste on the tongue is registered whether or not it was wanted, and it is registered completely in an instant. The word also carries a portion-size: one *tastes* a small amount of what one will then receive in full.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat uses the same verb for the same double sentence: *“So We sent against them a furious wind, for ˹several˺ miserable days, to make them taste a humiliating punishment in this worldly life. But far more humiliating will be the punishment of the Hereafter”* (41:16). The early taste is a sample, and the sample is meant to be read as a sample.

**A Path, Not a Position**

The object of the man's campaign is *sabīli Allāh* — the way of God. The word is *sabīl*, a road. The Qur'an's standing image for the religion is not a set of propositions but a route, and the verb used for what the man does to others — *yuḍilla*, to cause to stray — is the verb for losing a road.

The image carries a specific implication about method. You cannot refute a road; you can only walk someone off it. Misleading in the Qur'an is therefore rarely pictured as a superior argument winning a debate. It is pictured as distraction, delay, and the offer of an alternative route that looks shorter. The man in this verse is not described as out-arguing anyone; he is described as turning his own shoulder while making sure other people's shoulders turn too.

That is why the offence is measured by its effects on others rather than by the content of his view. A wrong opinion held quietly is a private loss. Turning people off a road is an act with victims, and the verse's doubled sentence — disgrace here, burning hereafter — matches the doubled nature of the harm: he ruined his own direction and worked to ruin other people's.

**Why the Verse Names the Effect Before the Cause**

One last feature of the ordering. The verse states what the man does — turning aside to mislead — and then states his sentence, without pausing to say why the sentence is just. That is what verse 10 supplies. Here the emphasis falls entirely on the act, and the reader is left with the picture of a man with his shoulder turned, working to make sure other people's shoulders are turned as well.
