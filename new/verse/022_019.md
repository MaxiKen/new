## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:19

> These are two opposing groups that disagree about their Lord: as for the disbelievers, garments of Fire will be cut out for them and boiling water will be poured over their heads,

**Expanded Commentary**

**"These Two Adversaries" — How Wide Is the Reference**

*Hādhāni khaṣmāni ikhtaṣamū fī rabbihim* — these two adversaries disputed concerning their Lord. The phrase is indefinite in form and dual in number, and the commentators split over how specific it is.

The general reading, which most prefer, takes the two adversaries as any two parties who dispute about God: believers and disbelievers, in every generation, and the verse that follows describes the two outcomes. A second reading takes it as the Muslims and the People of the Book, since the latter claimed antiquity and nearness to God and argued from it. A third holds that the dispute is between the Garden and the Fire themselves, over what each was created for.

The general reading is the strongest, and the reason is grammatical as much as theological. The dual *khaṣmāni* is used in Arabic for two sides rather than two men, and what follows is a description of two destinies rather than of two named parties. A specific occasion does not exclude the general sense even where a specific occasion can be identified.

**Why Badr Was Proposed**

The most frequently cited occasion is the opening of the Battle of Badr, when three champions came forward from each army: ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Ḥamzah and ʿUbaydah ibn al-Ḥārith for the Muslims, and three fighters from Quraysh against them.

The choice is not arbitrary. Badr was the first open battle of the new community, and it was fought by people who had argued about God for a decade before they argued about anything else. Whatever else was at stake that morning — trade routes, clan honour, the wells — the dispute the verse names is the one that had produced the quarrel: who is right about the Lord.

Reading the verse against that occasion gives the opening phrase its bite. Two sides, both claiming God, and the claim is going to be settled twice: once in the valley, and once in the terms this verse sets out.

**"Garments of Fire Cut Out"**

*Qutṭiʿat lahum thiyābun min nārin* — garments of fire have been cut out for them. The verb is from *qaṭṭaʿa*, to cut into pieces, to cut to shape. It is the vocabulary of a tailor working to a pattern, and the preposition *lahum*, for them, marks the garment as made to measure.

Every element of the image is pointed. Clothing is what a person uses to cover himself, to appear, and to be ranked by; the Qur'an's paradisal vocabulary uses garments the same way, as the visible sign of a state. Here the covering is fire, and it has been tailored to fit the wearer — the punishment is not a uniform issued to a class but a garment cut for a person.

The detail also works as irony. The disbelievers' dispute about their Lord was, in large part, a dispute about status, and the first thing the verse gives them is something to wear.

**Boiling Water Poured Over the Head**

*Yuṣabbu min fawqi ruʾūsihim al-ḥamīm* — scalding water is poured from above their heads. The verb is passive; who pours is not said, which leaves the machinery of the punishment as impersonal as the machinery of the world.

The detail that the water comes from *above the head* rather than from any direction is deliberate. Poured from above, it runs over everything: face, shoulders, chest, and the whole body in turn. A punishment aimed at one part could be endured by protecting the rest; this one reaches all of it at once, and the next verse describes what it does on the way down.

The word *ḥamīm* is the Qur'an's standard term for the boiling liquid of punishment, and it carries a second sense that sharpens it: *ḥamīm* also means an intimate friend, a close companion. The same root that gives the punished their comrades gives them their scalding.

**Why the Punishment Comes Before the Reward**

Verses 19 to 22 describe the destination of one group; verses 23 and 24 describe the destination of the other. The reward is mentioned second, and the ordering is a teaching choice rather than a preference for bad news.

The sūrah has been operating this way since verse 1. The earthquake comes before the argument, the argument before the promise, and the threat before the rescue. A promise placed first would be heard as a bribe; a punishment placed first establishes what is at stake, and the reward then reads as deliverance from something rather than as an entitlement.

The Qur'an does not keep this order rigidly — verse 14 gave the promise of the Gardens before any of these punishments were described — but where the two are set side by side as matching pictures, as they are here, the severity is stated first. The reader is meant to feel the weight of what he is being spared.

**When the Garden and the Fire Spoke**

On the reading that takes the two adversaries as the Garden and the Fire themselves, the report is a famous one and it is well attested. Abū Hurayrah narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said that Paradise and the Fire argued: the Fire said, "I have been given the arrogant and the tyrants," and Paradise said, "Why is it that only the weak and the humble enter me?" God then said to Paradise, "You are My mercy, by which I show mercy to whom I will," and to the Fire, "You are My punishment, by which I punish whom I will, and each of you will be filled." It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (4850, in the Book of Tafsīr) and in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2846), in the chapter headed "the arrogant will enter the Fire and the humble will enter Paradise."

The report explains the verse's phrasing on that reading — *ikhtaṣamū fī rabbihim*, they disputed concerning their Lord, meaning they laid their cases before Him — and it explains why the sūrah goes on to describe the two destinations in matching terms. The Fire is told it will be filled; the inhabitants described in this verse are its filling.

Even without that report, the reading is instructive, because the sūrah will go on to describe the two destinations in matching terms — garments of fire here, silk and bracelets in verse 23. The symmetry is the argument of the whole passage, and this verse is where it opens.
