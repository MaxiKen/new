## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:109

> Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wish Running Backward**

The verse names the opposition's desire at its most active: *many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you back to disbelief*. The wish of verse 105 was negative — no good descending on the believers; this wish is reversalist — the believers' good undone, the faith unmade, the conversion rolled back to its starting point. The direction is the verse's diagnostic: the object is not the Muslims' property, position, or life but their *belief after their believing*, the one possession whose loss is total. And the verse marks its subjects carefully — *many*, not all; the remnant-grammar the recital keeps running, the exceptions preserved inside every indictment so that the charge remains moral rather than racial. The exegetes supply the occasion in a report that makes the mechanism concrete: after the Battle of Uḥud, some who had embraced Islam from among the Jews were taunted by their former co-religionists about their condition — the defeat read aloud as a verdict on the conversion itself — and the taunting turned some away from Islam and back to Judaism (the reports of Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī converge here). The weapon was speech: not argument but ridicule deployed at the moment of the community's visible weakness, the reversal-engineered by pressing on the convert's new identity until it bent. The verse files the technique so that its audience recognizes every later application — the taunt timed to a setback, the reversal offered as consolation, the envy operating through the tongue.

**Because of Envy, After Clarity**

Two clauses fix the wish's origin and its culpability, and both are load-bearing. *Because of their envy* — the engine named, in their souls: not conviction, not concern for the believers' error, not loyalty to their own tradition's truth-claims, but ḥasad, the pain at another's good and the wish for its removal. The recital traced the same engine at verse 90 — resentment at Allah's grace descending where He wills — and the two verses describe one movement at two altitudes: there, the theological form, the objection to the Giver's freedom; here, the social form, the wish operating on neighbors. *After the truth has been made clear to them* — the aggravator: the envy is informed, its object recognized as truth before being resented. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān files the same sequence as the People of the Book's division-point: they differed *after the clear proofs had come to them, out of mutual envy* (3:19). The clarity makes the envy culpable in the recital's epistemology — the same principle verse 75 applied to distortion after understanding: knowledge does not prevent the sin but constitutes its specific weight. The tradition situates this envy at the head of its genealogy: Satan's refusal, whose recorded reasoning is the first *I am better than he is* (38:76), is read in the tradition as envy's founding act — and the verse's subjects are enrolled in that lineage, wishing backward what the first wisher wished against: the removal of a good Allah had bestowed on another.

**The Test Predicted, and the Response Commanded**

The Qur'an does not leave the taunt-technique undocumented; it predicts the technique and prescribes the counter. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the prediction as a certainty: *"You ˹believers˺ will surely be tested in your wealth and yourselves, and you will certainly hear many hurtful words from those who were given the Scripture before you and ˹from˺ the polytheists. But if you are patient and mindful ˹of Allah˺—surely this is a resolve to aspire to"* (3:186). The verse pairs the two camps the way verse 105 paired them — Scripture-people and polytheists, one coalition of hurtful speech — and names the response's two components: patience, the refusal to let the taunt dictate the tempo, and mindfulness, the Godward attention that keeps the hurt from becoming the heart's center. Verse 109 issues the same prescription in imperative form: *pardon and bear with them*. The two commands divide the work — pardon addresses the injury already inflicted, the release of the debt the taunter owes; bearing-with addresses the relationship's continuation, the tolerance that keeps the community in the field rather than exiting in anger. The recital's audience was being equipped for a specific vulnerability: the new community would face its reversals, its Uḥuds, and the reversal-agents would be waiting at each one with the taunt. The prescribed posture is not passive but strategic — pardon and patience held *until Allah delivers His decision*, the forbearance of a party that knows the case is scheduled, not abandoned.

**The Abrogation Debate, and Why It Fails Here**

The legal tradition debated whether this verse's command to pardon and bear was later superseded, and the debate belongs to the verse's reception. One stream of the exegetical record holds that the command was abrogated by the sword-verses — by the ruling on the jizyah at 9:29, on some accounts, or by the first permission of fighting at 22:39, or by 9:5 on others — reading the verse as a legal ruling of non-resistance, repealed when the community's circumstances changed. The counter-stream, led in the commentary by al-Rāzī, refuses the abrogation on two grounds. First, a command to virtuous conduct — pardon and forbearance as character — is not a ḥukm of the kind naskh operates on; virtue-legislation of this type stands outside abrogation, as verse 106's own boundaries specify. Second, the command does not actually contradict the just-war legislation: even inside the permission to fight, the sūrah's own war-verses (2:190–194) command restraint, proportion, and the virtues this verse names — so no repeal is needed to harmonize them. The weighing favors al-Rāzī, and the broader pattern of the debate confirms it: the expansive view of abrogation summarily repealed dozens of peace-and-forbearance verses wherever fighting was permitted, while the tradition's own careful scholars — Shāh Walī Allāh's count of five genuinely abrogated verses against Ibn al-Jawzī's two hundred forty-seven — show how much of the maximal list is reconciliation failure rather than actual replacement. The verse survives its debate with its command intact: pardon and bear are the community's standing posture toward the taunt, and the decision the verse awaits belongs to Allah's timing, not to the believer's repeal of his own virtue.

**Until Allah Delivers His Decision**

The command's horizon is the verse's third instruction: *until Allah delivers His decision*. The exegetes read the decision in its historical forms — the military outcomes that ended the taunting parties' capacity to taunt, the settlements that followed the community's consolidation, or the legal status later legislated for the People of the Book — and in its general form, the divine disposal that every case eventually receives. The clause's function is to time-limit the posture without ending it: pardon and bearing are commanded *until*, which makes them a phase of confidence rather than a permanent condition of weakness, and the confidence's ground is the verse's final sentence: *surely Allah is Most Capable of everything*. The capability-clause answers the taunt at its root. The reversal-agents pressed on the community's visible weakness after Uḥud; the verse answers that the community's case is held by the Power whose capability verse 107 just grounded in the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. Forbearance under a capable Ally is strategy; forbearance without one is capitulation — and the verse supplies the Ally in its closing name. The Muslims hearing the recitation were given, in one verse, the complete equipment for the envy-war: the diagnosis (reversal wished, from envy, after clarity), the prediction (hurtful words, certain, from both camps), the posture (pardon, bear, stand), and the horizon (a decision coming, held by capability over everything). The taunt's power is timing — it strikes when the taunted feels abandoned; the verse removes the timing's premise, and with it the taunt's whole mechanism.
