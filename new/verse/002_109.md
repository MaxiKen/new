## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:109

> Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wish Described**

The verse reports a desire rather than an act, and the sūrah has been handling this subject for several verses. The previous sentence warned the believers against demanding signs; this one tells them what to expect from those who had declined the message. The two are connected, since a community being warned about its own conduct is also being prepared for the pressure it will face from outside.

The Book describes the wish as envy, and the diagnosis matches what the sūrah said earlier about resentment that grace had been given to another people. The objection to the revelation is presented here in its emotional form, and the sentence states that the envy persisted after the truth had been made clear.

**The Occasion Reported**

Commentators connected the verse with a specific episode, in which Muslims who had formerly belonged to the Jewish community were taunted by their former coreligionists after a setback in battle, and some of them were drawn back to their earlier religion. The reports explain the tone of the instruction that follows, which is directed at a community under pressure rather than at one being rebuked.

Al-Rāzī and others noted that the verse applies more broadly than the occasion, since the wish it describes is not confined to one group or one moment. The Book's addresses often have a specific setting and a general function, and this sentence is written in a way that allows both. They account for the tone of what follows, since the command is given to a community under pressure rather than to one that needs rebuking. The Book's addresses frequently carry a particular setting and a lasting purpose, and this sentence is worded so that both are present.

**Envy After Clarity**

The phrase about the truth having become clear appears in the sūrah's account of the community's recognition of the messenger, and it recurs here to underline the nature of the offence. What the verse describes is not the envy of people who doubted the revelation, but of people who knew what they were resisting.

The Book treats knowledge that intensifies opposition as a particular danger, and it describes the same condition in other passages about communities that had been given scriptures. The Prophet ﷺ spoke of envy in a well-known report, limiting the acceptable form to the wish to have what another has been given in order to use it well, and the Book's warnings against the other kind are addressed to its own followers as much as to their opponents.

**The Command to Pardon**

The instruction that follows is among the most striking in the passage: pardon and bear with them until God delivers His decision. The communities had been described in the harshest terms, and the response commanded is forbearance rather than retaliation, with the matter left to God.

The sūrah uses the same instruction elsewhere when it addresses the Prophet's conduct toward those who opposed him, and the Book's treatment of hostility against the believers generally reserves the response to God. What the command does not do is require the believers to accept the behaviour; it requires them to endure it without making themselves the judges of it. The same command is given elsewhere in the Book, where the Prophet's conduct toward his opponents is guided by it, and the handling of hostility toward the believers is generally left to God. The command does not require the believers to accept the behaviour; it requires them to endure it without making themselves the judges of it.

**The Discussion About Abrogation**

The scholars discussed whether the command to pardon was later abrogated by the verses that permitted fighting, and they cited the passage about the tax paid by the People of the Book and the verse that grants permission to those who are being fought. The reports differ on which verse first authorized fighting and on whether this instruction was affected at all.

Al-Rāzī argued that the command concerns a virtuous disposition rather than a legal ruling, and that a virtue is not the kind of thing that abrogation affects. He pointed out that the Book commands patience and forbearance even in the conduct of war, and that the later verses about fighting do not contradict an instruction to endure provocation while the decision belongs to God.

**Until Allah Delivers His Decision**

The clause that closes the instruction places the timing of relief in God's hands, and the Book uses this formulation for matters that the believers cannot resolve by their own efforts. The sūrah's earlier verses have described communities that took their affairs into their own hands and violated their own covenants; this one tells the believers to do the opposite.

The ending also preserves the possibility that the decision will take a form no one expects. What the verse asks for is patience while the matter is open, and its closing attribute states that the One who will decide is capable of everything, which the sūrah has just said in the previous sentence. The clause places the timing of relief in God's hands, and the Book uses this formulation for matters the believers cannot resolve by their own efforts. It also leaves open the form the decision may take, which no one can predict.

**What the Passage Teaches About Opposition**

The verse supplies the believers with a model for living alongside people who resent them. The Book records the wish, identifies its cause, and instructs the community to endure it, leaving the outcome to God. That combination of clear diagnosis and restrained response runs through the sūrah's treatment of the People of the Book, and it is consistent with the invitation to debate with wisdom and kind advice that the Book gives elsewhere. What the verse supplies is a perspective rather than a policy, and the response to the attitude it describes is set out in the verses that follow. The commands to prayer and almsgiving give the believers work they can do while the matter remains in God's hands.

**Forbearance Until the Decision Arrives**

The instruction to pardon is paired with a time limit that no human being controls, and the pairing gives the command its character. The believers are not told that the hostility will end or that its authors will change; they are told to endure it while the matter is open. That arrangement places the community's conduct outside the outcome of the dispute, since patience is required whether or not relief comes. The Book's later verses about fighting do not contradict the instruction, as al-Rāzī argued, because the command concerns a disposition rather than a rule of war, and the Book requires the same virtues of those who defend themselves. What the verse therefore teaches is that a community's forbearance is not a concession to its opponents but an expression of its confidence that the decision belongs to God.
