## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:33

> And when Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival. They reassured ˹him˺, “Do not fear, nor grieve. We will surely deliver you and your family—except your wife, who is one of the doomed.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guest Who Was Also a Warning**

The envoys move from Abraham's household to Lot's, and the Book records the second arrival in terms of the host's alarm rather than the visitors' dignity. Lot sees young men arrive at his door in a town whose reputation he knows, and his first reaction is distress. The narrative does not present him as a man who failed to recognize God's messengers and needed to be corrected; it presents him as a man who understood his neighbours, and whose immediate concern was for the safety of people under his roof. The Book treats that concern as honourable, and it structures the following sentence as an answer to it. The scene therefore opens with a host terrified for his guests, which is precisely the virtue that the town around him had destroyed.

**Why the Arrival Worried Him**

The Book supplies the reason elsewhere when it describes the men of the town rushing to Lot's house, accustomed as they were to shameful deeds, and records his plea that they respect his guests and his offer of his daughters in marriage instead (11:78). His alarm in the present verse is the alarm of a man who has lived long enough in the place to know what an unfamiliar face means there. The narrative's timing is also worth noting: the visitors arrive in the evening, and the danger will be present within hours. The Book does not describe the intervening events in this chapter; it states the host's fear and the guests' reassurance, and it lets the reader supply the rest from the fuller account in Sūrah Hūd. What the reader is meant to register here is the human position of a prophet in a city that had made safety impossible.

**The Two Griefs the Reassurance Addresses**

The words spoken to Lot are brief and they cover two distinct conditions: do not fear, and do not grieve. Commentators read the first as directed at the danger that has just walked into his house, and the second as directed at the fate that he already suspects is coming for his people, and for the member of his own household who is part of that people. The Book's reassurances to its prophets often address fear and sorrow as a pair, because the two conditions have different remedies: fear concerns what may happen, and grief concerns what cannot be undone. What the envoys offer is the removal of both, and they attach the offer to something concrete, which is a promise about who will be delivered.

The Book is careful elsewhere to separate a household's rescue from a claim about the beliefs of everyone inside it, and it names the wives of Noah and Lot as examples of people who were joined to prophets by marriage and still ended on the other side of the line (66:10). Nothing in this verse requires the reader to assume that every person carried out of the town shared Lot's convictions; what it reports is the saving of a household, with the exception named in the same sentence. The Book's accounts of families are consistently written that way, and the reader who assumes otherwise will find the exception incomprehensible.

**Deliverance Announced Before It Happens**

The promise is stated flatly and in advance: you and your family will be delivered, with one exception. The Book's habit of announcing rescue before the event is consistent across its narratives, and the announcement is what turns a catastrophe into a deliverance for the people concerned. A reader who has followed the chapter's argument about trial will notice that the deliverance described here does not arrive before the danger; it arrives inside the danger, and it is spoken to a man standing in his own doorway at night. The sequence matches the pattern established with Noah, whose passengers were saved inside the flood rather than removed from the landscape, and the sūrah has been building toward this correspondence since its opening verses about the tested believer.

**The Exception Repeated Across the Accounts**

The wife of Lot is named as an exception in every version of the story, and the Book's insistence on the detail has a purpose that is greater than biography. A reader who assumes that proximity to a prophet guarantees safety is being corrected in advance of the disaster, and the correction is made by God's own envoys rather than by an interpreter. Sūrah al-Taḥrīm states the principle directly, citing the wives of Noah and Lot as examples set for those who disbelieve: each was married to a righteous servant of God, each betrayed him, and the standing of the husbands was of no use to them against God (66:10). The exception in this verse is therefore a doctrine about the limits of kinship, delivered on the last night of a city's existence.

Two fears are being answered at once. Lot dreads what his own townspeople will do to guests he cannot protect, and he dreads the ruin of a place where he has lived his whole life among people he knew by name. The reply addresses both without separating them, since it tells him that the visitors are not his responsibility to defend and that the town's fate is already settled. A reader who takes the verse as a report of angelic procedure will miss the human shape of it, which is the shape of a man receiving two kinds of relief in one sentence.

**The Instructions Given in Sūrah Hūd**

Sūrah Hūd completes the scene with the practical orders that belong to the rescue: Lot is told to travel with his family in the dark of night, not to let anyone look back, and that the appointment is the morning, with the question added of whether the morning is not near (11:81). The instructions confirm everything the sūrah's account implies. The family was not delivered by remaining where it was and being shielded; it was delivered by moving, under cover of night, at the cost of leaving home. The sūrah under study keeps the promise and omits the journey, because the chapter's subject is the promise and the pressure that produced it. Taken together, the two accounts give the tested believer the same answer from two directions: the rescue is certain, and it does not come without a departure.

Announcing the outcome before the event is one of the Book's standing practices, and its purpose is visible in the effect it has on the person receiving it. Lot was told that his household would be saved while he was still standing in a room with men he had failed to protect, so that the reassurance arrived before his own eyes could confirm it. The same arrangement appears wherever the Book prepares a believer for a loss he cannot prevent, and it places the comfort on the side of a promise rather than on the side of the visible facts.

**A Household That Was Not Entirely on One Road**

The exception attached to the promise also settles something the chapter has been developing about families. Earlier verses required a believer to honour parents who might press him toward idolatry, and later verses will describe harmony that holds a town together and descendants who carry a message. This night shows the limit of the arrangement from the other side: a prophet's own household contained someone who was not with him, and the rescue was drawn around her absence rather than stretched to include her. Faith in the Book passes through families without being guaranteed by them, and the sūrah's sequence of scenes about parents, kin, and descendants makes its point by holding both halves of that truth at once.
