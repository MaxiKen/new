## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:107

> Had Allah willed, they would not have been polytheists. We have not appointed you as their keeper, nor are you their maintainer.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Statement of Divine Will**

Had Allah willed, they would not have been polytheists. The sentence states that the existence of the audience's idolatry was permitted by God's will, and the sūrah has used the same form of statement elsewhere. It said that had God willed, the whole of humanity would have been one community, and it said that had God willed, no one would have associated partners with Him.

The Book's use of this statement is worth distinguishing from a claim the audience would later make themselves. In a verse near the end of this sūrah, the idolaters are recorded as pleading that if God had willed they would not have associated partners with Him or with Him forbidden anything, and the Book describes that argument as a lie (6:148). The Book makes the same claim as an acknowledgement of God's sovereignty and rejects it when it is offered by the idolaters as a justification for their conduct.

**Why the Two Uses Differ**

The difference between the two uses lies in who is speaking and to what end. When God states that the idolaters would not have disbelieved had He willed it, the sentence establishes His freedom and the inability of His creatures to compel anything. When the idolaters state the same fact, they use it to deny their own responsibility, and the sūrah's account of the final reckoning shows what that claim is worth.

The sūrah of the Bee records the same argument from the idolaters, who said that had God willed, neither they nor their ancestors would have worshipped anything besides Him (16:35), and the Book's answer is that the messengers were sent with clear proofs and that the communities' rejection did not alter the obligation to receive them. The verse in front of us belongs to that pattern, and it explicitly places the responsibility for rejection on the people who chose it, since the Book's very next words describe the Prophet's relationship to them as something other than supervision.

**The Keeper and the Maintainer**

We have not appointed you as their keeper, nor are you their maintainer. The verse names two roles and denies both to the Prophet, and the choice of the two words covers the two ways a person might imagine the messenger's authority. The keeper is the one set over a people to direct and manage them; the maintainer is the one responsible for their affairs and answerable for their condition.

The sūrah has already told the Prophet to say that he is not a keeper over the audience (6:104), and the repetition of the term in this verse joins the two passages as one teaching. The Book's description of the messenger's office elsewhere uses the vocabulary of reminding and warning rather than governance and responsibility: the sūrah of the Covering says that the Prophet is only a reminder and not a controller over the people (88:21–22), and the sūrah of the Prophets says that He was sent as a mercy to the worlds rather than as an enforcer of belief (21:107).

**The Comfort Contained in the Denial**

The sentence is addressed to a man whose people had rejected him, and its purpose is partly to lift a burden. The sūrah opened with the Prophet's grief over their refusal and its record of their arguments, and it has returned at several points to the fact that he does not carry their answer. The denial of the two roles removes from him the responsibility that his own compassion had put on him.

The Book returns to this reassurance in the passages where the Prophet's distress over his people is described. It tells him not to grieve over them and not to be distressed by what they plot, and it tells him that his duty is only the clear delivery of the message. The verse in front of us is one of the places where that reassurance is stated in terms of what God has not appointed him to be.

**The Order of the Two Verses**

Read with the verse that precedes it, the passage gives the Prophet three instructions and two denials. He is to follow what is revealed, to hold the confession, and to turn away from the polytheists; he is not their keeper and he is not their maintainer. The arrangement states what he is to do and then removes the anxiety that would otherwise have accompanied the doing of it.

That order is repeated in the Book's instructions to messengers at several points. The sūrah of the Anbiyā' and the sūrah of Hūd describe the messengers as delivering the message and leaving the outcome to God, and the sūrah of the Forgiver records a believer's appeal to his people with the words that the messengers have no duty other than clear notification. The sūrah has made the point concrete in a city where the messenger lived among the people who denied him.

**What the Verse Says About the Audience's Freedom**

The verse does not remove the audience's responsibility, since it is followed by verses that continue to address their conduct, their oaths, their insults, and their arguments. What the sentence establishes is that God has left the people to their own decisions in the present life, with the messengers sent to inform them of what those decisions entail.

The Book states the same arrangement in the sūrah of the Cave, where the truth is said to come from the Lord and each person is left to believe or disbelieve (18:29). The two verses that stand here combine that freedom with the messenger's own limits, and the sūrah proceeds to the next instruction, which concerns how the Prophet and the believers should speak about the gods the idolaters venerate.
