## Sūrah an-Naml 27:92

> and to recite the Quran.” Then whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good. But whoever chooses to stray, say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a warner.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Command**

The previous verse listed two instructions — worship and submission — and this one completes the set with a third: *wa an atluwa al-Qur'ān*, and to recite the Qur'an. The three together are the whole of what the Prophet reports being ordered.

The brevity of the list is its point. Nothing about building a community, defeating opponents, or securing acceptance appears in it. Two of the three items concern his relationship to God and the third concerns his relationship to the text. The chapter that opened by calling these the verses of the clear Book closes by identifying the reciting of that Book as the whole of what its reciter was told to do with it.

**Reciting, Not Enforcing**

The verb is *tilāwah*, reading aloud or reciting, and the absent verbs matter as much as the present one. He is not commanded to explain, to defend, to convince or to impose. He is commanded to say the words.

That narrowness follows directly from what verses 80 and 81 established. If there are people who cannot be made to hear, then the effectiveness of the delivery is not a variable the deliverer controls, and defining his duty by an outcome would make it undischargeable. Defining it by the act makes it something he can complete every day. Al-Rāzī's reading of the earlier verses, that relieving the Prophet of responsibility for belief strengthens his ability to declare the truth, has its practical expression here.

**"Whoever Is Guided Is Guided for Himself"**

The clause states a principle the Qur'an repeats in several chapters: the benefit of guidance accrues to the person guided, not to God and not to the messenger.

The form of words is precise — *fa-innamā yahtadī li-nafsih*, he is guided only for his own soul. Related verses make the negative side explicit, saying that whoever is grateful is grateful for his own benefit and that God is beyond need of him. What this removes is any sense that acceptance is a favour done to the one calling. A preacher who believes otherwise will inevitably start bargaining, and the chapter has already shown what bargaining looks like: Sheba's queen sent a gift to see whether Sulaymān could be bought, and he refused it on precisely this ground, saying what God had given him was better than what she offered.

**The Asymmetry in the Sentence**

Look at how the two halves are built. For the guided, a statement of fact in the third person. For the astray, an instruction to the Prophet: *say, I am only a warner.*

The imbalance is informative. The guided need no reply from him at all; their case is described and closed. The straying require a response, and the response he is given is not an argument but a declaration of his own limits. This is the last of several places where the chapter hands him something to say in a difficult exchange — verse 64 gave him a demand for proof, verse 65 a statement of what nobody knows, verse 72 an answer to the demand for a date. The sūrah equips him for conversations rather than for victories.

**"Only a Warner"**

*Innamā anā min al-mundhirīn* — I am only one of the warners. The restrictive particle appears for the third time in two verses, and the description occurs throughout the Qur'an.

A warner has a defined and limited office: he states what is coming and the matter passes out of his hands. That framing has been at work in this chapter's histories. Ṣāliḥ warned Thamūd and then watched them plot. Lūṭ's people were described as those who had been warned, and the rain that fell on them was named accordingly. Neither prophet compelled anyone. The word also carries a note the English can lose — a warner is someone acting in your interest, which is why the same root gives the Qur'an its description of a community that receives notice before consequence.

**One of the Warners**

The phrasing includes a detail worth drawing out. He does not say *I am a warner* but *I am one of the warners*, placing himself within a company.

This matches the previous verse, where he was commanded to be among those who submit rather than to be their head. Twice in two sentences the Prophet is located inside a group rather than above one, and the chapter has spent fifty verses populating that group: Mūsā at the fire, Dāwūd and Sulaymān given knowledge, Ṣāliḥ sent to his brothers, Lūṭ addressing his people. The claim being made at the chapter's close is one of continuity with them, which is the same claim verse 76 made about the Book and the disputes of the Children of Israel.

**Guidance and Straying Both Described as Choices**

The translation renders the two clauses as choosing to be guided and choosing to stray, and the Arabic verbs support that reading. Both are in forms that describe the person's own action rather than something done to him.

That matters given what the chapter said at verse 4, where deeds were made attractive to those who do not believe in the Hereafter so that they wander blindly. Read alone, that verse could suggest the outcome was arranged in advance. Read with this one, the sequence is clear: the disbelief comes first and the wandering follows it. An-Naml puts the two statements eighty-eight verses apart and does not reconcile them explicitly, but the closing verse's grammar returns the initiative to the person. The chapter's last word on the matter leaves him holding it.
