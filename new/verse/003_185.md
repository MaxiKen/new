## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:185

> Every soul will taste death. And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment. Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will ˹indeed˺ triumph, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence About Death**

The verse opens with the declaration that every soul will taste death, and the sentence appears in two other sūrahs in almost the same words. The commentators note that the Arabic construction also permits the sense that every soul is a taster of death, which suggests that death is not only an event at the end of a life but a condition that touches a person throughout it.

The verse is the sūrah's third statement on the subject, following the declaration that no soul dies except by God's permission and the reminder that those destined for death would have gone to the places where they were to die. The repetition in the course of the passage is deliberate, and the sentence in this verse gives the teaching its widest form.

**The Full Reward on the Day of Resurrection**

The verse states that the reward will be paid in full on the Day of Resurrection, and the phrase echoes the sūrah's earlier statements about the exactness of the settlement. The community had been told that every soul would be paid what it earned and would not be wronged, and this verse repeats the assurance in a form that covers all people.

The sentence places the completion of the account at a definite point, which is the resurrection rather than the moment of death. The Book's teaching in these passages is that the full settlement waits until every player in the story has been gathered, and the community's patience in this life is connected to that arrangement.

**The Definition of Triumph**

The verse states that whoever is kept away from the fire and admitted to the garden has triumphed, and the definition rearranges the meaning of success. The community had been measuring its fortunes in terms of the events of the battlefield and the position of its opponents, and the sentence supplies a different measure.

The Book uses the word for triumph in its descriptions of the reward, and it uses it in the verse about the exchange of lives and wealth for the garden. The sentence in this verse is the clearest statement of the Book's understanding of success: it consists of two events, one of them the avoidance of the destination the sūrah has been warning about throughout.

**The Book's Other Statements**

The Book declares the passing of everything that exists in several places. One passage states that everything is bound to perish except God's own self, and another declares that all that is upon the earth will pass away while the face of the Lord remains.

The verses about the tasting of death in other sūrahs add that humanity is tested with good and evil and that all will be returned to God. Read together with the verse before the reader, these statements describe a world in which nothing lasting is held by human hands, and they make the sentence about triumph a statement about what survives.

**The Life of This World as Delusion**

The verse closes by describing the life of this world as nothing but the enjoyment of a delusion, and the word carries the senses of vanity, deception, and peril. The Book uses the same phrase in the sūrah of Iron, where it compares the world to rain that produces a crop the planters admire until it withers, and it describes the Hereafter in that passage as either severe punishment or forgiveness and pleasure.

Another passage states that the present life is play and amusement and that the home of the Hereafter is better for those who are mindful. A third states that the Hereafter is the real life. The verse before the reader is the sūrah's own statement of the contrast, and the word it uses for the world's attraction is the one that describes being taken in.

**The Report About the Garden**

The commentary tradition records a statement attributed to the Prophet that a space in the garden the size of a whip's length is better than this world and everything in it. The report is used in the commentaries to illustrate the verse's judgement about the value of the two lives.

The sentence in the verse makes the same point in the Book's own words. What the community had been fighting over was of the order of the world, and the sentence about the garden's value places that order in a scale that does not require the community to despise its own life in order to assess it correctly.

**The Verse in the Community's Memory**

The verse has been among the most quoted in the community's life, and al-Qushayrī observed that the cup of death has been placed in the hand of every living being, so that a person either drinks it well and finds bliss or drinks it with a grimace and a wail. The recitation of the verse at funerals is a long-established practice.

The sentence is short enough to be carried in memory and complete enough to be used on its own. The community that recites it on such occasions is reminded of the destination of every soul and of the standard by which the outcome is measured, and the two ideas are given in the order the verse presents them.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse gathers the sūrah's teaching about death, reward, and the value of the present life into a single statement, and it does so at the point where the sūrah turns to its final instructions. The community is told what it will experience, where the accounting takes place, what success consists of, and what the world it was fighting over amounts to.

Those four claims form the basis for the conduct the sūrah asks of its readers in the passages that follow. The verses ahead deal with generosity, truthfulness, and the community's dealings with one another, and the sentence about the tombstone's worth of difference between the two lives is the ground on which all of those instructions rest.
