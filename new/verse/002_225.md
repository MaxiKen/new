## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:225

> Allah will not hold you accountable for unintentional oaths, but for what you intended in your hearts. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath of Habit, Excused**

The verse draws the first great distinction of the oath-block: God does not take you to task for the *laghw* in your oaths — the careless, the unmeant, the words that pass the tongue without the will's signature. The exegetes' primary identification is sociolinguistic: the habit of swearing by God — wa'Llāh — as a filler of ordinary speech, an emphasis-reflex woven into daily conversation. The practice was common among the Arabs, the commentators note, and continues today among non-Arab Muslims and non-Muslim Arabs alike — a linguistic fossil of the same habit the verse addresses. The exemption covers it entirely: the tongue's reflex is not the heart's pledge, and God does not audit the grammar of custom. The distinction protects both the law and the language: without it, every conversational wa'Llāh would carry the oath's legal weight, the expiation-rulings would engulf daily speech, and the serious oath would be indistinguishable from the tic. With it, the sworn word keeps its gravity precisely because its trivial uses are pardoned — the verse's leniency is what makes its strictness elsewhere meaningful.

**The Honest Mistake, and the Angry Word**

Two further cases were gathered under the exemption by the commentators. The first: a person swears to something he believes true — I did not do it, he was there, the goods are mine — and later discovers the belief was wrong. The oath was sincere, the assertion false, and the verse exempts it: no expiation falls on the man whose heart meant truth and whose information failed. The second case al-Qurṭubī records: oaths taken in anger are excluded from the tasking — the word sworn in the heat that the cooled heart never willed. The two extensions share a principle with the habit-exemption: the oath's legal substance is the heart's commitment, not the mouth's output. Where the mouth ran ahead of knowledge (the honest mistake), ahead of temper (the angry oath), or ahead of intention entirely (the conversational reflex), the verse declines to hold the person to the sound. The jurisprudence this generated distinguishes the laghw from the muʿaqadah — the oath knotted in earnest, which the sūrah of the Table's legislation binds with its expiation of feeding, clothing, freeing, or fasting (5:89) — and the line between the two runs exactly where this verse draws it: through the heart.

**What Your Hearts Have Earned**

The verse's second half states the positive rule: He takes you to task for what your hearts have earned. The earning-language — kasb — is the sūrah's accounting term: the portion from what they earned settled the two prayers (2:202), the hypocrite's corruption was his striving's earn, the believer's good is sent ahead as earnings. Here the heart itself is the earning organ, and the oaths it knots are its kasb: the deliberate pledge, the intended commitment, the sworn word that the will stands behind. The verse thereby relocates the oath's jurisdiction from the tongue to the heart — a move consistent with the whole domestic legislation's surveillance-theology, from the guardian's intention known to God (2:220) to the concealment of the womb forbidden before the Last Day's reckoning (2:228). The heart's earnings are the real ledger: the reflex-swear adds nothing to it, the knotted oath adds its full weight, and the expiation-rulings that follow exist to let the heart unwind what the heart bound. The sūrah's final verse will legislate the same interiority as its closing principle — God does not charge a soul beyond its capacity, and the accounting runs on what the soul earned and sent ahead (2:286) — with this verse as its early statement in the grammar of swearing.

**Forgiving, Clement**

The names that close the verse are chosen for its subject with unusual precision: God is Forgiving — and Clement, Forbearing, ḥalīm. The forgiveness answers the laghw directly: the careless words are not merely unprosecuted but forgiven, covered by a name whose function is the absolution of shortfalls. The forbearance is the deeper fit. The ḥalīm is the One who does not answer immediately, who bears with the creature's rashness, whose punishment is delayed while the opportunity to return remains — and the verse's whole matter is rash speech: the tongue's reflexes, the anger's oaths, the honest errors. A strict auditor would task every uttered name; the Clement bears with the distance between human speech and human intent, and legislates from that bearing. The pairing also completes the oath-block's movement toward its hardest case: the forgiving and forbearing names stand here, before the īlāʾ-oath against the wife (2:226–227), so that the four-months legislation that follows is received inside clemency rather than severity — the oath that harms a marriage is bounded by a God who first declared His forbearance with the oaths that harm nothing.

**The Speech-Legislation's Inner Court**

Read with its neighbors, the verse forms the oath-block's inner court. The verse before governed the oath aimed at good — God's name made a barrier against righteousness, taqwā, and peacemaking, with the Hearing and Knowing presiding (2:224). This verse governs the oath aimed at nothing — the habit, the mistake, the heat — with the Forgiving and Clement presiding. The verse after governs the oath aimed at a marriage — the forswearing of the wife, bounded and forced to resolve (2:226–227). Across the three rulings the sūrah legislates the full spectrum of the sworn word by its object: against good, against meaning, against a person — and calibrates both the ruling and the divine names to each. The spectrum's lesson is the heart's jurisdiction throughout: the barrier-oath is condemned because the heart wields the name against the good; the laghw-oath is excused because the heart is absent from the words; the īlāʾ-oath is bounded because the heart's harm cannot be allowed to run without term. The tongue is legislated exactly in proportion to the heart's presence in its speech — and the Forgiving and Clement names assure the community that the proportion is kept by a mercy that has already, in this very verse, pardoned the vast majority of what human tongues do with the name of God.

**The Verse's Standing Comfort**

The exemption has carried pastoral weight across the tradition's life precisely because the habit it excuses is universal. The believer who counts his casual oaths and fears their tally is answered by this verse before he asks: the wa'Llāh of conversation is not on the ledger. The person tortured by a remembered oath sworn to a false belief is answered: the honest heart is not tasked. The man haunted by words sworn in rage is answered: the anger's output is excluded. The verse's comfort is not leniency for its own sake but the accuracy of divine audit — God takes the heart's earnings and leaves the tongue's noise — and the accuracy is itself the mercy: an audit that counted reflexes would drive the scrupulous to despair, and the sūrah's whole project (the hope of 2:218, the ease of 2:185, the latitude of 2:220) refuses despair as a legislative outcome. The Clement's forbearance with human speech is the atmosphere in which the serious oath can be serious: because the careless is forgiven, the knotted can bind; because the tongue's dust is pardoned, the heart's pledges carry weight — and the community learns to swear rarely, mean always, and expiate when meaning must be unwound.
