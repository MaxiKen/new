## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:225

> Allah will not hold you accountable for unintentional oaths, but for what you intended in your hearts. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Distinction Drawn**

The verse states that God will not hold the community accountable for what is unintentional in its oaths but will hold it accountable for what the heart has earned, and the sentence draws a distinction between two kinds of swearing. The ruling follows the prohibition of the previous verse and supplies the boundary of the community's responsibility.

The Book's treatment of the subject separates the word that escapes a person from the intention that accompanies it, and the community's teachers read the sentence as a statement about where the accountability lies. Readers are shown that the law is concerned with what a person means rather than with the sound of what he says in passing.

**The Unintentional Oaths**

The commentators explained the phrase in several ways. Some read it as a reference to the habit of pronouncing an oath in ordinary speech without intending to swear, which was common among the people addressed, and others read it as a description of a person who swears to something he believes to be true and then discovers that he was mistaken.

The readings agree that the oath is not the kind on which a person's account rests, and the community's scholars applied the distinction in the rulings they compiled on the subject. Readers are given the account of a practice that the Book declines to punish, and the sentence's first clause supplies the assurance that the community needed.

**The Oaths Held Accountable**

The sentence states that God holds the community accountable for what the hearts have earned, and the phrase places the matter with the intention that accompanied the oath. The community's teachers read the clause as a statement about the deliberate swearing that the Book elsewhere describes as binding.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere speaks of the oaths that have been pledged in earnest, and the community's scholars connected the two passages. The passage shows that the distinction within the sentence is between the casual invocation and the vow by which a person commits himself, and the sentence's second clause supplies the standard by which the community's conduct is judged.

**The Reports About the Practice**

The reports describe the speech of the community in which the divine name was used for emphasis without the intention of swearing an oath, and the commentators noted that the habit continued in the speech of the people addressed. Other reports mention the oaths taken in anger, and the community's scholars read the sentence as a statement that such an oath does not bind the speaker.

The material shows how the community applied the rule, and the teachers used it when they discussed the expiation that a broken oath requires. The account describes a community whose ordinary speech was full of such invocations, and the sentence in this place supplies the line that the Book draws between what is serious and what is not.

**The Names at the End**

The sentence closes with the description of God as forgiving and forbearing, and the community's scholars read the pair as the ground of the distinction the sentence has drawn. The first name addresses the faults that the community commits, and the second describes the gentleness with which the Book treats them.

The community's teachers noted that the closing attributes match the leniency of the ruling, and they used the sentence when they spoke about the mercy that accompanies the law. The passage shows that the Book's legislation on oaths begins with an assurance, and the sentence's ending supplies the disposition in which the community is to receive the ruling.

**The Verse in the Passage**

The sentence stands between the prohibition of oaths that obstruct good and the rules about the separation of spouses, and it belongs to a section that governs the community's commitments. The Book has addressed the words by which people bind themselves and now supplies the distinction on which the rulings rest, so that the reader is given the principle before the applications.

The passage's concern is the reliability of a person's word together with the mercy that surrounds it, and the community's teachers read the verses as a single instruction. The sentence in this place supplies the general rule, and the verses that follow treat the oaths by which a man separates himself from his wife and the waiting periods that belong to divorce.

The distinction the sentence draws runs through the law that the community built on the subject, and the jurists arranged the oaths of a person into the kinds the passages describe. The hasty word that slips out in ordinary conversation is not held against the speaker, while the vow that a person intends and utters binds him to what the Book requires.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the distinction between the careless oath and the deliberate one, the location of the community's accountability in the intention, and the attributes of God that accompany the ruling. The community's teachers used the verse when they set out the law of oaths, and the reader is shown that the Book's legislation on speech attends to what a person means rather than only to what he utters.

The verse supplied the framework of the law of oaths in the community, and the jurists referred to it when they classified what a person says and what he intends.
