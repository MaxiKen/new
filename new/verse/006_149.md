## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:149

> Say, “Allah has the most conclusive argument. Had it been His Will, He would have easily imposed guidance upon all of you.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conclusive Argument**

Say, Allah has the most conclusive argument. The verse gives the Prophet the declaration that closes the exchange with the polytheists, and the Book's phrase describes a case that is complete and leaves no room for an answer. The term belongs to the Book's language about proof and disputation, and it states that the argument on God's side has reached its end.

The declaration answers the audience's appeal to the divine will. The audience had argued that God's will lay behind their practices, and the Book replies that God's case is conclusive, which means the point they raised does not leave the matter unresolved. The sūrah's earlier verses have stated the evidence of the created order, the testimony of the earlier scriptures, the mission of the messengers, and the clarity of the Book, and the sentence in front of us gathers the whole into a single declaration.

**The Counterfactual About Guidance**

Had it been His Will, He would have easily imposed guidance upon all of you. The clause states what God could have done, and the Book makes the same statement in several places. The sūrah of Yūnus asks the Prophet whether he would compel people to believe after stating that all the people of the earth would have believed had his Lord willed (10:99); the sūrah of the Adoration says that had God willed, He could have imposed guidance on every soul (32:13); and the sūrah of the Cattle itself, in its account of the Prophet's distress over the denial of his people, states that God could have guided them all had He willed (6:35).

The clause is the answer to the audience's argument in its own terms. If God's will were the standard by which conduct is excused, then the fact that He could have compelled belief shows that the present arrangement is not compulsion. The audience was left free, and the choice they made was theirs.

**What the Book Says About Compelled Belief**

The Book's statements about guidance consistently distinguish between the power to compel and the decision to leave people free. The sūrah of the Cave states that the truth is from the Lord and that whoever wills may believe, and the sūrah of the Bee tells the Prophet that his duty is to invite and that God knows best who has strayed and who is guided (16:125). The declaration in front of us belongs to that account of the relationship between God and human beings.

The Book's treatment of the earlier communities makes the same point from the other direction. The messengers were sent with clear proofs and were rejected, and the Book describes their people as having chosen their course. The sentence in this verse states the possibility of compulsion in order to make clear that the outcome the audience experienced was not compulsion.

**The Place of the Verse in the Exchange**

The verse completes the passage that began with the audience's appeal to God's will (6:148). The audience had argued that they were not responsible for their practices, and the Book's answer runs through the verse that preceded this one and this verse itself. The audience has no knowledge to produce, they follow nothing but assumptions, they do nothing but lie, God's argument is conclusive, and He could have imposed guidance on everyone had He willed.

The exchange therefore ends with the responsibility placed where the sūrah has placed it throughout: the message was delivered, the evidence was presented, and the response belonged to the people who heard it. The Book's claim in the next verse takes up the same subject from a different angle, with the Prophet told to demand witnesses from those who claim that God has forbidden certain things (6:150).

**What the Verse Says About the Audience's Excuse**

The audience's argument was an attempt to transfer responsibility to God, and the declaration in this verse removes the transfer without denying God's sovereignty. The Book affirms that God's will stands over everything and denies that the affirmation relieves the people who made the choice. The two statements are held together in a single sentence, and that arrangement is characteristic of the sūrah's treatment of the subject.

The Book's statements about the day of judgement give the same account. The people who are questioned at the resurrection are questioned about what they did, and the confession they make is their own, as the sūrah's earlier verse described when the assembled jinn and humans testified against themselves (6:130). The verse in front of us states the principle before the scene is described.

**What the Verse Requires of the Reader**

The verse requires the reader to accept both facts: God's power is complete, and human responsibility is real. The sūrah has described the guidance that God grants and the error that He permits, and it has stated throughout that the signs were made clear to people who were capable of receiving them. The declaration in front of us prevents the reader from using either fact to cancel the other.

The Book's addresses to the community return to the pairing repeatedly. The sūrah of the Troops states that God leaves astray whom He wills and guides whom He wills, and it commands the Prophet to remind, since he is only a reminder. The verse in front of us states the same arrangement in the form of a declaration the Prophet is to make in answer to the audience's plea.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse tells the Prophet to demand witnesses who can testify that God has forbidden the things the audience claimed were forbidden, and it instructs him not to testify with them, not to follow the desires of those who deny the proofs, disbelieve in the Hereafter, and set up equals with their Lord (6:150). The demand for witnesses continues the challenge the previous verses began, and it returns the discussion to the dietary rules the audience had invented.

The passage that follows begins the sūrah's long recitation of what God has actually forbidden, running through the commands about parents, children, indecency, life, the property of orphans, honest measure, and the path of God (6:151–153). The verse in front of us closes the debate about the audience's appeal and hands the reader over to the positive statement of the law.
