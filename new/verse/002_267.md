## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:267

> O believers! Donate from the best of what you have earned and of what We have produced for you from the earth. Do not pick out worthless things for donation, which you yourselves would only accept with closed eyes. And know that Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Sources of Wealth**

The verse legislates the material of giving, and its first clause catalogues the community's wealth in two streams: *from the best of what you have earned and of what We have produced for you from the earth*. The earned — mā kasabtum — is the wealth of acquisition: trade, labor, wages, the commercial economy of Medina's market. The produced-from-the-earth is the wealth of harvest: dates, grain, fruit, the agricultural economy the section's parables have been running on. The division is exhaustive for the verse's audience and deliberate in its grammar: the earned is attributed to the earners, and the earth's produce to God — *what We have produced for you* — a small asymmetry that quietly reassigns ownership at the moment the command to give is stated. The commentators read the pairing with the quality-command that governs both: the ṭayyibāt, the good things, are required from each stream alike — the merchant's best lawful profit and the farmer's best date-cluster, one standard across two economies. The verse's spending is thus not a levy on one class of wealth but a rule about quality at every source.

**What Ṭayyib Means**

The good things the verse commands are glossed by the commentators in three registers, and the three together define the standard. The wholesome-and-desirable: what the giver himself counts as good, the choice item rather than the stock. The acceptable: what one would not reject if it were offered — the verse's own second clause makes this the operative test. The lawful: the ḥalāl as against the forbidden, since wealth unlawfully acquired cannot be made good by being given away. The third register carries the tradition's harshest statements: the ḥadīth the exegetes cite here declares that God does not accept charity from unlawfully earned wealth — the servant who amasses wealth by forbidden means and donates it earns nothing by the donation, and the wealth's destination is unchanged; God, in the same report, erases evil with good but does not erase evil with evil. The three glosses interlock into the verse's ethics of the gift: the ṭayyib is simultaneously what the giver values, what the recipient would accept, and what the Law permits — and a donation failing any of the three fails the command, however large the amount.

**The Closed-Eyes Test**

The verse's sharpest instrument is its mirror: *do not pick out worthless things for donation, which you yourselves would only accept with closed eyes*. The ḥadīth-glosses state the principle bluntly — do not feed people what you would not eat yourselves — and the verse builds the test into the act of receiving: the khabīth-thing the giver selects is defined by the imagination of reversal, what the giver would take only with the eyes shut, in distaste, counting the transaction a loss survived. Al-Rāzī supplies the psychology behind the selection: the ideal is giving from the best, and the soul that begins by wanting to part only with the dregs is already on the slope the next verse will describe — the dreg-keeping that becomes the gift-withholding that becomes the giving-up. The closed-eyes test catches the slope at its first step, in the moment of choosing which item leaves the house: the verse does not ask the giver to feel generous but to perform a thought-experiment — would I accept this? — and to let the answer set the minimum. The Prophetic standard behind the test is the ḥadīth of the two Ṣaḥīḥs: no servant believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself — the love-of-self here functioning not as the ego's voice but as the measuring-rod the verse hands the ego.

**The Mosque's Date-Clusters**

Ibn Kathīr records the occasion that gave the verse its social photograph. A group of the Companions brought date-clusters from their palms and hung them in the Prophet's Mosque, on cords or poles, for the poorest of the community — the people of the veranda and the like — to eat from. It was an institution of open charity: the gift displayed where need walked daily. Then others, wishing to join the practice, brought inferior dates — clusters nobody would have chosen — and hung them with the good; and this verse was revealed, its closed-eyes test aimed exactly at the hung bunch. The occasion fixes three things about the legislation: the standard applies to voluntary public charity, not merely to zakāt's assessed dues; the comparison is social — the inferior gift hung beside the good one is a statement about the giver's estimation of the poor; and the mosque-wall is the verse's real setting, the place where the community reads each other's giving. The narrations preserve the rebuke's edge: the poor were to receive what the givers would have hung in their own homes for their own guests — and the verse makes the guest-standard the law.

**Piety's Price**

The verse's command has its summit-statement elsewhere in the Qur'an, and the commentators quote it here as the standard this verse legislates below: *You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish* (3:92). The relation between the two verses is the relation between the floor and the height — the Baqarah verse forbids the worthless and commands the good; the ʿImrān verse names the righteousness that begins where the cherished is released. The tradition preserves the Companions' response to that summit in the reports around its revelation — Abū Ṭalḥah's immediate gift of his most beloved property, his garden of Bayrūḥāʾ, upon hearing it — and the verse here builds the road to such responses by closing the opposite road: the soul that habituates itself to selecting the closed-eyes item trains its capacity for the cherished-release downward. The exegetes' moral-psychology of the section depends on this training-logic: giving is a faculty strengthened or atrophied by what it is repeatedly given, and the worthless-selection is the atrophy's daily exercise. The command of the good is therefore not fastidiousness about the poor's receipt — they would eat the inferior dates — but curriculum for the giver's soul.

**Self-Sufficient, Praised**

The closing names answer the verse's whole demand with the demand's own paradox: God commands the best and needs none of it. Ghaniyy — Self-Sufficient — states the needlessness: the ṭayyib-command enriches nothing divine, and the giver who calculates that God requires his good dates has misread the name. Ḥamīd — the Praised, the Praiseworthy — states where the giving's praise terminates: not in the giver's reputation (the rock's dust, 2:264), not in the recipient's gratitude (the reminder's collection, 2:262), but in the One whose praise is self-subsistent and who alone returns the gift's worth. The pair governs the quality-standard precisely: because God is Ghaniyy, the standard cannot be about His need — so it must be about the giver's formation and the recipient's honor; because God is Ḥamīd, the best is given where the giving is truly credited, and the closed-eyes item is not merely insufficient but misdirected — the worthlessness offered where all praise converges. The verse ends, as the section's verses keep ending, with a name-pair that restates its command as theology: give the good, because the Receiver of every gift is beyond every need of it and the only One whose praise makes a gift worth making.
