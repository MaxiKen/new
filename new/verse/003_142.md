## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:142

> Do you think you will enter Paradise without Allah proving which of you ˹truly˺ struggled ˹for His cause˺ and patiently endured?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put to the Community**

The verse asks whether the believers imagined that they would enter the garden without God knowing who among them had striven and who had been patient. The question form is the Book's way of exposing an assumption rather than merely contradicting it.

The assumption is a natural one for a community that had been told it was the best brought forth for humanity. The verse does not deny the community's standing; it insists that the standing is connected to the conduct the Book has been describing. Entry into the garden is not a verdict on a group but the outcome of what its members do.

**Striving and Patience Named Together**

The two qualities named in the verse are the ones the sūrah has been testing since its account of Uḥud began. Striving covers the effort that a person makes, including the effort of fighting, and patience covers the endurance of what the effort costs.

The Book habitually pairs them, and the pairing appears in other passages about trial: the community is told that God will test it until the truth about those who strive and remain steadfast becomes known, and it is told that the entry into the garden depends on proving which of its members struggled and endured. Neither quality is presented as an alternative to the other. Effort without endurance collapses at the first hardship, and endurance without effort is passive.

**The Commentators on the Wording About Knowledge**

Al-Rāzī addressed the wording directly and explained that the verse does not imply any incompleteness in God's knowledge. Its purpose is to state the condition on which the garden is entered, and the knowledge it mentions is the kind that is demonstrated rather than acquired.

Al-Zamakhsharī illustrated the idiom with a comparison from ordinary speech: saying that one knows no good in a person amounts to saying that the person is not good. The sentence in this verse works the same way. It states what will be evident about the community, not what God will discover, and the same explanation applies to the clauses about knowledge in the verses around it.

**Two Readings of the Second Clause**

Some commentators read the second clause as a separate condition, so that the verse requires both striving and patience as distinct qualifications. Others read it as a consequence, so that the sense is that God would know those who strove in such a way as to know those who were patient.

Both readings describe the same reality in the life of a community. Patience is the form that striving takes when the effort continues past the point where it stops being rewarding, and the two qualities are so closely connected in the Book's usage that they are frequently mentioned in a single breath. The difference between the readings concerns grammar rather than substance.

**Al-Rāzī on the Two Loves**

Al-Rāzī's comment on this verse is one of his best-known passages, and its argument is that the love of this world cannot coexist with happiness in the Hereafter. As one increases, he says, the other diminishes, because the heart's occupation with worldly concerns leaves no room for what the next life requires.

He adds that not everyone who affirms the religion is truthful, and that the difference appears when a person's attachments are tested. Love, in his description, is what does not diminish when difficulty comes and does not increase merely because desires are satisfied. The verse's demand for patience is, on this reading, a test of whether the affirmation of faith was love or only agreement.

**Comparison With Other Passages**

The Book states the same principle in several other places. One passage tells the believers that God will test them until He shows who among them strive and remain steadfast. Another asks whether people think they will be left alone once they have said they believe, without being put to the test. A third describes earlier communities afflicted with hardship and shaken to the point where the messenger and those with him asked when God's help would come.

Read together, these passages form a consistent teaching about how the community's claim is verified. The sūrah's question in this verse belongs to that body of teaching, and the community at Madinah had just been given a demonstration of it in the space of a single afternoon.

**What the Verse Asks at Uḥud**

The question had a specific force for the people who first heard it. They had suffered losses after a period of confidence, and the verse asks whether they had expected the garden as a conclusion that followed automatically from their membership in the community.

The answer the sūrah expects is visible in the verses around it. The community had among its members people who had stood firm and people who had wavered, and the battle had made the difference plain to everyone. The verse names the two qualities that separated them, and it does so in the form of a question that none of the listeners could answer in the affirmative.

**The Garden and the Road to It**

The verse's structure places the garden at the beginning and the two qualities at the end, and the distance between them is the road the community is told to travel. The Book never presents the destination as a reward for a status that a person holds.

That is the teaching the sūrah has been assembling throughout this section. The community is the best brought forth for humanity, and it is also a community whose members are tested individually by what they do with their wealth, their tempers, and their lives. The question in this verse is the point at which the two statements meet, and the verses that follow describe what the test looked like on the day it came.
