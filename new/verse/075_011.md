## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:11

> But no! There will be no refuge.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Is Three Words Long**

The cry of the previous verse is answered immediately, and the answer is shorter than the question. *Kallā* rejects what has gone before — no, the matter is not as the speaker hopes — and then comes the negation: *lā wazar*. There is no refuge. The economy of the reply is part of its force. No reason is given, no alternative is offered, no condition is attached, and the absence of qualification is what makes the sentence final. A promise of escape conditioned on something would have left the cry alive; an unqualified negative closes it. The sūrah has spent four verses dismantling the world and now spends one dismantling the last thing a person in terror reaches for.

**Wazar: Shelter, Stronghold, and Burden**

The word being negated is worth unpacking, because English *refuge* flattens it. *Wazar* is the place a person runs to when something is after him — a fortress, a mountain's protection, the shelter of a protector's tent. The same root yields *wizr*, the burden of sin a person carries, and *wazīr*, the minister who carries a ruler's weight. The family of meanings hangs together: a refuge is what bears the weight you cannot bear yourself. To say there is no *wazar* is therefore to say something stronger than *there is nowhere to run*. It is to say that nothing will carry the load for you. Every arrangement by which a person transfers his accountability to someone or something else has been voided in a single noun.

**Every Exit the Qur'an Has Already Closed**

The denial is not unique to this verse; it is the Qur'an's standard account of that Day, and the consistency is the point. *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53). *“˹Imagine˺ how many peoples We destroyed before them, and they cried out when it was too late to escape.”* (38:3). *“so those who dispute about Our signs may know that they have no refuge.”* (42:35). The same verdict is delivered in 34:51 and 41:48. What changes across these passages is the identity of the thing that fails — idols, protectors, distance, time — and what never changes is that the failure is total. The Qur'an is not describing a difficult situation from which the clever might slip. It is describing the end of slipping.

**Why There Is Nowhere Left to Go**

The refuges of this life are worth naming, because each of them is a good thing in its place and each of them fails here. Time was a refuge: while it lasted, nothing was settled. Company was a refuge: among people who shared his assumptions, a man's account of himself went unchallenged. Status, wealth, and the ability to move were refuges, and so was the simple power of not thinking about something. Every one of these is a created thing being used as though it were independent, and the Day arrives as the removal of that pretence. What is left is the person and what he did, and the Qur'an is insistent that the two cannot be separated. The record is not a file kept somewhere and produced at the hearing; it is what the person has become. *“And We have everything recorded precisely.”* (78:29), and the precision is the problem: nothing small is omitted, because nothing small was ever without weight. Nothing is taken from anyone; the intermediaries simply stop functioning, and the person is left alone with what he did, which is what he had spent his life arranging to avoid.

**The One Direction That Still Works**

The Qur'an does acknowledge a flight, and it is the only one, and its direction is the opposite of what the cry in v. 10 imagines. Of three men who had failed badly and knew it, the text says: *“their souls were torn in anguish. They knew there was no refuge from Allah except in Him. Then He turned to them in mercy so that they might repent.”* (9:118). The preposition does all the work. There is no refuge *from* God except *in* God, which means the flight that succeeds is the one that stops running. That is available now, in the interval the sūrah keeps insisting is closing, and the Qur'an's offer is stated without conditions attached to the size of the offence: *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53).

**A Warning Delivered Before, Not After**

The verse is addressed to people who are still alive, and that is what makes it a warning rather than a report. Nothing is gained by telling the damned that they are damned; the Qur'an's purpose in describing the closure of every exit is to make the hearer look at the exits he is currently using. The man who has been putting something off on the strength of time, or hiding behind the approval of people who think as he does, is being told that these are borrowed arrangements with a fixed term. The negative in this verse is severe, and while breath remains it is also entirely avoidable. Nothing about the reckoning comes as a surprise to the person who has been warned about it. That combination is the shape of the Qur'an's warning throughout: the sentence is real, and the door in front of it is open. The verse can therefore be read twice, and it should be. Read as report, it describes the closure of every exit on a Day that is coming. Read as address, it asks the hearer which exit he is standing in now, and how long he intends to stay there.
