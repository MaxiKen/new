## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:39

> ˹We˺ also ˹destroyed˺ Korah, Pharaoh, and Hamân. Indeed, Moses had come to them with clear proofs, but they behaved arrogantly in the land. Yet they could not escape ˹Us˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Names From a Single Story**

The chapter adds a trio drawn from the Egyptian narrative: Korah, whose wealth the Book describes at length; Pharaoh, whose claim to divinity is recorded in several chapters; and Hāmān, the official who executed the king's projects. The three represent different kinds of power rather than three unrelated criminals. One held money, one held the throne, and one ran the apparatus that turned royal ambition into buildings and persecutions. By naming them together the sūrah broadens its indictment beyond the tribes and merchant towns it has surveyed, and it shows the reader that the pattern being established is not a feature of any particular social order. Nomadic confederations, trading cities, and an imperial state all arrive at the same verdict.

The three names are taken from different chapters of one national history and placed in a single sentence, which is the Book's way of showing that the pattern outlasted any single ruler. A reader who knows how far apart these figures stand in time will see the verse treating them as one case with three exhibits.

**Korah and the Wealth That Went Into the Ground**

The Book gives Korah's story in another chapter, where he is a man of enormous fortune who is reminded that his wealth is a test and answers by claiming it was given to him for his own merit. His end is reported in a single sentence: the earth swallowed him along with his house, and there was no one to help him against God, nor could he help himself (28:81). The detail of the house matters because it ties his fate to the wealth it contained. What he had accumulated as security became the wreckage in which he was buried, and the assets that made him formidable in the city were irrelevant in the space of an instant. The Book's verdict on his prosperity is not that it was evil; it is that he mistook it for protection.

The wealth of Korah is described elsewhere as belonging to a man who attributed his fortune to his own knowledge, and the ground's treatment of him answers that claim directly. The verse under discussion keeps the burial at the level of a fact, since the sūrah's interest lies in the collapse rather than in the argument that preceded it.

**Pharaoh's Tower and Hāmān's Labour**

The role assigned to Hāmān in the Book is precise. He is the official ordered to light a fire and build a tower so that Pharaoh could look upon the God of Moses and, in the fuller accounts, so that he could be proclaimed a deity to the public (28:38). The project is a good illustration of the problem the chapter has been describing. Imperial power in the Book is not merely cruel; it is absurd, spending the labour of a nation to prove a claim that no structure can support. Pharaoh's real objection to Moses was never about evidence, since the proofs were displayed before him, and the tower is what his position produced when evidence failed: an architectural argument, built by the man standing beside him in this verse. The Book's portrait of the court is completed by the fact that the project required the labour of a nation and produced nothing usable. It is one of the few places in the scripture where a government's most formidable undertaking is described as an exercise in vanity, and the reader is meant to notice how much of the state's power was spent on a claim that the state could not make true.

**Clear Proofs Answered With Arrogance**

The sentence says the proofs came first and arrogance followed, and the sequence is the point of the accusation. The Book's vocabulary for arrogance describes more than a bad manner; it names the refusal of a person to accept a truth he knows, in order to preserve his standing. Pharaoh had seen the signs and knew the position he was occupying; his response was to hold to it anyway. The sūrah has already shown the same reflex in the towns it surveyed, where the people who could reason decided not to, and the Egyptian court gives the pattern its political form. A ruler surrounded by his own monument does not need to be convinced of anything in order to keep refusing it.

The proof was not a puzzle the audience failed to solve; it was a demonstration they understood and dismissed, and the Book's accounts of these figures describe men who knew what they were refusing. The charge concerns attitude rather than information, and the following sentence treats the refusal itself as the reason for what came next.

**"They Could Not Escape Us"**

The closing clause uses the exact word the sūrah employed earlier when it told its audience that no one escapes God on earth or in heaven (29:22). The repetition ties the two passages together and closes the distance between the imperious and the ordinary. A ruler with a treasury, an army, and a tower is in the same position as any other creature before the decree, and his apparatus cannot delay it by a day. The Book's reports of these regimes are consistently unimpressed by their scale, and the phrase reminds the reader that the empires that survive in the record do so only as material for the argument. The next verse gathers the verdicts of the whole chapter into a single statement.

The phrase closes the case against all three at once. None of them escaped, and the sentence draws no distinction between the man with the treasure, the man with the army, and the man with the building contract, which leaves the reader facing a limit that no kind of power can negotiate.

**Why the List Keeps Widening**

The sūrah has moved from a corrupt town, to a fraudulent market, to two Arabian tribes, to a king and his officials, and it has reported the same outcome for each. This is the technique of the whole passage: the reader is shown that the pattern holds across every type of community that has ever existed, and no listener can claim that his own society falls outside the categories. The Meccan audience that heard the chapter had merchants, tribesmen, and rulers of its own, and the list gives each of its constituencies a case to consider. What remains, after all the names have been gathered, is the single verdict with which the passage closes. The order of the list is itself instructive. It begins with the community that killed its prophet's credibility by force, moves to a market that robbed its customers, passes through two tribes that treated their messengers with contempt, and ends at a throne that tried to answer revelation with architecture. Every path away from the message is represented, and each one terminates in the same place.

**Korah's End Retold in Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ**

Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ reports Korah's destruction with a detail that belongs beside this verse: the earth swallowed him and his home, and the verse notes that those who had envied his position the day before were struck the next day by the realization that God enlarges provision for whomever He wills or withholds it (28:81–82). The observation attaches the story directly to the sūrah's later theme about provision, and it shows that the punishment did its work on the survivors as well. Korah's wealth had made him a model to be envied, and his end made him a warning in the same streets, which is the pattern the whole chapter follows: the ruin becomes a lesson for those still in a position to reconsider.
