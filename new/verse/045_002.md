## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:2

> The revelation of this Book is from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Names the Sender Before the Content**

This verse makes one claim, without ornament: what the reader holds was sent down, and God sent it. The form is a nominal sentence, a bare declaration of origin. Other chapters open in almost the same words — “The revelation of this Book is from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise.” (39:1) stands at the head of al-Zumar, al-Aḥqāf repeats the sentence word for word (46:2), and Ghāfir opens, “The revelation of this Book is from Allah—the Almighty, All-Knowing,” (40:2). The Qur'an does not always begin a chapter with a claim about itself. When it does, the order carries weight. Nothing is said here about the Resurrection, the angels, the messengers, or the Law; all of that comes later. First the reader is told who is speaking, because a speech is judged by its speaker. The rest of the chapter presses exactly this point. What the deniers reject, in the end, is not a set of propositions but a sender.

**The Almighty: Power Behind What Was Sent Down**

Almighty renders al-ʿAzīz, a name built on the root ʿ-z-z, which carries the sense of strength that cannot be overcome. The Unconquerable is a fair translation in plain words. Its work in this sentence is to answer a question about the sending itself, because a message is only as safe as the guarantee behind it. The man who received this Book in Makkah had no army, no treasury, and no standing among the powerful families that could have protected a document they valued. The claim that its author cannot be defeated tells the reader that the message will not be lost the way a human document is lost: no force can cancel what this speaker wills. The name also closes an obvious escape route for the sceptic — that the speaker is weak, or that other gods have overpowered him. The same name returns at the end of the sūrah, and it appears in other chapters in the same pairing: “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” (59:24).

**The All-Wise: Order in What Was Sent**

Al-Ḥakīm, the All-Wise, comes from a root that covers wisdom and also judgment, the settling of a matter with authority. If power answers whether the message can survive, wisdom answers whether it deserves to. The threats scattered through the sūrah are not gusts of anger; they describe a moral order that its author knows from the inside, down to its ends. That is what ḥikmah means when the Qur'an applies it to God: not cleverness, but complete knowledge of what everything is for. A ruler who had power without wisdom would be a danger, and a counsellor with wisdom but no power would be a bystander. The verse joins the two names in one breath so that neither can be read alone. The Book that teaches the Resurrection is guaranteed by strength, and the strength that guarantees it is directed by a purpose the reader is invited to trust before he can see it.

**Why the Two Names Come in This Order**

Power is named first. The sequence is not accidental, since the Qur'an's own habit is to put the attribute that answers the immediate objection at the front, and the immediate objection in this sūrah concerns authority — who has the right to command. Once that ground is cleared, wisdom explains the content. Truth by itself, spoken by a being of unlimited force, would be coercion rather than guidance; the wisdom of the One who sent the Book is the reason its warnings arrive as speech, addressed to minds that can weigh them, spread across a lifetime of chances to change. Other chapters close with the identical pair, as in al-Ḥashr: “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” (59:24), and in al-Taghābun: “˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and unseen—the Almighty, All-Wise.” (64:18). The Qur'an returns to the combination whenever it wants its readers to hold authority and purpose in the same hand.

**A Book That Speaks About Its Own Descent**

The claim is that the Book came down. Arabic expresses this with the word tanzīl, a form that pictures the process as gradual rather than instantaneous, and the Qur'an uses it for revelation again and again. The sūrah will keep both sides of that process in view: the Book is from Allah, and it is We who recite it to the Prophet — “These are Allah's revelations which We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth.” (45:6). Downward movement and spoken recitation are the two ends of one act. That is why the claim about origin is put where it is, at the start, before the reader hears any of the recitation's demands. He is told first that what follows is not the private wording of a poet or the report of a narrator, and then he is handed the words themselves to judge. A text that asks to be weighed does well to state its provenance in the plainest available sentence.

**What the Verse Does Not Argue**

There is no syllogism here, no appeal to the reader's experience, and no sign pointed at as proof. The verse asserts and stops. That austerity is the point, because a claim of this kind cannot be supported by the same means it supports everything else; if it could be proved by an argument that needed no revelation, the Book would be redundant. The chapter's confidence rests on stating the matter once, plainly, and then living with the consequences for thirty-five more verses. The reader who rejects the sentence is not left with a smaller claim but with a harder problem, since every description of the created order that follows in vv. 3–5 presumes a speaker with authority over what he describes. The argument of the sūrah begins exactly where this verse ends: with a sentence that can be denied in a moment and cannot be overturned by anything the chapter subsequently says or does not say.
