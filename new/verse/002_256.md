## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:256

> Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood. So whoever renounces false gods and believes in Allah has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold. And Allah is AllHearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rule Against Compulsion**

Lā ikrāha fī al-dīn — the sentence is a legal principle stated in four words, and it follows the description of God in the verse of the Pedestal, which supplies its ground: the One who owns the heavens and the earth does not require a servant's assent to be forced, because the assent is worthless if forced. Al-Ṭabarī records the occasions that the early authorities attached to it, and none of them changes the rule; they show the situations the rule settled.

The principle is not a concession to weakness. The messengers argue, warn, and invite (16:125), and the Book never presents the invitation as a contest of power. What the sentence removes is a specific expectation: that the community's authority over a city could be used to convert its households, when the earlier verses had already required the community to live among people of other faiths.

**"Sound Judgment Has Become Clear from Error"**

The second clause gives the reason: al-rushd — mature judgement, the capacity to see where a road leads — has become distinct from al-ghayy, the ruinous course. The Book's pairing of these two words recurs (49:7, where God "has made faith beloved to you and beautified it in your hearts"). The reasoning of the sentence is that compulsion is unnecessary where the difference is visible: a case must be argued, not enforced, and if it is argued then the hearer keeps the decision.

This is where the verse's theology and its law meet. The Qur'an's most frequent objection to the deniers is not that they are outvoted but that they refuse to look — "have they not considered" runs through the sūrah's arguments. The clarity assumed here is the clarity of evidence, and the sentence protects the process by which evidence does its work.

**The Handhold That Never Breaks**

The sentence then describes the person who renounces the false objects of worship and believes in God as one who has grasped al-ʿurwa al-wuthqā — the firmest handhold, the handgrip that does not give way. The Qur'an uses the same image again in 31:22, where "whoever submits his face to God while doing good has grasped the firmest handhold." The metaphor is physical and unglamorous: a person hangs, and the question is whether the thing above him holds.

The order of the two clauses matters. Renunciation comes before affirmation — the empty hand must let go before it can close on the grip. The jurists read the pair as the rejection of tāghūt and the acceptance of God, which is the whole content of the faith; the verse does not mention anything else the person must do to be secure, and that silence has been read by the commentators as the emphasis the sentence intends.

**What the Reports Say About the Occasion**

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr transmit three occasions from the generation of the companions. The best known concerns households of the Anṣār in Madinah whose children, by vows taken before Islam, had been placed with Jewish families; when the Naḍīr were expelled from the city, the parents asked whether the children should be compelled to return to the Muslim community. A second report tells of a man whose two sons had become Christians and left for Syria; a third of believers who had been nursed among Jewish households and wished to depart with them.

The reports give the verse its setting: it was revealed into mixed families, not into a theory of tolerance. The scholars graded the transmissions differently, but the rule they were used to illustrate was agreed, and the verse's wording is general enough to govern every analogous case.

**The People of the Book and the Jizyah**

A further discussion belongs to the jurists: how the sentence relates to 9:29, where the People of the Book are to pay the jizyah and be subject to the community's authority. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī record the readings — that the prohibition of compulsion concerned the People of the Book and those who paid the jizyah, or that it states the general principle while the sword applied to the idolaters of Arabia who had broken their treaties. The community's practice favored the first reading, and the law of the protected communities developed from it.

**Hearing and Knowing**

The verse closes with samīʿ and ʿalīm — Hearing and Knowing. The two names answer the two parties of every conversion dispute: the argument as it was spoken is heard, and the belief that a person keeps quiet about is known. Force can produce words; the attributes secure the point that only words can be produced. The sentence therefore ends where the conscience is examined.

**Parallels Across the Book**

The rule of the sentence is not isolated. Sūrah Yūnus puts the same point as a question addressed to the messenger: had your Lord willed, everyone on earth would have believed together, and then — "would you compel people until they believe?" (10:99). Al-Kahf states the principle as a permission: "So whoever wills, let him believe; and whoever wills, let him disbelieve" (18:29). Al-Ghāshiyah closes the matter: the messenger is a reminder, not a controller over people (88:21–22), and the Book repeats the formula in 76:3, where the path is shown and the choice left open.

Al-Ṭabarī draws the legal consequence that the community's part in the matter is bayān — the clear delivery of the message — and that the outcome belongs to God. Ibn Kathīr adds that the verse does not forbid calling people to the faith; it forbids the assumption that the call can be completed by force. The two positions together are the community's settled reading, and the sūrah's own command to argue with the People of the Book "in the best manner" (16:125) belongs to the same instruction.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence is the charter of the community's manner of invitation: no coercion, a case made clear, a handhold offered rather than imposed, and an account kept by God rather than by the community's enforcers. The verses that follow turn immediately to the guardian of the believers, so that the reader moves from the rule of the invitation to the One who guides.
