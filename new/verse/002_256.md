## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:256

> Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood. So whoever renounces false gods and believes in Allah has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold. And Allah is AllHearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence and Its Grammar**

Lā ikrāha fī'l-dīn — *no compulsion in religion* — is among the most cited sentences in the Qur'an, and its first interpretive question is grammatical: is it descriptive or prescriptive? Read as description, it states a fact about faith itself — religion cannot be compelled, because belief coerced is not belief; the heart under threat produces compliance, not īmān. Read as prescription — *let there be no compulsion* — it legislates the fact, forbidding what would in any case be futile. The traditional commentaries carry both readings without conflict, and the second clause supplies the reason either way: *the truth stands out clearly from falsehood* — rushd has become distinct from ghayy, and what is distinct does not need to be imposed. The Qur'an's parallel statements gather behind this verse: *Had your Lord so willed, all people on earth would have certainly believed. Would you then force people to become believers?* (10:99); and the command to say: *Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve* (18:29). The compulsion-verses share one assumption — that faith is a seeing, not a yielding — and this verse states the assumption as a principle of law.

**Rushd and Ghayy**

The two nouns of the reason-clause carry more than the translation shows. Rushd, rendered *truth* here, is the word for sound judgment, right direction, maturity — the same root as the rightly-guided (rāshid) and the growth-toward-sense that the sūrah of the Cave attaches to the orphans' treasure; ghayy, rendered *falsehood*, carries error but also transgression and seduction — going astray as an active falling-away. The clause thus says more than true-is-distinguishable-from-false: it says maturity is distinguishable from seduction, sound direction from the stumble. The verb — *stands out clearly* — is in the perfect: the distinction has been completed, accomplished by the revelation's own clarity, which is the sūrah's recurring claim (thus God makes His signs clear). A clarity this complete changes the function of force: where the road forks visibly, compulsion adds nothing to the seeing and subtracts everything from the chooser.

**The Occasions of Revelation**

The narrations behind the verse are specific and domestic, and they matter for its scope. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī record three occasions, all involving children and belonging. In the first, Muslim parents had children among the Banū Naḍīr — the Jewish tribe exiled from Medina after its plotted betrayal — and asked whether they might force the children into the Muslim community; the background is a pre-Islamic custom of Yathrib, where women whose infants kept dying vowed that any child who survived would be raised Jewish, and it was such vowed children who now stood between two communities. In the second, a Muslim man's two sons were converted by merchants and left for Syria as Christians. In the third, Muslims who had been wet-nursed by Jewish families wanted to depart with the exiled clans, and their families were forcing them to remain. In every account the verse arrives to stop a compelled belonging — and in every account the compelling party is the one with the better religion, which is precisely why the case is hard and the ruling general.

**Scope, Jizyah, and the Abrogation Debate**

The jurists asked how far the principle reaches, and the commentary records the debate honestly. Some sources restrict the verse to the People of the Book, or to anyone from whom the jizyah-protection tax may be taken (9:29's legislation) — noting that the idolatrous Arabs were not offered the option of remaining idolaters, and reading the no-compulsion rule as governing the recognized protected religions. Others claimed the verse was abrogated by the fighting-commandments, the sword-verse of sūrah 9 among them. The commentators answer the abrogation-claim on chronology: all three occasions of revelation follow from conflicts with the Jewish tribes — conditions resulting from banishments already carried out — so the verse is late, not early, and cannot have been repealed by verses whose campaigns preceded it. The deeper answer is doctrinal: compulsion in religion negates taklīf, the accountability before God that gives both this life and the Hereafter their meaning — and an ethical pronouncement of that kind is not subject to abrogation at all. The record adds a historical observation: forced conversions in later Islamic history, while not unknown, count as rare exceptions to the rule — the fighting Muslims carried out was, like the occasions of this verse show, motivated by political circumstance rather than by the desire to convert.

**Ṭāghūt and the Handhold**

The verse's second half describes the act of faith in two movements: renouncing ṭāghūt and believing in God. Ṭāghūt — rendered *false gods* — is built on the root of rebellion and trespass: al-Rāzī glosses it variously as Satan, as soothsayers and magicians, as idols, as any being who rebels against God and is followed in the rebellion; the longer treatment waits in the sūrah of Women (4:51). The renunciation comes first in the syntax, and the order is the theology: the handhold is grasped by a letting-go. The image — *the firmest, unfailing hand-hold* — has its parallel in the sūrah of Luqmān: whoever submits to God and does good *has grasped the firmest hand-hold* (31:22). The commentators identify the handhold as faith itself or as the shahādah-word, and they note the precision of *unfailing*: the Arabic break it denies is the fracture that does not show — the handhold that fails imperceptibly, the faith that parts silently under strain. What is grasped, the verse promises, does not give way even slightly, and the grasping is available to whoever performs the two movements — renounce, believe — with no one compelled into either.

**All-Hearing, All-Knowing**

The closing pair is the sūrah's audit-seal, set here over the verse's two interiors. The hearer of the shahādah-word is God — the confession spoken under a sword and the confession spoken in freedom reach the same hearing, and the name quietly completes the no-compulsion rule: since God hears the heart's speech directly, human coercion adds nothing to His knowledge and cannot manufacture what He alone receives. The Knower knows the renunciation's sincerity — whether the ṭāghūt let go of was outward or inward, whether the handhold was grasped or clutched at. The pair also answers the occasions of revelation: the parents wondering about their vowed children, the families forcing the wet-nursed to stay, the fathers watching sons leave for Syria — every party to those disputes is heard and known, and the verse releases them all from the work of adjudicating each other's hearts. Religion under this seal is a matter between the grasping hand and the Knowing God; the community's law is only to keep the space clear — no compulsion — and let the clarity of rushd from ghayy do what clarity does.
