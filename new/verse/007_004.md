## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:4

> ˹Imagine˺ how many societies We have destroyed! Our torment took them by surprise ˹while sleeping˺ at night or midday.

**Expanded Commentary**

**How Many Communities Were Destroyed**

The verse opens with a question about the number of communities that God has destroyed, and the question is put to the audience as a reminder of what lies in its own history. The Book uses the theme of the destroyed towns throughout its pages, and this sūrah contains the first of its long sequences on the subject, running from the account of Noah's people to the account of Pharaoh and his hosts, which occupies the middle of the chapter. The question form is deliberate, since it forces the hearer to reckon with a history he already knows.

The Book's other long treatments of the same theme appear in the sūrah of Hūd, where the sequence runs from Noah to the people of Shuʿayb (11:25–100), in the sūrah of the Poets, where the same sequence is repeated in shorter form (26:105–89), in the sūrah of the Ant, where the towns of Lot and Ṣāliḥ are described (27:45–58), and in the sūrah of the Moon, whose opening passages recount the fate of ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, and the people of Pharaoh (54:9–42). The verse in front of us states the conclusion of those accounts in a single question.

**The Timing of the Punishment**

The verse states that the punishment came upon the communities by night or while they were resting at midday. The two times describe the hours in which people are settled and unguarded, and the choice of such hours makes the point that the punishment can arrive without further warning. A person who is asleep at night or resting in the heat of noon is not prepared for a reckoning, and the Book's description of the earlier communities repeatedly presents them as secure in their homes and their livelihoods when the command arrives.

The Book's usage of this hour appears in its accounts of particular peoples, where the punishment is said to have come in the morning or at the end of the night, and in the warnings of the sūrah itself. The audience of the sūrah lived among the ruins of the towns of the earlier peoples and travelled past them on its trade routes, and the verse takes that knowledge as its starting point. The question is not whether such destructions happened but how many of them stand in the record.

**Security and the Illusion of Safety**

The verse's force comes from the false confidence of the destroyed. The Book describes the people of the towns as feeling secure against the arrival of punishment, and it answers that confidence with the reminder that God's command arrives at the hour He has set. The sūrah of the Cattle repeats the theme when it asks whether the people of the towns are safe from the arrival of punishment while they sleep or while they are in the open during the day.

The same illusion is addressed throughout the sūrah's later passages, where the prophets warn their peoples and the peoples answer with mockery. ʿĀd claims its strength, Thamūd points to its dwellings carved in the rock, and the people of Lot rely on the protection of their clan. The verse in front of us summarises the outcome of all of those encounters before the chapter has told them.

**What the Destroyed Towns Establish**

The destruction of the communities serves the sūrah's argument about the truth of the message. The peoples who were destroyed had received messengers, and their destruction followed their rejection of what had been sent to them. The Book's accounts of these peoples therefore provide evidence for the audience in Makkah: the pattern of warning, rejection, and ruin is the pattern in which the present audience now stands.

The Book's use of the destroyed towns as evidence appears in the sūrah of the Bee, where the audience is told to travel through the earth and observe the end of those who denied, and in the sūrah of the Romans, where the fate of the peoples before them is placed in front of the hearer. The verse in front of us belongs to that family, and it states the fact on which the sūrah's history of the prophets will rest.

**The Book's Restraint in Retelling**

The verse states the destruction without lingering on its details, and that restraint runs through the sūrah's treatment of the earlier peoples. The Book's accounts of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Lot are brief, and their function is to establish the pattern rather than to satisfy curiosity about the ruin. The verse in front of us keeps to the same method: the question is asked, the timing is named, and the hearer is left to draw the conclusion.

The Book's habit of leaving the ruins themselves unvisited and the lesson in place appears in its instructions to consider the end of those who denied. The sūrah's audience passes by the ruins on its journeys, and the verse makes the ruins speak without describing the stones.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands fourth in the chapter's opening section, and it follows the command to follow what has been sent down and the prohibition against taking protectors besides God. It supplies the consequence of refusing that command, and it prepares the account of the peoples that the sūrah will give in its middle section.

The verse that follows supplies the words the destroyed communities spoke when the punishment arrived, and the pair together state both the fact and the response. The opening section of the sūrah therefore moves from the revelation to the command, from the command to the warning, and from the warning to the confession of those who were overcome.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that the plea of the communities, when the punishment came upon them, was only to say that they had been wrongdoers (7:5). The confession follows the destruction and arrives too late to change its outcome, and the sūrah presents it as the last thing the condemned say in the world.

The two verses together establish the sequence that the sūrah's later narratives will repeat. The warning is given, the message is rejected, the punishment arrives without notice, and the confession is spoken when nothing can be done with it. The reader who has been told to follow the revelation is shown the end of the path that refuses it.
