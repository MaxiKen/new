## Sūrah an-Naml 27:78

> Your Lord will certainly judge between them by His justice, for He is the Almighty, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict Is Deferred, Not Cancelled**

The previous verses described a Book that recounts to a disputing community most of what they differ over. This one says who will actually settle the matter, and when: *your Lord* will judge between them.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both read the judgment as occurring in the Hereafter — God will decide there who was right. That timing is the substance of the verse. The Qur'an has just claimed to clarify the disputes, and it now distinguishes clarification from adjudication. Making the truth available and enforcing a verdict are two different acts, and only the first has happened. The disputes will continue in this world, and the Book's role is to inform them rather than to end them.

**Not the Prophet's Job**

Al-Rāzī draws the consequence explicitly: carrying out this judgment is not the Prophet's duty. The verse assigns it elsewhere, and the assignment is a limitation on the office.

That limit matters given what the chapter has been describing. A man told that his recitation settles the disputes of an ancient scriptural community might reasonably conclude that he is authorised to impose the settlement. The verse forecloses it in the same breath as the claim. He conveys; God judges. This is consistent with the instruction two verses earlier not to grieve over the refusers, and with the Qur'an's repeated statements that the messenger's responsibility is delivery rather than result. Nothing in an-Naml gives the Prophet a verdict to enforce.

**"By His Justice"**

The Arabic is *bi-ḥukmih*, by His judgment or His decision, which the translation renders as His justice. The phrase asserts that the criterion belongs to God rather than to any of the disputing parties.

That is the specific difficulty with long-running religious disputes: each side judges by a standard the other does not accept, and there is no neutral position available inside the argument. Communities that have quarrelled for centuries are not usually short of arguments; they are short of an arbiter both sides recognise. The verse names one, and locates the session after the point at which anyone can still be arguing. The Qur'an makes this promise in a dozen other places, telling the Prophet in several of them that God will inform people of what they differed about — the same formula, applied to Jews and Christians, to sects within them, and to the pagans of Mecca alike.

**Two Names Fitted to Judgment**

The verse closes with *al-ʿazīz al-ʿalīm*, the Almighty and the All-Knowing. The pairing is chosen for the task described, and the chapter has used the same technique before.

A judge needs two things: the knowledge to determine what actually happened, and the power to impose the finding. Human courts fail at one or the other constantly — they reach correct verdicts they cannot enforce, or enforce verdicts reached on incomplete information. The two names answer both failures in order. Note also the echo. These are the same two attributes named at the fire in verse 9, when God identified Himself to Mūsā as the Almighty, the All-Wise, and in verse 6, where the Qur'an was described as coming from the All-Wise, All-Knowing. The chapter's beginning and end are held together by the same small set of names.

**The Chapter's Fourth Deferral**

An-Naml has now declined four times to bring matters to a conclusion in this world. The Hour's timing was withheld at verse 65. The date of the reckoning was refused at verse 72. The Prophet was told not to grieve over the refusers at verse 70. Now the adjudication of religious dispute is placed beyond death.

Together these establish the position the chapter leaves its reader in. Nothing will be settled here; the evidence is available and the verdict is not. That is an uncomfortable arrangement and the chapter does not soften it. What it offers instead is the record of communities that were in exactly the same position and what they did with it — Sheba's queen, who investigated, and three nations that did not. The verse that follows tells the Prophet what to do while the judgment is pending.

**"Between Them" — Between Whom?**

The pronoun has no obvious antecedent in the immediate verse, and the commentators take it from the previous one: the Children of Israel and their internal disputes. The judgment is between the parties to that disagreement.

But the chapter has other disputing parties in view, and the phrasing is broad enough to hold them. The Meccans demanding a date, the believers enduring the demand, and the older scriptural communities are all in the passage's field of vision, and all of them are in disagreement about the same set of questions. The Qur'an's other statements of this promise are similarly unrestricted. What they have in common is a refusal to distribute the verdicts in advance. Nobody in the dispute is told they have already won it, which is an unusual position for a text written from inside one of the parties.

**Judgment as Disclosure**

Several of the parallel verses phrase the promise not as a sentence being passed but as information being released: God will inform them of what they differed about. That framing suits a chapter preoccupied with what is hidden and what is recorded.

Verse 75 said everything concealed is in a clear Book. This verse says the parties will be judged. Between them sits an implication the Qur'an makes explicit elsewhere — the judgment consists partly in the record being opened. Disputes about what really happened end when the account is produced, and the chapter has already established that an account exists. The people quarrelling are not waiting for an opinion. They are waiting for a document.
