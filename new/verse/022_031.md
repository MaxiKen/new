## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:31

> Be upright ˹in devotion˺ to Allah, associating none with Him ˹in worship˺. For whoever associates ˹others˺ with Allah is like someone who has fallen from the sky and is either snatched away by birds or swept by the wind to a remote place.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥanīf: a Word the Qur'an Had to Reclaim**

The verse opens by describing the required posture as that of *ḥunafāʾa lillāh* — ḥanīfs for God. The word is one of the Qur'an's most consequential borrowings, and its history is part of its meaning.

In pre-Islamic usage a *ḥanīf* appears to have denoted someone who turned away from the established cult toward the worship of the One God — a seeker outside the tribal system, and outside Judaism and Christianity as well. The Qur'an adopts the term and binds it to Abraham: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist’”* (2:135).

The significance is that the word describes a direction rather than a membership. A ḥanīf is not a person who has joined something; he is a person who has turned away from something. That is exactly the sense this verse needs, since it follows five verses of pilgrimage legislation and is about to insist that the rites mean nothing without it.

**The Condition Stated Twice**

*Ghayra mushrikīna bihi* — not associating partners with Him. The phrase is a second negation added to a first: be upright for God, *not* associating. The Qur'an regularly states monotheism twice, positively and then negatively, and the redundancy is not stylistic.

The reason is that the positive statement alone can be satisfied by a person who affirms one God and keeps his other objects of reliance. The negation closes that gap. It is possible to say that God is one and to live as though He were not, and the second phrase is what catches that.

Here the double statement is doing specific work: it is addressed to people who are about to perform the rites of the pilgrimage, a rite inherited from Abraham, at a sanctuary that had been filled with idols. Performing it correctly requires both the action and the refusal.

**The Parable of the Fall**

*Fa-ka-annamā kharra mina al-samāʾi* — it is as though he fell from the sky. The comparison is to a falling body, and the point of the image is helplessness.

A man who falls from a height has no control over what happens to him next. He did not choose the descent once it began, he cannot alter its speed, and he cannot decide how he lands. The commentators took the image in exactly this sense: the associator has put himself into a state in which he is acted upon rather than acting.

The choice of falling, rather than of being wounded or being lost, also carries a claim about the starting point. You cannot fall from the ground. The parable assumes a height the person was at — an original uprightness, a natural orientation toward the One God — and the association is what dislodged him from it.

**Two Endings, and Neither Is a Landing**

Then the image forks: *fa-takhṭafuhu al-ṭayru aw tahwī bihi al-rīḥu fī makānin saḥīq* — the birds snatch him away, or the wind sweeps him down into a far-off place.

The first ending was read by al-Rāzī and others as the condition of a soul exposed after its fall: vulnerable, seized by whatever is circling, torn apart by devils that had no claim on him until he descended. The second was read as the soul carried further by its own caprice and appetites, blown along past the point where it could have stopped.

The two endings are not alternatives in the sense of a choice. They are the two things that happen to a person who has detached himself. Outside the protection of the orientation he was made for, he is either taken or drifted — and a place that is *saḥīq*, remote and deep, is somewhere no one arrives at by accident.

**Why the Image Comes After the Rites**

The placement is the verse's final argument. Verses 26 to 30 gave the pilgrimage: the site, the call, the arrival, the sacrifice, the release, the circuit, the sacred things to be honoured and the false speech to be shunned.

Verse 31 says what all of it is worth without the turning. A person can perform every one of those rites and be falling, which is why the ḥanīf language was necessary: the religion being described is not a set of observances with monotheism attached, but a turning to which the observances are secondary.

**The Parable Describes a Condition, Not a Crime**

Notice what the associator is *not* compared to. He is not compared to a rebel, an enemy combatant, a criminal or a liar — though the sūrah has used all of those categories for other people.

He is compared to a man who has fallen and is now helpless. That is a diagnosis rather than an indictment, and the difference has consequences. A rebel is someone you fight; a falling man is someone you try to catch. The Qur'an's missionary posture toward idolaters follows from this parable: the associator is in mortal danger and, until the fall is complete, is still reachable.

This is consistent with the sūrah's own method. The argument of verses 5 to 7 was addressed to people *in doubt*, and reasoned with them rather than pronouncing on them. The parable of verse 31 does the same thing in imagery: it says what has happened to such a person, and leaves the door open for him to be told.

**The Wind and the Birds Are Not Agents**

One last detail matters here: in both endings, what carries the man off is not a person. The birds and the wind are the ordinary hazards of the air, and the verse does not populate the parable with a captor.

That absence is theologically important. Nobody is dragging the associator anywhere. He fell, and what happens to him afterwards is what happens to anything that falls. The punishment in this parable is not inflicted; it is what a fall is.
