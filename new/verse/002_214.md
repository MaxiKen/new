## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:214

> Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you? They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken that ˹even˺ the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, “When will Allah’s help come?” Indeed, Allah’s help is ˹always˺ near.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question the Community Must Answer**

The verse opens with a question that dismantles an assumption: do you suppose you will enter the Garden without the likes of what befell those who passed before you? The interrogative form is not inquiry but correction — the community is addressed as people who have been entertaining, perhaps unconsciously, the idea that paradise is an inheritance of confession rather than an outcome of trial. The sūrah states the same principle twice more in identical terms: do you think you will enter Paradise while God has not yet proven who among you struggled and endured (3:142), and the sūrah of the Spider's opening — do people think they will be left to say "we believe" without being tested? We tested those before them (29:2–3). The three verses form a single doctrine of the tested faith: belief declares itself in words, and the trial verifies it in fact; between the declaration and the verification stands the whole life of the community. The verse's reference to those who passed before installs that verification in history — the test ahead is not novel but inherited, the same furnace every believing generation has entered, and its heat is not evidence of abandonment but of membership.

**The Trench, the Exile, and the Taunts**

The reports of occasion place the verse inside the community's hardest hours. Some connect it to the Battle of the Trench in the fifth year, when the confederated tribes besieged Medina and the Qur'an's own sūrah of the Parties remembers the days — when forces came against you and God sent a wind and hosts you did not see (33:9); others connect it to Uḥud in the third year, the defeat on the mountain. Al-Rāzī and al-Thaʿlabī preserve the social texture behind the shaking: the Emigrants were bearing the cost of exile — property left in Mecca, livelihoods interrupted, families divided — while the locals and the hypocrites taunted them, asking how long they would sacrifice themselves and their wealth for a cause that looked, by every worldly reckoning, like a losing one. The verse answers the taunt by relocating it in history: the question "when will this cost end?" is not a new question invented by Medina's cynics but the old question of every community under trial — asked, the verse will say, even by the Messenger and the believers with him.

**Misfortune, Hardship, and the Shaking**

The middle clause names the trial's two instruments and its effect: misfortune and hardship befell them, and they were shaken — zulzilū, the reduplicated verb of violent trembling, the same root the Qur'an uses for the earthquake of the Hour. The two instruments are the sūrah's standing pair: baʾsāʾ and ḍarrāʾ, the suffering and the adversity the definition of righteousness had already placed patience within (2:177) and the test-announcement had listed as fear, hunger, and loss (2:155). The shaking is the trial's real measure — not the affliction itself but the affliction carried to the point where constancy trembles. The verse is precise about how far the shaking went: so far that the Messenger and those who believed with him cried out. The extremity is stated without apology and without minimization; the trial of the prophets' communities reached the limit of human endurance, and the limit is where the cry comes. This is the verse's first consolation: the reader's own shaken faith does not place him outside the company of the faithful but inside it — the tremor is part of the record, recorded by God rather than hidden by Him.

**"When Will God's Help Come?"**

The cry is quoted: *When will God's help come?* The exegetes handled the question with care, because it issues from the Messenger and the believers together. Some read the Messenger here as Muhammad specifically; others took it generally — every prophet and his people at their trial's depth, since all the prophets encounter severe trials. Al-Qurṭubī records the reading that protects prophetic certainty: the Messenger knew the help was coming and the question sought its hastening — not doubt about whether but impatience for when. The Qur'an's parallel portraits confirm that prophethood does not exempt its bearers from the weight of trial: the messengers despaired and their people thought them denied, until Our help came (12:110); the breast straitened by what they say (15:97 in substance); the torment of the self over a people who will not believe (26:3 in substance); Job's affliction and his cry (21:83). The station of prophethood, in the Qur'an's presentation, includes human vulnerability to fear, sadness, and discouragement — and the verse quotes the cry rather than censoring it because the cry itself, at the trial's limit, is not disbelief but the last form of address to God: the question asked *to* Him, not about Him.

**Indeed, God's Help Is Near**

The answer is a single word of emphasis and a single adjective: *indeed — God's help is near*. The particle removes all hedging from the promise; the nearness does not specify an hour but a relation — the help is near to the cry, near to the limit, near to the shaking rather than distant from them. The verse's structure performs this: the cry and the answer share one sentence, with no intervening narrative, no condition inserted between the question and its response. The nearness is also the trial's hidden geography — the furnace the community enters is not a place God has left; the help is near *inside* it. The tradition drew the practical lesson for every generation under trial: the point of maximum shaking is not evidence of maximum abandonment but the classical position of deliverance, the hour the previous communities cried in and were answered from. For the verse's first hearers — the taunted Emigrants, the besieged city — the nearness was historical as well as theological: Uḥud was followed by the Trench, the Trench by its wind and its unseen hosts, and the wind by the road back to Mecca.

**The Verse as the Passage's Foundation**

The placement of this verse after the one-community history (2:213) and before the legislation of fighting (2:216) gives it an architectural office. The previous verse ended with the guidance granted by God's leave to those who believe; this verse states what the guided community must expect on the road — the trial of those before, carried to the shaking-point — and the verse after will legislate the obligation the community finds hateful. The sequence is pedagogy: guidance, then testing, then the hard command — the community is not given the command to fight before it is told what commands cost and what trials precede paradise. The verse also answers the mockery it will meet two verses on: the disbelievers for whom the world is beautified and who ridicule the believers (2:212) are exactly the taunters the reports describe, and the trial-verse tells the mocked that their shaking has a company, a purpose, and a near help. Between the one community of the past and the divided communities of the present, this verse installs the constant: every community that receives the truth is tried by it — and the trial's last word, spoken over the cry of the Messenger and the believers themselves, is the nearness of the help.
