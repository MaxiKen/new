## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:214

> Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you? They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken that ˹even˺ the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, “When will Allah’s help come?” Indeed, Allah’s help is ˹always˺ near.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put to the Community**

The verse asks whether the believers supposed that they would enter the Garden without experiencing what came to those who passed away before them, and the question is put in a form that expects no answer. The Book addresses the community at a point when its difficulties had continued longer than it had expected.

The sentence does not deny the promise of the Garden; it denies the assumption that the promise removes the trial. Readers are shown that the way to the reward passes through the same conditions that the earlier communities met, and the question is therefore an instruction about what to expect rather than a rebuke.

**The Trials Described**

The sentence states that misfortune and hardship befell the earlier believers, and it adds that they were shaken severely. The vocabulary describes a disturbance that reaches the whole person, and the community's teachers read the pair of terms as a description of prolonged and varied suffering.

The Book's account of the earlier communities elsewhere includes exile, loss of property, and the hostility of those around them, and the sentence in this place gathers those experiences into two words. What the passage establishes is that the testing of a community is a settled pattern rather than an unusual event, and the reader is given the pattern before the account of the cry that follows.

**The Cry of the Messenger and the Believers**

The sentence describes the point at which the messenger and those who believed with him asked when God's help would come, and the Book records the question without censuring those who asked it. The community's teachers discussed the meaning of the cry, and several explained it as a request that the help be hastened rather than a doubt about its arrival.

The reports describe the circumstances of the community in Madinah, and they connect the verse with the campaign of the parties or with the aftermath of an earlier battle. The records of the companions describe the hardship of the emigrants, who had left their property behind and who bore the taunts of those who asked how long they would sacrifice themselves for a cause that appeared to be failing.

**The Prophets and Human Feeling**

The Book's account of the messengers includes passages in which they are described as troubled, and the community's scholars used them to explain that the station of prophecy does not remove a person's vulnerability to fear and sadness. The verse's record of the cry belongs to that body of material.

One passage describes the messengers despairing and thinking that they had been deemed liars, another describes the Prophet's ﷺ breast being straitened, and a third describes his grief at the refusal of his people. The reader is shown that the Book presents its messengers as human beings who feel the weight of their circumstances, and the community's teachers drew from this the assurance that the strain of difficult days is not a defect in a person's faith.

**The Answer at the End**

The sentence closes with the assurance that God's help is near, and the community's scholars read the answer as the counterweight to the question that preceded it. The Book places the promise in the same sentence as the cry, so that the two are held together.

The wording states the nearness of the help rather than its date, and the community's teachers read the clause as the assurance that the community's difficulty is not the whole of its situation. Readers are left with the question and the answer side by side, and the passage's arrangement supplies the perspective in which the trials described at the beginning of the sentence are to be understood.

**The Passage's Place in the Sūrah**

The sentence follows the legislation on the pilgrimage and the accounts of the hypocrites and the believers, and it prepares the community for the commands about fighting that follow. The Book has described the two responses to the message, and it now addresses the cost of holding to it, so that the reader is given the instruction about conflict after the account of what the community will endure.

**The Trials of the Earlier Communities**

The address places the community that received the Book within the same pattern as those who came before it, and the three terms the sentence uses describe the forms the trial takes. Poverty names the loss of what a person owns, hardship names the illness and hunger that reach the body, and the shaking names the disturbance that follows when the trial continues and the outcome remains unseen.

The early community met all three. The reports describe the companions who were tortured for their faith in Makkah, the family who were killed for it, and the hunger of the lean seasons in Madinah. Later sorrows were heavier still, and the Prophet ﷺ buried his wife and his uncle within the same year of the boycott in the valley.

The exegetes read the sentence as the assurance that difficulty is not evidence of abandonment. The verse before it names the reward, and the question here removes the expectation of reaching it without the cost. The Book states in another place that the community will be tried until the messenger and those with him ask when the help of God will come, so that the answer given in this verse stands within a revelation that anticipates the question.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes that the reward is not given without testing, that the testing of the earlier communities is the pattern for the community that follows them, that the strain of the trial does not remove the standing of the one who bears it, and that the help promised to the believers is near. The community's teachers used the verse when they comforted those whose difficulties had lasted longer than they had expected, and the reader is left with the question and with the assurance that answers it.

The community that received the verse lived at a distance from the worst hardships of the earlier peoples, and the teachers read the address as a warning against taking one's own comfort as a sign of favour. The sentence also prepares the reader for the obligations that the following verses impose, so that the effort they require is received as the continuation of the path the earlier believers walked.
