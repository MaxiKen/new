## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:49

> Then after that will come a year in which people will receive abundant rain and they will press ˹oil and wine˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Fifteenth Year That Was Not in the Dream**

The king saw fourteen items: seven fat cows, seven lean, seven green ears, seven dry. Joseph's answer accounts for all of them and then adds one more. Nothing in the imagery of verse 43 corresponds to a year of rain and pressing, and no interpretation of the cows or the ears could have produced it. The additional year is therefore not derived from the data given; it is knowledge supplied from elsewhere. The exegetes have long noted the point, because it establishes something the earlier verses could not: Joseph's information exceeds the dream. A skilled interpreter can decode symbols a dreamer supplies. Only someone receiving information can add a term the dreamer never saw. The addition is also a testable one. Joseph is not extending a symbolic pattern; he is introducing a specific claim about weather for which the dream supplied no basis. If the year of rain does not arrive, the interpretation is falsified along with the entire policy built upon it, and the chapter permits that exposure rather than insulating its prophet from it.

**"People Will Be Given Rain" Uses the Passive Deliberately**

The verb *yughāthu al-nās* is passive, and the noun behind it — *ghayth* — is the specific word for rain that comes as relief, rain sent to a people in need. The passive leaves the agent unnamed while the vocabulary makes him obvious. The relief is given, not produced; it arrives, and it arrives after seven years in which no human management could have created it. The verse closes the programme with an act that has no place in the plan. Joseph's storage scheme covers the first fourteen years. The fifteenth is handled by someone else, and the wording records the fact without comment. That is the sūrah's characteristic way of marking the limit of human technique. The passive also keeps attention where the plan requires it. Storage can be organised, measured and enforced by human decision; the ending of a drought cannot. By separating the two, Joseph's speech tells the court exactly which parts of the coming fifteen years are theirs to manage and which are not, and asks them to act on the first without presuming upon the second.

**"They Will Press" Returns the Cupbearer's Verb**

The word for pressing is from the root *ʿ-ṣ-r*, and the chapter has already used it. The first of the two prisoners said: *“I dreamt I was pressing wine”* (12:36). The verb that introduced Joseph's public career in Egypt — the dream whose interpretation restored a servant to his post and eventually carried a message to a king — reappears at the end of his forecast as the sign of national recovery. The echo is exact, and it does structural work. The small private vision and the large public one are joined by the same action, which is how the chapter ties the prison to the palace. A reader who notices the repetition hears the whole Egyptian section as one connected movement rather than two separate episodes.

**Two Readings of the Final Verb**

The commentators record a minority reading in which the verb is understood not as pressing but as receiving relief, drawing on a related noun meaning a year of abundance and ease. On that reading the clause would simply restate the rain: people will be given relief. The majority reading, reflected in the translation, keeps the literal sense of pressing oil and wine, which has the advantage of specifying what the abundance consists of. The disagreement is worth reporting because it shows how the exegetes handled an ambiguous term: one side preferred the concrete agricultural image, the other preferred a general statement of deliverance, and the first was judged stronger because it continues the pattern of concrete images that runs through the whole forecast.

**Hope Is Placed at the End of the Sequence, Not the Beginning**

The order of the three verses is a rhetorical choice as much as a chronological one. Seven years of instruction, seven years of consumption, then one year of rain. The relief is last, and it is the shortest of the three phases — a single year set against fourteen. A forecast arranged to comfort would have led with the recovery. This one ends with it, which means the hearers spend the whole of the message inside the difficulty and emerge from it only at the close. The effect on the king's court must have been considerable: a plan that requires seven years of restraint, survives seven years of famine, and is guaranteed to end in abundance. The guarantee is the only thing that makes the restraint acceptable.

**The Year of Rain Is Outside the Plan, and the Brothers Are Inside the Famine**

The timing of the narrative depends on this verse. Joseph's brothers arrive in Egypt during the years of scarcity, seeking grain from the stores he controls (12:58). Had the fifteenth year come earlier, they would have had no reason to travel and the recognition scene would never have occurred. The chapter is arranging a calendar. The famine is the mechanism that brings a family from Canaan to an Egyptian granary, and the year of relief is placed after that mechanism has done its work. Nothing in the text says so. The reader assembles it from the sequence, which is the method the sūrah has used from the beginning: give the facts in order and let the arrangement speak. Here that method produces a striking result: the one element of the future Joseph cannot control is the element that closes his forecast, and the sūrah leaves it standing at the end of the verse with nothing built upon it. The plan terminates exactly at the limit of what planning can reach, and the reader is shown where that limit falls.

**The Forecast Is Verified by Events Nobody Controls**

Over the following years the prediction will be tested in the only way a forecast can be tested. The sowing happens, the stores accumulate, the famine arrives on schedule, the grain is released, and the brothers of a man sold for twenty dirhams arrive to buy food from him. The verification is complete and public, and it is the reason the Qur'an can describe the interpretation as knowledge taught by God rather than as skill acquired by study. Verse 49 supplies the final element of that verification: the year of rain was not in the dream, was stated years in advance, and arrived. The chapter does not report the arrival. It does not need to.

The single year also sets a limit on the programme Joseph has described. His plan covers fourteen years and no further; nothing in it provides for a second famine or for a generation after the one that survived the first. The year of rain is where human management ends, and the verse marks the boundary without comment. A reader who expects a permanent solution to scarcity from a prophetic forecast will not find one here. What the chapter offers is something narrower and more useful: knowledge of when the hardship is coming, a technique for surviving it, and a fixed point beyond which the technique is no longer required. Each of the three is stated in the space of three verses.
