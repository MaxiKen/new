## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:77

> Then when he saw the moon rising, he said, “This one is my Lord!” But when it disappeared, he said, “If my Lord does not guide me, I will certainly be one of the misguided people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Candidate**

The moon rises next, and the language of the search is repeated: This one is my Lord. The sūrah is building a sequence, and the pattern of the previous verse is preserved: the appearance, the identification, the disappearance, and the rejection. What changes is the scale of the object, since the moon is brighter and more regular than the star, and the audience that venerated it would have considered it the stronger candidate.

The Book repeatedly describes the moon as a creature under command. The sūrah of Yā-Sīn says that God ordained precise phases for it until it ends up like an old curved palm stalk (36:39); the sūrah of the Heights includes the moon among the objects subjected by God's command, together with the sun and the stars (7:54); and the sūrah of Ibrāhīm describes the sun and the moon as constantly orbiting by God's arrangement (14:33). Each description leaves the moon doing what it was told, which is the argument the verse has Abraham making.

**How the Rejection Changes**

When the moon disappeared, Abraham did not only reject it. He said that if his Lord did not guide him, he would certainly be one of the misguided people. The sentence keeps the possibility of error wide open for himself, and it is the reason the verse is important in the sequence: the man who has just dismissed two candidates has no account of his own to offer, and he says so.

That admission is the opposite of the position Abraham's people were in. They had inherited a system and defended it, whereas Abraham had discarded a system and was asking for the guidance that would replace it. The Book records the same posture in the sūrah of the Cattle's later description of him when it says that God showed him the wonders of the heavens and the earth so that he might be among those who possess certainty (6:75), which means the guidance he asked for had already been promised.

**The Moon and Its Place in the Calendar**

The moon had a particular hold on the region's religious practice, since its phases governed the months and the times of pilgrimage and sacrifice. A man who abandoned the moon as a god was therefore not making a small adjustment to the local pantheon; he was cutting off the object most closely bound to the rituals of the community. The sūrah's own audience would have felt the weight of that, and the Book records elsewhere that the new moon is appointed for the timing of pilgrimage and for the reckoning of time (2:189).

The Book states the rule that Abraham was invoking when it says that whoever God guides is truly guided, and that whoever He leaves to stray will find no guardian besides Him (7:178). The sūrah of the Cave repeats the sentence in the middle of its account of the sleepers, and the sūrah of the Night Journey gives it in a longer form that ends with the warning that such people will be dragged before their Lord deaf, dumb, and blind (17:97; 18:17). Abraham's plea is therefore not a formula of humility but an accurate description of where guidance comes from, and the Book states elsewhere that the Prophet himself cannot guide whomever he loves, since it is God who guides whom He wills (28:56).

The contrast the verse draws is between the calendar and its maker. The Book nowhere suggests that timekeeping is a fault; it presents the sun and the moon as marks by which people can count the years and the reckoning. The fault is in the worship, and the verse shows Abraham separating a useful creature from the Creator whom it serves.

**The Declaration That Follows**

The sequence reaches its third and final candidate in the next verse, where the sun appears, receives the strongest identification of the three, and sets like the others. Abraham's final statement is not a rejection but a declaration of disownment: he turns his face toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth, upright, and declares that he is not among those who associate partners with God (6:78–79).

Read with this verse, the progression is exact. The star produces a rejection, the moon produces a rejection and a plea for guidance, and the sun produces the abandonment of the whole category. The sūrah is not merely recording a man's reasoning; it is showing the stages by which an honest observer leaves the visible world behind as a candidate for divinity.

**The Sufi Reading of the Stations**

The commentators with a mystical orientation read the three objects as stages of knowledge, and their reading of this verse holds that the moon represents the understanding that comes with growing clarity and reflection. The disappearance of the moon is then not an astronomical event but the collapse of a station that a seeker must pass beyond, and the plea for guidance is the voice of a person who has learned the limits of what he has reached so far.

Al-Qushayrī described the progression in terms of seeing things through God, then for God, and finally losing everything in God, and he took Abraham's successive statements as the verbal record of that ascent. The reading is a commentary rather than a substitute for the plain sense, but it captures something in the sūrah's arrangement: each candidate in the sequence is more impressive than the last, and each one is let go.

**What the Verse Contributes to the Case**

The Makkans' claim to be the heirs of Abraham is the target of the whole section, and this verse is part of the answer. Their ancestor stood under the same sky and used the same words about the moon, and then he left it. The sūrah places the audience in a position where they must either take Abraham as their model or explain why the founder of their tradition was wrong about the objects their tradition revered.

The next verse brings the sequence to its conclusion with the sun, the greatest of the visible powers, and it lets Abraham speak the sentence the audience was refusing to say.
