## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:244

> Fight in the cause of Allah, and know that Allah is AllHearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Between Two Scenes**

Nine words in English and two clauses in Arabic: the imperative, and the knowledge attached to it. Its position is the first interpretive question, and the exegetes answer it two ways. Some read the command as addressed to the revived thousands of the preceding scene — the community God killed and then restored, now conscripted by the One who has just demonstrated that its life is His to give twice. On that reading the verse completes the story's logic: you fled death and found it; you were returned to life and life now carries its obligation. Others, al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī among them, press the grammar: the verb-form is a direct address, not a historical narration, and the command belongs to the believers hearing the sūrah — a standing injunction delivered through the story rather than into it. The second reading has carried the tradition, and it makes the verse the pivot on which the whole narrative sequence turns: the fleeing thousands are the object-lesson, and the imperative is the lesson applied.

**In the Cause of Allah**

The qualifier *in the cause of Allah* — fī sabīl Allāh — is the command's entire legislation compressed into three words. The fighting the verse orders is defined not by its enemy, its territory, or its grievance but by its direction: the way, the road, the cause that is God's. The sūrah's earlier fighting-legislation (2:190–193) supplied the content of the phrase — fight those who fight you, do not transgress, until persecution is ended and the religion is God's — and this verse restates that whole architecture as an intention-test. Al-Rāzī draws the implication the tradition kept repeating: the same act of arms, performed in the same battle, is fī sabīl Allāh or it is not, depending on the cause it serves and the heart it proceeds from — and the difference between the two is invisible to every witness except the One the second clause names. The phrase converts the verse from a military order into a criterion, and it is why the tradition could read the struggle, as al-Qurṭubī records, as spiritual as well as physical: the sabīl is wide enough for the soul's campaign against itself, provided the campaign is really God's.

**The Imperative's Weight**

Read against the sūrah's own history, the command marks a threshold. The permission to fight had come recently — *Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought* (22:39) and the Baqarah's own fighting-verses — after years of Meccan patience under persecution; this verse is among the passages the exegetes cite as evidence of its post-permission revelation, since an imperative to fight presupposes the fighting's legitimacy already established. The sequence matters for the command's tone: it is not the eagerness of a new power but the obligation of a community that has learned, through the fleeing thousands and through its own thirteen years of Meccan endurance, that survival is God's gift and that gifts carry assignments. The imperative also stands bare — no enemy named, no campaign specified — which is why the general reading could carry it forward: whatever the community's battles, their legitimacy is measured by the sabīl-qualifier and their conduct by the knowledge-clause that follows.

**Fight and Know**

The two clauses are joined by a conjunction, and their joining is the verse's moral structure: *fight, and know*. Not fight because you know — fight while knowing, and let the knowing govern the fighting. The knowledge commanded is of two names: the All-Hearing and the All-Knowing. Samīʿ hears what the campaign produces — the battle-cry and the private complaint, the oath of sincerity and the muttered calculation, the words spoken over the wounded and the words spoken over the spoils. ʿAlīm knows what precedes the words — the cause the fighter actually serves, the resentment or devotion actually carried into the line, the intention beneath the intention. The commentators connect the pair to the fighting's characteristic temptations: war generates speech (boasting, rallying, bargaining) and war generates hidden motive (plunder, status, vengeance), and the two names cover both surfaces. The fighter is told that the audit of his campaign is total — acoustic and interior — and that the audit begins before the first blow.

**The Same Pair the Code Just Used**

The name-pair is not new to the sūrah, and its reappearance here does deliberate work. The Īlāʾ-verse closed with the Hearing, the Knowing (2:227) standing over the marriage's most private deliberations; the same pair now stands over the campaign's. The pairing's distribution across the code is instructive: God hears and knows wherever human acts have an unseen interior — the oath in the heart, the resolve about a wife, the intention in a war. Al-Rāzī's spiritual reading rests on exactly this continuity: the struggle legislated here, physical or inward, is subject to the same surveillance the domestic legislation invoked, and the fighter's sincerity is audited by the same names that audited the swearer's. The verse thus binds the sūrah's two great subjects — the house and the battlefield — under one divine attention, and its message to both is identical: the outward act is legislated; the inward cause is known.

**The Shortest of Commands**

The tradition noticed the verse's brevity and read it as density. After the twenty-verse code of marriage and divorce, after the four-months-and-ten, the halves and waivers and scaled provisions, the sūrah orders a campaign in a sentence with no subordinate clause — and then spends its second half on the names that supervise it. The disproportion is the point: the act commanded is simple to state and impossible to purify without the knowledge-clause, so the verse allocates its words accordingly. Ibn Kathīr reads it together with the narrative that follows — the requested king, the tested river-crossing, the few against the many — as one continuous instruction: the imperative states the duty, the story exhibits the duty's conditions, and the names guarantee the duty's audit. The believer addressed by the verse carries away three things: an order, a criterion, and two names — and the tradition's summary of all three is the phrase the verse itself supplies: fight in the cause of Allah, and know Who hears the fighting.
