## Sūrah an-Naml 27:45

> And We certainly sent to the people of Thamûd their brother Ṣâliḥ, proclaiming, “Worship Allah,” but they suddenly split into two opposing groups.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A New People, a Familiar Opening**

Sheba's story ended in a declaration of faith. The chapter now moves to Thamūd, and the shift in register is immediate. Where the Sulaymān narrative ran for thirty verses with dialogue, deliberation and tests, this one opens with a formula the Qur'an uses again and again: We sent to them their brother, saying worship God.

The compression signals what kind of story this is. Thamūd's account appears in several chapters — al-Aʿrāf, Hūd, ash-Shuʿarāʾ, al-Qamar — and readers of the Qur'an knew it. An-Naml is not retelling it but selecting from it, and what this chapter takes is the part about a plot and its unravelling. The people were an ancient Arabian nation, remembered in the Qur'an for carving dwellings out of rock, and their ruins in northwestern Arabia stood on routes the first audience used. This is not distant legend for them. It is a place they could visit.

**"Their Brother" Is a Deliberate Word**

Ṣāliḥ is called *akhāhum*, their brother. The Qur'an applies the same term to Hūd with ʿĀd, to Lūṭ with his people, to Shuʿayb with Madyan. The word means one of them by descent, sharing their lineage and their speech.

This detail forecloses a standard objection. A messenger who arrives from outside can be dismissed as a foreigner who does not understand the community, its history or its arrangements. Ṣāliḥ has no such distance available to him, and neither do his people. He is their kinsman, raised among them, speaking their dialect, and whatever he is telling them cannot be filed under the customs of some other nation. The Qur'an makes the same point about Muḥammad ﷺ to the Quraysh, who had known him for forty years before he recited anything. Sending a brother removes the easiest excuse before the message is delivered.

**The Whole Message in Two Words**

What Ṣāliḥ proclaims is *an uʿbudū Allāh* — worship God. That is the entire content of his mission as this verse reports it. No law, no ritual, no social programme, no argument against the existing gods.

The Qur'an's prophetic missions are consistently summarised this way, and the summary is not laziness. Everything else these messengers said — and Thamūd's story elsewhere includes warnings about a she-camel, about corruption in the land, about the treatment of the poor — follows from that one instruction and is unintelligible without it. Placing worship first also identifies where the conflict actually lies. Thamūd did not reject Ṣāliḥ over an argument about ethics; they rejected him over a question of who is owed worship, and the social consequences followed. A chapter that has just shown a queen redirecting her worship and a court refusing to redirect theirs states the demand here in its barest form.

**The Split Is Instantaneous**

The verb is *fa-idhā hum farīqāni yakhtaṣimūn* — and at once they were two parties, disputing. The particle *idhā* in this construction marks something sudden and unlooked-for, the narrative equivalent of a jump.

Al-Ṭabarī identifies the two groups plainly: those who believed Ṣāliḥ and those who denied him. What the grammar adds is the speed. Two words of proclamation, and a unified nation is a divided one before anything else has happened. This is a general observation the Qur'an makes about revelation in several places: a message that requires a decision cannot be received by a community without dividing it, because assent and refusal are the only available responses and both will be taken. The division is not the messenger's fault and it is not presented as a failure of his mission. It is what happens when a real demand reaches people who were previously agreed only because nothing had been asked of them.

**Two Parties Quarrelling, Not Two Opinions Coexisting**

The participle is *yakhtaṣimūn*, from a root meaning to litigate, to contend as adversaries. It is not the word for holding different views. It describes active conflict, each side pressing against the other.

That escalation is what the rest of the Thamūd narrative in this chapter will trace. Within four verses the dispute will have produced a conspiracy to murder Ṣāliḥ and his household by night. The Qur'an is showing the sequence rather than asserting it: a proclamation, an immediate split, a hardening into factions, and then violence planned in secret by nine men. Nothing in the progression requires an unusual amount of wickedness at any single step, which is why it is worth tracing. The chapter's earlier verses described people whose deeds are made to look good to them, and a faction that has spent enough time contending will find that murder has come to look like self-defence. Ṣāliḥ's first recorded words to them come next, and they are a question about why they are in such a hurry.

**Where Thamūd's Story Stands in the Chapter**

The chapter's four narratives are arranged with care. Mūsā before a court that knew and refused; Sulaymān and a queen who did not know and accepted; now Thamūd, and after them the people of Lūṭ. Two prophets sent to foreign powers, then two sent to their own kin.

That second pair changes the terms. Mūsā walked into Egypt as an outsider with a demand, and the hostility he met is at least intelligible. Ṣāliḥ speaks to his own nation, in their language, about the God they nominally acknowledged, and the response is a plot to kill him in the night. The chapter is closing off the explanations that let a reader keep the lesson at a distance. It is not foreignness that produces the refusal, and it is not the strangeness of the message. The Quraysh hearing this sūrah had a brother of their own reciting it to them, and the parallel needs no drawing.
