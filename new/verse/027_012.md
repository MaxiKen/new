## Sūrah an-Naml 27:12

> Now put your hand through ˹the opening of˺ your collar, it will come out ˹shining˺ white, unblemished. ˹These are two˺ of nine signs for Pharaoh and his people. They have truly been a rebellious people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Sign, Using the Body Rather Than an Object**

The staff was external — a tool Mūsā carried and could put down. The second sign uses his own hand. He is told to slip it inside the neck of his garment and withdraw it, and it emerges shining white, *bayḍāʾ min ghayri sūʾ*, white with nothing wrong in it.

That last qualification is doing precise work. Whiteness appearing suddenly on skin was, in the ancient Near East, the recognised mark of a disease, and a hand that turned white would have been read as leprous by anyone who saw it. The Qur'an blocks that reading in the same phrase that reports the miracle: white, but not from any affliction. The sign is light, not illness. Al-Rāzī notes that the Arabic construction *min ghayri sūʾ* functions as an explicit exclusion rather than a decorative adjective. Without it the miracle would be ambiguous, and an ambiguous sign is no sign at all in front of a court that will be looking for any reading that lets it dismiss what it sees.

**Shown in Private, Performed in Public**

Mūsā sees this happen on the mountainside, alone. The same transformation occurs again much later, in Pharaoh's presence: *“Then he drew his hand ˹out of his collar˺ and it was ˹shining˺ white for all to see.”* (26:33). The pattern matches the staff, which also quivered first in the dark and then before the court.

The rehearsal principle is consistent. A prophet is shown his signs before he needs them, so that when the moment arrives he is demonstrating rather than discovering. There is also a difference between the two rehearsals worth marking. When the staff moved, Mūsā ran. When the hand shines, no fear is recorded at all. Between the two moments he has been told that messengers have no fear in this presence and that his own past has a remedy. The absence of a reported reaction to the second sign is the narrative's way of showing that the reassurance took hold. Al-Qaṣaṣ preserves an additional instruction at this point — to draw his arm to his side to steady himself — which suggests the residue of alarm was still being managed even as the second sign was given.

**Two of Nine: Counting the Signs Given to Mūsā**

The verse places these two miracles inside a larger set: *among nine signs*. The Qur'an states the number again elsewhere — *“We surely gave Moses nine clear signs.”* (17:101) — without listing them in either place, and the commentators assembled the list from other passages.

The standard reconstruction begins with the staff and the hand, then draws the rest from the plagues recorded in al-Aʿrāf: *“So We plagued them with floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood—all as clear signs”* (7:133). That gives seven, and the remaining two are usually supplied as the famine years and the shortage of crops, which al-Aʿrāf mentions immediately before. Other arrangements circulated; some counted the parting of the sea, others substituted differently. The disagreement is over which items fill the list, not over the number, which the Qur'an fixes twice. What the number itself asserts is abundance. Pharaoh's court was not asked to accept a claim on the strength of one ambiguous event. Nine separate demonstrations were delivered, and the chapter's next verse reports what was done with them.

**The Signs Are Addressed to Pharaoh, Not to Mūsā**

The verse specifies the recipients: *for Pharaoh and his people*. Mūsā sees the signs, but he is not their audience. He is the carrier. This clarifies what the private demonstration on the mountain was for — not to convince him, since he is already in conversation with God and needs no proof, but to equip him.

The phrase also widens the target beyond one man. Pharaoh is named, but *his people* are included, which distributes the responsibility across a society rather than isolating it in a ruler. This is the Qur'an's consistent treatment of the Egyptian episode: the court, the chiefs and the population are all addressed, and the destruction that eventually comes falls on an army rather than on an individual. A system of oppression is maintained by many hands, and the signs were sent to all of them. Al-Qurṭubī makes the related point that the miracle given to each prophet matched what his people valued most — and Egypt, whose priests were expert in what looked like transformation, was given transformations no priest could reproduce.

**A Verdict Delivered Before the Mission Starts**

The verse closes with an assessment: *They have truly been a rebellious people.* The word is *fāsiqīn*, from a root meaning to break out — the image is of something bursting its casing, a date splitting its skin. A *fāsiq* is one who has broken out of the boundary he was inside.

Two things follow from the placement of this clause. First, the tense is past and continuous: they *have been* rebellious, a settled condition rather than a fresh accusation. Mūsā is being told what he is walking into before he walks into it, which is a kindness rather than a discouragement. Second, and more pointedly, God states the verdict before the signs are delivered. Foreknowledge of the outcome does not cancel the sending. The nine signs still go, the argument is still made, the case is still put — because the point of the mission is not to inform God of something He does not know, but to leave Pharaoh's court without an excuse. What that court did when the evidence arrived is stated in the following verse.
