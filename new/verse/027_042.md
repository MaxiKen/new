## Sūrah an-Naml 27:42

> So when she arrived, it was said ˹to her˺, “Is your throne like this?” She replied, “It looks to be the same. We have ˹already˺ received knowledge ˹of Solomon’s prophethood˺ before this ˹miracle˺, and have submitted ˹to Allah˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Journey Is Skipped Entirely**

She travelled from southern Arabia to Jerusalem, and the Qur'an gives it four words: *fa-lammā jā'at*, so when she came. No account of the caravan, the distance, the escort, or what she thought on the way.

The omission is consistent with how the chapter has handled her throughout. Her name is withheld, her city is unnamed, the contents of her gift are not described, and now her journey is compressed to a subordinate clause. What the text preserves is every word she speaks and every decision she makes. This is a portrait built entirely from speech and choice, which is why the question put to her on arrival carries so much weight — it is one of only four occasions in the chapter where we hear her think.

**The Question Is Carefully Unfair**

*A-hākadhā ʿarshuk* — is your throne like this? The phrasing does not ask whether this is her throne. It asks about likeness, and the object in front of her has been altered.

Al-Qurṭubī reports the observation that the question was a trap for the intellect. A plain yes would be wrong, because the throne has been changed and is not simply as she left it. A plain no would also be wrong, because it is in fact hers. The correct answer lies between the two, and Arabic has to be handled with some care to reach it. Nothing in her situation helps. She has arrived after a long journey in a foreign court, is being questioned without preamble by a king who refused her gift and threatened her kingdom, and is shown an object that should be a thousand miles away. The pressure is considerable and the question is a puzzle.

**"It Looks to Be the Same"**

Her answer is *ka-annahu huwa* — as though it were it. Three words, and they thread the needle exactly.

Al-Ṭabarī reads the reply as expressing doubt, and that is the surface of it. But al-Qurṭubī's reading is the better one: she assessed the object correctly. It was neither entirely the same nor entirely different, and she said so with a particle of comparison rather than a verb of identification. That is not evasion. It is a precise report of an uncertain perception, which is the hardest kind of honesty to produce under pressure, because both confident answers are socially easier. She could have claimed recognition and looked perceptive; she could have denied it and looked unshakeable. She stated what she actually saw. Everything the chapter has shown of her — the fair judgment of the letter, the consultation, the test by gift — comes to a point in these three words.

**Who Speaks the Second Sentence?**

The verse's second half is disputed among the commentators, and the two readings change its meaning considerably. On the reading this translation follows, the queen continues speaking: knowledge came to her before this, and she had already submitted. Al-Zamakhsharī records this as a minority position — that she is acknowledging the power of God and the truth of Sulaymān's prophethood before the throne and the pavilion were shown to her.

The majority took the words as Sulaymān's, spoken to his court: we were given knowledge before her and we were submitters. Al-Ṭabarī reads the following verse as continuing that speech. Both are grammatically available, since Arabic supplies no speech markers here. The minority reading has a real advantage: it explains why she is described as arriving at all, since a woman travelling to a king who demanded her submission has already conceded something, and it makes her later declaration in the palace a completion rather than a reversal. The majority reading has the advantage of the following verse, which speaks of her in the third person as one hindered by what she worshipped — an awkward thing for her to say of herself.

**Knowledge Before the Miracle**

Whichever speaker is intended, the sentence makes the same claim in substance: the knowledge preceded the wonder. Either Sulaymān's court had it before she arrived, or she had it before the throne was shown.

That ordering is the theological point and it runs against the obvious reading of the scene. A displaced throne looks like the sort of thing that would compel belief, and the chapter is at pains to say it did not do the work. Miracles in the Qur'an consistently confirm what reasoning has already made available rather than substituting for it; Pharaoh's court saw nine of them and remained where it was. If the words are hers, she is saying she had worked out enough before arriving that the spectacle in front of her is confirmation rather than cause. If they are his, he is saying the same about his own people. Either way the sentence declines to credit the miracle with the conversion, and the narrative bears that out — her declaration of faith comes two verses later, prompted by something far smaller than a flying throne.

**"It Was Said" — the Questioner Goes Unnamed**

The verse uses a passive: *qīla*, it was said. Sulaymān gave the order to alter the throne in the previous verse, but the question itself is attributed to nobody in particular.

The construction recurs in this passage — she is told to enter the palace in the same impersonal form two verses later — and its effect is to take the king out of the frame at the moments that matter most for her. She is not being interrogated by a rival monarch; she is being presented with a question and a doorway. Whatever she concludes, she concludes without a face to concede to. That anonymity is consistent with how the whole encounter has been managed, from a letter that put God's name before Sulaymān's to a correction about the floor delivered as a flat statement of fact. The Qur'an is arranging a sequence in which nothing she does can be read as deference to the man in the room.
