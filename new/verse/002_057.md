## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:57

> And ˹remember when˺ We shaded you with clouds and sent down to you manna and quails, ˹saying˺, “Eat from the good things We have provided for you.” The evildoers ˹certainly˺ did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Desert Provisioned as If Indoors**

The verse describes two arrangements made in a wilderness: shade overhead and food arriving without cultivation. The Arabic for the shade describes a covering that travels with the people, and the two foods named are the bread that came down and the birds that were sent; the Book mentions them together in every passage where it describes this period. What the community received was not a victory or an inheritance but the ordinary requirements of survival, supplied daily.

The Book tells the same story elsewhere with the springs added: *“We shaded them with clouds and sent down to them manna and quails, ˹saying˺, ‘Eat from the good things We have provided for you.’”* (7:160). The earlier sūrah also records the twelve springs that appeared from a rock at the same period, so that water completes the list. A people travelling through barren country were given all four necessities without producing any of them.

**The Instruction Attached to the Gift**

The provision comes with a sentence: eat from the good things We have provided for you. The Arabic term for good things carries the sense of what is wholesome, pleasant and lawful at once, and the Book uses it regularly for what God permits. The instruction to eat the good therefore has a restrictive edge as well as a generous one, since a community that is told to eat what is wholesome is also being told where the boundary lies.

The Book returns to this terminology when it corrects people who invent prohibitions, and it applies the correction specifically to the Children of Israel: they are told not to forbid the wholesome things God has made lawful (5:87), and another verse attributes to them a set of restrictions they imposed upon themselves (4:160). The gift in this verse therefore comes with a reminder about who decides what may be eaten.

**"They Did Not Wrong Us"**

The closing sentence states a correction of the usual way of putting such matters. The Book does not say that the community's failures injured God; it says the opposite, and then it relocates the damage: they wronged themselves. The same phrase appears when the springs and the quails are described in al-Aʿrāf (7:160), which makes it a fixed formula of the Book's account of this period.

The sentence is therefore a summary of everything the sūrah has narrated about these people so far: the calf, the demand to see God, and the failure that the following verses will record. Each act was committed by a community that had been given its existence and its food by someone else, and none of it reduced its benefactor in any way. The harm stayed with the perpetrators, which is why the Book keeps insisting on the point in the middle of a list of gifts.

**The Gifts and the Grumbling Side by Side**

This verse stands between two accounts of discontent. The community has just been raised after a thunderbolt; in a few verses it will ask for vegetables instead of the food it has been given. The Book places the provision in the middle so that the reader sees what was supplied before he reads the complaint, and the arrangement turns the complaint into something other than a request for help.

The Qur'an uses this technique whenever it describes human ingratitude, and the pattern is consistent: the gift is stated in detail, and the response is recorded without defence. What the reader takes from the sequence is not a verdict on an ancient people but a description of a mechanism, and the Book will name the mechanism in the next passage when it reports what the community said about the food.

**Shade, Bread and Birds as Signs**

The Book treats provision as evidence in the same way it treats the created world as evidence. The sūrah's earlier passage argued from the earth being made a dwelling and the sky a canopy; this verse reports a smaller, sharper instance of the same arrangement, in which the sky produces food and the sun is blocked by cloud. Anyone present would have been able to see that the arrangement had no human author.

The Book's practice here is the one it follows throughout: it points to what its audience already knows and asks who arranged it. The question is not put in this verse, since the next passage will supply the community's answer, and the reader reaches that answer with the full picture of what had been done for them beforehand.

**The Formula That Keeps Israel's Failures in Proportion**

The phrase about wronging themselves appears in the sūrah whenever the account of this community's breaches threatens to become a story about a rejected people. The Book keeps the charge attached to the offenders, and it keeps the mercy attached to the one who provided, so that the record holds both facts at once. The community is neither written off nor excused, and the sentence that says neither is placed here, between the gift and the ingratitude that followed it.

**Provision in the Book's Vocabulary of Signs**

The Book treats the food given in the desert as evidence of the same kind as the sky and the earth. It later tells the community that they were given what they asked for and that their provision arrived without cultivation, and it counts the whole arrangement among the favours whose memory the sūrah keeps invoking. What is unusual about the gifts in this passage is their daily character: bread arriving each morning and birds arriving with it are provisions a community can observe rather than a demonstration it must remember.

That ordinariness is why the Book attaches the instruction to eat to the gift itself. A meal that appears without a farm behind it is a fact about the world that no explanation can account for except the one the sūrah has been giving. The community's later complaint about the menu is judged against this arrangement, and the judgment is already implicit in the sequence of verses.
