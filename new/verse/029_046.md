## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:46

> Do not argue with the People of the Book unless gracefully, except with those of them who act wrongfully. And say, “We believe in what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to you. Our God and your God is ˹only˺ One. And to Him we ˹fully˺ submit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Instruction About Method, Not Retreat**

The verse does not forbid discussion with Jews and Christians; it regulates the manner and then hands the Muslim a formula to use. Disagreement is assumed, and the assumption is realistic, since the community that received this chapter lived alongside communities that held different scriptures. What the sentence excludes is the kind of argument that treats an opponent as an adversary to be defeated, and the Book elsewhere gives the fuller instruction to invite with wisdom and kind advice and to debate only in the best manner (16:125). Commentators explain the phrase as requiring that evidence be drawn from God's proof and that the tone remain good even when the other party's tone does not. The rule is stated at the level of method because the method is what the Muslim controls.

The method also sets the terms on which a believer's own case is to be made. Someone who argues in this manner is not required to win, only to present what the two sides share and to state his position clearly, and the instruction assumes that his opponent may still refuse after the argument has been put.

**The Exception for Those Who Act Wrongfully**

The clause that carves out an exception has been read in two ways. One reading applies it to members of the People of the Book who turn away from what God has sent and persist in arrogance; the other applies it to those who take up arms against the Muslims and refuse any agreement. The distinction between the readings is narrow, and both confine the exception to conduct rather than to identity. Some later authorities held that the verse was set aside by the command to fight those who reject the faith among the People of the Book, and al-Ṭabarī argued against that position, holding that a text cannot be treated as superseded without decisive proof. What matters for a reader of the sūrah is that the default rule remains courteous argument, and hostility is a response to specific aggression rather than to a category of people.

**What Muslims Are Told to Say**

The formula given in the verse is a statement of shared ground: we believe in what was revealed to us and in what was revealed to you, one God for both, and to Him we submit. The same declaration appears in a fuller form earlier in the Book, where believers are told to affirm the revelation given to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes, and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets, making no distinction among any of them (2:136; 3:84). The wording is carefully constructed. It does not concede that the two revelations are identical, and it does not deny the scriptures of the other community either. It affirms a common object of worship and a shared posture of submission, and it leaves the differences to be discussed under the rule of the first sentence.

**Submission as the Common Noun**

The final phrase of the formula returns to the root of the word Islam, describing the believers as those who submit themselves completely to God. The Book uses the same vocabulary for Abraham, who was neither a Jew nor a Christian but a man of pure submission and no polytheist (3:67), and its point in doing so is that the category existed before either of the two later communities acquired its name. What the Muslim is told to announce, then, is not the novelty of his religion but its continuity with the submission that the prophets practised. This framing is the reason the sūrah can address the People of the Book as parties to a shared history rather than as strangers, and it sets the terms on which the disagreement is to proceed.

The choice of a common noun also gives the reader a way to describe both communities without flattening the difference between them. What they share is a posture toward God; what distinguishes them is the record each accepts, and the verse leaves that second question to the argument it recommends.

**The Prophet's Instruction About Reports**

The manner prescribed in this verse found practical expression in a rule the Prophet gave his companions. When some Jews recited the Torah in Hebrew and explained it in Arabic to the Muslims, he told them not to believe the People of the Book or to disbelieve them, but to say that they believe in God and in what has been revealed (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4485, narrated by Abū Hurayrah). The rule is a discipline for the mind as much as for the tongue. Anything brought from another scripture falls into one of three classes — consistent with what God has revealed, contradictory to it, or unclassifiable without independent verification — and the Prophet's instruction distinguishes the three without permitting either credulity or blanket rejection.

The instruction also protects the believing community from a habit that damages its own case. A message defended with invented stories loses the authority it claims, and the Book's concern in this verse is with the reliability of what is repeated in the Prophet's name as much as with the conduct of the debate.

**Where the Two Communities Actually Agree**

The formula given in this verse is short because the shared ground it names is short, and the Book develops the same list elsewhere without altering it. Believers are told to affirm the revelation given to them and to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the descendants, and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and the prophets from their Lord, making no distinction among any of them (2:136), and the same declaration is repeated with the note that to God they submit (3:84). One God, one line of messengers, one final accounting, and one posture of submission: those are the points on which the two communities agree, and every one of them is a matter the Book treats as fundamental. The disagreement that remains is real, and the verse's rule deals with it by requiring argument rather than war, which is only possible because the parties already share something to argue from.

**Why the Rule Outlives Its Occasion**

The conditions of the first audience have long since passed, and the sentence still functions, because every generation of Muslims meets people who hold other scriptures and other claims. The verse supplies the community with a position it can hold without either hostility or surrender: argue well, refuse aggression, and state plainly what one believes about the shared and the unshared. The Book's method in controversial passages is typically to fix the principle and leave the tactics to circumstance, and this verse does exactly that. Its formula is short enough to be recited in a marketplace, and precise enough to prevent a Muslim from claiming that another community's scripture is simply false or that it is simply his own. The verse also teaches the community how to hold a position without becoming its prisoner. A person who states what he believes, concedes what he shares, and argues in good faith can leave a disagreement unresolved without concluding that it must be settled by force, and the Book's default rule in these verses is exactly that patience. What the exception permits is a response to aggression, not a licence to convert a difference of scripture into a quarrel.
