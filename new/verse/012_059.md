## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:59

> When he had provided them with their supplies, he demanded, “Bring me your brother on your father’s side. Do you not see that I give full measure and I am the best of hosts?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Supplies Come First, the Demand Second**

The order of the verse matters. Joseph arranges their provisions — the food they have come to buy, loaded and measured out — and only then makes his request. Nothing has been withheld as leverage; the brothers already have what they travelled for when the condition is attached to their next journey. The sequence distinguishes this demand from extortion. A ruler who wanted to force compliance would hold the grain back until the brother arrived. Joseph gives the grain and asks for the brother afterwards, which means the brothers leave Egypt with food in their possession and an obligation outstanding. The pressure he applies operates on their return journey, not on their present need. That ordering is what makes the scene a test rather than a hostage-taking. The brothers leave Egypt with their grain loaded and their money restored; nothing is withheld from them at the moment the condition is announced. What they carry home is therefore both a provision and an argument, and the argument travels with them all the way to Canaan.

**"A Brother of Yours From Your Father"**

The phrasing is precise and unusual. Joseph does not say "your youngest brother" or name anyone. He asks for a brother of theirs *from their father* — the Arabic specifies the paternal line, which implies that this brother is not from their mother. The commentators identify him as Benjamin, the full brother of Joseph, and note that the Qur'an never gives his name. The specification does two things at once. It tells the reader that Joseph knows the family's composition in detail, which he should not know from anything the brothers have said in this verse. And it identifies the one brother Joseph has most reason to want to see: the son of the same mother, the child left behind when he was taken.

**Why He Wants the Brother Brought**

The commentators offer a practical reason alongside the personal one. The ten brothers arrived with eleven camels and requested eleven loads — ten for themselves and one for a dependent brother at home (12:65 refers to the additional camel-load). Joseph's demand for the brother's appearance functions as verification: a claim about a dependant justifying an extra measure of food during a famine should be supported by the dependant's presence. On this reading the demand is administrative, and it is the kind of control a granary official exercising rationing would naturally impose. The personal motive runs alongside it without contradicting it, and the chapter does not choose between them. Both are consistent with everything the narrative has shown. The chapter is content to leave the motive unstated at this point, and the withholding is a technique it uses elsewhere. Joseph's plans are announced as actions before their reasons are given, so the reader travels alongside the brothers through a sequence whose purpose becomes clear only in the closing scenes, when the family is finally assembled in Egypt.

**"Do You Not See That I Give Full Measure"**

Joseph argues from his own conduct. The brothers have just watched him load their provisions without short measure, and he points at that fact as the ground of his credibility. The appeal is not to his authority, his rank or his power to refuse them. It is to what they have observed with their own eyes: this official does not cheat. The argument has a specific force in a famine economy, where short measure is the ordinary expectation and the buyer's protection is scarce. By identifying himself as the man who gives full measure, Joseph places himself in the category of traders the brothers can trust, and then asks them to trust him with a condition that will cost them a difficult conversation at home.

**"I Am the Best of Hosts"**

The second claim is about hospitality. *Khayr al-munzilīn* — the best of those who provide lodging or provision — describes a host who exceeds what is required of him. The verse has already demonstrated the claim by giving the brothers their supplies before attaching any condition. The rhetorical effect is to make refusal awkward. A guest who has been treated well and fed fully is in a poor position to decline his host's single request, particularly when the request concerns a member of his own family. Joseph is not threatening them in this verse; he is establishing that the demand comes from a man who has already been generous, and that generosity is the leverage. The claim is also verifiable by his guests. They have been fed, lodged, given full measure, and had their capital returned inside their own sacks, so the statement is not self-praise but a summary of what they have just experienced. Joseph grounds his authority over them in a record they cannot dispute, and he does it in a handful of words.

**A Demand That Tests the Demanders**

The brothers once took a pledge involving a son of Jacob. They asked their father to entrust Joseph to them, promising they would look after him and that they were his sincere advisers (12:11–12). The request in this verse repeats the shape of that earlier one: a demand that a son be handed over, made to the same father through the same intermediaries, and carrying the same implicit promise of safe conduct. The brothers will hear the echo, and their response in verse 61 — we will seek to win him from his father — uses the language of persuasion, which is what they used the first time. The test is whether they will keep the promise they are about to make.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The biblical parallel has Joseph accuse the brothers of being spies who have come to see the weakness of the land (Genesis 42:9), and use that accusation to justify detaining one of them. The Qur'anic account contains no accusation. Joseph does not charge the brothers with anything, does not question their honesty, and does not imply that their story about an eleventh brother is a lie. He asks for the brother and states what will happen if he is not brought (12:60). The difference is consistent with the chapter's treatment of Joseph from the beginning: he applies pressure toward a definite end, and the end is the arrival of a specific person rather than the punishment of the men in front of him.

Two further silences deserve notice. The verse does not record Joseph's identity being concealed by any device — no disguise is mentioned, no interpreter is introduced, no claim is made about why the brothers failed to recognise him beyond what verse 58 already stated. And the verse does not explain the demand to the brothers themselves. They are told to bring a brother and given two reasons for trusting the man asking, neither of which discloses who he is. The chapter keeps Joseph's interior hidden from them exactly as it keeps it hidden from the reader, and the effect is that the demand reads as administrative until verse 69 discloses what it was for.
