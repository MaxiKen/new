## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:3

> ˹Remember˺ when the Prophet had ˹once˺ confided something to one of his wives, then when she disclosed it ˹to another wife˺ and Allah made it known to him, he presented ˹to her˺ part of what was disclosed and overlooked a part. So when he informed her of it, she exclaimed, “Who told you this?” He replied, “I was informed by the All-Knowing, All-Aware.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Confidence Given and Broken**

The verse narrates a small domestic event with unusual precision. Something was told to one wife in confidence; she passed it to another; the Prophet ﷺ was informed of the breach from above. The commentators identify the parties: Ḥafṣa was the wife who had been asked to keep the matter private, and ʿĀʾisha was the one she told. The subject was the episode behind the sūrah's opening rebuke. When she was later asked whether she had spoken of it, she denied it.

What makes the narration unusual is how little it moralises. No adjective is attached to the disclosure, no denunciation follows, and the woman is not named in the text. The Qur'an states the sequence and moves on to what the Prophet ﷺ did about it. That restraint is itself a comment on how such matters should be handled: the offence is recorded accurately and left without embellishment, which is close to the opposite of how a betrayed confidence is usually discussed inside a family.

**Two Wives, and the Ordinary Pressures of a Shared House**

The event is trivial in its content and instructive in its setting. A household in which several wives share one husband on a fixed rotation generates exactly this sort of friction, and the Qur'an declines to pretend otherwise about the Prophet's home. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, asked years later who the two women were that had backed each other against the Prophet ﷺ, answered without hesitation: ʿĀʾisha and Ḥafṣa (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4913). The same reports describe him warning his own daughter about arguing with the Prophet ﷺ, and describe the community's alarm when the Prophet ﷺ withdrew from his wives for a month.

Preserving this material served a purpose beyond history. It established that the Prophet's wives were not a category of being above scrutiny, which is precisely the conclusion the chapter will drive home in its closing verses with the wives of Noah and Lot. It also gave the community a realistic picture of the man they were to imitate. A household presented as frictionless would have been useless as a model and, to anyone who knew the participants, obviously false.

**Disclosure That Comes From Outside the House**

*“and Allah made it known to him”* (66:3) is the hinge of the verse. The Prophet ﷺ did not learn of the breach through gossip, a servant or his own suspicion; the information was given to him. This is stated plainly and it changes the character of the episode from a domestic quarrel into a demonstration.

The demonstration concerns the nature of prophecy itself. A man who is receiving legislation about marriage, inheritance and war from beyond himself is here shown receiving something small and verifiable — a private conversation in his own house, repeated back to a woman who knew for certain whether he could have discovered it any other way. Her reaction, *“Who told you this?”* (66:3), is the response of someone who has just tested a claim and found it holds. The Qur'an offers grand evidence for revelation elsewhere; here it offers a piece of evidence that only one person in the world was positioned to check, and records her checking it.

**Telling Part and Withholding Part**

*“he presented ˹to her˺ part of what was disclosed and overlooked a part”* (66:3) is the detail the commentators dwell on, and rightly. The Prophet ﷺ had been given the full account and chose to confront her with some of it. The restraint is read in two ways. Most take it as kindness: he spared her the full exposure because there was no purpose in it beyond humiliation. Others read the partial disclosure as God's, so that the Prophet ﷺ was given a portion and the rest was withheld from him.

The first reading is stronger, because the sentence attributes the selection to him — he made part known and turned away from part. It also matches the Qur'an's picture of his character in dealing with those who had wronged him: *“Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you”* (3:159). The lesson generalises easily and is rarely practised. Knowing everything about another person's fault does not oblige a man to say everything. Enough must be said for the wrong to be named and stopped; the remainder is his to leave unspoken, and leaving it unspoken is a decision about the relationship's future rather than about the past.

**Two Names That Answer Her Question**

She asks who informed him, and the reply names no one in the house: *“I was informed by the All-Knowing, All-Aware”* (66:3). The pairing of *al-ʿAlīm* with *al-Khabīr* is deliberate. The first denotes knowledge in its full extent; the second denotes acquaintance with the inner detail of things, the hidden particulars that leave no outward trace. A private remark passed between two women in a Madinan apartment is exactly the kind of thing *khabīr* covers.

The answer also settles the question of what kind of failing a broken confidence is. It could have been treated as a social lapse, dealt with by apology and forgotten. Naming God as the source of the disclosure moves it into a different register: what was said in a room where nobody else was present was heard, and reported, and came back. The chapter will build on this steadily — the wives are told their hearts have inclined, believers are told to guard their families, and everyone is called to a repentance that must be sincere rather than performed. A God described as *al-Khabīr* is one before whom the distinction between sincere and performed is not available to be blurred.
