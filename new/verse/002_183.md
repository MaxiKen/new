## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:183

> O believers! Fasting is prescribed for you—as it was for those before you—so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Kutiba**

The verse begins with the sūrah's legislative formula — *kutiba ʿalaykum*, it is written upon you, it is prescribed — the same verb that established the retaliation in cases of murder (2:178), and the formula carries the same weight here: fasting is not recommended, not ancestral custom, not a spiritual option; it is written. The obligation it creates became, in its final form, the fast of Ramadan, counted by all the schools of law among the five pillars of Islam: complete abstinence from food, drink, and sexual relations from dawn until sunset, across every day of the lunar month. The verse that prescribes it is addressed to the believers specifically — O you who believe — where the food permission had gone out to all humanity (2:168); the fast belongs to the covenant community, and its prescription dates, the exegetical sources record, to shortly before the Battle of Badr, in the second year of the Hijrah — the same season of legislation that gave the community its qiblah. A people turned toward the Kaʿbah, given its murder-law and its wills-law, now receives the law of its months: the body itself enters the legislation.

**As It Was for Those Before You**

The prescription arrives with a company clause: fasting was prescribed for those before you as well. The exegetes identified the predecessors at several scopes — the Christians, the People of the Book generally, or all peoples in general — and the clause does its work at every scope. It answers the objection the new obligations invited: this is not a burden invented for you, not a novel austerity by which this community is tested more harshly than the righteous of history. The fast is old — older than the qiblah controversy, older than the sects, recognizable in the practice of the very communities whose claims the sūrah had just adjudicated. The clause also establishes the fast's ecumenical intelligibility: a discipline every prophetic tradition received is a discipline about something universal in the human condition, and what it addresses — appetite's sovereignty over the will — is the same in every generation that practiced it. The believers are enrolled in an ancient order rather than initiated into a private one, and the enrollment is itself consolation: the difficulty ahead has been borne before, by people the Qur'an elsewhere honors, under the same prescription's essential form.

**So That You May Become Mindful**

The purpose clause names the fast's product: taqwā — mindfulness, reverence, the guarded consciousness of God that the sūrah has made the end of every law it legislates. The retaliation was given so that the community might become mindful (2:179); the bequest is an obligation on the mindful (2:180); and the fast is prescribed so that perhaps the fasting will produce the same quality. The fit between the discipline and the goal is exact, and it is why the verse needs no elaboration. Taqwā is the state of a person whose actions are governed by an unseen standard — who abstains from what he could take because a Presence he cannot see forbids it. Fasting is that state rendered physical and continuous: for the whole bright day, with food in reach and appetite intact, the fasting person abstains from what is otherwise lawful to him, and no observer can tell whether he is fasting — the discipline's compliance is invisible, its inspection internal. A month of this is a month of practiced taqwā, the heart learning by repetition that the unseen command outweighs the visible opportunity. The other pillars have public forms — the prayer is seen, the alms are counted, the pilgrimage is traveled; the fast alone is a secret between the servant and his Lord, which is why the tradition reports, in the sacred ḥadīth of the fast, God saying that every deed of the human being is for himself except the fasting — it is for Me, and I reward it.

**The Hunger That Teaches**

The pedagogy of the fast runs through the body, and the verse's prescription takes the whole body as its classroom. Hunger and thirst are the two appetites no human can argue with; for one lunar month the believer argues with them anyway, daily, and wins by abstention rather than satisfaction. The training generalizes immediately: the appetite that is refused at noon is the same faculty that must refuse the forbidden morsel of 2:168, the bribed judge of 2:188, the illicit gain of 2:174 — the fast is a rehearsal of every refusal the law will later demand, performed in the easiest case, where the refused thing is lawful and only the hour forbids it. The tradition drew the same conclusion from the fast's effects on speech and temper, requiring the fasting person to abstain from false and idle talk as well as food, so that the discipline covers the tongue and the appetite together. This is taqwā being manufactured: not the abstract reverence of doctrine but the drilled reflex of a body that has learned, thirty times running, that a command can stand between it and its desire — and a body that has learned this in the small case is being prepared for the large ones the sūrah is about to legislate, on the battlefield where appetite's cousin, fear, will make the same demands in the opposite direction.

**The Ease Already in the Prescription**

The verse prescribes absolutely, but the legislation it opens is famous for the exemptions built into its following verses — the ill, the traveler, those who bear the fast only with difficulty, all provided for with alternatives of makeup days and feeding the poor. This verse states the obligation without stating the concessions, and the sequence is deliberate: the command comes first in its full weight, so the community receives the fast as written law rather than as negotiable advice, and the ease arrives afterward as the Lawgiver's own qualification — God intends ease for you, the coming verse will say, not hardship. The pedagogical order matters. A law announced with its exemptions already attached is received as a suggestion; a law announced in full, then softened by the One who announced it, is received as authority tempered by mercy — and the tempering teaches more about the Legislator than an originally soft law ever could. The believers of Medina heard the prescription whole, and heard the easements as gifts; the structure produced the Ramadan the tradition describes, a month whose severity is undertaken with joy, precisely because the joy is the mercy inside the severity, legislated in that order.

**A Community Between Two Laws**

The verse's placement completes a legislative triptych that defines the new community from three sides. Blood was regulated — the feud replaced by equivalence, pardon, and mercy (2:178–179). Property was regulated — the deathbed governed by fairness, custody, and correction (2:180–182). Now the body is regulated — appetite governed by the month, the day, and the unseen standard of taqwā. The three laws answer the three domains in which human societies destroy themselves: violence, wealth, and desire; and the same purpose-clause runs through all three, so that you may be mindful. The fast is the triptych's central panel in a further sense — it is the one discipline every believer performs personally and daily, the retaliation being the rare case and the bequest the final one, and it therefore carries the community's moral formation through the ordinary month rather than the exceptional event. Between the sūrah's food permissions and its coming war-legislation, this verse installs the training ground: a people learning, one hunger at a time, that what God has written upon them is written for what it makes of them — and what it is making here, the verse says in its last word, is the reverent.
