## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:15

> **Still the pagans have made some of His creation out to be a part of Him. Indeed, humankind is clearly ungrateful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Part Cut from the Whole**

The verse turns from what God gave to what the idolaters gave back. The Arabic says they made for Him, min ʿibādihi juzʾan, from His servants a part or portion. Juzʾ is a piece of a whole, and the verb jaʿala, to make or assign, is the same verb used in verses 10 and 12 for God making the earth a cradle and making ships for people. The echo is bitter. God made the world for them; they made a part of His creation into a part of Him. The word ʿibād, servants, is chosen carefully. The beings the idolaters elevated, whether angels or anything else, are God's servants by nature, owned and dependent. To assign a servant the status of a portion of the Master is to confuse the two categories that the whole passage has been separating: the Creator who arranges everything and the creatures who are arranged. The verse's "still" or "yet", wa, marks the contradiction. After all that has been listed, after their own admission that He made the heavens and the earth, they still did this.

**Three Readings of the Portion**

The exegetes differ over what the portion is, and the difference is instructive. The majority, including al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī, read the portion as offspring. The Quraysh claimed that the angels were God's daughters, and a child is in Arabic idiom a piece of its parent, a juzʾ, so that to attribute a child to God is to claim that a part of Him was cut off and became a creature. The verses that follow, which speak of daughters and sons, support this reading strongly. Ibn Kathīr records a second view, drawn from 6:136: the portion is the share of crops and cattle that the Makkans set aside for their idols, saying this is for God and this is for our partners. On that reading the verse describes ritual practice rather than doctrine. Al-Rāzī adds a third: the idolaters assigned only a portion of God's servants to Him and gave the remainder to the partners they invented, so that God received a share of His own creatures rather than the whole. The first reading fits the immediate context best, since the next verse asks about daughters, but the three readings share one structure. In each, something that belongs wholly to God is divided, and part of it is handed to what is not God.

**Why Offspring Divides God**

The theological weight of the verse lies in the word juzʾ, and al-Rāzī draws it out. To have a child is to be divisible, since a child is a portion of the parent that separates and becomes another being of the same kind. Whatever is divisible is composed of parts, and whatever is composed of parts depends on those parts and could in principle be taken apart. None of this can be said of the Creator of all pairs, as verse 12 described Him. The attribution of offspring to God is therefore not merely disrespectful; it is incoherent, because it would make Him a member of the system of pairing and division that He made. The Qurʾān states the same objection in 112:3, He begets not nor is begotten, and in 19:88–93, where the claim that the Compassionate has taken a child nearly causes the heavens to split, because it attributes to Him what belongs to creatures. The verse does not argue this at length. It says "a part", lets the word do its work, and moves to the verdict.

**Kafūr Mubīn: An Ingrate Beyond Concealment**

The second sentence widens from the idolaters to al-insān, the human being, and delivers a verdict: he is kafūr mubīn, clearly and openly ungrateful. The word kafūr is an intensive form; it does not mean one who is sometimes ungrateful but one whose ingratitude is habitual and deep. Kufr in its root sense is covering, and the ingrate covers over a favour as if it had never been given. Mubīn, clear, is the same word applied to the Book in verse 2, and the contrast is pointed: the Book is clear in its guidance, and the human being is clear in his refusal of it. The shift to the general noun al-insān is significant. The verse does not say the Quraysh are ungrateful; it says humankind is. The exegetes read this as a statement about a tendency in human nature that the Quraysh exemplify rather than invent. After a list of blessings, earth, rain, animals, ships, the natural human response is not thanks but the invention of rivals to the giver. The Qurʾān says the same of man elsewhere: he is an ingrate to his Lord (100:6), and he is unjust and ungrateful (14:34).

**The Passage's Hinge**

This verse is the hinge between two halves of the argument. Verses 9 through 14 built a case from admission and blessing: you say God made everything, and here is what He made for you, down to the animal beneath you and the prayer you should say on it. This verse states the response the case received. Instead of gratitude, a portion of His servants was made into a portion of Him. The word "still" carries the whole weight of the disappointment. What follows in the sūrah takes up the specific form the ingratitude took, the claim that the angels are God's daughters, and examines it for what it is. The verse itself does not yet name daughters. It names the act of division and the character that produces it, and it lets the reader hold the two together: a Lord who gave everything, and a creature who answered by cutting a piece from Him and calling it a god. The next verse asks the question that exposes the absurdity.
