## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:16

> And so We revealed this ˹Quran˺ as clear verses. And Allah certainly guides whoever He wills.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Thus" — a Demonstrative With a Long Memory**

The verse opens with *wa-ka-dhālika* — and thus, and in that way. The demonstrative reaches back over everything preceding it: the disputants, the brink-dwellers, the useless patrons, the furious man reaching for a rope.

That is the sentence's first claim, and it is a strange one at first reading. In that way — in the way just described, amid that opposition — We sent it down. The revelation is not described as having arrived into a neutral or receptive setting. It arrived into exactly the resistance the preceding verses have catalogued, and the manner of its arrival is presented as the fitting response to it. Clear signs are what you send to people who are arguing in the dark.

The demonstrative also reaches forward. The word *ka-dhālika* is used in the Qur'an to mark a pattern rather than a one-off: thus does God do, thus have We sent, thus will it be. The sending down of clear signs is being described as the standing method, not a single event.

**What "Clear Signs" Means, and What It Does Not**

*Āyātin bayyinātin* — signs that are clear, evident, self-explaining. The Qur'an uses the phrase for previous revelation as well as for its own: *“Indeed, We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ clear revelations. ˹But˺ none will deny them except the rebellious”* (2:99), and in the description of what scripture does: *“He is the One Who sends down clear revelations to His servant to bring you out of darkness and into light”* (57:9).

The adjective describes the signs, not the reception. This distinction is the one most often collapsed, and collapsing it produces the objection that a clear revelation ought to produce universal agreement. Clarity in the Qur'an's usage is a property of the message: it is unambiguous about what it is asking and what it is warning of. It is not a guarantee about the audience.

That is why the same word can be used for the revelation and, in verse 8 of this sūrah, for the scripture the disputant does not have. The illumination is real and the failure to see it is also real. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān makes the same point in its own vocabulary: *“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood”* (2:256). The standing-out belongs to the truth; whether a person looks is another matter.

**"God Guides Whom He Wills" — the Standard Misreading**

The closing clause is the one readers stumble over. Taken in isolation it sounds like a decree issued at random, and the Qur'an's own usage rules that reading out.

The clause sits at the end of a passage about people who argue without knowledge, worship on a brink, and reach for ropes. Guidance in this context is not being distributed arbitrarily; it is being withheld from a described type. The will of God in the Qur'an is never presented as a substitute for reasons — it is presented as the ground of a decision whose reasons are also given, in this case at length.

Sūrah al-Kahf states the same pairing without any suggestion of arbitrariness: *“Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. But whoever He leaves to stray, you will never find for them a guiding mentor”* (18:17). The emphasis there falls on the consequence for the person left, not on an unexplained selection.

**Clarity and Guidance Are Two Different Gifts**

The most useful thing this verse does is hold two facts together that readers tend to separate.

The first is that the signs are clear, which means no one is condemned for failing to understand something that was never explained. Accountability in the Qur'an always follows a message that actually arrived, and this verse guarantees that it did.

The second is that guidance is God's to give, which means clarity does not compel belief. A person can see a clear sign and refuse it, and verses 8 to 13 of this very sūrah are a catalogue of people doing exactly that. If clarity automatically produced faith, the disputant of verse 8 could not exist — and he plainly does, and the sūrah spends six verses describing him.

So the verse is not a puzzle to be solved but a pair of truths to be held: the message is sufficient to leave no one with an excuse, and the response is a gift rather than a mechanical effect. The Prophet's own limits are described elsewhere in these terms; he cannot guide whom he loves, but the revelation he brought is clear. Between those two facts runs the whole of the Qur'an's account of why people believe and why they do not.

**The Sending Down Is Continuous, Not Only Historical**

The verb *anzalnāhu* is in the first-person plural past — We sent it down. In the Qur'an's usage the sending is not confined to a single descent; the same verb describes the rain that revives the dead earth in verse 5 of this sūrah.

The parallel is not accidental. Both are described as something sent down from above onto ground that cannot produce the result by itself, and in both cases the effect depends on what the ground does with what it receives. A field that has been rained on and stayed brown is the image the sūrah used for the dead earth; the disputant of verse 8, who has the clearest possible signs in front of him and argues anyway, is the same field in human form.

This is why the verse pairs the clarity of the signs with the freedom of the guidance. The rain is sufficient, and it does not coerce the harvest.

**Where the Passage Lands**

Verses 8 to 16 form a single movement: the disputant, his sentence, the man on the brink, the false patrons, the rage, and finally the nature of the revelation itself. Verse 17 will widen the frame to all the religious communities, and the sūrah will not return to this intimate scale for some time.

The placement of this verse is therefore a hinge. Before it, the sūrah has been describing individuals in their failure. After it, the failures are set against a revelation whose clarity is not in question, and against a guidance that is not in human hands. That is the last word on the matter, and the sūrah delivers it in eleven words before moving on.
