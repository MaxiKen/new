## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:122

> O Children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse Repeated From Earlier in the Sūrah**

The sentence repeats almost word for word what the sūrah said near its beginning, where the children of Israel were told to remember the favours granted to them and the preference given them over others. The Book is not repeating itself by accident; the return of a formula marks a new phase in the argument.

The earlier passage ended with a warning about the Day on which no soul helps another and no intercession is accepted. The repetition here returns the reader to the same starting point, and what follows is a different route through the same material, this time leading to Abraham and the House. The repetition also frames the material between the two occurrences, since the first reminder was followed by charges against the community and the second will be followed by the narrative of Abraham. The reader meets the same exhortation twice and attends to it differently each time, which is how the sūrah uses repeated formulae throughout.

**The Favours Recalled**

The Book's account of the favours granted to this community is spread across the sūrah: deliverance from the forces of Pharaoh, the parting of the sea, the provision of water and food in the desert, the giving of the Book and the criterion, and the raising up of prophets among them. The command to remember covers all of it.

The Book repeatedly tells its audiences to remember, and the instruction is always directed at a specific failure. What is being asked for is not information, since the community knew its history, but attention to what that history requires. The reminder is a call to draw the conclusion rather than to recite the record. The list also serves the sūrah's treatment of the community's claims, since the favours were the basis on which its standing rested and the occasion of its responsibility. The Book presents them as gifts to be used rather than distinctions to be displayed.

**"How I Honoured You Above the Others"**

The verse states that the community was favoured above other peoples, and the Book elsewhere explains what such favour means. The honour granted to them consisted in prophecy, scripture, and a role among the nations, and the Book adds that the honour carried obligations rather than exemption.

The sūrah has already made this point in its account of the covenant, where the community's acceptance of the terms was followed by the record of its conduct. The preference conferred on them was a trust, and the Book's treatment of the theme is consistent: the greater the provision, the heavier the accounting for what was done with it. The phrase also anticipates the argument about the covenant that occupies the following verses, where the honour given to the community is measured against the fidelity with which it was repaid. The verse states the gift, and the narrative that follows examines what was done with it.

**The Function of the Repetition**

The return of the formula signals a shift in the sūrah's method. The first block of material about this community consisted largely of charges, and the second will consist largely of narrative. What is added in the second pass is the story of the person the community most honoured: Abraham, whose faith the Book holds up against the claims of those who treated his legacy as their property.

That structure gives the passage its shape. The sūrah reminds the community of what it was given, and then it examines the ancestor to whom it traced its identity, with the result that the account of Abraham serves as the criterion for the claims made in his name.

**What the Community Was to Do With the Reminder**

The Book's commands to remember are followed in other passages by statements about what the memory obliges. In this sūrah, the command precedes a warning about the Day when no ransom or intercession will avail, and the pairing indicates that gratitude is expressed in conduct rather than in acknowledgment.

The phrase about honouring the community above others also prepares the reader for the transition to Abraham, since the Book will show that the honour granted to a people was never a substitute for the obedience of individuals. The sūrah's argument is that lineage and provision create responsibility, and the verses that follow develop that theme through the story of the man the community claimed as its father.

**A Reminder to All Who Hold a Scripture**

The sentence is addressed to the children of Israel, and the Book's addresses of this kind are recorded for later readers. The sūrah's own community would receive the same kind of reminder in later passages, where it is told what it has been given and warned against the conduct of communities that let their provision go to waste.

The verse therefore stands as a statement of a principle: what a community receives from God is a trust, the record of how it was used is kept, and the difference between honour and dishonour lies in what was done with what was given. The following verses make that principle specific by turning to Abraham's obedience and to the covenant established through him.

**Why the Sūrah Repeats Itself**

The repetition of this address is one of the clearest examples of the sūrah's method. The Book returns to the community's origins before it opens the account of Abraham, so that the reader approaches the patriarch's story with the favours granted to his descendants fresh in mind. What follows the reminder is not a further list of charges but a narrative, and the narrative carries the argument more effectively because the reader has been reminded of the generosity that preceded the breaches. The sūrah uses this technique in other places as well, returning to a formula after a long passage in order to mark a change of approach. What the reader receives is a structure in which the same truth is presented twice, once as accusation and once as instruction, and the second presentation is understood in light of the first. The Book's repetitions are therefore not redundancy but a way of organizing a long chapter for its hearers.
