## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:72

> ˹This is˺ when a man was killed and you disputed who the killer was, but Allah revealed what you concealed.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Case Behind the Command**

The verse discloses the matter the cow was ordered for, and the disclosure arrives after the order — a narrative sequence the Qur'an chose deliberately, examined below. The case is stated in three clauses: a man was killed; you disputed over him; Allah brought out what you were hiding. The victim is nameless — *nafsan*, a soul, a person — and the anonymity is kept by the text through every filing of the story: the Qur'an gives no name, no lineage, no town. What is given is the barest possible homicide report, and its bareness concentrates attention where the verse wants it: not on the victim's identity but on the community's state. A killing has occurred inside the covenant camp — the people of the sea, the mountain, and the manna — and the killing has produced not justice but dispute. The verse's grammar files the killing and the disputing as one continuous event: *you killed a soul and fell into mutual accusation about it*, the two verbs joined until the second reads as the first's natural consequence. An unsolved murder, in the Qur'an's presentation, does not merely leave a case open; it dissolves the community into suspicion, which is the damage the coming disclosure exists to stop.

**Faddaraʾtum: The Accusation Passing Hand to Hand**

The verb of the dispute is *fa-ddaraʾtum* — from *tadāraʾa*, to push off against one another, to repel in mutual exchange. The form is one of the Qur'an's most physical words for argument: not merely differing but shoving — each party thrusting the charge away from itself and onto another, the accusation circulating through the camp like a hot object no hand will keep. The root's meaning fixes the social scene: an unsolved homicide among people who cannot adjudicate it, every suspect with an alibi, every family with a theory, the dead man's blood-claim suspended over a community that can neither settle nor drop it. The reports the exegetes preserve supply the case's shape: a wealthy man murdered, the tradition relates through al-Ṭabarī and the commentators after him, by an heir impatient for the inheritance — the killing committed for the estate and the estate's discovery of the killing impossible by ordinary means, since the one person with the answer had the strongest motive to keep it. The reports are the tradition's reconstruction rather than the verse's text, and the verse's own datum is broader and worse: the pushing was communal. A murder that one man committed became a dispute that everyone joined, which is the Qur'an's account of how private sin becomes public disorder — the concealed act generating suspicion in every direction, and suspicion doing to the camp what the act itself did to one body.

**“Allah Revealed What You Concealed”**

The verse's closing clause is its theology in six words: *and Allah is the one who brings out what you were hiding*. The concealment is double — the killer hiding his act, the community hiding its knowledge, its suspicions, its complicities of silence — and the clause names Allah as the discloser of both. Nothing in the verse suggests the disclosure was invited: the camp disputed; it did not pray for resolution. The disclosure is divine initiative against a stalled human case, and the initiative states the principle the whole recital runs on: concealment is not a stable state before Allah. The sūrah has been filing the principle since its head — the One who knows what the breasts conceal, before whom the hidden and the manifest stand equal — and verse 72 is the principle enacted as narrative: a case with no witnesses, no confession, and no human route to truth receives truth from the only party who possesses it. The clause's tense is also precise: *mā kuntum taktumūn* — what you *were* hiding, the imperfect of a settled practice. The concealment had become the community's operating condition; the disclosure breaks a habit, not a moment. And the verse announces the disclosure while withholding its manner — the how of the case's resolution belongs to the recital's next movement, and the withholding keeps this verse's weight where it belongs: on the fact that Allah discloses, which is the fact every concealer in every generation is being told.

**Why the Murder Comes After the Cow**

The chronological order of events and the narrative order of verses diverge here, and the divergence is the Qur'an's pedagogy. In time, the murder preceded the command: the killing happened, the dispute arose, and the sacrifice was ordered as the instrument of resolution. In narration, the command comes first (verse 67), the negotiation fills four verses (68–71), and the case is disclosed last (72). The effect on the reader is exact: for four verses the reader occupies the questioners' position — holding a command whose rationale is undisclosed, watching a sacrifice specified without apparent purpose, sharing the camp's ignorance of why a cow. The disclosure at verse 72 then arrives for the reader as it arrived for the community: after the obedience, as its vindication. The narrative order thereby argues against the order modern readers instinctively impose on commands — rationale first, then compliance — and files the divine order: compliance first, rationale after, sometimes long after, sometimes only at the end. The questioners' sin, read inside this structure, sharpens: they interrogated a command whose wisdom was not merely present but concrete, pending, and about to save their community from a feud. The verse's placement teaches every holder of an unexplained command where the explanation is: on the far side of the performance, in Allah's keeping, arriving when its arrival does the most good.

**The Heir's Crime and the Law It Founded**

The reports' identification of the killer — the heir, murdering for the inheritance — is more than narrative color; it is the case from which Islamic jurisprudence drew one of its firmest rules. The jurists reason from the episode's logic: a crime must not profit its author, and the person who kills an heir-of-record forfeits the inheritance his crime was meant to accelerate. The ruling — the killer does not inherit from his victim — stands in the legal schools as settled doctrine, grounded in the prophetic tradition that states it and in the case this verse discloses, where the inheritance motive is the crime's engine. The rule's wisdom is the verse's own arithmetic: the murderer's plan works only if the estate flows to him, and a law that damns the flow dismantles the motive at its source. More broadly, the episode installs the principle that hidden crimes are legislated against, not merely condemned — the law addresses what people conceal by removing concealment's payoff. The verse's disclosure clause does the same work eschatologically: whatever the law cannot reach, the Discloser of hidden things reaches, and the recital files both jurisdictions in one case. A murder for an estate, solved by divine disclosure, producing a legal rule that makes such murders self-defeating — the case law of verse 72 runs from the wilderness camp into every inheritance court the ummah has ever convened.

**A Community on Trial for One Body**

The verse's ecclesiology is its last claim and its widest. One body, one killing — and the entire community is named in the dispute: *you disputed*, second-person plural, the whole camp pushing the accusation around. An unsolved murder, in the Qur'an's presentation, is never a private event; it is a failure of the body politic, which exists precisely so that blood does not go unavenged and the innocent do not stand suspected. The dispute's communal character explains the disclosure's communal form: the resolution will be given to the whole camp, publicly, as a sign — not as a private consolation to the victim's kin. And the second-person address does its by-now-standard work of inheritance: the Madinan descendants are filed as parties to the dispute, the community's case file running forward through the generations, every bearer of the covenant addressed by every entry in it. For the sūrah's permanent readership the verse keeps its double edge. The disclosure clause stands over every concealment — the crime hidden from a court, the sin hidden from a community, the motive hidden from a spouse — because the Discloser is not a wilderness provision but an attribute: Allah brings out what hearts hide, in His timing, by His chosen means. And the dispute clause stands over every community where a wrong has occurred and no one will hold it: suspicion circulating, accusation shoved hand to hand, the body unburied in the middle of the camp. Verse 72 files the only exit — truth from the One who owns it — and the recital's next verse records what the exit cost: one slaughtered cow, and a command obeyed too reluctantly to have guessed its purpose.
