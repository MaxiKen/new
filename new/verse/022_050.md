## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:50

> So those who believe and do good will have forgiveness and an honourable provision.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Forgiveness Comes First**

*Fa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti lahum maghfiratun* — so those who believe and do sound deeds, for them is forgiveness.

The order is fixed and it is the whole structure of the promise. Forgiveness is mentioned before provision, and the Arabic gives it the front position in the sentence.

The reason is not rhetorical balance. Forgiveness deals with what a person has already done; provision deals with what he will be given. The first is a settlement of the past and the second is an arrangement for the future, and a religion that offered the second without the first would be offering payment without pardon. Every person who comes to this verse arrives with a record.

The sūrah said as much in verse 10: what is at the judgment is what your hands sent ahead, and the record is complete. Forgiveness is the removal of that, and it is placed first because it is the thing that has to happen before anything else could matter.

The noun is *maghfirah*, from the root meaning to cover — to put something out of sight so that it no longer stands against a person. That is the sense the sūrah needs. The record described in verse 10 is not erased; it is covered, which is why the verse can promise it to people who arrive with one. The condition attached to the promise is faith and sound deeds, and the gift is forgiveness — the two halves are not the same kind of thing, and the verse keeps them apart.

**"A Noble Provision"**

*Wa-rizqun karīm* — and a noble provision. The adjective is *karīm*, a word with a wide field: generous, noble, honourable, plentiful, fine.

The range matters, because the promise is not that the provision will be large. *Karīm* describes the manner of the giving at least as much as the quantity given. A provision that is *karīm* is given generously, by a giver who is not counting, in a way that honours the recipient rather than merely sustaining him.

The same pairing appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in the same formula, which confirms that this is a standing description of the reward rather than a phrase coined for this verse. What is promised to the believers is not a wage computed against their works; it is provision given in the manner of a generous host.

The formula is not the sūrah's own. It appears in almost these words in Sūrah al-Anfāl, attached to a description of the believers' character: *“It is they who are the true believers. They will have elevated ranks, forgiveness, and an honourable provision from their Lord”* (8:4). The recurrence is what settles the meaning: this is the standing vocabulary of the reward, and *karīm* is doing the same work in both places — describing the standing of the recipient as much as the size of the gift.

**The Third Statement of This Promise**

This is the sūrah's third formulation of the believers' reward, and the repetition is worth tracking rather than skipping. Verse 14 gave the Gardens with rivers beneath them. Verse 23 added the bracelets of gold and the silk. Verse 50 gives forgiveness and a noble provision.

The three are not competing accounts. They describe the same thing from three directions: location (the Garden), honour (the adornment), and relationship (forgiveness, then provision). Together they cover what a person could want from a reward — somewhere to be, dignity, and a settled account.

The sequence is also a progression, and it runs inward. Verse 14 names a location, verse 23 names an adornment, and verse 50 names a relationship — an account settled and a table set. A reward described only as a place could in principle be given to a person who was still at odds with the giver. The third formulation rules that out.

**The Two Halves Match the Sūrah's Two Failures**

Read against the sūrah's earlier figures, the pairing is exact. The man on the brink of verse 11 wanted the provision without the forgiveness: he was content with what he was given and wanted no part of the accounting. The disputants of verses 3 and 8 wanted to argue about the Recipient and had no interest in either half.

Forgiveness and provision, in that order, are the answer to both. The man who wants only the second is the man who lost both worlds; the man who accepts the first receives the second as something other than a wage.

**Provision, Not Wages**

The word is *rizq*, provision — the same word used for the sacrificial animals in verses 28 and 34, and for what the believers spend from in verse 35. It is not a word for payment.

That distinction carries weight in a sūrah that has been insistent about grace. Verse 14 said God *admits* the believers to the Gardens; verse 23 said they are *adorned*, passively; and this verse offers provision rather than remuneration. Nothing in the sūrah's vocabulary of reward suggests a transaction that God is settling.

The practical consequence is that the reward is not proportioned to the works, and the verse does not invite anyone to compute it. Karīm provision is what a generous giver gives, and what he gives is his to determine.

The word's range elsewhere confirms the reading. Provision in the Qur'an is what God gives to creatures who have done nothing to earn it: *“There is no moving creature on earth whose provision is not guaranteed by Allah”* (11:6). To make the reward *rizq* rather than a wage is to place the believers' recompense in the same category as the sustenance of everything that lives — given, guaranteed, and not negotiated.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse also declines two things that readers regularly add to it.

It does not say the provision is in the Hereafter only. The word is unqualified, and the Qur'an uses the same phrase for what God gives in this life as well as the next; the promise is not dated.

And it does not say that the believers will be spared suffering in the interval. The sūrah has just described a community authorized to fight after being expelled from its homes, and it will describe martyrs in verse 58. What is promised is forgiveness and a noble provision, and neither is a promise that the interval will be comfortable.

That omission is itself part of the sūrah's honesty. A promise of ease would have been contradicted within a generation, and the Qur'an does not make promises that experience can falsify. What it promises is that the account is settled and the provision will come — neither of which is the same as comfort.

There is a third omission worth naming. The verse does not say that the two halves come separately, or that the provision is deferred in its entirety to the Hereafter. Read in a sūrah that has just promised to settle the believers in a land and establish their religion (22:41), the promise sits naturally across both lives, and the sūrah is content to leave the dating open.

**The Verse Answers the Demand of Verse 47**

There is a direct line from the taunt to this promise. The opponents demanded that the punishment be brought quickly, and the sūrah's answer has been about the measure of time. Verse 50 says what is actually on offer in the interval: not the punishment of the mockers but the forgiveness of those who believe.

That is the asymmetry the sūrah keeps returning to. The warning is real and the punishment is certain, and what the Prophet was sent to announce in the meantime is a promise. A community that hurries the punishment is asking for something that would close the door this verse opens — and the punishment, when it comes, arrives only after the door has been open for as long as anyone wanted it.
