## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:99

> And He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky—causing all kinds of plants to grow—producing green stalks from which We bring forth clustered grain. And from palm trees come clusters of dates hanging within reach. ˹There are˺ also gardens of grapevines, olives, and pomegranates, similar ˹in shape˺ but dissimilar ˹in taste˺. Look at their fruit as it yields and ripens! Indeed, in these are signs for people who believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rain and What It Produces**

And He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky, causing all kinds of plants to grow. The sūrah moves from the origin of human beings to the provision that sustains them, and it does so with a description that is unusually detailed for this part of the chapter. Grain in clustered heads, dates hanging within reach, vineyards, olives, and pomegranates: the verse names the crops of the region and invites the reader to look at them.

The Book returns to this list of fruits in several places. The sūrah of the Bee describes the rain producing crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit, and calls it a sign for those who reflect (16:11); the sūrah of the Believers names gardens of palm trees and grapevines with abundant fruits (23:19); and the sūrah of the Thunder describes neighbouring tracts of land irrigated by the same water, with some fruits made better in taste than others, calling this a sign for those who understand (13:4). The details in this verse belong to a pattern the Book uses whenever it wants its audience to look at what they eat.

**The Pairing of Similar and Dissimilar**

Similar in shape but dissimilar in taste. The phrase appears in this sūrah's own passage on the gardens (6:141) and again in this verse, and the observation it records is one the audience could verify in any market. The sūrah of the Thunder makes the same point about the same water feeding different soils and producing different flavours (13:4), and it draws the conclusion that this is a sign for people who understand.

The choice of that particular observation shows the method at work. The Book is not pointing to a rare wonder but to a familiar contrast that a person stops noticing after childhood. Olive and pomegranate, similar in form and unlike in taste: the sentence asks the reader to treat that familiarity as evidence, and it does so in the middle of a passage about the resurrection and the power of God.

**"Look at Their Fruit"**

Look at their fruit as it yields and ripens. The command to look is the Book's ordinary way of directing attention, and it appears throughout the created-order passages. The sūrah of ʿAbasa tells the reader to consider his food and then describes the rain, the earth, and the crops that come from it (80:24), and the sūrah of the Cow gives the same sequence in a passage about the revived earth.

The instruction to watch the fruit as it yields and ripens is precise. The verse does not ask for an opinion about the origin of the world; it asks for attention to a process the reader can follow over weeks. The Book's arguments in this sūrah are built on the things people handled every day, and this verse's command makes the reader's own observation the evidence.

**The Signs for People Who Believe**

Indeed, in these are signs for people who believe. The clause closes the series that began with the seed by naming belief as the capacity in question, and it completes the progression across three verses. The signs of the sky were clear for people who know (6:97); the sign of the single soul was clear for people who comprehend (6:98); the signs of the harvest are clear for people who believe (6:99).

The variation in the closing clauses is not a stylistic flourish. The Book's vocabulary of reception moves from knowledge to understanding to belief, and it treats the last as the fullest response. A person may know that the rain produces the harvest, and understand that the same water feeds different soils; belief is what happens when he attributes the arrangement to the One the sūrah has been naming.

**The Verse and the Audience's Religion**

The crops named in this verse were also the crops the Makkans offered to their gods. The sūrah's later passages describe the shares they assigned to God and to their idols, and the Book's account of those practices includes the division of harvests between the Creator and the beings He had nothing to do with. The passage in front of us prepares the argument for that section by describing the produce as the gift of the One who sends the rain.

The sūrah is therefore laying out the evidence for the claim it will make in a few verses' time. The fruits the audience ate and offered had come from the sky and the earth in the order described here, and the Book's argument against the division of the harvest begins with the account of who produced it.

**The Completion of the Series**

The catalogue of signs that began with the splitting seed has now covered the sky, the human person, and the field, and it ends with the verse that follows it moving to the claims the audience made about the divine nature. The sūrah has provided the evidence and will now address the beliefs that ignored it, starting with the association of the jinn with God and the attribution of children to Him.

That transition gives the passage its shape. The created order, with all its provision and variety, is set before the reader; the reader is asked to look and to believe; and the sūrah then describes what the audience believed instead. The contrast is the argument.
