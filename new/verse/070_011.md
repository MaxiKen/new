## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:11

> ˹although˺ they will be made to see each other. The wicked will wish to ransom themselves from the punishment of that Day by their children,

**Expanded Commentary**

**They Will See and Still Abandon**

Yuṣṣarūnahum — "they will be made to see them" — grants vision while withholding mercy. Friends behold friends, kin recognize kin, yet verse 10's silence holds. Exegetes debate the tableau's arrangement: most hear mutual sight, all humanity exposed to all, recognition without rescue. Some report God displaying the damned to the believers as vindication; others hear angels shown the states of souls for witness. The majority reading carries the verse's main terror — seeing and abandoning together. Blindness would have been kinder; sight sharpens solitude into spectacle. Each soul watches beloved faces pass toward ruin, hands lowered, voice stilled. The passive verb assigns heaven's agency: God shows them to each other, staging recognition as part of reckoning. What eyes meet, hearts cannot help. Vision becomes a new faculty of torment.

**Vision Becomes Accusation**

Sight on that Day accuses. The wicked see the righteous ascending and read their own failure; the righteous see the wicked sinking and read mercy's magnitude. Believers behold mockers they once feared, now begging; mockers behold warners they once scorned, now serene. Every glance testifies. This mutual exposure reverses earthly privacy, where hearts hid behind faces and destinies stayed veiled. The Day flays all covering — states visible, ranks manifest, no pretense possible. Such transparency itself punishes: shame needs spectators, and heaven supplies multitudes. The verse's first clause therefore functions as sentence, not scenery. To be seen truly, by all, forever — this is exposure no curtain can soften. Eyes become courtrooms. What the wicked hid from neighbors, neighbors now witness; what they flaunted as strength stands confessed as folly. Seeing is judging.

**The Wicked Bid Their Children**

Al-mujrim — "the wicked" or "the guilty" — names the Day's defendants by their verdict. Mujrim denotes the criminal cut off from good, the convicted felon of eternity. Such a soul, beholding ruin, opens bargaining with its dearest currency: banīhi, his sons, his children. Ransom (yaftadī) means buying oneself free by substitute payment — a prisoner offering gold for his own neck. Here the gold is offspring. That children lead the bidding measures both love and terror: love, because nothing exceeds their worth; terror, because even that worth gets tendered. The verse's "will wish" (yawaddu) stresses futility — wishing, not transacting. No counterparty accepts; heaven's justice takes no substitutes. Yet the wish itself testifies, revealing what the wicked valued and how ruin revalues all. Love's summit becomes terror's tender.

**Love Inverted by Terror**

Parental instinct counts among nature's deepest laws — mothers shield young with their bodies, fathers spend lives for children's bread. The verse inverts that law absolutely: the parent offers the child as shield for himself. Such reversal gauges horror beyond description; whatever unmakes parenthood has unmade everything. The Book elsewhere shows the same inversion — mothers forgetting their nurslings, the pregnant dropping their burdens — each image targeting affection's root. Psychology cannot follow here; it can only shudder. Terror that dissolves parenthood dissolves the dissolver too — the bidder dehumanizes himself in the bidding. Readers should feel nature itself testifying: a Day that reverses love reverses all. What remains of a soul that auctions its children? Only the wish, unanswered, echoing. The bid fails; the shame of bidding endures.

**Ransom Refused in Advance**

Scripture pre-refuses every such offer. Were the wicked to possess earth's full weight in gold and tender it entire, acceptance would still be denied (3:91); though they owned all the world and its double besides, ransom would fail (13:18); no soul's bargain avails, no intercession profits the unpermitted (10:54; 2:48). The dossier is comprehensive — wealth refused, substitutes refused, mediation restricted. Justice on that Day runs non-negotiable: each soul answers for itself, tendering only its record. This foreclosure explains "will wish" — the verb of futile desire. Wishing replaces transacting because no market operates. The verse thus tortures with impossibility: the wicked conceive the bid heaven has already voided. Hope of ransom becomes another torment, imagination drafting contracts the court shreds. Severity here is honesty — better to learn refusal now, while repentance still functions as currency, than to draft bids in fire.

**The Auction Opens With the Priceless**

Children first — then spouses, siblings, clan, and finally all earth's inhabitants. The sūrah stages an ascending auction across verses 11-14, each lot dearer and each bid vainer. Opening with the priceless sets the scale: if offspring cannot purchase escape, lesser tender hasn't a chance. Yet the wicked will keep bidding upward, desperation inflating offers toward the infinite. Rhetoric of escalation drives dread's crescendo — readers watch the lots mount and grasp, lot by lot, that no sum suffices. The auction's structure also anatomizes attachment: what humans love most gets tendered first, revealing love's hierarchy under pressure. Children crown the earthly loves; their tendering confesses that even supreme love weighs nothing against ruin. Lot one fails. Three lots remain, each huger, each hollower. Each hollow lot hammers home heaven's refusal until no hope of purchase remains.

**The Bidding Climbs Higher**

Verse 11 opens the auction; verses 12-14 will raise it. The sūrah paces desperation deliberately — one party per verse, intimacy widening toward totality. Readers should brace for escalation: household next, then clan, then the whole planet tendered for one skin. Each coming verse deepens both pathos and futility. The wicked bid upward because terror permits no other grammar; heaven refuses downward because justice admits no substitutes. Between bid and refusal stretches the Day's longest torment — hope mechanically extended and logically void. What follows will complete the lots. The next verse widens the ransom to spouse and sibling.
