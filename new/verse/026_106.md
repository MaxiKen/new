## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:106

> when their brother Noah said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah Speaks as a Brother to His People**

Noah is introduced as their brother before his question is recorded. The description establishes closeness and shared origin. He is not a stranger arriving with no knowledge of the people or their ways. He belongs to them in the ordinary human sense, has lived among them, and addresses them from within their community.

That closeness strengthens the force of the warning. They cannot dismiss him as someone who does not understand their circumstances. He knows their speech, customs, and public life. If they reject his message, they are not rejecting an unknown voice but a familiar person who has been sent to correct what they have made normal.

**Brotherhood Does Not Mean Approval**

Noah's relation to the people is warm in address but firm in truth. Calling them brothers does not mean he accepts their worship or treats their conduct as harmless. Prophetic compassion does not erase moral disagreement. He speaks as someone who wants his people rescued from a danger, not as someone seeking to flatter them into temporary agreement.

This balance is important for religious counsel. A person can address a community with belonging and still challenge its errors. Truth does not become less clear because the messenger shares the people's language and history. Nor does strong warning require contempt. Noah's brotherly address holds closeness and correction together.

The phrase will you not fear Allah invokes taqwa. Taqwa is not mere panic before punishment. It is careful awareness of God that guards a person from disobedience. Noah asks whether the people will develop this awareness before their habits and idols carry them further away from truth.

The question assumes that fear of God should lead to a change in conduct. It is not an emotion kept inside while worship remains unchanged. If the people truly recognize Allah's authority, they will stop treating created objects as rivals and will examine the customs that have led them there. Taqwa makes worship answerable to the Lord.

People can resist the word fear because they associate it with weakness or loss of freedom. Noah's question uses it differently. To fear Allah is to protect the self from the consequences of arrogance. It frees a person from fearing idols, leaders, ancestors, and the crowd as though they were ultimate powers.

A person who fears God may speak truth to a community because the community no longer controls his final hope. Taqwa gives moral courage. It also gives moral restraint, since a servant who fears God will not use truth as an excuse for cruelty. The proper fear makes the heart watchful and the tongue responsible.

Noah begins with the heart's posture rather than with a catalogue of arguments. Before a person can benefit from evidence, he must be willing to be answerable to God. A proud heart can turn even a clear proof into another object of dispute. Taqwa prepares the hearer to consider the message as a matter of life and accountability.

This does not mean that Noah avoids reasoning. The story will show his responses to the people's objections. The opening question identifies the spiritual condition needed for any argument to bear fruit. Will the people allow awareness of God to govern their response, or will they make status and custom the judges of truth?

The parallel account in 7:59 records Noah calling his people to worship Allah because they have no other god. The present question gives the moral entry point to that call. Fear of God means recognizing that worship belongs to Him alone and that no rival has a rightful claim.

Another parallel in 7:63 describes the warning as coming through one of the people so that they may beware and receive mercy. That wording confirms that Noah's nearness is part of the mercy of the message. He is one of them, yet he brings a warning they could not safely ignore.

**Taqwa Is More Than Social Respectability**

Noah's people may have considered themselves honorable because they belonged to an old community and upheld its rituals. Noah asks about a deeper measure. Do they fear God when custom, rank, and group loyalty pull them away from Him? Social respectability cannot substitute for taqwa if it protects false worship.

The question therefore reaches private motives as well as public actions. A person may appear devout while fearing the loss of status more than displeasing God. Taqwa is tested when obedience costs approval. Noah's people must decide which fear will govern them: fear of God or fear of abandoning the crowd. The question remains searching because public honor can hide private resistance to the Lord.

**The Messenger's Closeness Makes Refusal More Serious**

Rejecting an unfamiliar speaker can be blamed on distance or uncertainty. Noah's people have fewer such excuses. Their brother stands before them, known and accessible, asking a direct moral question. The familiarity of the messenger makes the responsibility heavier because the community can evaluate his character and hear his warning without translation into a foreign world.

The verse does not say that closeness guarantees acceptance. It shows why refusal cannot be explained simply as lack of access. The people have a messenger from among themselves and a question that reaches the conscience. They must answer whether they will fear Allah before the consequences make fear unavoidable. The question is a mercy because it arrives while the community can still change its direction. The known face of the messenger makes the moral invitation harder to dismiss as someone else's concern. It asks the community to respond personally, not merely to judge an outsider.
