## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:33

> The lowly will say to the arrogant, “No! It was your plotting by day and night—when you ordered us to disbelieve in Allah and to set up equals with Him.” They will ˹all˺ hide ˹their˺ remorse when they see the torment. And We will put shackles around the necks of the disbelievers. Will they be rewarded except for what they used to do?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Followers Produce the Detail Their First Answer Lacked**

Their opening plea had been vague — had it not been for you (34:31) — and the leaders had answered it easily by pointing out that nobody physically blocked the road. Now the followers come back with specifics, and the specifics change the case. *Bal makru al-layli wa-l-nahār* — rather, it was the scheming of night and day. Then the concrete charge: *idh taʾmurūnanā an nakfura bi-llāhi wa najʿala lahu andādā*, when you were ordering us to reject God and to set up rivals to Him. The verb *amara* means to command, not to suggest, and it names a relationship of authority rather than of influence. This is a materially different accusation from the first. The leaders had denied obstruction; they are now charged with instruction, which their denial did not touch.

**Scheming by Night and Day**

*Makr* is the Qur'an's word for a plan laid out of sight, the stratagem that works because its target cannot see it being made. Attaching it to *night and day* has been read two ways, and both are in the Arabic. It may describe duration — plotting that never stopped, running through every hour. Or it may describe method, with the night for laying plans and the day for executing them. The second reading gives a sharper picture of how the opposition in Mecca actually operated: agreements reached in private houses about what would be said in public, who would be boycotted, which poets would be paid. What the phrase establishes either way is continuity. A man exposed to a sustained, organised campaign is in a different position from one who heard a discouraging remark, and the followers are right to say so.

**The Detail That Convicts the Leaders**

The most damaging word in the followers' speech is *taʾmurūnanā*, you were commanding us. The leaders' defence rested on the claim that they only called and did not compel (34:32). A command issued by a man who controls a person's livelihood, protection and standing in a tribal society is not a suggestion, and the followers have found the one fact that survives the leaders' technicality. Note also what they were commanded to do: to disbelieve, and to set up *andād* — equals, rivals, peers. The word is significant because the Meccan elite did not describe their own religion in those terms; they spoke of intercessors who bring one nearer to God (39:3). The followers, at the hearing, use the accurate description rather than the polite one. Under judgment the euphemisms drop away, and the arrangement is named for what it was.

**Remorse Concealed at the Moment It Becomes Useless**

The quarrel ends without a verdict, and the narration takes over: *wa asarrū al-nadāmata lammā raʾawu al-ʿadhāb* — they concealed their regret when they saw the punishment. The verb *asarra* means to keep something inside, to hide it from view, and the subject is plural without distinction — both parties, leaders and led. The concealment is the strangest detail in the scene and the most human. Why hide regret at the point where nothing further can be lost? Because acknowledging it in front of the other party would be the final concession in an argument both have been fighting to win, and pride outlasts its own usefulness. Some have read the concealment as coming after an earlier open admission, comparing the moment when the guilty cry *"Our Lord! We have now seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good"* (32:12) and are refused. On that reading the regret is swallowed because it has already been voiced once and rejected. Yūnus describes the same silence: *"They will hide ˹their˺ remorse when they see the torment"* (10:54).

**Shackles on the Necks**

*Wa jaʿalnā al-aghlāla fī aʿnāqi alladhīna kafarū* — We will put the shackles on the necks of those who disbelieved. *Ghull* is a specific object, the iron collar fitted around a captive's neck, often joined to the hands. The image recurs across the Qur'an: al-Raʿd says the deniers will have shackles around their necks (13:5), Yā-Sīn describes collars reaching to the chin so the head is forced up (36:8), and Ghāfir shows them dragged by shackles and chains (40:71). What makes the image apt here is its relation to the argument just concluded. The two parties have spent three verses disputing who was free and who was constrained, each claiming the other had the liberty to choose differently. The collar settles the question in the only way it can now be settled: both are restrained, visibly and identically, and the difference they were arguing about has ceased to exist.

**A Closing Question That Answers the Whole Quarrel**

*Hal yujzawna illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — will they be repaid except for what they used to do? The rhetorical question is the verse's verdict on the entire exchange, and it cuts through the apportionment both sides were attempting. Neither is being punished for the other's share. Each is being repaid for his own conduct: the leaders for commanding, the followers for obeying, and the fact that these two are causally linked does not merge them into a single account. Al-Ṣāffāt states the rule in the same words as a general principle — *"and will only be rewarded for what you used to do"* (37:39) — and Ghāfir applies it to both directions of the ledger (40:40). The verb form *kānū yaʿmalūn* is continuous past: what they **used to** do, habitually, over a lifetime. Neither party is convicted on a single act. Both are convicted on a pattern, which is the one thing about a man that no leader installs and no follower can disown.
