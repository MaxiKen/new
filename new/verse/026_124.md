## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:124

> when their brother Hûd said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hūd Addresses ʿĀd as Their Brother**

Hūd is called their brother before his question is heard. The relation identifies him as one of the people of ʿĀd, not a distant critic with no knowledge of their world. He shares their human community and understands the habits, ambitions, and fears that shape them. His warning comes from within their society.

That closeness makes the question more direct. Hūd does not address an unknown population through abstract theory. He speaks to people who know his character and can recognize the language of their own community in his words. Their rejection cannot be explained by saying that the messenger did not understand them.



The word brotherly does not soften the truth into approval. Hūd asks whether they will fear Allah because he wants them rescued from a direction that will harm them. The question is severe because the danger is severe, but its source is concern rather than hatred.

A messenger's belonging also gives his warning a human voice. The people can hear both judgment of their actions and hope for their return. Hūd is not celebrating their weakness or waiting for their collapse. He is asking them to recognize God before their power becomes a cause of ruin.

**Taqwa Is the Alternative to Arrogant Strength**

The question invokes taqwa, the awareness of God that guards a person from disobedience. ʿĀd's strength tempts them to measure security by what they can build, control, and force. Hūd redirects that measure. Real security begins when power is placed under the Lord rather than treated as evidence that the people need no guidance.

Taqwa does not require ʿĀd to abandon every skill or resource. It requires them to stop using those resources as proof of self-sufficiency. A people can build and prosper while fearing God, provided it remembers that every ability and provision is received. Arrogance begins when the gift is mistaken for ownership without accountability.


Hūd does not ask whether the people feel a passing emotion. He asks whether fear of Allah will govern their worship and conduct. Taqwa changes allegiance. It turns the heart away from idols, pride, and the need to dominate others, and it turns the will toward obedience.

That change reaches public behavior. A person who fears God cannot use strength without restraint, treat weaker people as disposable, or assume that no judgment awaits. The question therefore opens a moral examination of the whole society. Will ʿĀd's power serve justice, or will it serve the pride of those who possess it?

**A Verified Parallel Gives Hūd's Exact Setting**

Quran 7:65 records the same address to ʿĀd: Hūd calls them to worship Allah alone and asks whether they will not fear Him. The parallel confirms that the present question is the core of his mission, not a passing comment within a larger political dispute.

Quran 46:21 places his warning among the sand-hills and notes that warners had come before and after him. Hūd's brotherhood therefore joins local knowledge with universal guidance. He is one of them, yet he speaks in continuity with the messengers.


People sometimes assume that a message from a stranger is easier to reject because the stranger lacks credibility. Hūd's people show the opposite difficulty. They know him, yet they still refuse the question. Familiarity can become a reason to dismiss a person as ordinary rather than to consider the truth he brings.

The messenger's closeness removes one excuse but does not remove the heart's freedom to resist. A community can know a person's honesty and still dislike the command he conveys. Hūd's task is to remain faithful even when the people use their shared history as a reason not to listen.

**Taqwa Protects Blessings From Becoming Idols**

ʿĀd's material strength is not condemned simply because it exists. It becomes dangerous when the people forget the Giver and make their possessions proof of superiority. Taqwa turns blessings into trusts. The people can enjoy what God has given while using it with gratitude, restraint, and justice.

Without taqwa, every blessing can become a rival lord. Power demands more power, wealth demands protection, and public honor demands submission from others. Hūd's question interrupts that cycle. Fear of Allah tells the strong that they remain servants and that every use of strength will be examined.


A society ruled by taqwa cannot treat weakness as permission for abuse. The strong remember that God sees the powerless person and will judge the one who harms him. Hūd's call therefore benefits more than the private conscience of an individual. It challenges the social order that allows power to become cruelty.

This is why a community may resist the question. Taqwa limits the freedom of chiefs to use others as tools. It makes every person accountable to a Lord higher than the tribe's prestige. Hūd calls ʿĀd back to God so that their strength can cease to be a threat and become a trust.

**Brotherhood Does Not Cancel Moral Distance**

Hūd calls them brothers while opposing their worship and arrogance. The example keeps compassion from becoming compromise. A person can share a people, language, or family and still need to challenge that group when it turns from God. Belonging gives the warning tenderness; truth gives it firmness.

The question remains open in the verse: will they fear Allah? Hūd's work is to make the choice clear. Their brother has warned them from within their own world, and the Lord's claim has been stated plainly. What remains is whether familiarity will lead them to listen or become another shield for pride.

The pause in the question is itself an opportunity. Fear of Allah can still redirect a powerful people before its gifts become a testimony against it.
