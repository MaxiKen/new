## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:43

> O believers! Do not approach prayer while intoxicated until you are aware of what you say, nor in a state of ˹full˺ impurity—unless you merely pass through ˹the mosque˺—until you have bathed. But if you are ill, on a journey, or have relieved yourselves, or been intimate with your wives and cannot find water, then purify yourselves with clean earth, wiping your faces and hands. And Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Prayer Guarded by Awareness**

The verse opens with a direct address — *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā taqrabū al-ṣalāta wa antum sukārā ḥattā taʿlamū mā taqūlūn* — O believers, do not approach prayer while intoxicated until you are aware of what you say. The condition *ḥattā taʿlamū mā taqūlūn* — until you know what you are saying — makes awareness the threshold for standing in prayer. Prayer in the Qur’an is described as remembrance, recitation, and supplication where the heart and tongue must align; intoxication removes that alignment. Early reports place this verse in the Medinan period before the full prohibition of wine, when drinking remained common and a person might come to prayer with speech blurred. The sūrah therefore holds the line at the meeting point of worship and heedlessness: do not bring the state that empties words to the act that depends on words. The Qur’an elsewhere says that wine has benefit and great sin and that sin outweighs benefit (2:219), and later it will say *“wine, gambling, idols, and fortune arrows are defilement from the work of Satan, so avoid them entirely”* (5:90). This verse is the middle step in that gradual legislation, keeping prayer clear while the community is being prepared for the final bar.

**Impurity and the House of Prayer**

The second bar *wa lā junuban illā ʿābirī sabīl ḥattā taghtasilū* — nor in a state of full impurity unless you merely pass through the mosque until you have bathed — guards the same awareness in the body. *Junub* — one in major impurity after intimacy or discharge — is not impure in essence; the instruction is about readiness for prayer, which the Qur’an elsewhere describes as standing before God purified. *ʿĀbirī sabīl* — passing through, crossing a way — was read as walking through the mosque to reach another place without lingering to pray, which is permitted, while stopping to pray without bathing is not. The distinction shows that the law aims at the act of approaching prayer, not at banishing the person from the community’s space entirely. The sūrah has already linked intimacy and covenant — *how could you take it after you have enjoyed each other and a firm commitment was taken* (4:21) — and here it links intimacy to purification, so that the same closeness that is honoured in marriage is followed by a washing that prepares for the next standing before God. The body’s state and the tongue’s clarity together frame prayer as an act that gathers the whole person.

**When Water Is Not Found**

The concession *wa in kuntum marḍā aw ʿalā safarin aw jāʾa aḥadun minkum min al-ghāʾiṭ aw lā mastumu al-nisāʾ fa lam tajidū māʾan fa tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa amsaḥū bi wujūhikum wa aydīkum* — but if you are ill, on a journey, or have relieved yourselves, or been intimate with wives and cannot find water, then purify with clean earth, wiping faces and hands — was read as the sūrah’s lightening because humankind was created weak (4:28) made concrete. Four cases are named: illness where water would harm, travel where water is scarce, relieving oneself, and intimacy after which bathing is normally required. If water cannot be found — *fa lam tajidū māʾan* — the alternative is *tayammum* — seeking clean earth — *ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban* — clean, wholesome ground — and wiping faces and hands. That wiping was detailed in practice as striking the earth with palms, dusting, then passing over face and hands, a gesture that replaces washing where washing would be harm or is impossible. The concession keeps prayer daily without forcing the ill to worsen or the traveller to chase water beyond reach. It shows that the bar on approaching prayer while intoxicated or impure is not a bar on prayer itself; it is a bar on approaching without the awareness and purity that prayer requires, and where those cannot be achieved through water, they are achieved through earth.

**Earth as Purifier Where Water Is Absent**

The choice of *ṣaʿīd ṭayyib* — clean earth, high ground, dust — as purifier was noted for its availability. Earth is where people stand, whether healthy or ill, at home or on a journey; to make it a means of purification is to make purification available in the very place where water fails. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“Allah does not wish to place hardship upon you, but to purify you and complete His favour upon you”* (5:6), and this verse is the classic instance. The sūrah has already said God wills to make things clear and to lighten burdens (4:26–28); here clarity and lightening meet in a single rite: clear instruction about when not to approach, and a lightened way to be ready when water is absent. The wiping of face and hands — the most visible parts — also carries meaning beyond convenience. The face is the direction of prayer and the site of prostration; the hands are the instruments of trade and giving that the sūrah has guarded from devouring by falsehood (4:29). To wipe both with clean earth is to mark the whole person — seeing and doing — as ready, even where full bathing is not possible.

**Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving Over Slip and Need**

The closing *inna Allāha kāna ʿafuwwan ghafūrā* — and Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving — matches the two bars and the one concession. *ʿAfuww* — pardoning, effacing — was read as wiping out the trace, as earth wipes the requirement of water; *Ghafūr* — forgiving, covering — as covering the shortfall where the believer tried to prepare but was hindered by illness, travel, or lack. The sūrah has closed similar lightenings with Forgiving, Merciful (4:25) and with clear, wise guidance (4:26); here the pardon covers both the intoxicated person who once approached without knowing what he said before the final prohibition, and the junub person who feared prayer would be lost where water was absent. The gradual bar on wine shows *ʿafw* in legislation itself: the community was not condemned as though it had always known the final rule, but was taught until *ḥattā taʿlamū* — until you know what you are saying — became the standard for standing. The earth that purifies where water fails is the image of that pardon: where the means are lacking, the way remains, and the One who commands awareness is also the One who pardons its absence when the excuse is real and who forgives what still falls short.

**A Verse That Holds Worship and Weakness Together**

Placed between the scene of witnesses and dust (4:41–42) and the exposure of those who trade guidance for misguidance (4:44), this verse holds worship and weakness together without loosening either. It keeps prayer guarded by knowledge of what is said and by bodily readiness, and it keeps weakness honoured by providing clean earth where water cannot be found. A community that learns not to approach prayer without awareness will learn not to approach any act requiring awareness — division of estates, giving of dowries, trade by consent — without knowledge either. A community that learns that illness and travel do not cancel prayer but change its purification will learn that limits remain but their burden is lightened. In that double training, the sūrah’s larger purpose becomes visible again: to make things clear, to guide to noble ways, to turn in mercy, and to lighten because the human was created weak (4:26–28). Prayer guarded by awareness and eased by earth is the daily rehearsal of that purpose, where the tongue knows what it says and the body is made ready, or where earth stands in for water and pardon covers what remains.
