## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:6

> O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable and severe angels, who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Widens From One House to Every House**

Five verses have dealt with the Prophet's household — an oath, a broken confidence, two hearts that tilted, a warning about replacement. Now the vocative changes: *“O believers!”* (66:6). The particular family drops away and every family in the community is addressed with an instruction built on what has just been shown. If the household of the Messenger required correction, no household is beyond needing it.

The command is also unusual in its direction. Across the Qur'an, believers are normally described asking God to shield them from the Fire — *“Our Lord! Shield us from the punishment of the Fire”* runs through the supplications recorded in the Book. Here they are told to do the shielding themselves. Protection from the Fire becomes a task assigned rather than only a favour requested, and the responsibility extends past the individual to the people in his care.

**Guarding a Family Means Teaching It**

The verse says protect, and it does not say how, which has left commentators to supply the content from the rest of revelation. The consistent answer is that one shields a family by obedience to God and by cultivating that obedience in them — instruction, correction, and the ordinary example of a household where God's commands are visibly kept. There is no other mechanism available. Nobody can stand between a grown son and the consequences of his own choices on the Day of Judgement, which is exactly the lesson the wives of Noah and Lot will illustrate at the end of this chapter.

Placing this command immediately after the household narrative gives it a sharp edge. The Prophet ﷺ had responded to friction in his home by taking an oath, and was corrected for it. What the verse requires instead is the harder and slower work: forming the people in the house rather than managing their moods. A man who indulges his family in everything they want and neglects what they need to know has not protected them; he has made himself popular. The sūrah has already shown that seeking to please one's household is not a sufficient standard even for a prophet.

**Fuel That Is People and Stones**

The description is deliberately hard: *“a Fire whose fuel is people and stones”* (66:6). The same pairing appears in the challenge issued early in the second sūrah: *“then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers”* (2:24). What makes the image disturbing is the category error at its centre. Fuel is ordinarily something gathered and consumed to produce heat for someone's benefit; here human beings occupy the position of the firewood.

The stones have been explained most persuasively as the idols. They were carved from stone, they were worshipped, and the Qur'an elsewhere describes false objects of worship accompanying their devotees into the Fire, where they are of no use whatever. If that reading is right, the image contains a complete argument: the thing a person gave his life to, and the person who gave it, burn together in the same furnace. Others read the stones as a particular kind of stone that burns with extraordinary heat. Either way, the verse describes a fire that does not need feeding from outside, because what is thrown into it is what sustains it.

**Angels Who Do Not Negotiate**

The Fire is *“overseen by formidable and severe angels”* (66:6), described with two adjectives — *ghilāẓ shidād*, harsh and strong — and then with a clause about their obedience: *“who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”* (66:6). The Qur'an specifies their number elsewhere: *“It is overseen by nineteen ˹keepers˺”* (74:30).

The detail about their obedience is the functionally important one, and it removes a hope people do not admit to holding. Every human system of punishment has slack in it, because the people administering it are people — they can be moved by a face, worn down by pleading, bribed, or simply tired. The verse closes that gap in advance. These keepers have no independent will to appeal to and no sympathy that can be worked on; they execute precisely what was commanded, and nothing else. A person hoping to find a soft place in the machinery is being told that the machinery has none. The next verse will show what happens when the attempt to make excuses is finally made.

**Why This Command Sits in a Chapter About Marriage**

The placement of a warning about Hellfire in the middle of a sūrah dealing with the Prophet's wives is not a change of subject. It supplies the reason the earlier corrections mattered. A quarrel about a rotation of days, a confidence carried to a co-wife, an oath sworn to keep the peace — these are small matters by any worldly measure, and the natural response to them is to keep them small. The verse refuses that scaling by naming what a household is ultimately for.

Read this way, the chapter has an architecture. It begins with the management of a marriage, moves to the state of hearts inside it, and then states that the family is a unit with a destination, which every member is responsible for helping the others reach. The instruction to guard oneself *and* one's family makes the household the smallest religious community, with duties running in both directions inside it. This is also why the sūrah will end with four women whose fates were decided independently of the men they lived with: the family is the arena in which salvation is worked on, and it is not the thing that delivers it.
