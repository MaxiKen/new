## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:29

> He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens. And He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Expanded Commentary**

**For You — Creation as Trust, Not Only Provision**

“He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you” (2:29) is creation language that includes purpose. Lakum — for you — does not mean ownership without accountability; it means provision with responsibility. The earth is furnished before the tenant arrives, like a house prepared for a guest. The Qur’an elsewhere says He has made subservient to you what is in the heavens and what is on earth (31:20; 45:13) and singles out particular provisions (16:5; 16:13). The furnishing precedes the vicegerency of the next verse and explains it: responsibility corresponds to endowment.

The phrase “everything in the earth” (2:29) is read by commentators as generality qualified by context: everything that benefits and is made serviceable, which in Qur’anic register can mean the whole earthly system set in service to human life and trial — soil, water, metals, plants, animals, measure and season.

**Then He Turned Towards the Heaven — What Istawā Means Here**

“Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens” (2:29). Thumma istawā ilā al-samāʾ — istawā here renders as turning, attending, establishing. The same verb is used elsewhere for mounting the Throne (7:54; 10:3; 20:5; 25:59; 32:4; 57:4), where theology treads with care, affirming what has come without likening to creation. Here the sense is intent and fashioning: He directed His creative will to the heaven and fashioned it into seven.

The source cautions against reading spatial movement into the divine; the idiom signals will and formation, not relocation. The note is consistent with the Qur’an’s own reserve about modality.

**Seven Heavens — Layered Heavens, Not Only Stars**

Seven heavens (sabʿa samāwāt) appears elsewhere as tiered construction: heavens one upon another (67:3), with levels (71:15), and with six-day creation contexts (41:12) and “the like thereof” for earths (65:12). Early authority Mujāhid, cited in the source, speaks of heavens above one another and earths above one another, a layered cosmology that resists collapsing “heavens” into “sky we see.” The Qur’an keeps precise mechanism open while insisting on structure and order: the heavens are built, proportioned, and held, not happenstance.

For the listener of 2:29, the number seven signals completeness and perfection in ancient idiom while also asserting real created multiplicity beyond the immediate visible dome. What matters is not a diagram to draw but a Creator to know: He who furnished the earth and raised the heavens is One, not several.

**“Perfect Knowledge of All Things” — Closing with Encompassing Knowledge**

“And He has perfect knowledge of all things” (2:29) — wa Huwa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿAlīm — seals the verse with the attribute that makes the preceding creation intelligible. He creates what He knows, and He knows what He creates. The “perfect” added in translation signals the breadth of the Arabic participle: not partial or successive knowing, but encompassing. This closure also prepares the next scene, where the angels’ limited knowledge will meet Adam’s taught names.

The “then” (thumma) in “Then He turned towards the heaven” (2:29) has prompted discussion about chronological sequence versus narrative presentation. The sūrah’s aim is not a laboratory timetable but a theological ordering: earth prepared for the khalīfah, then heavens perfected as canopy. The Qur’an gives creation over six days elsewhere, with differing emphases of detail; this verse supplies a summative portrait — total provision below, ordered heavens above, knowing Creator over all — rather than competing minutiae.

2:28 ended with return to Allah; 2:30 will announce a vicegerent on earth. 2:29 bridges them: the return is to the One who created everything for you and who knows all things. Trust is therefore grounded, and succession is not abandoned provision: the One to whom all return is the One who knows each thing He made, down to its measure and season. Morally, this secures both hope and accountability: nothing you do is unknown, and nothing you need has been withheld from provision.

**Stewardship Without Arrogance**

If everything on earth is “for you,” two errors threaten: waste, as if abundance licenses excess, and contempt, as if subservience justifies ruin. The Qur’an corrects both by adding knowledge: “He has perfect knowledge of all things” (2:29). A knowing Creator’s provision implies a known measure; to exceed that measure is to be known doing so. Stewardship (khilāfah) grows precisely here — freedom within knowledge, use within mending. The eco-moral note already seeded in 2:11 and 2:27 finds its positive pole here: repair is fidelity to a furnished world, not merely preference.

**Reading “For You” in a Shared World**

“For you” is plural. The provision is collective before it is individual; the heavens are canopy for communities, not private ceilings. Greed that hoards “everything” for a few contradicts the lakum that addresses all. The Qur’an’s later legislation about wealth, water, and measure can be read as jurisprudence of this verse: keep the “for you” plural in distribution as it is plural in address.

**Living the Closure of Knowledge**

The final clause steadies both gratitude and caution. Gratitude, because a perfect knowledge means provision is not random; caution, because the same knowledge means deeds are not lost. The verse thus prepares the heart for the angelic scene next: when limits of knowledge are named before the angels, they will first be grounded in this claim — Allah Himself is Knower of all things (2:29), so any creature’s knowledge is derivative, taught, and bounded.
