## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:1

> ˹This is˺ a discharge from all obligations, by Allah and His Messenger, to the polytheists you ˹believers˺ have entered into treaties with:

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Public Release From a Public Obligation**

The opening noun is barāʾah, a declaration that one party is no longer bound by a former connection. It is stronger than private disappointment and more exact than a general warning. A treaty had created obligations between the Muslim community and certain polytheist tribes; this verse announces that those obligations have been discharged. The wording also makes the authority clear: the release is from Allah and His Messenger. It is not a local commander improvising a policy, nor a crowd abandoning a promise in anger. A covenant of public consequence is ended by a public authority and in a form that can be heard by those affected.

That legal precision matters because the surrounding verses are severe. The severity is not a license to erase the difference between a faithful treaty partner and a violator. The verse identifies the people connected by treaties; the next verses set out notice, time, exceptions, and the continuing force of agreements that were kept. Read alone, the first line can sound like a blanket severance. Read as the beginning of a carefully bounded proclamation, it is the first entry in a legal record. The opening does not celebrate treachery. It names the moment at which the Muslim side is no longer required to carry an arrangement whose political basis has failed.

**Barāʾah Means Separation With Consequences**

The root b-r-ʾ conveys freedom from something that has attached itself to a person: a liability, a fault, or a relationship. In this setting, barāʾah is not a claim that every individual in the other community has the same inward state. It is a separation from a treaty framework. The distinction protects two truths at once. A government may withdraw from an agreement for a lawful reason, while individuals among the other side may still be treated justly. A political relationship and a human soul are not identical objects.

The verse also shows that covenants are not decorative language. In the Qur'an, a promise creates a real moral burden. Allah commands, Keep the covenant of Allah when you have pledged it, and do not break oaths after confirming them (16:91). That command remains relevant even when an agreement later has to be ended. Ending a pact is therefore not the same as pretending it never existed. The very need for a declaration proves that the pact had weight. A community that must announce its release acknowledges that the other side had been entitled to expect performance until a lawful change was communicated.

**The Historical Setting Is Part of the Meaning**

The verse is tied to the period after the conquest of Makkah and to the collapse of the treaty order that had followed Ḥudaybiyah. The well-known breach involved Banū Bakr attacking Banū Khuzāʿah, an allied tribe, with Quraysh support. The incident was not an isolated quarrel between private men. It threatened to turn a truce into a cover for renewed warfare. The Prophet's response made the principle visible: a party that uses a treaty while helping its allies attack the other side cannot demand the treaty's protection as though nothing happened.

Exegetes nevertheless differ over which groups verse 1 directly addresses and how its four-month notice relates to treaties of different lengths. Some connect it closely to the broken pact of Ḥudaybiyah. Others hold that the proclamation concerned several Arabian groups after Makkah, while faithful treaty holders retained their agreements. The second reading fits the explicit exception in verse 4, but the first preserves the immediate historical trigger. The stronger conclusion is not that every disputed date must be forced into one scheme. It is that the revelation addresses a changed political reality while refusing to hide the legal differences among the tribes involved.

**The Messenger as Herald of the Notice**

The proclamation was delivered during the pilgrimage season, when representatives of the Arabian tribes could hear the new policy directly. Abū Bakr led the pilgrimage in the ninth year after the Hijrah, and ʿAlī was sent to proclaim the relevant terms. This arrangement gave the announcement a public character and removed uncertainty about who spoke for the Muslim authority. Reports preserved in the major hadith collections describe the announcement as including the end of pagan pilgrimage practices and the protection of treaties that had not expired through breach.

That setting illuminates the verse's political ethics. A powerful community may be tempted to use changed circumstances as an excuse for surprise. The proclamation instead gives affected parties knowledge of their position. They are not left to infer a new policy from rumors or from an unexpected attack. Even when a covenant is being withdrawn, notice protects human choice and limits confusion. The verse begins a passage in which justice is expressed not only by the final decision but also by the manner in which the decision is communicated.

**A Boundary, Not a Blank Cheque**

Verse 1 must therefore be read as a boundary statement. It establishes that the Muslim polity is no longer bound to certain treaty relations, but it does not itself identify every person who may be fought, nor does it cancel the command to honor agreements that remain sound. The rest of the passage supplies those distinctions. Verse 4 protects the treaty keeper. Verse 6 protects the seeker of asylum and commands safe escort. Verse 13 names breach, attempted expulsion, and first aggression as the features of the hostile case.

This verse also supplies a lasting lesson about public trust. Political communities sometimes speak as if agreements are useful only while convenient. The Qur'an rejects that casualness in both directions. A treaty cannot be broken secretly, and it cannot be treated as sacred after the other party has decisively destroyed its basis. Lawful separation requires truth about the breach, a clear announcement, and a disciplined distinction between the guilty arrangement and every person who might be caught near it. Barāʾah is grave because covenants are grave. The chapter begins by making that gravity impossible to miss.
