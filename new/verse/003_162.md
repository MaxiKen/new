## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:162

> Are those who seek Allah’s pleasure like those who deserve Allah’s wrath? Hell is their home. What an evil destination!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Places Two Figures Side by Side**

The verse asks whether a person who pursues the contentment of God is like a person who earns a burden of anger from God and whose refuge is the fire. The question expects a negative answer, and the whole sentence is built to make the difference visible.

The Book uses such comparisons in several places, and the commentators gathered the passages where the righteous and the wicked are set against each other in the same form. The sentences are not arguments in the technical sense; they present two conditions and leave the reader to judge whether they could possibly be the same.

**The One Who Pursues Contentment**

The phrase about pursuing God's contentment describes a person whose aim is the pleasure of God, and the Book attaches this aim to specific forms of conduct: keeping a trust, speaking the truth, spending from what one has, and holding to an agreement.

The verb carries the sense of seeking and following after what is desired, and its use in this verse makes the pursuit continuous rather than a single decision. The person described is not one who happens to have pleased God on an occasion but one who is in the business of seeking that pleasure, and the sūrah has just described what such conduct looked like on the field where the community's trust was tested.

**The One Who Earns a Burden of Anger**

The second figure is described as one who returns with anger from God laid upon him, and the word for what he earns describes something borne, a load that a person carries away with him. The Book uses the same expression in the passage about those who earned anger among the earlier communities.

The pairing of the two descriptions is exact in structure and total in contrast. One person is going toward something he seeks, and the other is coming away with something he has brought upon himself. The verse places the two directions in one sentence without adding any argument for the difference, and the question's answer is left to the reader's own judgement.

**The Refuge That Is Named**

The verse states that the man's refuge is the fire and calls it an evil journey's end. The word for refuge describes the place a person ends up in and settles into, and the word for the journey's end describes what an undertaking finally produces.

The two terms together describe a movement that has been completed. The person who spends his life in pursuit of what is other than God arrives somewhere, and the verse names the destination in the imagery the sūrah has used through this passage. The exclamation with which the sentence closes is one of the Book's standard ways of marking a matter as beyond dispute.

**The Contrast as the Book's Method**

The Book's method of teaching by contrast appears throughout this sūrah. The verses about the two companies on the field, the two desires in the community, and the two faces on the Day of Resurrection have prepared the reader for a sentence as direct as this one, and the verse gathers their various contrasts into a single question.

The method is effective for a community that had just seen both kinds of people among its own members. The comparison in this verse is not between the community and its enemies alone; it is between two possible directions for a human life, and the sūrah has shown both of them at close range within the space of a few days.

**The Anger of God in the Book's Language**

The Book refers to anger from God in several passages, and it uses the expression for those who have incurred punishment after the truth has come to them. The phrase in an earlier sūrah describes the state of a people on whom anger was placed, and the same term appears in the sūrah's own description of the People of the Book.

The vocabulary adds weight to the verse's contrast. The person seeking God's contentment is working toward an aim that can be achieved; the person who has earned anger carries an outcome that follows from what he has done. The book presents the two not as two feelings of God but as two results that differ as much as the destinations named in the verse.

**What the Question Leaves Undecided**

The verse states the contrast and stops, and the decision it implies is left to the reader. The Book often ends its arguments in this way, presenting the two paths and letting the plain sense of the comparison do the work.

The next verse states that the two groups are ranked in degrees with God, which answers the question's premise directly: they are not equal in rank. The sūrah's treatment of its themes in consecutive sentences is one of its features, and the answer supplied in the following verse tells the community what to conclude from the question it has just been asked.

**The Aim as a Way of Life**

The verse describes a life in terms of what it is pursuing, and the description applies to the ordinary conduct of a person as much as to a decisive choice. The Book's word for contentment covers the approval that a person seeks in what he does, and the pursuit of it gives shape to the ordinary details of a person's dealings.

The community at Madinah had just been through an examination of exactly this kind. The spoils, the positions on the hill, and the question of who took what were all part of an afternoon in which the community's aims had shown themselves, and the verse that sets the two aims side by side is written for people who had seen them both at work.
