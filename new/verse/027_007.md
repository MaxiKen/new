## Sūrah an-Naml 27:7

> ˹Remember˺ when Moses said to his family, “I have spotted a fire. I will either bring you some directions from there, or a burning torch so you may warm yourselves.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prophet Discovered While Solving a Small Problem**

The first story of the chapter begins with a man who is lost and cold. Mūsā is travelling with his household, night has fallen, and he sees a light in the distance. His plan is entirely practical: walk over, either ask the way or pick up a burning brand to start a fire. Nothing in his statement anticipates revelation. He is a traveller managing an inconvenience.

This is how the Qur'an stages the largest moment in his life. Prophethood arrives while he is doing something ordinary and doing it well — he has noticed a resource, considered two possible uses, and told his family to expect him back. The narrative gains its force from the gap between what he expects and what he receives. He set out for directions and a torch; he came back with a mission to Pharaoh. Other prophetic calls in the Qur'an share this shape, but none quite so sharply. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the Qur'an presents the fire as something Mūsā perceived, using a verb of perception rather than one of certainty, which suits a man reporting an impression at distance in the dark.

**Two Requests, Both Modest, Both Answered Beyond Asking**

His sentence offers alternatives: *some directions* or *a burning torch*. The Arabic is *bi-khabarin*, literally a piece of news, and *bi-shihābin qabas*, a flame taken from a larger fire. Both are small requests, and the commentators observe that both were granted in a form vastly larger than the request.

He asked for news of the road. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī agree that the family had lost their way in the dark, and news here means the information a local would give a stranger. What arrived was news of an entirely different order — the identity of the speaker, the fate of Pharaoh, the mission of the man himself. He asked for fire to warm his family. What he found was a fire that did not warm, that did not consume, and that gave him something he could not carry back. The structure is a pattern the Qur'an uses elsewhere: a servant asks within the limits of his situation, and the answer breaks the frame of the question. Mūsā's request was reasonable and his need was real. Neither is mocked. They are simply overtaken.

**The Parallel Accounts Fill Each Other In**

The same night is reported three times in the Qur'an, and the versions are not identical. Ṭā Hā gives the setting: *“When he saw a fire, he said to his family, “Wait here, ˹for˺ I have spotted a fire. Perhaps I can bring you a torch from it, or find some guidance at the fire.””* (20:10). Al-Qaṣaṣ supplies what neither of the others does — the timing and the place: *“When Moses had completed the term and was travelling with his family, he spotted a fire on the side of Mount Ṭûr.”* (28:29).

Reading them together gives the full scene: Mūsā has finished his years of service in Madyan and is on the road with his household when he sees the fire on the flank of the mountain. The three accounts also differ in emphasis. Ṭā Hā records that he told them to wait; an-Naml omits that and keeps only his plan; al-Qaṣaṣ keeps both but adds the geography. This is how the Qur'an handles repeated narrative — not by copying a passage but by selecting from it according to what each chapter is doing. An-Naml is building toward a series of encounters with the unseen, so it keeps the perception and the plan and drops the logistics.

**The Word for Family and Who Was Actually There**

*Ahl* is translated *his family*, and the word's range in Arabic is wide. It can mean a household, a clan, the people of a town, or a wife alone. Al-Rāzī records the view that only Mūsā's wife was present, which is one legitimate sense of the term and fits a man returning from Madyan with the woman he married there.

Later reports add children and servants to the party, but the Qur'an does not, and the reticence is probably deliberate. What the narrative needs is that Mūsā was not alone and that he had dependents who were cold. The exact headcount changes nothing. There is a further point in the word's ambiguity: the plural address in his speech — *bring you*, *so you may warm yourselves* — uses a form that Arabic also employs for respectful address to one person. The grammar permits either reading, which is why the commentators could not settle it from the text alone. Where the Qur'an leaves a detail open, the responsible position is to say so rather than to pick.

**Seeing a Fire, Then Reaching It**

There is an old reading of this episode in Islamic spiritual writing that treats the three stages of Mūsā's encounter with the fire as three grades of knowing. To know of a fire by report is one thing; to see it with your own eyes is another; to be inside it is a third. The scheme maps onto a triad the Qur'an itself uses in Sūrah at-Takāthur, where knowledge of certainty and the eye of certainty are named in sequence.

Applied here, the reading is suggestive rather than exegetical, and it should be taken as such. But it does catch something real in the narrative's structure. Mūsā begins at a distance, with a perception he is not fully sure of — he says he has *spotted* a fire, hedging slightly. He then closes that distance physically, walking toward it across dark ground. What he finds when he arrives is not what he saw from far off, and the story turns on that discrepancy. The next verse reports what met him at the fire's edge.
