## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:12

> so that We may make this a reminder to you, and that attentive ears may grasp it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Salvation Seeks a Sequel**

Li-najʿalahā — "so that We may make it" — subordinates rescue to remembrance. The Ark's voyage aimed beyond survival: heaven saved a remnant in order to send a reminder. Instrumentality runs through the verse's grammar — deliverance serves doctrine, the vessel serves the lesson. Exegetes debate the pronoun's referent: "it" may mean the Ark itself, preserved as memorial, or the whole flood event with its drowned world. Both readings preach. A ship remembered warns; an event remembered warns louder. The strongest view layers them — vessel and voyage together constitute the reminder, hardware and history preaching jointly. Purpose clauses like this reveal heaven's economy: God does nothing merely, but everything meaningfully. Floods recede; their lessons must not. The verse turns survivors into students and students into transmitters. Rescue ends; remembrance must not.

**The Ship Became a Sermon**

Tadhkirah — "reminder" or "admonition" — names the Ark's second career. Timber became text; planks preached after passengers disembarked. Whether the vessel's remains survived on a mountainside or only in narrative, its memorial function outlasted its nautical one. Reminders of this kind anchor the Book's pedagogy — ruins preach (ʿĀd's valleys), relics preach (Pharaoh's preserved body), and vessels preach (Noah's Ark). Material memory aids moral memory; what eyes can visit, hearts retain. The preposition "to you" (lakum) delivers the sermon to the Prophet's contemporaries across the centuries — the reminder sails through time, docking at every generation. Each must unload its cargo of warning and gratitude. Ships rot; sermons do not, provided ears receive them. The verse's second half names the ears that qualify.

**Ears That Hold What They Hear**

Udhun wāʿiyah — "attentive ear" — pictures hearing that retains. Waʿā means to contain, gather, preserve — the ear as vessel, memory as cargo. Such ears do not merely vibrate with sound but store sense, carrying warnings from assembly into conduct. The singular "ear" with indefinite force generalizes: any ear thus heedful, every listener thus retentive. A parallel passage describes the same organ — whoever gives ear as witness, with heart present (50:37) — confirming that the Book distinguishes hearing from heeding. Anatomy serves theology here: ears become moral instruments, graded by retention rather than acuteness. The deaf of spirit hear sermons as noise; the heedful hear noise as summons. What turns on the adjective is everything — floods profit only the wāʿiyah. All others drown in forgetfulness drier but deadlier than Noah's seas.

**Hearing Is Not the Same as Heeding**

Two congregations heard every prophet, though only one is named here. The many heard sounds — eloquence admired, threats shrugged, stories enjoyed as theater. The few heard summons — warnings internalized, mercies treasured, commands enacted. Quraysh exemplified the first congregation: they heard the Prophet's recitation, praised its beauty, then persecuted its reciter. Retention, not reception, divides them. The verse's "may grasp" (taʿīhā) stresses active seizure — the heedful ear grabs truth the way hands grab lifelines. Passivity drowns; only gripping saves. This convicts comfortable audiences in every century, including those who admire scripture's cadence while ignoring its claims. Aesthetic appreciation without moral retention is precisely un-wāʿiyah hearing. The flood's lesson demands vessels, not sieves. What the ear will not hold, the heart will never heed.

**Remembrance Is the Ark's Cargo**

What must the heedful ear carry? The flood's double lesson — judgment certain, mercy available. Remembrance here means more than recall; tadhkirah implies warning kept warm, admonition kept ready for use. The Ark's true freight was not pairs of beasts but this portable moral, shipped to every generation after. Memory functions as prevention: peoples that remember floods build arks of obedience; peoples that forget build towers of defiance. Israel forgot and forged a calf within weeks of the sea; Quraysh heard and hardened within years of the warnings. The verse commissions its hearers as memory's crew — keeping the reminder aboard through storms of distraction. Forgetfulness is the real flood, drowning more souls than water ever claimed. Against it the Book launches verse after verse, each a plank of the same Ark.

**The Gallery Closes, the Trumpet Waits**

Verse 12 seals the history gallery that began at verse 4. Tribes, empires, perverts, and floods have all testified; remembrance is their collected verdict. The sūrah now pivots from past judgments to the future Judgment — from local hours to the Hour, from exhibited ruins to the trumpet's blast in verse 13. Structure serves escalation: what water did to a world, the trumpet will do to the cosmos. Readers should feel the hinge turning. History argued by precedent; eschatology will argue by spectacle. Both aim at the same ears — the wāʿiyah that retain. Those who grasped the flood's lesson can face the trumpet's; those who let history leak will find the future flooding in. The gallery exits directly into apocalypse. No intermission separates the remembered flood from the approaching blast.

**Attention Is the Price of Rescue**

A final symmetry closes the passage. Noah's company was saved by heeding a preacher; this verse's audience is saved by heeding the preacher's story. Attention purchases rescue in both cases — ears opened, arks boarded. The price has not inflated across the millennia: hear, retain, act. Those unwilling to pay it reenact the drowned, who also heard Noah and also shrugged. Every recitation re-floats the Ark and re-opens its gangway; every heedful ear boards again. The verse thus turns reading into rescue, reception into boarding. What the flood did with timber, the Book does with syllables — carrying the attentive above the waters. With history's lesson entrusted to heedful ears, the sūrah sounds the trumpet that ends the world.
