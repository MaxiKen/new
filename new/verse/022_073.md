## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:73

> O humanity! A lesson is set forth, so listen to it ˹carefully˺: those ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah can never create ˹so much as˺ a fly, even if they ˹all˺ were to come together for that. And if a fly were to snatch anything away from them, they cannot ˹even˺ retrieve it from the fly. How powerless are those who invoke and those invoked!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable Announced Before It Is Told**

*Yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ḍuriba mathalun fa-stamiʿū lahu* — O humanity, a parable is struck, so listen to it.

The verse opens by demanding attention before delivering anything, and the address is once again to all humanity rather than to the believers. The sūrah has used this address at its first verse and at verse 49, and both times what followed was something everyone is asked to weigh.

The word *mathal* is the term for a parable, a similitude, a comparison used to make a point land. The verb *ḍuriba* — struck — is the one used for striking a coin or a seal: the parable is being impressed rather than merely offered.

**The Test Is Set at the Lowest Possible Bar**

*Inna alladhīna tadʿūna min dūni Allāhi lan yakhlūqū dhubāban wa-law ijtamaʿū lahu* — those you call upon apart from God will never create a fly, even if they gathered together for it.

The challenge is not to create a universe, a mountain, or even a living thing of any size. It is to create a fly.

The choice of creature is the argument. A fly is small, ubiquitous, unregarded and, in the ancient world as now, faintly contemptible. If the test had been to create a camel or a human being, a worshipper could have replied that his god works in larger matters. There is no such reply available here. The bar has been set where no one can pretend it is too high.

The Qur'an uses the same logic elsewhere in the same vocabulary: *“Do they associate ˹with Allah˺ those ˹idols˺ which cannot create anything, but are in fact created”* (7:191), and the challenge issued directly: *“Those ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah are created beings like yourselves. So call upon them and see if they will answer you, if your claims are true!”* (7:194).

**The Second Half Is Worse Than the First**

*Wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan lā yastanqidhūhu minhu* — and if the fly snatches anything from them, they cannot recover it from it.

The escalation is deliberate and it is the verse's sharpest move. The first clause said the idols cannot *make* a fly; the second says that a fly can rob them and they cannot get it back. A thing that cannot recover what a fly has taken is not merely uncreative. It is weaker than the smallest creature in the room.

The scenario also echoes Abraham's confrontation with his people, where he broke the idols and left the largest one intact, and his people were forced to concede the point themselves: *“You already know that those ˹idols˺ cannot talk”* (21:65). In both passages the reduction is total, and in both it is achieved by the idolaters' own admission of what an idol can do.

**"Feeble Are the Seeker and the Sought"**

*Ḍaʿufa al-ṭālibu wa-l-maṭlūb* — weak is the seeker, and the sought.

The closing verdict names both parties, and the symmetry is the point. The worshipper is weak and the worshipped is weak, and the verse declines to grade them. A person who has spent his life venerating something weaker than an insect has been measured by what he venerates.

The two nouns are from the same root: *ṭālib* is the one who seeks, *maṭlūb* the one sought. Between them they describe the entire transaction of prayer, and the verdict covers both ends of it.

**The Fly in the Prophetic Teaching**

The choice of a fly has a further resonance in the tradition that is worth noting, because it runs in the same direction as the verse.

Abū Hurayrah narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: *“If a housefly falls into the drink of any of you, let him dip it all in and then throw it away, for on one of its wings is a disease and on the other is a cure”* — recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (3320). Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī's version adds that the fly protects itself with the diseased wing, and the report also appears in *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* (3844, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī) and *Sunan Ibn Mājah* (3505).

The relevance is the creature's agency. The fly in that report acts, chooses a wing, protects itself, and carries both a disease and its cure. It is, on the Qur'an's and the Prophet's account, a creature with capacities — which is precisely why it can be set against an idol that has none.

**The Parable Is Not an Insult but an Argument**

It is worth being clear about what kind of speech this is, because the tone is easily misread as mockery.

A parable in the Qur'an is a *mathal*: a comparison constructed to make a point that a direct statement would not make as well. The fly is not chosen to humiliate the idolaters' gods — the gods are not the audience and cannot be humiliated. It is chosen because the smallest available unit is the clearest test of a capacity claim.

If the claim were about dignity, the comparison would be unfair. The claim is about power, and a claim about power can be tested at any scale. That is why the parable works, and why the sūrah follows it immediately with the charge that these people have not measured God correctly: the fly was the measuring instrument.

**Why This Parable Rather Than Another**

The sūrah is near its end, and it has deployed several arguments against idolatry already. This is the last, and it is the most reductive.

The reduction is the method. Earlier arguments established that the idols cannot help (22:12), that they harm (22:13), that no warrant was sent down for them (22:71), and that God is the only truth (22:62). The parable of the fly takes all of that and makes it small enough to hold in one image: the thing you are praying to cannot swat an insect.

Nothing more needs to be said after it, and the sūrah does not say it. The next verse draws the conclusion in one line.
