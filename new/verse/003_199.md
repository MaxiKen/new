## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:199

> Indeed, there are some among the People of the Book who truly believe in Allah and what has been revealed to you ˹believers˺ and what was revealed to them. They humble themselves before Allah—never trading Allah’s revelations for a fleeting gain. Their reward is with their Lord. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception Among the People of the Book**

The verse states that within the People of the Book there are people who hold belief in God, in the revelation granted to the Prophet's community, and in the revelation granted to their own community, and who are humble before God. The sentence qualifies the criticism the sūrah has directed at that group in the preceding verses.

The Book distinguishes between the members of a group and the group as such, and it does so in several passages of this sūrah. One of them states that the group is not uniform, and describes an upright party within it whose members recite God's revelations through the night.

**The Occasion Reported**

The commentators record that the verse was connected with the passing of the Negus, the ruler of Abyssinia who had sheltered the believers who left Makkah before the migration to Madinah. The report places the verse in the middle of a controversy about how the community should regard a ruler who had protected its members without adopting its outward forms. Others connect the same event with the verse about the direction of prayer in the second sūrah.

The Prophet reported the ruler's death to his companions and prayed over him, and the reports describe the funeral prayer being offered for him in his absence, with the rows arranged behind the Prophet. The verse is read by the commentators as an answer to the objections that the prayer provoked.

**The Objection Reported**

Among the objections raised was the claim that the Negus had been a Christian, and this objection is said to have been voiced both by sincere followers and by the hypocrites, who used it to cast doubt on the Prophet's conduct. The verse answers such objections by describing the qualities of the person who is accepted rather than by debating the objections themselves.

The passage therefore addresses a question raised by the community's own conduct toward a non-Muslim ruler whom the Prophet had treated as a brother. The sentence settles the matter by describing the qualities of the person who is accepted, rather than by discussing the objections that were made.

**The Identity of the Believers Named**

The commentators offer several identifications for the people the verse describes. Some name forty men from Najrān, others thirty-two from Abyssinia, and others eight from Byzantine lands who had lived by the teaching of Jesus before they accepted the message. Mujāhid is reported to have said that it covers every believer within that group, without specifying what their belief involves.

Others applied the verse to people like ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām, whose case the sūrah's earlier passages had in view. The variety of the reports reflects the openness of the sentence, which describes a quality rather than naming a group of individuals.

**The Qualities Listed**

The verse lists belief in God, belief in what was revealed to the community of the Prophet and in what was revealed to the earlier communities, humility before God, and the refusal to sell the signs of God for a small price. The last item recalls the earlier verse in which the People of the Book were described as casting the covenant behind them and trading it for a paltry amount.

The qualities are arranged as a single description, and each element answers something the sūrah has criticized in the group. What replaces the concealment of the earlier verse is a refusal to trade the signs, and what replaces the arrogance of the group's learned men is humility.


The verse states that the people described will have their reward with their Lord, and it closes with the declaration that God is swift in reckoning. The promise is placed with God rather than in the community's hands, and the closing declaration gives the sentence its urgency.

The same declaration appears at the end of the sūrah's earlier discussion of the reckoning, and it belongs to the sūrah's vocabulary for the settling of accounts. The reward of the people described is secure because the account will be settled by the one who is swift at it.

**The Question the Verse Leaves Open**

The sentence treats that group as a category separate from the community addressed by the Prophet, and it states that some of its members accept what was revealed to him. The commentators were generally silent about how that belief relates to their position as members of their own community.

The questions that follow from the verse have occupied the community's scholars in later periods, and the sūrah's other passages about the People of the Book are read alongside it. The commentators' silence on the classification of such a person is itself instructive, since the verse states the qualities that matter and leaves the outcome to God. The verse's own contribution is the recognition it extends and the qualities it names.

**What the Verse Affirms**

The verse affirms that belonging to a community that has received a scripture does not by itself exclude a person from the reward. What counts is the belief, the humility, and the refusal to trade the signs, and the sentence describes those qualities without reference to genealogy or group membership.

The community that recites the verse is taught to judge by the description the verse gives, and the sūrah ends shortly after with an address to the believers. The last verse turns from the discussion of the other groups to the community's own obligations, and it closes the sūrah with an instruction that gathers its teaching.

**What the Community Did With the Verse in Practice**

The community's own record shows how the sentence was applied in the weeks after it was heard. When the ruler of Abyssinia died, the Prophet told his companions the news and arranged them in rows, and the funeral prayer was offered for a man who had died in another land. The report of that prayer is the occasion the commentators connect with this verse, and the practice it established became a subject of the community's law: the jurists who permitted prayer for a person who dies away from his own town cited the case of the Negus, and those who restricted the practice to that single case argued that the Prophet's action was particular to him.

Al-Nawawī recorded the position of the school of al-Shāfiʿī in favour of the practice, observing that the report about the Negus is sound and that its opponents have no answer to it. The sentence in this verse thus has a place in the community's jurisprudence as well as in its history, and the two are connected: the verse describes the qualities of a believer among the People of the Book, and the Prophet's conduct toward a ruler who possessed them supplied the precedent.
