## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:5

> Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wealth Made to Support, Not to Scatter**

The verse opens with a prohibition that sounds like withholding, but its aim is preservation. *Wa lā tuʾtū al-sufahāʾ amwālakum allatī jaʿala Allāhu lakum qiyāman* — do not entrust the incapable among your dependants with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you. The noun *qiyām* — support, standing, establishment — describes wealth by its purpose. It is what keeps a household upright, what keeps obligations met and needs covered across time. The Qur’an elsewhere calls wealth *“a means of support”* precisely to teach that spending is not only about desire but about keeping a life standing. Al-Rāzī notes that the phrase *allatī jaʿala Allāhu lakum qiyāman* reminds the owner that wealth is not purely his creation; it is entrusted by God to keep people standing, and stewardship includes not placing it where it will be quickly wasted. The verse therefore does not deny the eventual rights of dependants; it orders a better way of delivering those rights, as the second half immediately shows with feeding, clothing, and kind speech.

**Who Are the Incapable**

The term *al-sufahāʾ* — the incapable, often translated the feeble-minded — was explained by the early authorities as those unable to manage property soundly, whether due to youth, inexperience, weak judgment, or a condition that impairs prudence. The word is masculine plural, and commentators who considered that grammar concluded it does not single out women as such. That matters, because verses 2 and 4 have just secured women’s ownership of property, and verse 6 will return that property to orphans when they show sound judgment, regardless of gender. To read *sufahāʾ* as women in general would contradict that immediate context, and the sūrah’s later inheritance laws that name women as obligatory heirs (4:7, 4:11–12). The sounder reading, held by the majority including al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī, is that the term covers any dependant whose judgment is not yet reliable for guarding wealth — minors, those with cognitive impairment, or those whose habits show they would scatter funds quickly. Some added that the term also warns against handing financial control to those outside the faith who would not observe the limits the sūrah is laying down, such as the prohibition of consuming wealth illegally (4:29), though the primary reference remains within the household.

**Your Wealth and Their Wealth**

The phrasing *amwālakum* — your wealth — has been discussed carefully. Does it mean the guardian’s own wealth, or the wealth of the incapable that is legally theirs but presently in the guardian’s hands? Both readings have support, and both lead to the same care. If it is your wealth, the verse warns a provider not to hand the household’s capital to a member who will dissipate it, because the whole household will then need help. If it is their wealth held in trust, the verse warns not to hand it over prematurely, which would simply undo the protection the sūrah has been building since verse 2. The sūrah will clarify that orphans’ wealth must be returned only after testing and when sound judgment appears (4:6), with witnesses and with *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6) as the closing audit. This verse sits between those two points and supplies the principle that timing matters as much as ownership. To recognize that someone owns a thing is not the same as deciding he is ready to manage it today. The law can affirm the first while delaying the second for his own protection.

**Feed, Clothe, and Sustain From It**

The alternative the verse commands is not to withhold care but to provide it directly: *wa arzuqūhum fīhā wa aksūhum* — feed and clothe them from it. The preposition *fīhā* — from it, literally in it — was noted by grammarians to suggest spending from the wealth itself while keeping its capital intact as a standing support, investing and drawing yield rather than liquidating all at once. The sūrah elsewhere describes wealth as a trust to be grown and guarded, not merely stored or spent. The practical image is a guardian who, instead of handing a lump sum to a young heir who would spend it in weeks, maintains the property, uses its produce to feed and clothe the heir, and hands over the principal when maturity and judgment align. That pattern also appears in the rule for witnesses at handover (4:6) and the warning against hasty consumption before the orphan grows up (4:6). The food and clothing here are therefore not a reduction of the dependant’s right; they are its faithful delivery in installments, matched to capacity, until full delivery becomes safe.

**Speak to Them Kindly**

The third element, *wa qūlū lahum qawlan maʿrūfā* — and speak to them kindly — adds a dimension that law alone cannot enforce. *Qawl maʿrūf* is speech that is decent, familiar, and recognized as good — words that acknowledge the person while managing the delay. A dependant told you cannot yet control this wealth hears that as a verdict on his maturity; the verse requires that the message be given with dignity, explanation, and reassurance. The Qur’an pairs this speech elsewhere with kindness at distribution time to non-heirs present (4:8), and with equitable speech when imagining one’s own children left behind (4:9), showing that how the community speaks in financial transactions is part of justice itself. Ibn ʿĀshūr observes that the command to speak kindly prevents two harms at once: the guardian becoming harsh or contemptuous toward the incapable, and the dependant feeling humiliated and then acting recklessly to prove independence. Words that keep the relationship intact make the waiting period humane. Without them, the law may be observed while the bond is damaged; with them, the law protects both wealth and kinship.

**Keeping Law Inside Human Weakness**

This verse keeps the sūrah’s legislation humane by acknowledging weakness without exploiting it. The same chapter that will say *“humankind was created weak”* (4:28) and *“Allah wishes to lighten your burdens”* (4:28) already acts on that truth here. Weak judgment is not mocked, it is managed with provision and kindness until strength appears. The approach differs from two equal errors: handing everything over immediately out of sentimentality, which harms the weak by giving him what will ruin him, and withholding everything indefinitely out of mistrust, which harms him by denying him his right. The middle path — preserve the standing support, provide ongoing care from its yield, and accompany the delay with decent words — honours both ownership and welfare. It also prepares the reader for the testing that follows. The next verse will set the time and the test for return — reaching a marriageable age and showing *rushd*, sound judgment — and it will require that return to be documented and witnessed. Together, verses 5 and 6 build a complete guardianship ethic: keep wealth standing, meet daily needs from it, speak well while you wait, and restore fully when the person is ready.
