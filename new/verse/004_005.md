## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:5

> Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly.

### Expanded Commentary

**Property as a means of communal support**

The verse forbids handing wealth to those unable to manage it while still requiring that their needs be met from it. The term *sufahāʾ* can include minors, people lacking financial judgment, and dependants whose incapacity makes immediate control of assets dangerous. The wealth is described as a means by which Allah sustains the community, not as a badge of personal prestige. Its purpose is nourishment, clothing, shelter, and the maintenance of human dignity.

This is not a command to deprive an incapable person forever. It is a rule of protection. A child or cognitively impaired adult may be the lawful owner of property while another person administers it. Ownership and management are therefore distinguished: the guardian protects the asset without treating the beneficiary as disposable.

**Needs must be supplied from the trust**

The instruction to feed and clothe them from the property prevents two opposite abuses. One guardian might release a large sum prematurely and expose the dependent to manipulation; another might hoard the estate while forcing the dependent to beg. The balanced duty is to spend reasonably for the person’s welfare and preserve the principal as far as possible. Medical care, education, housing, and ordinary support fall within the same logic in contemporary circumstances.

**Honourable speech is part of guardianship**

The verse joins financial administration to speech. A person who cannot be given unrestricted control must not therefore be insulted, infantilized, or treated as a burden. “Speak to them kindly” requires explanation, patience, and language that preserves self-respect. The dependent should be told the truth in a form he or she can understand, rather than lied to for the administrator’s convenience.

This pairing is significant. A family can technically preserve a trust while morally destroying its beneficiary through contempt. The Qur’anic standard measures both the ledger and the relationship. A guardian who pays for food but humiliates the person has not exhausted the duty.

**A principle for institutions**

The verse extends naturally to schools, charities, care homes, public funds, and anyone responsible for another’s resources. The administrator’s skill must serve the beneficiary’s life, not the institution’s image or the administrator’s comfort. Transparent records, periodic review, and qualified advice are modern means of fulfilling the ancient rule. Wealth is placed in human hands as stewardship; the person with less capacity is not a lesser person, and the guardian’s greater capacity is a reason for restraint, not domination.

**Capacity and supported decision-making**

The rule has an important distinction for modern care: protecting a person from financial exploitation does not justify erasing the person’s choices wherever he or she can make them. A guardian should support understanding, use the least restrictive arrangement, and review capacity over time. The Qur’anic aim is welfare with honour, not permanent control by the person who happens to manage the account.
