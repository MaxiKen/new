## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:98

> when we made you equal to the Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Real Sin Was Giving Rivals God's Place**

The condemned explain their error: they made the idols equal to the Lord of all worlds. Equality here need not mean that they believed a stone had the same power as the Creator in every abstract sense. Their practice gave the idols a share in worship, obedience, fear, and hope. They treated a created object as though it could occupy a position that belongs to God alone.

This is the heart of association. The rival does not need to be declared equal in a philosophical statement. It becomes equal in the way a person responds to it. A created thing is raised when its approval governs the heart, its displeasure is feared as unseen power, or its commands override the Lord's command.

**The Lord of All Worlds Leaves No Room for Rival Lords**

The title Lord of all worlds makes the contrast comprehensive. Allah is not one local deity among other local powers. His lordship reaches every realm, every creature, and every cause. To place an idol beside Him is therefore not a small addition to worship. It is a contradiction of the reality that everything else already depends on Him.

The phrase also protects monotheism from becoming tribal. Abraham's God is not only Lord of one family or region. He is the Lord of all worlds. The idols belong to a place, a lineage, or a ritual system; God is the source and governor of the whole order in which those things exist.

**Equality Can Be Practiced Without Being Claimed**

A worshipper may verbally deny that an idol equals God while continuing to sacrifice, call upon, or obey it as a sacred power. The confession exposes that practical contradiction. What matters is not only what the tongue says about rank but what the life assigns to the object.

This principle reaches beyond physical idols. A leader can be treated as unquestionable, wealth can become the condition of hope, and group loyalty can silence God's command. None of these things is necessarily named a god. Yet if they receive the allegiance due only to Allah, they have been made equal in practice. The heart's behavior reveals the hidden comparison.

**The Confession Does Not Blame the Idols Alone**

The speakers say we made you equal. The verb places responsibility on the worshippers. The idols did not raise themselves into divine status. People assigned them the place, protected the assignment, and organized a society around it. The final admission therefore rejects the excuse that the objects forced themselves upon human beings.

Leaders and tempters may have encouraged the practice, but a person who participates remains accountable. The confession is valuable because it restores agency to the people who had tried to locate salvation outside themselves. They now admit that the false equality was something they did, not a natural fact the world imposed.

**The Error Corrupts Both Worship and Ethics**

Once a created object is made equal to God, moral obedience becomes unstable. The person begins to ask what the idol, leader, ancestor, or group wants rather than what God commands. A false sacred authority can then approve injustice, protect privilege, and punish anyone who questions it. The theological error spreads into public conduct.

This explains why Abraham did not treat idolatry as a harmless private preference. Worship establishes the highest authority in life. If that authority is false, the entire moral order can be bent around it. The confession in the Hereafter names the root from which many visible corruptions grew.

Quran 16:20–21 describes the objects invoked besides God as created and unable to create. That parallel supports the confession's logic. An object that is itself dependent cannot be placed beside the One on whom all depend. The comparison fails before any elaborate argument about ritual.

The same principle appeared in Abraham's questions about hearing, benefiting, and harming. The confession now gives the conclusion in the worshippers' own words. Their idols did not become rivals because they possessed rival power. They became rivals because people assigned them rival worship.

Some forms of idolatry preserve the language of God's supremacy while treating idols as necessary mediators. The confession shows why that arrangement remains dangerous. If the mediator receives devotion as a sacred power, it has been given a place beside God regardless of the claim that God is still higher.

Mediation in the created world can mean that one person helps another through ordinary means. That is not the same as assigning an object control over the unseen. The difference lies in worship and ultimate reliance. The condemned recognize that they crossed the line by making the idols equal in the life of the heart.

**Equality With God Is an Inversion of Reality**

The idols depend on the people who carve, carry, clean, and protect them. God sustains the people and the whole world. To make the objects equal is therefore to turn dependence upside down. The worshipper serves what he can move, while neglecting the Lord who gives him movement and life.

Such an inversion can feel normal when inherited from ancestors. The final confession strips away the normality. The worshippers see that the order they accepted was not merely old but false. Truth does not become less decisive because a society organized itself around the opposite.

**The Confession Calls for Undivided Worship Now**

The condemned can name the error, but they cannot undo its history. The living still have the opportunity to refuse every rival claim. They can honor family without worshipping ancestors, use wealth without serving it, follow leaders without obeying them against God, and accept means without making them lords.

The Lord of all worlds does not need a rival to complete His authority. He asks for the heart's return. The confession in this verse is therefore both a verdict and an invitation: see how false equality is formed, and do not wait until judgment to admit that Allah alone deserved the worship given to Him.
