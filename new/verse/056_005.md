## Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah 56:5

> and the mountains will be crushed to pieces,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Strongest Visible Things Are Not Permanent**

After mentioning the shaking of the earth, the verse turns to the mountains. Mountains are among the clearest images of firmness. They rise above the landscape, resist ordinary human power, and appear to have been fixed in place from the beginning of time. The statement that they will be crushed to pieces overturns that appearance. What people call immovable is still created, and what is created remains subject to the command of its Creator.

The verse does not describe a small change in scenery. It presents the breaking of a symbol. Human beings often borrow the language of mountains for institutions, dynasties, convictions, and personalities that seem too strong to fail. The Qur’an uses the mountain to teach that apparent permanence is not the same as true permanence. The final Event will show that only God possesses unconditioned strength.

**Crushing Is a Deliberate Image**

The verb translated “crushed to pieces” carries the sense of being broken, pounded, or reduced by force. It makes the mountain’s destruction vivid. A mountain will not merely be hidden by darkness or left behind as the world changes. Its solidity will be dismantled. The image matches the scale of the Event: the foundations of the visible world are being transformed, not merely rearranged at the surface.

This language also answers those who imagine that resurrection requires only a minor adjustment to the present world. The Qur’anic picture is larger. The Day of Judgment is preceded by a remaking of the cosmic scene so complete that familiar measures of strength no longer apply. The mountain’s destruction is a sign that the conditions of the world of testing are giving way to the conditions of disclosure and recompense.

**The Mountain as a Lesson in Humility**

Mountains can inspire wonder, and that wonder is appropriate. They display scale, order, and beauty. But admiration can become misplaced when created grandeur is treated as ultimate. The verse allows the mountain to retain its majesty while denying it permanence. Its greatness is real within creation, yet it remains dependent. That dependence is the central fact.

The same correction applies to human pride. A wealthy person may possess influence; a scholar may possess knowledge; a leader may command obedience. None should mistake a granted capacity for independent power. The mountain does not become insignificant because it will be crushed. It becomes a more accurate sign: it points beyond itself to the One whose command can change what seems fixed. Human beings become more honourable, not less, when they accept the same dependence.

**The End of a World Built on Appearance**

The present world trains people to judge by what can be seen. Height, mass, wealth, numbers, and public authority often control the imagination. The mountains are used here to challenge that habit. At the final Event, appearance will no longer hide reality. The impressive will be reduced, and the hidden will be brought forward. A mountain’s material grandeur will not benefit the person whose heart was small, while a forgotten act of sincerity may become weighty.

This is why the verse belongs to a chapter concerned with the division of people into distinct groups. The cosmic collapse and the moral sorting belong together. When mountains are crushed, the human scale of prestige is also crushed. People will not be able to rely on the setting that made their rank seem permanent. They will stand as servants before God, carrying what they actually did.

**Let the Temporary Teach the Lasting**

The verse is not a command to despise the created world. It is a command to read it correctly. A mountain can be enjoyed, studied, and protected while still being regarded as temporary. Its beauty should awaken gratitude, not idolatry. Its strength should remind the observer of divine power, not encourage confidence in material permanence.

The practical question is what a person is building his trust upon. If he has made status, possessions, or social approval into mountains of the heart, he should remember that they can be crushed long before the Last Day. If he has made obedience and reliance upon God his foundation, then the collapse of created supports will not be meaningless. The mountains will fall, but the truth they were meant to signify will remain: God alone is unshaken. This perspective gives a disciplined meaning to ambition. A person may seek excellence, establish a useful institution, or leave behind knowledge that benefits others. He should not pretend that the work itself is eternal. Its value lies in whether it was offered to God and whether its benefit continues after the builder is gone. The mountain image therefore opposes both arrogance and nihilism: created achievements are temporary, but faithful use of temporary things can receive an enduring reward. A mountain also gathers water, shapes climates, and gives direction to travellers; its created usefulness does not depend on being eternal or permanent. Likewise, a temporary human deed can have lasting moral weight when it relieves suffering or teaches truth. What passes away in form may remain in consequence, and God knows every consequence that human memory loses. A mountain’s fall is therefore not a lesson in meaninglessness. It is a lesson in where meaning is kept. The created object passes, while the divine knowledge of what it signified and how people responded remains. The believer does not ask temporary things to carry an eternal burden of hope. He receives them with gratitude, uses them with care, and returns his ultimate hope to the Lord who cannot be crushed or diminished. His permanence is the believer’s secure and lasting anchor.
