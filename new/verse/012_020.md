## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:20

> They ˹later˺ sold him for a cheap price, just a few silver coins—only wanting to get rid of him.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb That Means Both Buying and Selling**

The word behind *they sold him* is from *sharā*, one of the small number of Arabic verbs that carries an action and its opposite: to sell and to buy, to give something up and to take it on. The Qur'an exploits the double sense repeatedly. The same root describes those who exchange the hereafter for this world, and it describes God purchasing from the believers their lives and wealth in exchange for Paradise (9:111). Here the ambiguity has a practical consequence for interpretation. If the pronoun refers to Joseph's brothers, they sold him; if it refers to the company that found him, they bought him. The transmitted reports divide on the question, with Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and al-Ḍaḥḥāk reading the brothers and others reading the caravan. Both readings produce the same price and the same indifference, which suggests the ambiguity is not a problem to be solved but a description of a market in which nobody is clearly the responsible party.

**"A Cheap Price" Names a Deficiency, Not a Bargain**

*Bakhs* means deficient, reduced, fallen short of what is due. It is not a comment on the buyer's skill but on the worth assigned to the thing sold. The same word appears in the speech of the jinn who hear the Qur'an: whoever believes in their Lord fears neither *bakhs* nor *rahaq* — neither a shortfall in reward nor an excess in punishment (72:13). The pairing there is instructive, because it defines *bakhs* as the specific injustice of being given less than what is owed. Applied to Joseph it says something precise: a value was set below his due, and the deficiency was not an accident of negotiation. Some commentators push the point further and hold that any price would have been a *bakhs*, since no sum can be the equivalent of a person, still less of a prophet. On that reading the adjective describes the transaction itself rather than the amount.

**"Numbered Dirhams" Tells the Reader How Small the Sum Was**

The specification *darāhima maʿdūdah* — dirhams counted out — is a piece of commercial information. Al-Qurṭubī records the practice behind it: Arab traders settled large transactions by weight and small ones by count, so describing a payment as numbered fixes it below the threshold at which weighing became worthwhile, conventionally fewer than forty coins. The detail is therefore not decorative. It tells the hearer that the sum was small enough to be handled one coin at a time, in a transaction too minor to require scales. Ibn Kathīr transmits the amounts reported by the early commentators: twenty dirhams according to Ibn Masʿūd, with Ibn ʿAbbās, Nawf al-Bikālī, al-Suddī, Qatādah and ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī saying the same, and ʿAṭiyyah adding that the brothers divided it among themselves at two dirhams each. Other reports give twenty-two, and others forty. The biblical account agrees with the lowest figure, recording twenty pieces of silver (Genesis 37:28).

**"They Had No Desire for Him" Uses the Vocabulary of Detachment**

The closing phrase, *wa-kānū fīhi min al-zāhidīn*, describes the sellers as *zāhidūn* toward him. The noun *zuhd* names indifference and detachment from a thing, and in religious usage it becomes the term for asceticism — the deliberate non-attachment to the world that the tradition praises. The verse applies it to men disposing of a child. The irony is not accidental but structural: the same posture that is a virtue when directed at wealth is a crime when directed at a person, and the difference lies entirely in the object. Two motives are offered for the indifference. One is that they regarded him as scavenged goods, found rather than owned, and therefore worth nothing to keep. The other is fear: they did not want to hold him because his real owner might appear and claim him. Both readings agree that the sellers wanted the transaction over rather than the profit large.

**Twenty Coins for a Prophet, and Egypt Will Pay More**

The valuation is the moral centre of the verse. A boy of the house of Abraham, described in the sūrah as one whom God will choose and teach (12:6), is priced at twenty silver coins and handed over to strangers by people who cannot wait to be rid of him. The chapter then spends the next thirty verses raising that price. The official who buys him instructs his wife to give the boy an honoured place (12:21); the women of the city are undone by his appearance (12:31); a king calls him trusted and esteemed (12:54); and Joseph himself asks to be put in charge of the storehouses of the land (12:55). The movement from twenty dirhams to the treasury of Egypt is not a change of fortune so much as a correction of an error. The verse records the error at its lowest point so that the correction can be measured.

**The Transaction Fixes the Crime in Economic Terms**

What is bought and sold here is a free person, and the lowness of the price is evidence of the illegality rather than of the market. Al-Rāzī's reading makes the point explicitly: the sum is small because the sellers hold no title, and everyone involved senses it. A legitimate slave would have fetched more; a kidnapped child must be moved quickly and quietly, and speed suppresses price. The verse therefore contains its own indictment in the arithmetic. Nobody in the scene states that a wrong is being done — not the brothers, not the caravan, not the buyers — and the only comment the narrative supplies is the amount. The sūrah will return to this figure much later, when the same brothers stand before the man they sold and confess that God preferred him over them (12:91). Twenty dirhams is the number the story keeps, and it is the number by which they will finally measure themselves.
