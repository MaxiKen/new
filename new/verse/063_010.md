## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:10

> And donate from what We have provided for you before death comes to one of you, and you cry, “My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Order to Spend, With a Term Attached**

The command is to give, and the phrase attached to it removes any claim of ownership from the giver: donate from what We have provided for you. The property in a man's hand arrived as a grant, and the giving is a return of a portion of the grant rather than a surrender of his own. The preposition is small and the theology behind it is large.

Then the deadline. Before death comes to one of you — not to all of you, and not at a time announced in advance. The Arabic keeps the event individual and unspecified, so that every reader of the verse stands inside the command without knowing where in the sequence he is standing. The rest of the sentence shows what the deadline will feel like when it arrives, by quoting the person as he arrives there.

Nothing in the verse limits the giving to alms-tax. The term used is a general verb of spending, and the situation it describes is the most ordinary one in a believer's life: money in hand, time in hand, and no plan to spend either of them on God.

**Charity as Proof of a Claim**

Arabic derives the word for charity, ṣadaqah, from the same root as ṣidq, truthfulness, because the money given is how a claim of faith is verified. The word appears in this verse in its verb form, and the man pleading for time uses it as the thing he failed to do. He does not say he would pray more or fast longer. He names the transfer of what he was given.

The linguistic background is worth holding beside the sūrah's opening, where a declaration of faith was shown to be a lie because the heart did not hold it. A claim to believe is verified by conduct, and the conduct the Qur'an keeps returning to is the handling of money. Wealth is the point at which invisible conviction becomes visible, because it is the thing people are least willing to part with for an unseen return.

The verse therefore joins the two halves of the sūrah. The hypocrites of its opening used wealth as a shield; the closing verses show a man learning too late that wealth was the test.

**The Plea That Comes One Moment Too Late**

The words put in the dying man's mouth are a confession disguised as a request. He concedes that he had what he needed and that he did not use it; he asks for an extension not to enjoy himself but to repair the omission. He offers a promise about the future that he had his whole past to keep.

The same plea is recorded in another sūrah in nearly the same form, and the response there is an emphatic refusal: *“When death approaches any of them, they cry, “My Lord! Let me go back, so I may do good in what I left behind.” Never! It is only a ˹useless˺ appeal they make. And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected.”* (23:99–100). Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the rule that governs both texts: *“However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, “Now I repent!” nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.”* (4:18).

What these verses refuse is not repentance. It is repentance as the conversion of a debt into a payment due at the last possible minute.

**What He Promises to Give and to Be**

Two things are promised, and their order is instructive. First he says he would give in charity, an act with a measure and a receipt. Then he says he would be one of the righteous, which is a state of the whole person rather than a transaction. The first is the instrument; the second is the goal. The man who spent nothing had also failed to become what he meant to become.

That is the humbling part of the request. He does not claim to have been righteous and misunderstood. He says he would have become righteous, which is an admission that he never did. Nothing about his own record is defended in those words.

The Qur'an uses the same pairing when it speaks of wealth set in motion toward those who will not repay it in kind: the reward promised is not simply the return of the sum but a station among the people of God. Charity in this Book is never only a subtraction from a balance.

**The Prophet's Warning About Withheld Alms**

The Messenger ﷺ described the withheld payment in terms designed to be unforgettable. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1403, from Abū Hurayrah, reports that whoever has been given wealth by Allah and does not pay what is due on it will find that wealth on the Day of Resurrection in the form of a bald-headed poisonous snake with two dark marks above its eyes, which will be wound around his neck and will bite at his cheeks and say, *I am your wealth, I am your treasure*. The Prophet ﷺ then recited the verse in the Qur'an that warns those who hoard.

The image takes the thing that felt like safety and turns it into the thing that attacks. Every man in the sūrah's audience would recognise the shape of the horror: what you refused to let out of your hands will be the creature that fastens onto you.

**The Delay He Asked For Is the Life He Has**

Nothing in this verse is addressed to a dying man. It is addressed to people who can still open their hands, and the plea it quotes exists in the text so that those people hear the argument in advance, from the losing side.

That is the whole strategy of the closing verses of the sūrah. The man speaks, the answer comes, and the reader of the verse notices that his own hands are still open, that the provision is still in his keeping, and that the moment in which the question is decided has not yet passed. Every day lived in that condition is the reprieve he did not receive.

The next verse states the rule that ended his request.
