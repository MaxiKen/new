## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:133

> And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Hasten**

The verse tells the believers to move quickly toward forgiveness from their Lord and toward a garden described as wide as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those who are mindful of God. The verb used for the movement is the one the Book employs for racing.

The same command appears in another sūrah in almost the same form, where the believers are told to compete with one another for forgiveness from their Lord and for a garden as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those who believe in God and His messengers (57:21). The Book's habit of repeating the instruction shows how it treats the pursuit of the Hereafter: as a task that requires speed rather than a conclusion that can be reached at leisure. The order of the two objects is significant, since forgiveness is named before the garden; what the race reaches first is the pardon that opens the way to the reward.

**Forgiveness Stated Before the Garden**

The verse places forgiveness first in the order of what is to be sought, and the garden second. The arrangement follows the logic of the relation between the two: the garden is entered by those who have been forgiven.

The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter consistently treat forgiveness as the entry and the garden as what follows. The sūrah has already spoken about the naming of God as forgiving and merciful, and the passage under discussion will shortly describe repentance in detail. The order in this verse is the frame for that description.

**A Garden as Wide as the Heavens and the Earth**

The description of the garden's width is the Book's way of conveying magnitude without giving measurements, and the commentators discussed how the phrase should be understood. Some took it as a literal description of the garden's extent; others read it as an image of abundance.

The wider point is that the place is proportioned to the generosity of the one who gives it. The Book nowhere describes the garden as a reward that balances the deeds performed for it; it describes it as vast, and it attaches its preparation to the quality of the people rather than to the quantity of their works. The image does that work in a single phrase, and it does so without inviting the reader to measure what cannot be measured.

**Prepared for the Mindful**

The garden is described as prepared for those who are mindful of God, and the same word appeared in the verse about guarding against the Fire. The Book thus describes both destinations with reference to the same quality.

The passage then explains the quality by describing the behavior of the people who have it. The verses that follow list the practices of those for whom the garden is prepared: giving in ease and hardship, restraining anger, pardoning, remembering God after a fault. The description in this verse states the category, and the verses that follow give the content.


The commentaries record a question raised about the verse by an emissary of the Byzantine emperor, who asked where the fire could be if the garden is as wide as the heavens and the earth, and report that the Prophet answered by asking where the night goes when the day comes.

**Hope Placed After Warning**

The verses around this one move from a warning about the Fire to an instruction about obedience and then to this command to seek forgiveness. The sequence gives the passage its balance: the danger is stated, the response is described, and the destination of those who respond is placed in view.

The arrangement reflects the Book's treatment of its own warnings. A threat without an alternative produces despair; an alternative without the statement of the danger produces complacency. The passage supplies both in consecutive sentences, and the community reading it is expected to hold both in view at once.

**What the Verse Asks of a Reader**

The instruction is to hasten, and hastening implies that the opportunity is not permanent. The Book's language of urgency in such passages is directed at people who have the capacity to act and who may not always have it.

What the verse asks for is not an emotional state but a course of conduct begun without delay, and the verses that follow describe what that conduct looks like. The reader who reaches the end of the passage has been given the destination, its extent, the condition for entering it, and a description of the people who satisfy that condition in their daily lives.

**Hastening and the Book's View of Time**

The command to hasten rests on an assumption the Book states in many places: the time available for action is not guaranteed to anyone. The Book tells people to spend from what they have been given before death arrives, and it warns against the moment when a person asks for a delay that will not be granted.

The instruction in this verse is addressed to people who have the capacity to act now. What they are told to hasten toward is forgiveness and the garden, and the urgency is not an emotional pressure but a practical judgement about the shortness of the interval in which the pursuit is possible. The Book's treatment of time in such passages treats delay as a decision like any other, and the verse's verb places the decision in front of the reader at the moment he hears it.
