## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:87

> O my sons! Go and search ˹diligently˺ for Joseph and his brother. And do not lose hope in the mercy of Allah, for no one loses hope in Allah’s mercy except those with no faith.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Order to Search, Not to Endure**

Verse 86 ended with a claim to knowledge. Verse 87 turns that knowledge into a commission. The verb Jacob uses denotes diligent inquiry — searching with effort, tracking something down, not waiting for news to arrive. The instruction is active and it is addressed to the same sons who have just returned from Egypt and who have just rebuked their father for his grief. He does not ask them to grieve less. He sends them back out. The chapter's sequencing is deliberate: complaint directed to God, knowledge claimed from God, and then a task assigned to human beings. Nothing in the verse suggests that reliance replaces effort, and the effort required is a second journey into a famine.

**Joseph Is Named Alongside Benjamin**

The commission covers two people, and the inclusion of the first is the verse's central fact. Benjamin is in custody, which is why the brothers were sent to inquire about him; but Jacob adds Joseph, a son he has been told for decades was devoured by a wolf and whose name he has spoken once in the entire chapter, after turning away (12:84). The conjunction is not casual. To send men to search for a living detainee and a dead child in the same breath is to state a belief, and verse 86 has already stated it. The sūrah places the belief in an instruction rather than in a declaration, which makes it actionable and testable. Naming both sons also refuses to rank the losses. Joseph has been gone twenty years and Benjamin a matter of weeks; the search is commissioned for both in a single sentence, which treats the older loss as no less recoverable than the newer. That equality is the substance of Jacob's hope and it is carried by the structure of the instruction rather than by any claim about it.

**Why Jacob Believes Joseph Is Alive**

The commentators offer grounds. One account holds that he saw it in a dream. Another reports that he was told by the Angel of Death that Joseph's soul had not been taken. Others connect the conviction to the hope expressed in verse 83 and the knowledge claimed in verse 86, without specifying a source. The Qur'an itself gives no ground at all: it reports the instruction and the prohibition against despair, and leaves the conviction standing on the claim that its author knows from God what his sons do not. The chapter's method throughout is to report prophetic knowledge as a fact and withhold its mechanism, and this verse follows the method exactly. The withholding preserves the claim's force. Had the verse explained how Jacob knew, the knowledge would belong to the explanation; left unexplained it belongs to him, and the reader is asked to accept that a man can know what his evidence does not support. The chapter tests that acceptance over eight verses and vindicates it in verse 96.

**"Do Not Lose Hope in God's Mercy"**

The noun behind the translation denotes relief, ease, the breathing-space that comes after constriction. Jacob is not telling his sons to expect a specific outcome. He is telling them not to abandon the expectation that relief is possible, and he attaches the instruction to a journey whose likely outcome — a second refusal in Egypt, a detained brother, an unrecovered son — argues against it. The commentators read the phrase as God's mercy, and connect it to the forgiveness the brothers will eventually receive (12:92, 12:97). The mercy in view is therefore not only the return of two sons but the restoration of a family that has committed a crime against one of its members and never confessed it.

**"No One Despairs of God's Mercy Except the Disbelievers"**

The prohibition is given a theological ground, and the ground is severe. Despair of divine mercy is placed in the category of unbelief rather than of weakness. The Qur'an makes the same connection elsewhere in explicit terms. A prophet tells his people: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins”* (39:53). Abraham's own household asks: *“Who would despair of the mercy of their Lord except the misguided?”* (15:56). Verse 87 stands inside that tradition, and it is spoken by a father to sons who have every worldly reason to despair and no revealed reason to. The audience makes the sentence an instruction rather than a comfort. These are men who have lost a brother, a surety and now their father's sight, and who are being sent back to the country where all three losses happened. Jacob tells them that despair would be a statement about God, and that the evidence does not entitle them to make it.

**Despair as a Claim About God**

The reasoning behind the prohibition is worth stating. To despair of mercy is to assert that a situation has exceeded the range of what God will do, which is a claim about God rather than about the situation. Jacob's own circumstances are the test case: two sons gone, a third self-exiled in Egypt, his sight failing, a famine over the region, and a family report he does not believe. The verse asks men inside those circumstances to continue expecting relief, not because the circumstances suggest it but because the alternative is a judgement about divine capacity. The chapter then spends thirteen verses showing the expectation met in a way none of the parties could have predicted. The unpredictability is part of the lesson. What Jacob expects is news; what arrives is a reunion, a restoration of sight, the relocation of an entire household and the fulfilment of a dream reported eighty-three verses earlier. Hope directed at God's mercy is not a prediction about method, and expecting one particular outcome is a different act from refusing to despair.

**The Verse Sends Them Back to Egypt**

Structurally verse 87 is the mechanism that produces the climax. Without it there is no third journey, no plea in verse 88, no question in verse 89, no disclosure in verse 90, no reunion in verse 100. A grieving father's conviction becomes a commission, the commission becomes a journey, and the journey becomes a recognition scene. The sūrah is careful to attribute the movement to Jacob rather than to the brothers: they do not decide to return, they are sent. That attribution matters for the shape of the ending, since the reconciliation the chapter arrives at is initiated by the person who was injured and by the father who lost both sons. Neither of them has to act, and both do. Joseph could have kept his family in Canaan and simply continued the grain shipments; Jacob could have refused to risk a tenth son after losing two. The search is commissioned by the two people with least to gain from it and most to lose, and the chapter records the initiative without praising it.

**Searching for a Dead Son as an Act of Faith**

The commission to look for Joseph is, on the family's own account, an order to search for a corpse that was never recovered. Jacob accepted the report about the wolf in verse 18; he did not dispute the fact of Joseph's disappearance, only the explanation of it. Sending men to inquire after a son lost decades earlier is therefore not a practical measure in any ordinary sense, and the verse does not present it as one. It presents it as the consequence of two claims made in the preceding verse — knowledge from God, and a refusal to despair of mercy. The brothers carry out the instruction, which is itself notable: nine men who do not share their father's conviction travel into a famine to look for a brother they believe was eaten by an animal. The chapter records the obedience without commenting on the belief that motivated it.
