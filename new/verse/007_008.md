## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:8

> The weighing on that Day will be just. As for those whose scale will be heavy ˹with good deeds˺, ˹only˺ they will be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Weighing Set on That Day**

The verse states that the weighing on that day is just, and the phrase identifies the judgment with a balance. The Book uses the image in several passages about the last things: the sūrah of the Prophets says that God will set up the scales of justice on the day of resurrection, so that no soul will be wronged in the least (21:47); the sūrah of the Believers states that those whose scales are heavy will prosper and those whose scales are light will lose their souls (23:102–3); and the sūrah of the Clatterer describes the same division between the one whose scale is heavy and the one whose scale is light (101:6–9).

The image gives the reckoning its public and measurable character. The deeds are not weighed by a standard that is chosen for the occasion; the verse states that the weighing itself is just, and it fixes the outcome to the balance that has been set. The commentary tradition reads the phrase as a statement of God's justice, since the measure is applied without partiality.

**Whose Scales Are Heavy**

The verse states that those whose balance is heavy are the ones who will prosper, and the Book's usage of the image indicates that the weight belongs to good deeds. The sūrah of the Cattle has stated the principle of the reckoning in the multiplication of rewards before this scene, and the sūrah of the Bee describes the reward of the believers in terms of what they have earned. The heavy scale is therefore the record of a life in which the good outweighed the evil.

The Book's statements about the weight of deeds include the report that the deeds of the believers are made heavy by what they have done in obedience, and the commentary tradition notes that the weight is not measured by quantity alone. The verse in front of us does not describe the deeds themselves but the outcome of the measuring, and it leaves the content of the scale to the rest of the revelation.

**The Sound Report on Good Character**

The Prophet is reported to have said that nothing placed on the scale is heavier than good character, and that the person of good character attains the rank of the one who fasts and prays (al-Tirmidhī 2003); another narration of the same Companion's report states that nothing is heavier upon the scale of a believer on the day of resurrection than his good character (al-Tirmidhī 2002). The reports identify the deeds that carry weight in the balance, and they place conduct toward people among the heaviest of them.

The Book's own emphasis on conduct alongside worship appears in its repeated pairing of faith with righteous action, and the sūrah of the Cave places the believers who do good in the same category. The verse in front of us states the outcome; the reports name one of the things that fills the scale.

**The Use of the Balance in the Book's Teaching**

The image of the balance runs through the Book's instructions about trade and measurement as well as its descriptions of the judgment. The sūrah of the Cattle commands the giving of full measure and weight with justice (6:152), the sūrah of the Heights itself commands Shuʿayb's people to give full measure and weight and not to defraud people of their goods (7:85), the sūrah of Hūd repeats the command (11:84–85), the sūrah of the Night Journey states that the measure should be given in full (17:35), the sūrah of the Poets repeats it (26:182–83), and the sūrah of the Merciful states that God has set up the balance and commands that it not be transgressed (55:7–9).

The connection between the two uses of the image gives the verse its force for its first audience. A community of traders that adjusted its scales for profit is told that its conduct is measured on a scale it did not control. The Book elsewhere states that God created all things in due measure and sent down the balance (42:17; 57:25), and the reckoning in front of us applies the same order to human acts.

**Why the Deeds Are Weighed**

The tradition of commentary explains that the balance does not supply God with information, since the deeds are already known and recorded. Its function is to confront the wrongdoer with the accuracy of the outcome, so that he acknowledges the justice of what has been decided and abandons the excuses he would otherwise offer. The Book's descriptions of the condemned include their admissions of guilt, and the verse in front of us supplies the ground of those admissions.

The image serves the same purpose for the reader of the sūrah. The verse that precedes this one has stated that the account is given with knowledge, and the verse in front of us adds that the account is measured with justice. Nothing in the arrangement leaves room for a complaint about the standard.

**The Heaviness and the Lightness**

The verse names only the heavy scale and its reward, and the verse that follows names the light scale and its loss. The Book's habit is to state the two outcomes together, as it does in the sūrah of the Believers and in the sūrah of the Clatterer, and the sūrah in front of us follows the same pattern. The reader is given the reward of the heavy scale in one verse and the ruin of the light scale in the next.

The light scale is not the absence of all good but the insufficiency of what was brought against what was earned. The Book's statements about the deeds of the deniers describe them as dust scattered and as a mirage in a plain, and the sūrah of the Light states that the deeds of those who disbelieve are like a mirage in a desert which the thirsty takes for water until he comes to it and finds nothing. The verse in front of us states the outcome for those who arrive with weight.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands eighth in the chapter's opening, and it follows the description of the questioning and the recounting. The verses that come after it state the fate of those whose scales are light, and the passage then turns to the establishment of people on the earth and the provision placed in it. The balance in front of us is the hinge on which the opening section's account of the last day turns.

The reader who has been told to follow what has been sent down is now given the standard by which his following will be measured. The deeds are recorded, the account is exact, and the weight is set in the balance.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that those whose balance is light are the ones who have ruined their souls by treating the signs of God wrongfully (7:9). The verse names the conduct that produces the light scale, and it ties the loss to the denial of the signs rather than to any deficiency in the measure.

The two verses together present the division of humanity at the reckoning. One group prospers by the weight it brings, and the other loses itself by what it denied. The sūrah's opening then states the provision that has been placed on the earth and the gratitude that is owed for it.
