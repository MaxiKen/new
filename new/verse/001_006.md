## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Request From People Already on the Road**

The Fātiḥah has already described its reciters as worshippers who ask God for help, and then the first thing they ask for is guidance. The commentators noticed the apparent oddity: people who praise God, name His mercy and declare their worship are asking for something they appear to have. They answered with several readings of *ihdinā* — keep us firm on the path, increase us in guidance, guide us to what we still do not know, or guide us in the acts of a day that has not yet been lived.

All the readings agree on a point that shapes Muslim piety: guidance in the Qur'an is not a single event that a person banks once and owns. It is a condition that has to be granted and maintained, which is why the request is put in a prayer recited daily rather than in a statement of achievement. *“Whoever holds firmly to Allah is surely guided to the Straight Path”* (3:101), and the Qur'an speaks of nations whose members had already been granted what they then asked for again.

**The Word Behind "Path"**

*Ṣirāṭ* is a road, and the sūrah does not leave it bare: it is the *mustaqīm* path, the one that stands straight. The word pairs with guidance in a formula used across the Book: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Surely my Lord has guided me to the Straight Path, a perfect way, the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists’”* (6:161), and God recalls a covenant with Israel as *“but to worship Me ˹alone˺? This is the Straight Path”* (36:61).

The verses show that the phrase is not a moral abstraction. It is the route by which Abraham walked, the command by which Israel was bound, and the destination to which a person is guided rather than one he calculates. A path is walked one step at a time and reached only by arriving; the sūrah asks for the road rather than for the map, which is a different and more demanding request.

**Straight Means Standing, Not Narrow**

The Arabic root behind *mustaqīm* is *q-w-m*, the root of standing upright. The word does not mean small or squeezed; it means erected, level, not leaning. What it opposes is not breadth but crookedness, a road that curves away from its destination even when it is wide and well travelled. When the Qur'an sets the phrase against its opposite it uses that pair: *“Who is ˹rightly˺ guided: the one who crawls facedown or the one who walks upright on the Straight Path?”* (67:22).

The metaphor carries a practical claim. A crooked road can look busier than a straight one, and a traveller can spend a lifetime moving without arriving. The verse does not ask for an easier route or a shorter one, and it does not ask to be spared difficulty. It asks for the road that actually arrives, and it asks for it as the one thing a person asks from the Lord of the worlds.

**Guidance Is Given, Not Manufactured**

The request itself settles a doctrinal question. If guidance could be constructed by effort, a prayer for it would be unnecessary; if it were imposed without conditions, a prayer for it would be pointless. The Qur'an holds both sides: *“He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path”* (2:142), and *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth.”* (41:53).

Guidance in this Book therefore comes as an offered light and as a condition that a person accepts, keeps or loses. The sūrah's request is for the first act of God and the continuing response of the servant at once. Nobody recites 1:6 while claiming to have arrived; the person who says it has just defined himself as still on the way.

**The Most Repeated Request on Earth**

The Fātiḥah is recited in every unit of the five daily prayers, and the report of ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit makes its recitation constitutive of prayer at all: *There is no prayer for one who does not recite the opening of the Book* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 756; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 394). The sentence asking for the straight road is thus among the most frequently spoken sentences in human life, said a minimum of seventeen times each day by every worshipper who keeps the five prayers.

What Muslim worship asks for most often is not health, wealth or victory; it is direction. The Qur'an places that request at the centre of the prayer that opens the Book, and it pairs it with the marks of the guided: *“Successful indeed are the believers: / those who humble themselves in prayer”* (23:1–2). Guidance, on this account, is not information received once. It is a road, and the prayer for it is said again every time the sun comes up.

**A Path With One Name and Many Walkers**

The Qur'an keeps the Straight Path singular and describes many people walking it. Abraham is called *“a model of excellence: devoted to Allah, ˹perfectly˺ upright—not a polytheist—”* (16:120), and the same way is said to have been decreed for Noah, Abraham, Moses and Jesus in a single command: *“Uphold the faith, and make no divisions in it”* (42:13). The sūrah's request for guidance is therefore a request to join a road that is already occupied.

That history gives the prayer its scale. The reciter of 1:6 is not asking to be led somewhere no one has been; he is asking to be placed on the route the Book traces from Abraham to Muḥammad, and to remain on it. The way is singular because its destination is singular, while the walkers are countless, and the Qur'an insists that revelation ran along that road before the community that now recites the Fātiḥah existed.

Read this way the verse is a request about company as much as about direction. To be guided along the Straight Path is to be guided into a line of people who took the same route, and the plural *us* in the reciter's request already anticipates the company he is asking for.
