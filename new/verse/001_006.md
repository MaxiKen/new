## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Thing Asked For**

Four verses establish who God is and one establishes the worshipper's posture toward Him. Only now does a request appear, and it is not the request a person in difficulty would think to make first. No provision is asked for, no health, no relief, no forgiveness yet — only direction. The sūrah treats guidance as the one gift without which every other gift can be misused.

The report that tracks the recitation as a dialogue gives this half of the sūrah a distinctive ending. When the worshipper reaches the request for the path, the response is *This is for My servant, and My servant will receive what he asks for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). The previous clauses were answered with statements about God — praised, lauded, glorified — and this one is answered with a grant. The structure of the sūrah therefore carries its own argument: a creature who does not know which way to walk has no use for anything else he might be given, and a creature who does know can be given everything.

**"Guide Us" Presumes a Road Already Found**

*Ihdinā* is usually rendered "guide us," but the request is being made by people who already have a revelation, a prophet, and a community. Several commentators therefore read the verb here as asking for something other than first-time direction: perseverance on the path one is already on, and firmness in the truth one already holds. The same verb covers both, and the Quran uses both senses — *And Allah increases in guidance those who are ˹rightly˺ guided* (19:76) describes guidance arriving to people who already have it.

The two readings are complementary rather than rival, and the difference between them is the difference between conversion and endurance. A worshipper who recites this line as a new Muslim is asking to be shown the road. The same worshipper reciting it forty years later is asking not to be taken off it. The Quran promises the second as a consequence of faith rather than a reward for it: *Surely those who believe and do good, their Lord will guide them ˹to Paradise˺ through their faith* (10:9) — their faith is named as the instrument of the guidance, not its price.

**Straight, Not Short**

The adjective is *mustaqīm*, from a root meaning to stand upright. A straight path is defined by the absence of crookedness, and the Quran's own definition of it is negative and then positive in the same breath: *Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way* (6:153). The straightness is a property of the road; the danger is in the alternatives, which are plural while the path is singular.

Some commentators took the straightness to imply a middle way in conduct as well as in belief — a life that neither indulges the world nor flees it, and a religion that holds the outward discipline of the law together with the inward work of the heart. The Quran supports the balance from its own vocabulary, describing the community as *an upright community* (2:143) placed between extremes. What the adjective rules out, in any case, is the idea of a path that bends to suit the walker. A road that accommodates every preference is not straight, and the request in this verse is to be kept on one that does not.

**What the Commentators Said the Path Is**

The identifications differ, and the disagreement is worth reporting with its grounds rather than as a list of opinions. Some said the path is the Quran itself. Others said Islam, or the Prophet. Shiʿi commentaries frequently identify it with ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, or with the Prophet and the Imams after him; several Sunni commentaries name the Prophet together with his first two successors, Abū Bakr and ʿUmar.

These are not competing definitions of a word. Each names what a given tradition regards as the surest guarantee of being on the road, and the guarantee is what is at issue, since the verse is a request and not a puzzle. The reading that best fits the Quran's own usage is the one that keeps the path a direction rather than an institution: *As for those who believe in Allah and hold fast to Him… He will… guide them to Himself through the Straight Path* (4:175). The path leads to God, and everything else — scripture, messenger, community, exemplar — is named as the means by which a person is kept on it. That is also why the complaint of the prophets is phrased as it is: *Surely my Lord’s Way is perfect justice* (11:56).

**The Parable of Two Walls**

The Prophet is reported to have described the path in a parable. Al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān relates that he said God has struck a parable of a straight path with walls on both sides, open doors in the walls, and curtains hanging on the doors. A caller at the head of the path cries, *O people! All of you enter the path and do not deviate*, and a caller above the path warns anyone who moves to open a door: *Woe to you! Do not open it, for if you open it, you will enter it.* The Prophet then supplied the keys: the path is Islam, the walls are the limits set by God, the doors are the things He has forbidden, the caller at the head of the path is the Book of God, and the caller above it is God's admonishment in the heart of every Muslim (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2859; Musnad Aḥmad 17634).

The parable answers an objection the request itself raises. If the road is straight and God is asked to guide along it, why do people leave it? The answer the parable gives is that leaving is never a single leap but an ordinary movement toward an opening that looks like part of the journey. Protection comes from two directions at once — a text outside the walker and a warning inside him — and both are described as callers, which means neither forces the outcome.

**The Road That Becomes a Crossing**

Later tradition read *ṣirāṭ* in a second sense as well: the traverse every creature must make at the end, set over the Fire. The Prophet is reported to have said that the bridge will be laid across Hell and described it as slippery, fitted with clamps and hooks like the thorn of as-Saʿdān, which people cross at speeds matching their deeds — some like the blink of an eye, some like lightning or wind or racing horses, some scratched and saved, and some falling, with the last dragged across (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7439).

The same word thus covers the road of this life and the crossing of the next, and the two are held together by the request in this verse. Guidance now is what makes the passage then. [UNVERIFIED: the widely repeated description of the traverse as "finer than a hair and sharper than a sword" could not be located in the canonical wording; the verified description is the one in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7439.]
