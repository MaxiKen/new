## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:17

> If Allah touches you with harm, none can undo it except Him. And if He touches you with a blessing, He is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Directions, One Hand**

The verse takes the two experiences that define a human life — injury and benefit — and assigns both to a single source. Harm cannot be lifted by anyone else once it arrives, and the second clause adds the mirror: blessing comes from the same hand, and that hand is capable of everything. Between them the two halves leave no space for a competing power, whether an idol, a star, a healer, or a tribal patron.

That is the argument the sūrah has been building from the start. Ownership of the heavens and the earth, knowledge of the hidden, and mercy prescribed for Himself are large claims; this verse makes them personal. The question a man in pain actually asks is not who owns the universe but who can remove this. The answer is given in the second person: if He touches you with harm, none can undo it except Him.

**Why the Book Says "Touch" Instead of "Strike"**

The verb chosen here describes contact, the light laying on of a hand, and the Book uses it for both harm and good in the same sentence. That choice does two things. It keeps the divine action close and immediate rather than remote and institutional, and it allows one verb to carry both halves of the verse, so that the reader feels the same nearness in the blessing and in the wound.

The alternative vocabulary was available. The Book can speak of striking, of smiting, of sending down, and it does so where severity is the point. Here the softer word fits the argument, which is about who is nearest rather than about how hard the blow lands. A person touched by something is in contact with whoever touched him, and the verse denies that any other hand was there.

**The Same Sentence Elsewhere in the Book**

The wording of this verse recurs almost identically in the sūrah of Jonah: if Allah touches you with harm, none can undo it except Him; and if He intends good for you, none can withhold His bounty, granting it to whom He wills of His servants (10:107). The Book repeats it because the audience kept needing it, and the repetition is itself the argument — a claim this central is stated in more than one sūrah in nearly the same words.

A second passage makes the same point from the opposite direction: whatever mercy God opens up for people, none can withhold it, and whatever He withholds, none but Him can release it (35:2). Two sūrahs, the same doctrine, and both place every gain and every loss inside a single decision that no intercessor can move.

**The Objects That Could Not Respond**

The idols of Makkah were credited with the power to hurt and to help, and the Book dismisses the claim from several angles. How can you worship, besides Allah, those who can neither harm nor benefit you (5:76)? Of the calf that the Israelites fashioned, the question is put with heavy irony: did they not see that it could not respond to them, nor protect or benefit them (20:89)? And the lords taken besides God are described as unable to benefit or protect even themselves (13:16).

Set beside that catalogue, this verse is compact and decisive. The complaint against the idols is not that they are weak in comparison with a stronger god. It is that the category of harm and benefit — the two levers by which a person's life is actually steered — is closed to them entirely, and belongs to one power alone.

**The Messenger Does Not Own the Power He Announces**

An obvious suspicion follows from such a claim, and the Book addresses it in the Prophet's own voice. He is told to say that he has no power to benefit or protect himself except by God's will, that he is only a warner and a bringer of good news to believers (7:188). He is told to say that he does not claim the treasuries of God, or knowledge of the unseen, or angelic status, and that he only follows what is revealed to him (6:50).

The two verses belong together. The messenger announces that God alone can harm and help, and then explicitly removes himself from that power. Arabia was used to men who claimed a share of the sacred; the Prophet's testimony is designed to be unusable as a claim of that kind. He is the announcer, not the dispenser.

**Even Sorcery Sits Under the Same Claim**

Makkan anxiety about magic was real, and the Book does not simply ridicule it. It places sorcery inside the doctrine of this verse: the practitioners of magic in the story of Babylon could not harm anyone except by Allah's will, and the harm they caused was ultimately to themselves (2:102). The verse's claim about harm therefore extends over the very practice the audience feared most.

That inclusion is a quiet mercy. People who live in fear of curses and charms are usually offered a stronger charm in exchange. The Book refuses to sell one. It tells them that the harm that reaches them was permitted by the One who alone can lift it, and that the cure is therefore addressed to Him and not to a counter-spell.

**Why the Verse Ends With Capability, Not Comfort**

The closing attribute is not mercy but capability: He is Most Capable of everything, able to do all things. The ending is deliberate. The second clause concerns blessing, and a person asking for blessing needs to know that the One being asked is able to grant it; the attribute supplied is precisely the one the request requires.

Mercy has already been settled in the sūrah: God prescribed it for Himself (6:12). With mercy established and capability stated at the end, the verse leaves its hearer in a position where neither fear nor hope has been left without a foundation. What the sentence rules out is the middle ground of going to a stone for the removal of a harm that only God can lift.
