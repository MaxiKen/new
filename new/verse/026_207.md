## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:207

> would that enjoyment be of any benefit to them ˹at all˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Questions the Value of Enjoyment Without Faith**

The question is not whether the people enjoyed their years. They did. It asks whether that enjoyment will benefit them when the promised consequence arrives. Benefit is measured at the point of final accountability, not only by the comfort felt while life is easy.

The answer implied by the question is no. Wealth, food, influence, and long life cannot pay a debt of deliberate injustice. Enjoyment may have provided opportunities for gratitude and repentance, but if the people used it to deepen denial, the same years become part of the evidence against them. What looked like advantage did not become lasting safety.

**A Gift Can Become Evidence Against Its Owner**

Provision carries a responsibility. A person who receives wealth can use it to honor agreements, feed dependents, and support what is right. If he uses it to increase fraud or silence correction, the gift exposes the direction of his heart. The problem is not the existence of enjoyment but the refusal to acknowledge the Giver and the rights of other people.

Quran 3:178 warns that extra time can increase the sins of those who disbelieve, and 6:44 describes abundance followed by sudden seizure after people become proud. These parallels explain why the question is so sharp. Enjoyment is not judged by how much pleasure it supplied but by whether it moved the person toward gratitude or further away from truth.

**The Hereafter Gives the Correct Measure of Gain**

Human beings often call a life successful when it contains comfort, status, and a long period without visible loss. The verse changes the measure. If those achievements end with a person standing before Allah while his wrongs remain unrepaired, they have not delivered the benefit he imagined.

This does not make lawful worldly good worthless. It makes it accountable. A comfortable believer can receive reward for gratitude and generous use, while a comfortable denier can find that comfort has only made his case clearer. The same object has different consequences according to the faith and conduct of the person who receives it.

**The Question Is Mercy to Those Still Living**

The rhetorical question is addressed to people who can still reconsider their own idea of benefit. Do not envy enjoyment that is being used against its owner, and do not assume that a difficult season means Allah has abandoned the faithful. Measure life by the direction in which it is taking the heart.

The best benefit of time is a return to Allah before the promised consequence arrives. A person can turn wealth into charity, influence into protection for the weak, and years into repentance and service. Then enjoyment becomes a means of good rather than a witness to the refusal that made it empty.

The question also addresses envy. A believer may see people enjoying what he does not have and conclude that Allah has favored them, while forgetting that visible comfort is only one part of the account. The Quran teaches that enjoyment becomes beneficial when it is joined to gratitude and right conduct. Without those qualities, abundance can increase a person's attachment to the world and make the eventual loss more painful.

Benefit should be measured by what remains after the temporary scene changes. A possession that fed a family lawfully and supported a good cause has a lasting moral result, even if the object itself disappears. A fortune built by reducing the rights of others may appear impressive, but its apparent success does not follow its owner as a defense. The verse asks what enjoyment can do at the moment when its owner needs real benefit.

This does not mean that believers must refuse lawful pleasure or apologize for every comfort. Islam does not make deprivation the proof of sincerity. The issue is whether enjoyment is received as a trust or worshiped as an identity. A grateful person can enjoy a meal, a home, a business, or a period of rest while remembering that the gift came from Allah and must not be used to injure anyone.

The rhetorical question therefore makes comfort answerable to the Hereafter. It turns the listener away from the glitter of uninterrupted years and toward the direction of the heart. Enjoyment helps the person who uses it to prepare for Allah. It harms the person who lets it persuade him that he will never have to answer.

A final measure of benefit is what the gift does to a person's relationships. If comfort makes him more reliable, generous, and protective of the weak, it has become a means of worship. If it makes him contemptuous of those with less and careless about the claims of the Hereafter, its shine is already carrying loss. The verse asks the living to choose which result their enjoyment will produce before the account makes the answer plain.

Enjoyment is safest when it remains a servant of gratitude. It may pass quickly, but the justice and generosity practiced through it can remain in the record. The question therefore does not condemn provision; it asks whether provision has led its owner toward Allah or away from Him.

A person who understands this measure can enjoy what Allah gives without being owned by it. He thanks the Giver, pays what is due, and remembers that comfort will be questioned just as hardship is endured. The benefit of enjoyment is finally found in the obedient heart it helps to form, not in the temporary pleasure that disappears. Such gratitude turns provision into preparation for the meeting with Allah and keeps pleasure under the rule of worship.
