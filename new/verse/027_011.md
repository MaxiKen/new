## Sūrah an-Naml 27:11

> ˹Fear is˺ only for those who do wrong. But if they later mend ˹their˺ evil ˹ways˺ with good, then I am certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Exception That Points at the Listener**

The previous sentence said messengers have no fear in God's presence. This one adds a qualification, and the qualification is aimed. Mūsā is standing at the fire as a man who left Egypt because he had killed someone. Al-Qaṣaṣ records the incident plainly: he intervened in a fight, struck an Egyptian, and the man died — *“So Moses punched him, causing his death.”* (28:15). He fled the country over it.

Al-Ṭabarī reads the clause as an oblique reference to that event, and the reading fits the psychology of the scene. Mūsā has just been told that messengers are safe here. A man with his history could reasonably wonder whether the reassurance covers him, or whether he has been listed under a category he no longer qualifies for. The verse answers the unasked question. It first admits that wrongdoing is a real ground for fear, then immediately supplies the route out of it. Nothing about this is abstract theology; it is a specific man being told, at the moment of his appointment, that the thing in his past has a remedy.

**"Replacing Evil With Good" Is a Definition of Repentance**

The Arabic is *baddala ḥusnan baʿda sūʾ* — put good in place of evil, afterwards. Al-Ṭabarī takes the phrase as a straightforward description of repentance, and the wording repays attention because of what it does not say. It does not say the person regretted, felt sorrow, or declared himself sorry. It says he substituted. The old conduct is removed and different conduct is put in the space it occupied.

That framing makes repentance a construction project rather than an emotional event. Feeling is not excluded — remorse is what usually starts the process — but the verse measures the outcome, which is a changed pattern of action. The same logic appears elsewhere in the Qur'an, where the repentant are described as those who repent, believe and do righteous work, three stages with the last one doing the verifying. The order in this verse also matters: *good after evil*, with the sequence stated explicitly. What is being described is not a clean record but a repaired one, and the repair is visible precisely because the damage came first.

**Why Some Commentators Fought the Plain Reading**

The particle *illā*, rendered here as the exception introducing *those who do wrong*, normally means *except*. Read straightforwardly, the two verses together say: messengers have no fear before Me — except one who did wrong. That puts a wrongdoer inside the class of messengers, and a number of scholars resisted the implication.

Al-Ṭabarī preserves their alternative. They read *illā* in a connective sense, giving: messengers have no fear in My presence, and neither does one who did wrong and then replaced evil with good. On that reading the clause introduces a second, separate category of the unafraid rather than carving an exception out of the first. The grammar permits it; *illā* does have such uses. But the motive behind the preference is not grammatical. It is the doctrine of prophetic protection from sin, held by most Muslim theologians, which makes any attribution of wrongdoing to a prophet uncomfortable.

**Where the Doctrine Actually Stands**

Al-Qurṭubī, who was no minimiser of prophetic dignity, made the point that settles most of the difficulty: many scholars accept that a prophet could commit an error before receiving prophethood. Mūsā's blow fell years before this night at the fire. Nothing in the doctrine of ʿiṣmah as most theologians framed it requires that a man be faultless in the period before he was appointed.

There is a second observation in al-Qurṭubī's discussion that is more interesting than the first. Fearing that one's repentance has not been accepted is itself a mark of sincerity, not of deficient faith. The man confident that his account is settled has stopped examining it. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī both transmit a saying attributed to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī on this passage — that prophets sin and are taken to task for it — which states the position at its bluntest. I record it as they transmit it, without a chain I have been able to verify. I have definitely wronged my soul, so forgive me.” So He forgave him”* (28:16).

**Two Names Chosen to Close a Frightening Scene**

The verse ends with *ghafūr raḥīm*, All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, and the choice is deliberate against everything that has preceded it. Two verses ago God named Himself the Almighty, the All-Wise — the attributes of a sender commissioning an agent against Pharaoh. Now, addressing the private matter of a man's own record, the names change entirely.

*Ghafūr* comes from a root meaning to cover, the same root behind the word for a helmet. Forgiveness in Arabic is imagined as something laid over a fault, concealing it rather than pretending it never happened. *Raḥīm* comes from the root for the womb, and names mercy of the enveloping, sustaining kind. Placed at the end of this verse, the pair completes an argument: the wrongdoer has ground to fear, the remedy is to build good where the evil was, and the one receiving that repair is disposed to cover and to shelter. Mūsā came to the fire cold and lost. He is being sent from it with a mission, a sign, and his own past settled. The next verse hands him the second sign.
