## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:264

> O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words, like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. Their example is that of a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone. Such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity. Allah does not guide ˹such˺ disbelieving people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Annulment Forbidden**

The sentence addresses the believers and forbids them to annul their charity through reminders of generosity and through injury, and the community's teachers read the instruction as the statement that an act of goodness can be destroyed by the conduct that follows it. The Book has named the reward of the givers who preserve their gifts, and the sentence here states what removes it.

The wording is addressed to the givers themselves, so that the danger the passage describes lies in their own conduct rather than in the conduct of those who receive. The community's scholars noted the point, since the poor who are given and then reproached have no part in the loss that the sentence describes.

The instruction also implies that the act and its manner belong together. What the Book calls the annulment is the destruction of the value of the gift, and the teachers described the affair in terms of a person who works and then dismantles what he has built.

**The Show-off Named**

The sentence compares the conduct of the one who annuls his charity with the conduct of a person who spends his wealth to be seen by people and who does not believe in God or the Last Day. The commentators noted the pairing, since the display of a gift places its purpose in the eyes of the community rather than in the pleasure of God.

The teachers explained the phrase about the belief as the statement about the root from which the conduct grows. The person who looks for the praise of his neighbours has taken them as the audience of his act, and the Book's description places him outside the company of those who give for its sake.

The community's scholars also noted that the sentence does not describe the scale of the gift but the state of the giver. A small gift given in the manner the passage commends is preserved, while a large gift offered for display has nothing behind it.

**The Parable of the Rock**

The sentence presents the figure of a smooth rock with a thin layer of soil upon it, and a downpour that strikes it and leaves it bare. The commentators explained the image as the description of an act that appears to be productive and is found to be empty, since the rain washes away the soil on which a crop might have grown.

The teachers connected the meaning of the figure with the subject of the passage. The thin layer of dust stands for the charitable act, and the downpour stands for the reminder and the injury that remove it, so that the giver is left with nothing at the time he needs it most.

The image also describes the condition of the surface, since the rock offers nothing for the seed to take hold of. The community's scholars read the detail as the statement about the heart in which the act was not rooted in the intention that the Book requires.

**The Rain and the Bare Stone**

The commentators drew the comparison with the parable that follows, where the same rain falls on a fertile garden and produces a double harvest. The two figures share the water and differ in the ground, so that the sentence's teaching concerns the condition of the person who receives the heavenly gift rather than the gift itself.

The community's teachers read the pair as the Book's method of presenting two ends of the same act. The rainfall of the world's opportunities reaches both kinds of people, and what they produce depends on what they have made of their hearts.

The material also shows the care with which the community's scholars handled the figures of the Book, since they identified each element of the parables and then drew the whole together. The reading they transmitted presents the section as a single instruction about the intention of the giver.

**The Closing Statement**

The sentence closes with the statement that these people have no power over anything of what they earned, and the community's scholars read the clause as the account of the loss that the parable describes. The teachers noted the form of the phrase, since it does not deny that the gift was made but states that its value has passed.

The Book's use of the language of earning for the spiritual result appears in the passages that treat the account of the Hereafter, and the community's scholars gathered the instances when they explained the sentence. The reward of an act is described as a possession that a person carries, and the verse states that the person in question holds nothing.

The final clause states that God does not guide the disbelieving people, and the teachers read it as the statement about the condition of those whose conduct has replaced the purpose of the act. The sentence in this place therefore ends where the previous passage ended, with the guidance that the Book grants to those who seek it.

**The Passage's Concern**

The verse stands within the long treatment of charity in the sūrah, and the community's teachers read the parables of the section as a series of instructions about the manner of giving. The sentence in this place supplies the warning that accompanies the promise of the earlier verse, and the passage then supplies the comparison of the good giver.

The section's concern is the honesty of the act before God, since the community's giving was visible to its members and the temptation to seek their approval was near. The Book's answer is the account of the two grounds and the two harvests, and the sentence here supplies the first of them.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the prohibition of annulling charity through reminders and injury, the description of the giver who acts for display, the parable of the rock and the rain, the loss that follows for such a giver, and the statement about the guidance that the unbelieving people do not receive. The community's teachers used the verse when they warned the wealthy about the manner of their giving, and the sūrah's next verse supplies the corresponding account of the giver whose act is rooted in the pleasure of God.
