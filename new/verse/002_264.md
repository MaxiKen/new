## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:264

> O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words, like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. Their example is that of a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone. Such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity. Allah does not guide ˹such˺ disbelieving people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Do Not Annul**

The command's verb is the verse's center: lā tubṭilū — *do not waste*, do not nullify, do not void. The charity is assumed to have happened; the transfer is complete; and the verse legislates over its aftermath with the language of cancellation. The two annulment-instruments are the pair the previous verse named — reminders of generosity and hurtful words — and the verb assigns them their true power: they do not reduce the charity or discount it but void it, the way the sūrah's own war-legislation used the same root of deeds that come to nothing. The annulment-theology is the verse's first instruction: a completed good can be unmade by a following evil, and the unmaking is total rather than proportional. The commentators will read the coming garden-parable (2:266) in exactly these terms — the old man's orchard as the lifetime of good works, the fiery whirlwind as the annulment arriving at the worst possible hour — and this verse is that parable's command-form: what the whirlwind does to the garden, the reminder does to the gift.

**The Show-Off**

The verse then widens from the spoiling believer to the corrupted giver outright: *like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day*. The riyyāʾ-giver — the spender li-ruʾāʾi'l-nās, to be seen of men — is the section's negative pole, and the verse diagnoses him with two coordinated failures. The first is the substitution of audience: the gift is performed for human eyes, which makes the eyes the paymaster and the seen-ness the wage. The second is the belief-structure that permits it: no faith in God, who would have been the actual recipient, and no faith in the Last Day, which would have been the actual payday. The commentators connect the portrait to the sūrah of Small Kindnesses — those who pray and are heedless of their prayer, who act to be seen and refuse even the small courtesies (107:4–7): the same riyyāʾ governing worship there and wealth here. The verse's comparison — *like those* — does a specific work for its believers-addressed audience: the reminder-giver is not the show-off, but he is *like* him, because the reminder reveals the same appetite — the gift completed for God's sake cannot need its echo among people, and the giver who needs the echo has quietly changed his paymaster.

**The Rock and the Rain**

The parable is geological: *a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain — leaving it just a bare stone*. The commentators read the image's three elements against the giver it depicts. The ṣafwān — the smooth, hard stone — is the heart on which nothing grows: not evil soil but no soil, the surface that cannot receive a seed. The dust upon it is the deed's appearance: a film that looks, from a distance, like ground — the show-off's charity has the color of generosity and covers nothing. And the downpour is the test: the same rain that doubles the fertile garden's yield (2:265) strips the dust from the stone, and the stripping is not an attack but an exposure — after the rain, the rock is what it always was. The commentators read the downpour variously as the Day of Judgment, as death, as the reckoning's scrutiny; every reading keeps the parable's mechanics: the rain does not create the barrenness but reveals it. The show-off's charity and the reminder-giver's charity are one substance — dust on stone — and the verse's weather is coming for both.

**No Power Over What They Earned**

The sentence after the parable states the loss in the ledger's own terms: *such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity* — they have no power over anything of what they earned. The verb kāsabū, earned, is the Qur'an's word for the deed-as-acquisition, and the verse's claim is that the acquisition has slipped their hands: what looked like an asset deposited by the gift was never held. The commentators connect the clause to the section's custody-theology from two verses earlier — the un-reminding givers have their reward *with their Lord* (2:262), a deposit in a keeping nothing can reach; the show-offs chose a different custodian, the audience, and the audience's memory is the dust on the rock: present while it is watched, gone at the first real weather. The clause also answers the giver's own arithmetic: the riyyāʾ-giver did earn something — the seen-ness, the praise, the standing — and the verse concedes the earning precisely to void its preservation: everything collected in the world's currency stays in the world's currency, and the Day is a jurisdiction where that currency is not legal. The power they lack is the power to carry anything across.

**No Guidance for Such People**

The verse's final clause is its heaviest: *Allah does not guide disbelieving people*. The non-guidance echoes the king's dumbfoundment two scenes earlier (2:258) — the same withheld guidance, the same ẓulm-shaped cause — and its application here to the giver rather than the arguer extends the verse's diagnosis into the ethics of wealth: the show-off's disbelief is not a doctrinal position but a structure of the heart, the audience-instead-of-God that functions, in the verse's judgment, as kufr whatever the tongue professes. The commentators read the clause against the believers-addressed opening deliberately: the command not to annul was given to believers because the annulment-pattern, followed far enough, is the disbeliever's pattern — the reminder, the injury, the performance, the dust-deep faith that survives no weather. Guidance is named at the verse's end as the thing at stake in the section's smallest transactions: how a gift is closed, what is said at the door, who the giver imagines watching — these are not manners but orientation, and the orientation is either toward the One who sees in secret or toward the eyes the rain will wash away.

**The Parable-Pair**

This verse and the next form a matched diptych the commentators treat as one teaching in two images: the same rain falls on both grounds. The downpour that strips the rock doubles the garden's fruit; the identical weather of trial, reckoning, and time produces, on the two substances, exposure or multiplication — which locates the entire difference in what was there before the rain. The section's legislation between the parables (the honorable word, the forgiveness, the secret giving, the good grain) is the agriculture of the heart's soil: the practices that deepen it. And the diptych's addressees are not two populations but one: the believer commanded not to annul his charity carries, in every gift, a little dust and a little ground, and the verse's warning is that the rain will report the ratio. The show-off is held up not as another kind of person but as the same person fully dust — the giver whose every act was surface — and the bare stone is what the section asks every reader to check his own ground against, before the weather does.
