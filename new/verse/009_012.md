## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:12

> But if they break their oaths after making a pledge and attack your faith, then fight the champions of disbelief—who never honour their oaths—so perhaps they will desist.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Breach Can Become an Organized Threat**

Verse 12 addresses a more advanced stage of betrayal. The hostile party has not merely failed to keep a promise; it has broken oaths after making a binding pledge and has attacked the religion of the believers. The two features belong together in the verse. A political breach becomes especially dangerous when it is joined to a campaign aimed at weakening the community's faith and resolve.

The Arabic verb for breaking an oath can evoke the untying of a firmly twisted cord. That picture explains the moral force of the verse. An oath is a bond deliberately strengthened through words. To break it after confirming it is to pull apart something one had just claimed to secure. The addition of attacking the religion shows that the breach is not an accidental failure or a private dispute. It has become an active effort against the community's identity and public order.

**Why the Leaders Are Named**

The command directs attention to the champions or leaders of disbelief. This wording matters. It identifies the people who organize, finance, and direct hostility rather than treating every member of the opposing population as equally responsible. Qur'anic narratives often describe the leading figures of a people as the ones who turn rejection into a social program. Leadership is named because leadership has consequences.

Classical commentators differed over whether the verse's command is restricted to the leaders or extends to the forces acting under them. Their disagreement reflects two concerns. One is that an organized threat cannot be ended by ignoring its directors. The other is that accountability should not be expanded carelessly to those who did not plan the breach. The verse's focus at least establishes a priority: confront the people whose authority keeps the conflict alive, rather than treating every follower as though he authored the policy.

**“They Have No Oaths” Describes the Consequence**

The phrase that they have no oaths does not mean that the written words never existed. It means that their own conduct has stripped those words of protective force. A party cannot invoke an oath as a shield after deliberately destroying the oath's conditions. The legal consequence follows from the breach; it is not an arbitrary cancellation imposed upon a faithful partner.

This principle is familiar in ordinary contracts. A person who repudiates the central promise of an agreement may lose the benefit he expected from the other side's performance. The Qur'anic verse places that principle in a setting where the failed promise also exposes a community to danger. The point is not that every minor disagreement dissolves a treaty. The point is that a decisive breach, joined to hostility, changes the status of the relationship.

**Attack on Faith Is Not Mere Offense**

The phrase attack your faith can be misunderstood if it is reduced to personal hurt feelings. The verse is addressing an organized political situation. Its language concerns leaders who have already broken their oaths and are then attacking the religion. The broader Qur'anic record shows that the Prophet endured insults and accusations without turning every insult into a military case. The decisive issue is the use of speech and influence to generate hostility, incite violence, or destabilize the covenant community.

This is why jurists disagreed over cases involving treaty holders who insulted Islam or the Prophet. Some held that a grave insult joined to breach removed protection; Abū Ḥanīfah's reasoning, as reported in the commentaries, distinguished disbelief as a belief from public speech that threatened political peace. The disagreement shows that the legal tradition did not simply treat every offensive word as the same act. It asked what the speech did within the treaty and whether it formed part of an aggressive campaign.

**The Stated Aim Is Desistance**

The verse ends with perhaps they will desist. This purpose clause limits the command by naming the desired outcome. The goal is cessation of the breach and attack, not endless punishment. If the hostile leadership stops, the reason for fighting has been removed. The word perhaps does not express uncertainty about Allah's knowledge; it describes the community's action as aimed at producing a different behavior in the opponent.

That aim is important for judging conduct in war. Force that exists only to satisfy anger has moved beyond the verse. Force intended to stop a concrete aggression may be lawful within its limits. The end condition must remain visible throughout the conflict. A community that forgets why it began fighting will eventually treat the continuation of fighting as its own justification.

**Leadership, Accountability, and Restraint**

The verse offers a framework for public accountability today. When an institution violates an agreement and uses its platform to recruit hostility, identify the decision-makers, document the breach, and seek the cessation of the harmful policy. Do not punish people merely because they belong to the same population. Do not excuse the leaders because the machinery of harm is complex. Responsibility follows influence.

For believers, the verse also warns against using religious defense as a cover for personal revenge. The Prophet's endurance under insult, the requirement of an actual breach, and the command that the aggressors desist all restrain the believer. Defending faith may require firmness, but firmness is not measured by how much anger is displayed. It is measured by whether the response is truthful, proportionate, and directed toward ending harm. The verse names champions of disbelief so that zeal does not become a substitute for judgment. Its severity is therefore controlled by its reason and its stated end.
