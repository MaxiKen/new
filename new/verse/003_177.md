## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:177

> Those who trade belief for disbelief will never harm Allah in the least, and they will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Image of a Trade**

The verse describes those who have purchased disbelief at the price of belief, and it states that they will not harm God and that a painful punishment awaits them. The commercial image is one the Book uses frequently for moral choice.

The commentators drew attention to the pattern. One sūrah describes people who trade guidance for misguidance and observes that the trade is profitless. Another describes the same exchange and adds that the traders are persistent in pursuing the fire. A third, on the same image, describes those who purchase the present life at the price of the next. The Book's use of the market as a picture of a person's decisions is one of its characteristic ways of explaining consequences.


The verse's exchange is between belief and disbelief, and the order of the words is important. What the group had is named as belief, and what they took in its place is named as disbelief.

The Book's descriptions of the same transaction usually mention what was given up in the process: guidance, forgiveness, the next life. The sentence in this verse is brief, and the brevity makes the loss clear without adding anything. What was given up was the more valuable of the two, and the sentence assumes that any reader knows which is which.

**The Profitlessness of the Exchange**

The Book repeatedly states that the trade does not profit those who make it, and the earlier sūrah says as much in a single phrase. The verse before the reader does not use the word for profit, but the sentence about their punishment states the same outcome in a different register.

The commercial language matters because it treats belief as something a person possesses and may dispose of badly. The Book does not present faith as an external label that can be worn or set aside without cost; it presents it as property whose exchange has consequences, and the market image gives the reader a familiar way to weigh what happened.

**Who the Traders Were**

The commentators applied the verse to the People of the Book who knew the truth and turned from it, and to the groups who had received clear evidence and preferred something else. The sūrah about the Repentance uses the same image and speaks of those who chose a fleeting gain over God's revelations.

The hypocrites and the Makkan idolaters are included in the same category by the commentators, since all of them gave up a claim for an advantage they thought would last. The verse's list of punishments is stated in general terms, which allows it to be applied wherever the exchange occurs. The sūrah's own audience had seen the pattern in individuals who had been close to the message and had turned away from it.

**The Repetition About Harm**

The verse states again that the group does not harm God, and the repetition in consecutive verses gives the point its weight. The sentence is not a description of the group's power but a statement about the limits of what any created being can affect.

The Book uses the same construction in the sūrah's earlier passage about those who turn on their heels, and it states in the sūrah of the Repentance that whoever withholds from giving harms only himself. The community that recites these sentences is being taught where the damage from a person's defection lands, and the answer is always the same.

**The Punishment Named**

The verse describes the outcome for the group as a painful punishment, and the term is the one the Book applies to those who knowingly exchange the greater for the smaller. The sūrah of the Bees makes the exception of a person who is compelled to disbelieve while his heart remains firm in faith, and that exception marks how deliberate the conduct in this verse is.

The word describes the pain of the outcome rather than its duration, and the Book uses it in passages that deal with conduct chosen with knowledge. The verse before the reader therefore places the group in the class of those who acted deliberately, and the punishment named suits that description.

**The Fleeting Gain in the Book's Language**

The Book describes the advantage that people take in exchange for their faith with the word for something passing, and it uses the phrase in its warnings about the traders. The gain is described as brief in itself, and the reader is left to compare the two parties to the exchange.

The sūrah's readers had watched the community's own members make calculations of this kind during the campaign. The verse states the accounting in plain terms, and its placement after the instruction not to grieve gives the Prophet the confirmation that the conduct of the traders carried its own cost. The gain they took is described in the Book as fleeting in the passages that use this image, and the description leaves the reader to compare the two sides of the exchange.

**The Verse's Place After the Consolation**

The verse restates the sentence that preceded it and adds the punishment, and the repetition is the Book's way of confirming a matter for a person who is grieving. The instruction not to grieve is followed by an explanation of why the grief was unnecessary.

The community that reads the two verses together is given the same lesson twice, once in the vocabulary of harm and once in the vocabulary of trade. The next verse turns to the question that the sight of unpunished deniers raises, and the sūrah answers it by describing what a long life can be for those who use it badly.
