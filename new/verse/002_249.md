## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:249

> When Saul marched forth with his army, he cautioned: "Allah will test you with a river. So whoever drinks ˹his fill˺ from it is not with me, and whoever does not taste it—except a sip from the hollow of his hands—is definitely with me." They all drank ˹their fill˺ except for a few! When he and the ˹remaining˺ faithful with him crossed the river, they said, "Now we are no match for Goliath and his warriors." But those ˹believers˺ who were certain they would meet Allah reasoned, "How many times has a small force vanquished a mighty army by the Will of Allah! And Allah is ˹always˺ with the steadfast."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Test Announced Before the River**

The army marches, and the king's first act of command is to publish the coming test: *Allah will test you with a river*. The announcement itself is part of the test's design, and it marks the difference from every comparable trial in scripture — the men are told, in advance, exactly what will be asked of them and exactly what the asking means. The terms are stated with legal precision: whoever drinks his fill from it *is not with me* — the full drink severs the soldier from the army; whoever does not taste it *is definitely with me* — total abstention confirms belonging; and between the two, the concession — *except a sip from the hollow of his hands*, the single cupped handful permitted to the thirsty. The commentators note the concession's function: it distinguishes need from indulgence. The soldier who takes the handful acknowledges thirst and stops; the soldier who drinks his fill obeys it. The test measures not thirst but the thirst's governance — and the army knows, before reaching the water, that the water is not water.

**The Sifting at the Crossing**

The result is stated in the verse's shortest and most devastating sentence: *They all drank their fill except for a few!* The exclamation belongs to the revelation's own voice, and the numbers invert every expectation the chiefs' council began with: the army that marched out — the narrations describe it as large, and the tradition's numbers run into the tens of thousands — arrives at the far bank as a remnant. The verse's structure makes the river, not the enemy, the campaign's decisive engagement: Goliath's host has not yet appeared, and already the army has lost the overwhelming majority of its strength to a drink of water. The exegetes draw the sifting's lesson for the story's whole sequence — the chiefs who pledged and fled, the objectors who demanded a sign, the sign that came: every stage has separated the said from the done, and the river separates them finally and physically, with belonging to the army defined by a mouthful. The few who crossed with their king are the community the remainder of the narrative belongs to.

**Gideon's Water, Saul's Water**

The commentators set this scene beside its scriptural twin: Gideon's army at the water in the book of Judges (7:4–7), reduced by a drinking-test to three hundred men. The parallels are typological rather than historical — Gideon lived generations before Saul — and the differences are as structured as the similarities. Gideon's test was known only to Gideon: the soldiers were not told they were being sorted, and the criterion (lapping from the hand versus kneeling to drink) was applied after the fact, its meaning explained by God alone. Saul's test is proclaimed to the whole army, its criterion stated as a command, its consequence named as belonging or exclusion. Both tests reduce an army to a few so that the victory's attribution will be unambiguous — Judges states the purpose explicitly, that Israel might not boast against me, saying, my own hand has saved me — and both echo the Qur'an's wider water-motif, the parted sea of the exodus and the Jordan crossed before Jericho: the waters as the threshold where God's people are separated and reconstituted. The reenactment-reading follows naturally: sacred history repeating its forms, the river-test as a recurring station on the road God's armies travel.

**Two Voices on the Far Bank**

The crossed remnant divides once more, this time by speech. The first voice: *Now we are no match for Goliath and his warriors* — the arithmetic of the visible, conducted by men who have just passed a divine test and draw the wrong conclusion from it. The second voice belongs to *those who were certain they would meet Allah*, and the commentators define the certainty two ways that converge: certainty of the Hereafter — the meeting named in the verse as an appointment, not a possibility — and readiness for death, the soldier's state when the meeting is expected rather than feared. Their reasoning is the verse's famous maxim: *How many times has a small force vanquished a mighty army by the Will of Allah!* The grammar of the maxim is its argument — kam, how many, introducing not a hope but a record: the small-over-large is not an exception to be prayed for but a pattern to be counted, and every instance in the pattern carries the same attribution, bi-idhn Allāh, by God's leave. The two voices together state the campaign's real front: Goliath across the field, arithmetic inside the camp — and the second voice defeats the first before either reaches the enemy.

**The Company of the Certain**

The verse's designation of the second group — *those who were certain they would meet Allah* — is the story's theological center, and it explains the maxim's power. Certainty (yaqīn) about the meeting reorders the soldier's arithmetic completely: if death is an appointment kept with God, then the numerical disparity between two armies concerns only the manner of the meeting, not its fact or its outcome. The few who drank nothing at the river had already demonstrated the same ordering — thirst governed, appetite deferred, belonging chosen over comfort — and the maxim is that governance extended from the mouth to the mind. Al-Qurṭubī connects the certainty-clause to the fleeing thousands of the sūrah's earlier scene (2:243): those fled death and met it; these expect death and thereby meet God, and the difference between the two multitudes is the verse's whole instruction. The remnant that survives both sifts — the river's and the arithmetic's — is defined not by strength but by a settled relation to its own end, which is why so few have it and why the campaign needs so few.

**Allah Is with the Steadfast**

The maxim closes with the companionship-claim: *And Allah is always with the steadfast* — maʿiyyah, divine withness, attached to ṣabr. The formula is the sūrah's own standing promise, announced in its patience-verses — seek help through patience and prayer, for God is with the patient (2:153) — and its repetition here, in the mouth of the certain few, shows the promise functioning as doctrine under fire: not a comfort quoted after the battle but a premise used inside the arithmetic. The companionship is what makes the small-over-large record explicable — the mighty army fights alone in the sense that matters, and the small force does not fight at all alone — and it converts the maxim from battlefield folklore into theology. The verse's final word-order leaves the army on the far bank with the claim spoken: the river has sifted the indulgent, the maxim has answered the counters, and the companionship of God has been declared over what remains. What remains is very small, very certain, and — as the next moment will show — at prayer.
