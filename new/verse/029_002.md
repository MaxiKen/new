## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:2

> Do people think once they say, “We believe,” that they will be left without being put to the test?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Framed So That the Answer Is No**

The opening word is an interrogative of rebuke, not of inquiry. Arabic uses this pattern when a supposition has been formed without evidence and the speaker intends to expose it, so the sentence is a verdict dressed as a question. Its subject is *al-nās*, people at large, rather than believers or disbelievers named apart. Nobody who files the claim is exempt. The verb *ḥasiba* means to reckon something to be so, and the Book reserves it for calculations that flatter the one making them. Two claims are placed side by side in the sentence: that we have believed, and that this settles the matter. The verse does not deny the first claim; it strikes down the inference drawn from it. A declaration of faith is a statement about conduct that has not yet happened. To be left alone, in the sense the verse rejects, would be to be abandoned to rest, and no one who makes the claim is left there, because the examination begins the moment the claim is made.

**Fitna: The Word for Assaying Metal**

The Arabic root *f-t-n* carries the memory of the furnace. In its earliest attested sense it describes the assaying of gold and silver: the metal is placed in fire until the alloy separates from the pure. The word then travelled from the workshop into moral life, where it means any pressure that shows what a person is made of — trial, sedition, temptation, the pull that tests a loyalty. Every shade of that range is present when the Book asks whether people will go untried. The image behind the question is not a courtroom but a crucible. Fire is applied by the metalworker for a purpose, in a controlled way, by a hand that then takes the metal out. A sūrah that uses this word is announcing that hardship has an intelligent cause and a limited term, and that the alternative to refining is scrap rather than safety. Being left untested is not protection; it is neglect.

**The Doorway al-Tustarī Described**

The early ascetic al-Tustarī is reported to have said that affliction is a doorway between those who know God and God. The image is not of an obstacle to be resented but of an opening through which entry happens, and the report matches the shape of the verse it explains. Knowledge of God that has been paid for in patience has a different quality from knowledge held cheaply. The Prophet described the same law of proportion when he was asked which people are most severely tried: the prophets, then those nearest to them in rank, then the next best, and a person is tested according to his religion, so that trials continue until he walks the earth with no sin upon him (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2398, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī). The report establishes a scale. Severity follows rank, which reverses the assumption behind the verse's question: the untested life is not the privileged life, and hardship is a sign of terms being taken seriously.

**Two Reports About One Complaint**

Commentators connect the verse to concrete grievances. In one report it was revealed about Mihjaʿ, a man from the entourage of ʿUmar, killed by ʿĀmir ibn al-Ḥaḍramī and remembered as the first Muslim martyr at Badr; his wife and parents were shattered by his death, and the verses came down explaining that death in the path of God was not the evidence of abandonment it appeared to be. Muqātil is the authority behind that account. In a second report, the complaint came from Muslims in Makkah who had failed to leave for Madinah, had been stopped by the idolaters when they tried, and were answered with these words before the tenth verse of the chapter descended. The wording stays general while the occasions stay particular, and that is how the chapter should be read: the reports show what the sentence meant to the people who first heard it, not the boundary of whom it covers.

**What the Verse Refuses to Say**

Careful reading is as much about the claims absent from a sentence as the ones present in it. Nothing here says God needs the information a trial would produce, and nothing says the outcome is in doubt; such questions belong to the verse that follows. Nothing says suffering is punishment. Nothing exempts the sincere, and nothing accuses the claimant of fraud — the error identified is a mistaken inference, not a lie. The Book elsewhere states the same principle in the language of purpose rather than rebuke: God created death and life in order to test which of people is best in deeds (67:2), and loss of life, wealth, and food are named as the instruments (2:155), attached to a promise of good news for those who hold firm (2:155). The question in this verse, then, is narrow and severe. It asks only whether a person expects to buy the destination without the road.

**The Congregation That Could Not Leave**

For the first Muslims in Makkah the test was not a figure of speech. A believer might be isolated from family, watched in his trade, and prevented from joining the community gathering in Madinah, and the Book treats the failure to emigrate as a grave matter: those who wronged themselves are asked by the angels what they were doing, and the only excuse accepted is genuine helplessness, with a promise of pardon for the truly oppressed (4:97–99). The same emigrants are named later in the Book among the forgiven: those who left their homes after being compelled, then struggled and persevered, have a Lord who is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful (16:110). Taken together, the two passages frame the test as a real cost with a real remedy. The claim to faith made in Makkah was measured by what a person was prepared to lose in the open, and by whether he would try again after failing.

**A Declaration Is a Commitment, Not a Certificate**

The sentence under discussion is the shortest possible creed, and its brevity is exactly why the question follows. A certificate is issued after inspection; a commitment is issued before it and is tested afterwards. The Book treats *īmān* as the second kind of statement, which is why the test is inseparable from the claim instead of imposed on it. Once this is understood, the elements of the verse lock into place: the question is a rebuke because the supposition cannot be reasoned from the claim; the vocabulary of smelting fixes the purpose of the pressure; the reports fix the first audience and their particular losses; and the refusal to name a limit on the trial keeps the principle alive for every later claimant. The confidence that saying the words finishes the business is the exact target of the verse, and the chapter will spend sixty-seven more verses dismantling it.

**The Same Rule Stated to the First Community**

The claim that believers must be proven before they are rewarded is not new with this chapter. The Book puts the question directly to the community of Madinah: did they suppose they would enter the Garden while God had not yet distinguished those who struggled and those who held firm (3:142), and it repeats the challenge in the account of the wilderness generation, where the shaking reached even the Messenger and those with him before help arrived (2:214). The three passages differ in audience and vocabulary while making one demand. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt states the rule at the level of the individual claim, 3:142 states it at the level of the community, and 2:214 states it at the level of history. Placed together, they make it impossible to read the verses about trial as a lesson aimed only at the first generation, since the Book deliberately repeats the demand to each new audience that assumes the matter is settled.
