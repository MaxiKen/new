## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:7

> He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive. Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah. As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Book Whose Verses Are Not All of One Kind**

The verse distinguishes two categories. *Muḥkam* comes from a root that means to curb, to judge, and to make firm; a well-built structure is called *muḥkam*, and so is a knot that will not slip. *Mutashābih* comes from resemblance: things that look alike, that answer to more than one reading, where the meaning does not close the door behind it.

That is the whole of the classification as this verse states it. The precise verses are called the foundation of the Book, *umm al-kitāb*, the mother of it, and their function is to hold the rest in place; the elusive ones are the material that a skilled reader can turn this way and that. What belongs to each category is not listed here — the commentators supply their own lists, and the lists differ. Everyone agrees on the principle and few agree on the borders, which is itself a fact about the verse.

**Both Descriptions Apply to the Whole Book**

The Book is not divided into two libraries. Elsewhere it describes itself in terms that overlap the two labels: *“˹This is˺ a Book whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained”* (11:1), where the root is the same as *muḥkam*, and *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons”* (39:23), where the word is the same as *mutashābih*.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī both record the consequence: the entire Book can be called precise in that it is true, firm, and from God, and elusive in that its parts resemble one another in beauty, confirm one another, and carry depth beneath a plain surface. The distinction in this verse is therefore about the reader's task, not about two grades of scripture. Some passages present their meaning so directly that they can judge everything else; others require that standard to be brought to them.

**The Deviant Heart and the Use It Makes of Ambiguity**

The charge is not that some people read difficult passages. It is that they *follow* them — the verb implies pursuit, choosing the loose stones out of a wall while ignoring the beams. Their aim is named twice: seeking *fitnah*, trouble and discord, and seeking an interpretation of their own.

The grammar exposes the method. Ambiguity is not dangerous in itself, but it is useful to anyone who wants a particular conclusion badly enough to select the passages that permit it. The Qur'an notes elsewhere that God uses such tests deliberately: a parable or a number in the text becomes a dividing line, *“In this way Allah leaves whoever He wills to stray and guides whoever He wills.”* (74:31), and the same function is attached to the smallest of the parables: *“Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many.”* (2:26).

The deviant heart does not deny that the Book says what it says. It decides in advance which parts of it are allowed to be discussed.

**A Criterion for Telling the Two Kinds of Reader Apart**

The Prophet gave his community a way to recognise the method rather than the person. ʿĀʾishah reports that he recited this verse and then said: *If you see those who follow what is elusive in it, they are the ones God has named, so beware of them* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4547).

The instruction is practical to the point of being uncomfortable, because it does not ask for a verdict on someone's sincerity. It asks what he prefers to argue from. A reader whose first move is always to find the verse that can be turned, and whose interest in the plain commands is thin, has already identified himself in the way he works. The criterion is public, observable, and independent of the reputation of the person being judged — which is exactly what a criterion needs to be if a community is going to use it without becoming a tribunal of intentions.

**Where the Sentence Stops: An Old Disagreement About This Very Line**

The words "none grasps their full meaning except Allah" can be read as the end of a sentence, with the next words beginning a fresh one: *and those well-grounded in knowledge say, "We believe in it."* On that reading, the interpretation of the elusive passages belongs to God alone, and the scholars' contribution is faith. The other reading joins the two halves: the well-grounded know the meaning too, and their words of faith follow as their response to it.

Each position has a reason. The first preserves the verse's own warning: if even the skilled can assign meanings to the elusive, the door the deviant heart walks through is left ajar, and the scholars keep the field. The second rests on the sentence that follows, which has the well-grounded speaking with knowledge in their possession rather than confessing ignorance.

Neither reading changes the practical demand. On both, the well-grounded are described as people who accept the whole Book.

**"We Believe in It; It Is All From Our Lord"**

The answer given by the well-grounded is not an interpretation. It is a statement about the source: everything in the Book, the plain and the difficult, comes from the same Lord, so the difficult passages are not a reason to distrust the plain ones, and the plain ones are not a reason to discard the difficult.

That is a posture, not a technique, and the verse closes by naming the faculty it requires: *none will be mindful of this except people of reason*. The Qur'an repeats the phrase at 2:269 and 13:19 in connection with wisdom and with recognising revealed truth. Reason in this vocabulary is not the instrument that audits God's Book until it agrees with itself. It is the mind that understands what it has been given and, having understood, accepts it whole — a mind whose submission is itself an act of intelligence. Verse 8 will give the words such people say next.
