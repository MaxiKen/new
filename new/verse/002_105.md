## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:105

> The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want you to receive any blessing from your Lord, but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Attitude Described**

The verse states that the two groups outside the faith did not want the believers to receive anything good from God, and it names them both: the disbelievers from among the People of the Book and the polytheists. The sentence describes a disposition rather than an act, and the disposition is the one the passage has been documenting in the form of conduct.

The sūrah has already named the motive behind the rejection of the revelation as envy that God's grace had been given to another people (2:90). This verse returns to the same subject from the perspective of the believers, and it describes what their position looks like from those who declined the message. What the verse reports is a state of mind, and the passage has been building a portrait of that state through the conduct it describes. The verse states the wish behind the conduct so that the reader sees the conduct as the expression of something settled.

**The Blessing in View**

Commentators understood the blessing mentioned here as the revelation itself and the wisdom that came with it, since the verses immediately before and after concern the sending down of clear revelations and the guidance they carry. The gift the two groups resented was therefore not a material advantage but the prophethood granted to Muhammad ﷺ.

That reading ties the sentence to the verse about the angels, where the objection to Gabriel was traced to a preference about who should carry the revelation to whom. The Book identifies the resentment as resistance to God's choice of a recipient, and it presents that resistance as the common ground between two groups that were otherwise opposed to each other. The reading ties the sentence to the verse about the angels, where an objection about who carried the revelation was traced to a preference about who should receive it. The Book identifies the resentment as resistance to God's choice of a recipient.

**Allah Selects Whom He Wills**

The middle clause restates a principle the sūrah has used already, and its repetition in this passage is deliberate. Divine favour is not distributed according to a community's merits or a people's lineage; it is granted by God to whomever He chooses, and the statement is made without an explanation that would satisfy the objecting parties.

The Book uses the same formula when it describes the granting of prophecy, of mercy, and of the ability to receive guidance, and it applies the formula to believers and communities alike. The sūrah's opponents are not answered with an argument about who deserved the gift; they are answered with a statement about who decides. The same wording appears wherever the Book speaks of prophecy, of mercy, and of the capacity to accept guidance, and it is addressed to communities as well as to individuals. The sūrah's opponents are not answered with an argument about who deserved the gift.

**Lord of Infinite Bounty**

The closing attribute describes the abundance of God's provision, and it is attached to the statement about selection in order to show that the grace given to one people does not diminish what is available to another. The Book's understanding of divine bounty is not a fixed quantity distributed among claimants, and the resentment described in this verse rests on a premise the attribute denies.

The sūrah uses this name for God in other places where it describes gifts given without measure, and it attaches the same attribute to the guidance sent down to the believers. The sentence therefore ends with a claim about God's generosity that makes the envy described in its first clause both groundless and revealing. The Book's understanding of divine bounty is not a fixed quantity distributed among claimants.

**Envy as a Spiritual Condition**

The verse treats envy as a matter of the heart before it becomes a matter of conduct, and the Book elsewhere warns its own community against the same sickness. The Prophet ﷺ described the envy that is permitted and the envy that is not, defining the acceptable form as a wish to have what another has been given in order to do good with it, which is the opposite of wishing the blessing away.

The distinction is important for the reader, because the verse's first clause could be mistaken for a general judgment on two communities. What it describes is a particular wish, and the Book's treatment of envy as a disease of the heart applies to believers as well. The command to the Muslims later in this sūrah not to envy what others have been given belongs to the same teaching.

**The Believers' Position**

The sentence is addressed to the believers as an explanation of hostility they had encountered, and it assures them that the opposition was not evidence of a defect in what they had received. The Book often pairs its instructions with accounts of opposition, and the pairing serves to steady a community that is being resisted.

What the verse supplies is a perspective rather than a policy. The response to the attitude it describes is set out in the following verses, in the commands to prayer and almsgiving and in the reminder that whatever good the believers send ahead will be found with God. The closing attributes of the two verses stand side by side: the Lord of bounty on one side, and the All-Seeing who records what is done on the other.

**Why Envy Is Diagnosed in the Heart**

The verse locates the problem in a wish, and the Book's treatment of envy elsewhere shows why that diagnosis matters. A person who resents another's gift has already decided that the gift was misdirected, and from that position arguments become instruments rather than inquiries. The sūrah has already recorded the community's objection about the angel who carried the revelation, and its observation about the angel covered a complaint about the people who received it. Naming the wish exposes what the argument was for. The Book does not leave the diagnosis with the opponents, since it warns the believers against the same condition and describes the acceptable form of envy as the wish to have what another has in order to use it well. What the passage establishes is that a community's reaction to another's blessing is a test of its own relation with God.
