## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:105

> The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want you to receive any blessing from your Lord, but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Coalition of One Negative Wish**

The verse begins with a survey of the opposition, and the survey's finding is remarkable for its unanimity: *the disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists would not want you to receive any blessing from your Lord*. Two camps that agree on almost nothing — the scriptural monotheists of Madina and the idolatrous Arabs of Makkah, whose mutual relation the recital has already documented as warfare and contempt — converge on a single desire, and the desire is negative: not that they possess some good, but that the Muslims lack it. The verse's construction puts the negation first: what unites the opposition is not a shared creed but a shared withholding, the wish that no good descend on this community. The analysis matters because it diagnoses opposition at its root. Doctrinal conflict can be argued; interest-conflict can be negotiated; but a will organized around another's deprivation is not reachable by either means, because the Muslims' gain is precisely what it refuses. The recital has watched this refusal operate at every station: the private counsel managing what the believers might be told (76), the ransom-paradox funding captives while expelling neighbors (85), the resentment at grace's freedom (90), the wish to reverse belief itself (109, ahead in the file's own unfolding). Verse 105 states the common denominator behind all of them, and in stating it, frees the community's strategy: against a coalition whose bond is your deprivation, the response cannot be appeasement, because your flourishing is the thing the coalition exists to refuse.

**“Any Blessing from Your Lord”**

The object of the withheld wish is defined with a phrase whose every term works. *Any blessing* — *khayr* in its widest sense: every good, without specification, which makes the wish's breadth total — not opposition to particular Muslim advantages but to the category of Muslim advantage. *From your Lord* — the source named as the Muslims' own Lord, in the possessive form the recital uses for intimacy: the good that is begrudged is not wrested from a rival but descending from a Patron, which relocates the conflict entirely — the coalition's real quarrel is with the descent's Source. And the exegetes specify the blessing's primary referent in this context: the revelation itself and its wisdom, bestowed through the prophethood of Muhammad ﷺ — the mercy the community actually received, which is why the verse's second clause answers in the same terms. The specification exposes the wish's futility in advance. A blessing that descends from a Lord is not a commodity in circulation that competitors can corner or blockade; it is a bestowal, and bestowals are governed solely by the bestower's will. The coalition's wish is therefore not a strategy but a posture — the stance of people who believe, against the structure of the case, that the flow of divine good is subject to their consent. The verse lets the posture stand for one clause, then dismantles it in the next.

**Selection by Mercy, Not by Lobbying**

The answer arrives as the verse's theological center: *but Allah selects whoever He wills for His mercy*. The verb carries the sense of singling out — choosing one from among many for a specific bestowal — and its subject is Allah alone. The commentators connect the singling to the occasion directly: the mercy in view is the prophetic office given to Muhammad ﷺ rather than to a candidate more agreeable to the idolaters or to the People of the Book. The doctrine is the recital's running answer to every objection of lineage and preference this passage has filed — the resentment at grace descending where it willed (90), the objection to the courier that masked objection to the sender's choice (97) — and the Qur'an states it as a standing principle of revelation wherever it describes descent: He sends down the angels with the revelation *to whoever He wills of His servants* (16:2); He *chooses whoever He wills as a messenger* (3:179). The selection's ground is mercy, not merit-claims: the verse does not say Allah selects the most deserving by any human ledger, but that He singles out for *His mercy* — the choice originating in the chooser's nature rather than in the chosen's résumé. This is why the coalition's wish is not merely futile but category-mistaken: lobbying, pressure, and lineage-arguments have no purchase on a selection whose criterion is the Selector's mercy. The verse's comfort to the chosen community is equally precise: your standing is not held by the tolerance of your opponents, who do not tolerate you, but by a selection whose author does not consult them.

**The Lord of Infinite Bounty**

The closing name answers the withholding with its opposite in divine terms: *Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty* — *dhū al-faḍl al-ʿaẓīm*, the Possessor of bounty great beyond measure. The name's placement is the verse's final argument. The coalition's posture is scarcity: good imagined as a limited stock, descending on one group only at another's expense, worth blocking because blocking seems possible. The divine name is abundance: faḍl is grace in overflow, good given beyond desert and beyond calculation, and its possession by Allah means the supply is not a stock but a spring. Against the begrudging wish, the verse sets not a larger treasury but a different kind of giving — bounty that loses nothing by descending, that is not partitioned by recipients, that the wish of withholders does not shrink by a single measure. The sūrah's economics completes another circuit here: the trades of this passage — signs sold cheap, souls sold for magic, the Hereafter sold for the world — were all scarcity-logic, acts of parties who believed value was fixed and had to be seized. The bounty-name is the counter-economics: the Giver's abundance makes seizure irrational and withholding futile, and the community addressed is being trained in both directions at once — never to join the coalition of the begrudging, and never to fear it. Whatever the opposition wishes against the believers' blessing, the blessing's Source is described, at the verse's end, by the one attribute that makes every wish of scarcity void.

**The Instruction in Withholding's Mirror**

The verse instructs its community by showing it the posture it must not adopt, and the instruction is exact because the mirror is exact. The coalition's sin is not enmity in open combat — the recital legislates for conflict elsewhere and does not pretend it away — but the interior wish against another's good, the quiet refusal of descent. That wish is the seed-form of the passage's whole catalog: it grows into the counsel of verse 76, the rationing of verse 85, the resentment of verse 90. The Muslim community hearing the verse is being inoculated at the seed: whoever finds in himself the reluctance that another's blessing exists — a rival's success, a neighbor's rise, a peer's recognition — is holding the coalition's bond in his own hand, and the verse's second clause is the cure prescribed for it. Selection is Allah's; the distribution of mercy is not the creature's referendum. To accept the selection — in prophets, in communities, in the ordinary allotments of daily life — is to exit the coalition of withholders at its only real point of membership, which is the heart's consent. The verse thereby completes the recital's treatment of envy before the subject receives its full portrait later in the sūrah: here as a coalition's negative wish, diagnosed and answered by two names of divine freedom — the Selector of whoever He wills, and the Lord of bounty that no wish can dam.
