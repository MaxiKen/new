## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:55

> on the Day the punishment will overwhelm them from above them and from below their feet. And it will be said, “Reap what you sowed.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Encirclement Given a Shape**

The previous verse stated that the fire would encompass the disbelievers; this one describes the enclosure in spatial terms. Punishment arrives from above their heads and from beneath their feet, and no direction is left open. The Book uses the same construction elsewhere when it describes a torment that comes from above and from below, and the image belongs to its wider vocabulary for a situation that cannot be escaped by movement. The people addressed in this sūrah had spent its argument looking for exits through patrons, wealth, and demands for proof. The sentence gives them a picture of the moment when every exit is closed at once, and it does so without any description of fire or smoke. The geometry does the whole of the work.

**Why the Two Directions Are Named**

The Book's historical punishments in this chapter came from exactly these two directions, and the correspondence is not accidental. The towns were overturned and rained upon from above; Korah was swallowed from below; floods came up and storms came down. The verse therefore presents the final punishment as a summary of everything the reader has already witnessed in the sūrah's narratives, and it treats the earlier judgments as previews of a single event. A community that had read about stones from the sky and the opening of the earth now finds both directions occupied at once, and its own body standing between them.

The two directions also cover the two ways people imagine escape. One group plans to cross a border, and another counts on the solid ground beneath its feet; naming the sky and the earth removes both options without an argument, since the pair describes the whole of the space in which a person could hope to hide.

**"Reap What You Sowed"**

The sentence that closes the verse is a harvest saying, and the Book's use of agricultural imagery for the consequences of deeds appears throughout. The Qur'an states that every soul will be paid back for what it has done (45:22), and it addresses the people of the fire with the command to taste what they used to do in several places (32:14). What the harvest maxim adds is the interval between planting and reaping, which is precisely the interval the sūrah has been discussing in the mockery of its opponents. The disbelievers had asked for the punishment to be hurried, as though the harvest could be pulled forward by the laborers. The answer given here is that the field belongs to the owner, and the crop arrives when it is ripe.

**Who Delivers the Verdict**

The verse says that it will be said to them, without naming the speaker, and the anonymity is characteristic of the Book's descriptions of the Hereafter. The absence of an identified voice makes the statement a conclusion drawn by the scene itself, and the effect is to deprive the condemned of anyone to argue with. The sūrah has presented these people throughout as debaters: they questioned the messenger, demanded signs, and mocked the warning. At this point the conversation has one direction only. The tone of the Book's Hereafter scenes generally matches this: the disbelievers speak, but their speech consists of wishes, admissions, and quarrels with each other, and no one negotiates.

Placing the sentence in the passive leaves the reader's attention on the outcome rather than on the officer who announces it. The Book often handles the Hereafter that way, reporting verdicts without naming the announcing voice, and the effect is to present the result as part of the order of things rather than as the decision of a particular official.

**The Mockery Answered in Its Own Currency**

The people who receive this sentence are the same people who asked their question in mockery two verses earlier. The Book's response to them does not reproduce the mockery; it simply describes the arrival of what they demanded, and lets the reversal stand. This is one of the sūrah's consistent techniques. Lot's town asked for the punishment if the warning were true and received it during the night; the Meccans asked for stones and were told what would encompass them; the same request appears in several chapters with the same outcome. The Book does not punish the jeer with a jeer. It records the sentence the jeerers will hear, and the record is left in the reader's hands.

The answer also removes the audience's assumption that they are spectators. The earlier verses describe a challenge being made by a group, and this sentence describes the same group receiving what their challenge implied, so that a reader following the argument watches a rhetorical question turn into a verdict.

**The Finality of a Sentence With No Appeal**

The verse's position at the end of a passage about hastening judgment gives it a closing force. There is no mention of a second chance here, and no indication that the people addressed will respond or repent. The Book's warnings in this life are characteristically open, and its descriptions of the Hereafter are characteristically closed, and the difference between the two is one of the reasons its warnings still function. The reader who hears about a fire that encircles from above and below is being told what the alternative is to the door that remains open while he lives. The sūrah's next sentences turn immediately to that door, and they address the believers directly.

Nothing in the passage provides for a hearing, a delay, or a second round of argument, and that absence marks the difference between this sentence and the punishments described earlier in the sūrah. Towns were destroyed after warnings that lasted for generations; the sentence in these verses is delivered once and stands as the last word on the matter.

**The Sentence Heard by Everyone Standing There**

The words of this verse are addressed to the people who mocked the warning, but they are spoken aloud in the hearing of everyone present, and that public character belongs to the scene. The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter consistently include the condemned party's own admissions, and the effect of those admissions is to remove the possibility of a defence at the only moment when a defence would matter. The voice that says "reap what you sowed" is not answering an argument; it is describing a harvest in front of the people who planted the field. The sūrah's method of argument in this world has been to present evidence and let the audience decide, while the world to come operates by disclosure, and the verse marks the boundary between the two methods.

**Why the Punishment Is Described Rather Than Argued**

A reader may notice how little argument accompanies the warning in these final verses. The sūrah has spent the bulk of its length reasoning with its audience — pointing at creation, at ruins, at the spider's web, at the fate of five nations — and it now states outcomes without argument because the time for argument has passed within the scene described. The Book's structure in this chapter mirrors the structure of a life: reasoning while there is room for it, and a verdict once the room is gone. Readers who encounter the verse while still living are therefore in the position of the people who have not yet reached the harvest, and the sentence is offered to them in the only form that can still be useful, which is a warning rather than a report.
