## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:56

> This is how We established Joseph in the land to settle wherever he pleased. We shower Our mercy on whoever We will, and We never discount the reward of the good-doers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Thus" Ties This Verse to Verse 21**

The opening word is *kadhālika* — thus, in this manner — and the chapter has used it before about the same subject. When the boy was bought in Egypt, the narrator said: *“This is how We established Joseph in the land, so that We might teach him the interpretation of dreams”* (12:21). The identical formula now describes the same establishing, thirty-five verses later, with a different purpose attached: so that he might settle wherever he pleased. The first was the mechanism of an education; the second is its outcome. The repetition is not redundancy but a bracket. The chapter opens the Egyptian section with an establishing whose aim is knowledge and closes it with an establishing whose aim is freedom of movement, and the reader is left to connect the two.

**"To Settle Wherever He Pleased" Describes Real Authority**

The clause is spatial and it is political. A man who may settle wherever he wishes in a country is not merely housed well; he holds a position from which no province is closed to him. The commentators read it in exactly those terms — that Joseph could establish his home in any city of Egypt because his authority extended over all of them. The phrase therefore functions as a measure rather than a detail. It tells the reader the size of the office without naming a title, a salary or a rank, and it does so by reference to the one thing an administrator's power most visibly determines: where he can go and live without asking.

**Mercy Is Given, Not Calculated**

The middle clause interrupts the sequence with a general statement: *We send Our mercy upon whom We will.* It sits between the description of Joseph's establishment and the statement that God does not discount the reward of the good-doers, and its position is significant. Nothing in the chapter has presented Joseph's rise as the automatic output of virtue. He was sold for a few counted coins by people who set no store by him (12:20), beaten and imprisoned after being declared innocent inside a household (12:35), and forgotten by the one man who owed him a message (12:42). Each of those events delayed rather than advanced him. The clause names the missing variable: mercy, distributed according to a will that the verse does not explain and does not need to.

**"We Do Not Discount the Reward of the Good-Doers"**

The closing phrase is one the Qur'an returns to. The same assurance appears in a Meccan instruction to the Prophet ﷺ: *“And be patient! Certainly Allah does not discount the reward of the good-doers”* (11:115), and Joseph himself will use it at the end of this chapter, speaking to his brothers after the recognition: *“Surely whoever is mindful ˹of Allah˺ and patient, then certainly Allah never discounts the reward of the good-doers”* (12:90). The verb carries the sense of causing to be lost, letting a reward go to waste. The claim is that nothing good is wasted, including the years that produced no visible result. Joseph's imprisonment is the case in point: it produced nothing for him and everything for Egypt.

**Grace and Justice Held in a Single Sentence**

The verse contains two principles that could pull apart. Mercy falls on whom God wills, which is grace without condition. The reward of the good-doers is not discounted, which is justice with a promise attached. The chapter holds them side by side rather than choosing between them, and the placement tells the reader which is doing the work at this moment. Joseph's establishment is described as mercy first and as reward second. That order is the sūrah's theology in miniature: a person's goodness matters and is never wasted, but the arrival of the good outcome at the particular moment it arrived is not something goodness can compel. The verse refuses to let either principle absorb the other.

**The Tradition About the Chief Minister's Office**

The commentators add a report that when the Chief Minister — the ʿAzīz who had bought Joseph and whose household had imprisoned him — died, the king gave Joseph his former position over the treasury, and with it the title of ʿAzīz. The report is not in the Qur'anic text and cannot be verified from it, but it fits the chapter's method of closing loops. The man who said *“Take good care of him”* in verse 21 is succeeded by the person he was told to care for. The title that once designated Joseph's owner now designates Joseph. Whether or not the report is historical, it expresses what the narrative has already shown: every position held over Joseph in Egypt eventually passes to him, and none of them by his own taking.

**The Distance Covered in Fifty-Six Verses**

Measured in the chapter's own terms, verse 56 is the furthest point from verse 19. A boy is thrown into a well by his brothers, pulled out by a passing caravan, sold for a handful of counted dirhams to a man who regards him as a useful purchase, and ends up in a prison after a charge that his own accuser's household had already disproved. Verse 56 reports that the same person now settles wherever he pleases in the country that bought him. The sūrah supplies no explanation for the reversal beyond the two clauses in this verse: mercy given according to a will, and reward that is not discounted. Everything between them — the dreams, the prison, the forgetting, the king's enquiry — is the working out of those two statements.
