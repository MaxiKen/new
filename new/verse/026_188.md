## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:188

> Shu’aib responded, “My Lord knows best whatever you do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shuʿayb Returns the Matter to Divine Knowledge**

Shuʿayb does not answer the challenge with a personal threat. He says that his Lord knows best whatever the people do. The response places their conduct before the One who sees the measure, the intention, the hidden agreement, and the public challenge. The messenger does not need to claim control over punishment in order to remain truthful.

This is not uncertainty about Allah's knowledge. It is a declaration that the final account belongs to Allah. Shuʿayb knows enough to warn the people, but he does not pretend to know the unseen in the way the Creator knows it. His restraint keeps the prophetic role clear: convey, counsel, and leave judgment to the Lord.

The phrase whatever you do turns attention away from the insult and back to behavior. The people have reduced measures, damaged property, spread corruption, and now mocked a warning. Their actions form a record, whether or not they admit what those actions mean.

Allah's knowledge includes both the visible deed and the motive that surrounds it. A person may call fraud custom, call intimidation business, or call defiance courage. The name given by the wrongdoer cannot conceal the act from the One who knows its substance. Shuʿayb's answer refuses to be trapped by their preferred description.

**Restraint Is Not Weakness**

A speaker under attack may feel compelled to prove strength by predicting a punishment or cursing his opponents. Shuʿayb chooses another kind of strength. He does not surrender the truth, but he declines to make his anger the measure of justice.

That restraint protects the message from becoming a personal feud. If punishment comes, it comes by Allah's judgment, not because Shuʿayb has won an argument or lost his temper. The messenger can oppose corruption firmly while recognizing that the people remain answerable to a Lord whose knowledge is more complete than any human response.

**Divine Knowledge Makes Hidden Conduct Public Before God**

The community's dishonest dealings may be difficult for victims to prove. A hidden shortage, a pressured agreement, or a conversation held away from witnesses can disappear from human records. Shuʿayb's answer assures the people that the missing evidence is not missing from Allah.

Quran 28:84 states that good and evil deeds receive their fitting recompense. Shuʿayb's brief reply rests on the same moral order. The Lord knows what each person does, so no injustice becomes harmless merely because the victim lacks power to expose it. Knowledge precedes judgment and makes judgment exact.

**The Messenger Does Not Need to Own the Outcome**

Shuʿayb's duty is faithfulness, not control over the audience. He has explained the command, named the corruption, and warned of accountability. Whether the people repent, persist, or mock does not alter the truth he was sent to convey.

This frees the caller from two errors. He must not soften God's law to secure acceptance, and he must not force a result to prove that his speech had authority. The Lord knows every action and will deal with it rightly. A servant can speak with courage, answer with composure, and trust divine knowledge without claiming the throne of judgment for himself.

The reply also prevents the messenger from being drawn into a dispute over signs while the original injustice remains untouched. The people have tried to move the conversation from their conduct to a spectacle in the sky. Shuʿayb's answer points back to what Allah already knows: the ordinary acts by which they gain, conceal, pressure, and corrupt. The most important evidence is not the performance they demand but the record of the lives they are living.

This is a model for moral speech. A caller should not pretend to know every hidden motive, yet he can identify an action that is plainly harmful. He can say that Allah knows the full account and leave the unseen to Him. Such restraint keeps accusation from becoming slander and keeps humility from becoming silence.

The phrase whatever you do includes the good that a person may hide as well as the wrong he may hide. Divine knowledge is not only a threat. It is also the guarantee that an honest measure, a returned possession, and a private refusal to join corruption are not wasted. Human observers may overlook the deed, but the Lord does not.

Shuʿayb therefore answers the demand without abandoning the people. He does not say that their case is beyond knowledge or mercy. He places them before the Lord who knows their conduct and can judge it rightly. The people remain responsible, the messenger remains truthful, and the final decision remains where it belongs: with Allah.

This response is especially fitting after a demand for a heavenly sign. Shuʿayb does not claim that he can command the sky, nor does he claim that the people's private motives are hidden from him in a way that only he can uncover. He points to Allah's knowledge of their actual conduct. The people asked for an extraordinary event, while the answer directs them to the ordinary deeds for which they are already accountable.

The phrase best knows does not weaken the messenger's warning. A judge may know that a theft occurred without claiming to know every secret in the thief's heart. Shuʿayb knows the public wrong and conveys God's instruction, while Allah knows the full relation between deed, motive, opportunity, and consequence. This division of knowledge makes the messenger more trustworthy, not less.

It also teaches the hearer how to respond to provocation. Do not answer a cruel challenge with a promise you cannot fulfill. Do not turn another person's wrongdoing into permission for your own anger. State what is true, keep the matter before Allah, and continue to protect the rights that have been violated. Divine knowledge is a reason for courage and restraint at the same time.
