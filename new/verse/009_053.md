## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:53

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹Whether you˺ donate willingly or unwillingly, it will never be accepted from you, for you have been a rebellious people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Money Cannot Purchase Acceptance**

The verse rejects the idea that a donation automatically repairs a rebellious relationship with Allah. The hypocrites may offer wealth willingly or grudgingly, but the gift is not accepted because the giver has refused the faith and obedience that give charity its spiritual meaning. The command exposes a common bargain: keep disloyalty, then use money to buy the appearance of belonging.

This does not mean that every act of generosity by a person lacking faith has no worldly benefit. A hungry person may still be fed, and a just project may still receive resources. The issue is acceptance before Allah as an act of worship. A donation can produce a public good while failing to cleanse the heart of the donor's rebellion.

**Willingness and Reluctance Can Share One Defect**

The two words cover opposite emotional states, yet both are rejected in this case. A willing gift can be offered for reputation, influence, or political protection. An unwilling gift can be paid only to silence criticism. Neither motive is submission. The outer movement of wealth does not settle the inner question of whom the servant is trying to please.

This teaches the believer to inspect intention without despising lawful wealth. Charity is not made pure by making the donor poor or miserable. It is made pure when the giver recognizes Allah's claim, gives what is due, and seeks no rival reward. Joy in obedience is good; resentment does not become piety merely because money has changed hands.

**A Gift Does Not Replace Repentance**

The people addressed are called rebellious because their central problem remains. They do not need a financial gesture alone. They need faith, truthfulness, prayer, and submission. A person cannot compensate for rejecting Allah by increasing a secondary action while protecting the rejection itself.

The principle applies to modern forms of religious display. A person may fund a building, sponsor public events, or give generously while treating worship as optional and justice as an obstacle. Such giving may impress observers, but it cannot be used to bargain with Allah. Repentance begins when the servant stops managing appearances and returns to obedience in the whole of life.

**The Same Action Can Have Different Moral Directions**

Two people may give the same amount and receive different judgments. One gives from gratitude and concern for Allah's creatures. Another gives to gain access, silence an accusation, or preserve an identity he does not believe. The visible amount cannot reveal the complete act. Allah judges the heart and the relation between the gift and the giver's conduct.

This should make the sincere donor cautious, not paralyzed. He should correct his intention repeatedly and still give. Fear of mixed motives is not an excuse to stop every good action; it is a reason to ask Allah for sincerity and to remove the motives that can be removed. The hypocrites' failure is not that they felt imperfect. It is that they maintained rebellion while using gifts as a shield.

**Acceptance Is Allah's Decision**

The verse speaks of acceptance, a matter beyond the donor's ability to command. A servant may hope, repent, and do the work correctly, but he does not claim entitlement. This protects worship from pride. Even a large sacrifice remains small before Allah's generosity, and even a hidden act may be weighty because Allah knows the truth of its intention.

The believer therefore gives without demanding applause. He does not calculate his spiritual worth by the size of a public donation. He asks whether the wealth was lawful, the need was real, the method was sound, and the heart was turned toward Allah. Then he leaves acceptance to the Lord whose judgment is more exact than the donor's self-assessment.

**Charity Begins With Allegiance**

The verse does not reduce Islam to a private feeling. Faith produces obligations toward people, including the proper use of wealth. But charity cannot be separated from allegiance to Allah. When belief is present, giving becomes one expression of a life already directed to Him. When belief is rejected, giving may remain socially useful but loses the character of accepted worship.

The honest question is therefore larger than, “How much did I give?” It is, “What does my wealth say about what I serve?” A gift that follows repentance, prayer, and justice is a sign of return. A gift that hides rebellion is only a covering, and no covering can conceal the heart from Allah.

The verse also teaches that religious service cannot be financed by a guilty conscience while the heart rejects the Lord of the service. A donor may try to purchase a place among the sincere, but acceptance is not a market transaction. The proper response is to repair belief and conduct together. Pay what is owed, give to genuine need, return stolen wealth, and abandon the attitude that treats money as a substitute for repentance. When the heart returns, generosity becomes a sign of that return. Before it returns, the largest gift cannot command Allah to call rebellion obedience.

A sincere gift begins after this false bargain has ended. It is given because Allah deserves obedience and because His creatures have rights, not because money can force a place among the faithful.

The donor must also remember that public usefulness does not erase private rebellion. Good effects should be acknowledged, but worship requires the heart to return to Allah rather than merely improve its image among people.

A gift cannot command acceptance; only repentance and sincere obedience can prepare the heart to hope for it.
