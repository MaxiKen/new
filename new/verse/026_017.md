## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:17

> ˹commanded to say:˺ ‘Let the Children of Israel go with us.’’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Universal Claim Reaches the Enslaved**

The command now gives the first concrete consequence of God's lordship: Pharaoh must let the Children of Israel go with the messengers. The demand is not an abstract dispute about names. It concerns people held under a power that treats them as a resource. Divine sovereignty becomes visible in the release of those whom an earthly ruler has claimed as his own.

The phrase with us indicates movement out of Pharaoh's control. Moses and Aaron are not asking to become privileged intermediaries within the same system. They are calling for the community's departure from the arrangement that has confined it. The destination is not developed in this verse; the immediate point is that Pharaoh has no rightful ownership of the Israelites. A human ruler cannot convert a people created by God into permanent property.

**Letting Go Means Ending a Claim of Ownership**

The Arabic command has the sense of sending away or releasing. It is therefore stronger than a request for a holiday or a temporary suspension of labor. Pharaoh must relinquish his hold. The demand exposes the moral meaning of the political order: its power depends on refusing the Israelites the freedom to leave and to serve the Lord outside Pharaoh's control.

That is why the order is placed in the mouth of messengers. Liberation is not presented as a favor that a benevolent ruler may grant when convenient. It is a right grounded in God's authority. A ruler can exercise stewardship over a people only within justice; he cannot claim a people against their Creator. When power refuses release, it reveals that it has confused guardianship with possession.

**The Message Joins Worship and Social Freedom**

The demand to let Israel go is not separate from the proclamation of the Lord of all worlds. Worship and freedom belong together because the tyrant's control is also a claim over whom the people may obey. Pharaoh has made the Israelites serve his order; Moses calls them out so that their relation to God is no longer mediated by a ruler who oppresses them.

This does not mean that every political demand can be baptized as revelation. The verse is tied to a specific divine command and a specific wrong. It does show that devotion to God cannot be used to excuse the domination of human beings. When a religious claim leaves the enslaved in the hands of a ruler who treats them as disposable, it has failed to hear the practical meaning of God's lordship.

**Release Must Include the Whole Community**

The wording names the Children of Israel as a people rather than selecting a few acceptable individuals. Pharaoh cannot answer by releasing only the most useful, respectable, or least troublesome members. A community held under oppression is not liberated when the ruler chooses which families count as human enough to leave.

The collective name also protects memory. The Israelites have a history, kinship, and identity that precede Pharaoh's policy. Oppression often tries to rename its victims according to their labor or legal status. The command restores the name of the people and places their future under God's care. They are not merely workers being transferred between owners. They are a community whom the Lord addresses through His messengers.

**The Messengers Ask for Action, Not Sympathy**

Pharaoh might express concern, promise better treatment, or accuse the Israelites of disloyalty while leaving the structure intact. The command prevents such substitutions. Let them go is measurable. It requires a change in who controls their movement and labor. A ruler cannot answer a demand for justice with a speech about his good intentions.

This practical quality gives the mission its seriousness. Moses and Aaron must state a request that can be obeyed or refused in the public world. Their message will not be reduced to an inward comfort for the oppressed while oppression continues. The Lord of all worlds claims the right to alter the arrangement that keeps the people captive.

**Freedom Has a Direction Beyond Escape**

Release is not the final meaning of the command, but it is its necessary beginning. A people cannot freely obey God while a tyrant insists that their bodies, time, and worship belong to him. The verse therefore starts with removal from wrongful control. It does not yet describe every responsibility that follows freedom, and it should not be stretched to answer questions assigned to later verses.

What it establishes is enough: God's messenger must confront the ruler with the demand that the Israelites be released. The oppressed are not forgotten in the grandeur of theology. They stand at the center of the first practical command given to Pharaoh, and their freedom becomes evidence that divine lordship is not an empty title.

**Release Is a Test of Pharaoh's Claim to Rule**

A ruler who truly governs for justice should be able to release people whose captivity has no rightful basis. Pharaoh's resistance will show that his authority depends on treating a people as an instrument rather than as human beings under God. The request therefore tests more than his temper. It exposes what his kingdom is built to preserve.

The command also gives the oppressed a place in the public speech of revelation. They are not an afterthought added once theology has been settled. Their condition is one of the first matters through which God's lordship is declared. A claim about the Lord of all worlds must reach the bodies, families, labor, and future of those held under another man's power.

That is why the order remains concrete. Justice is not complete when a ruler admits a principle while keeping the captive under his hand. The hand must open, and the people must be allowed to move.
