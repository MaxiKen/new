## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:20

> **And they argue, “Had the Most Compassionate willed, we would have never worshipped them.” They have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this ˹claim˺. They do nothing but lie.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument from Fate**

Asked in the previous verse for evidence about the angels, the idolaters change the subject to God's will. Had the Compassionate willed, they say, we would not have worshipped them. The sentence is an appeal to divine decree as justification. Their reasoning, as Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn lay it out, ran like this: everything that happens, happens by God's will; our worship of the angels happens; therefore God wills it; and if He wills it, He must approve of it, or He would have stopped us. The argument had a certain plausibility in Makkah, because the Quraysh did believe in a high God whose will governed events, and because no punishment had yet fallen on them for their worship. They read the absence of punishment as consent. The Qurʾān records the same argument from them in 6:148, had God willed we would not have associated partners, nor our fathers, and in 16:35, had God willed we would not have worshipped anything besides Him. It is answered in each place, and the answer is always the same: this is not knowledge.

**The Confusion Between Will and Approval**

The exegetes identify the flaw in the argument as a confusion between two things Arabic and theology keep apart: mashīʾah, God's creative will, by which everything that exists is permitted to exist, and riḍā, His approval, by which He is pleased with some things and not others. God wills that human beings be able to choose, and therefore wills that their wrong choices be possible; He does not approve of the wrong choices. The Qurʾān states the distinction in 39:7: if you disbelieve, God has no need of you, and He does not approve of disbelief for His servants. Disbelief exists by His will and is condemned by His judgement. The idolaters collapsed the two, treating everything He permitted as everything He endorsed. On that logic every crime ever committed would be divinely approved, since every crime was permitted to occur. The Makkans would not have accepted that conclusion for theft or murder; they accepted it for idolatry because it let them keep their gods. The argument was not a belief they held but an excuse they reached for, and the verse treats it accordingly.

**A Question of Knowledge, Not of Fate**

The verse answers the fatalist argument with a claim about epistemology: mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilm, they have no knowledge of that. This is the same charge the previous verse made about the angels' sex. The idolaters said something about God's will, His approval of their worship, that they had no means of knowing. The verse does not deny that God's will encompasses all things. It denies that the idolaters knew what that will meant for the standing of their worship. They inferred approval from permission, and inference from an unwarranted premise is not knowledge. The Qurʾān holds the same line in 53:28, where the naming of the angels as female is called following conjecture, and conjecture avails nothing against the truth. The pattern across this passage is consistent. Verse 19 asked for evidence about the angels; verse 20 exposes the lack of evidence about God's will. In both cases the Quraysh had substituted assertion for knowledge, and the verse names the substitution.

**Yakhruṣūn: Guessing That Becomes Lying**

The verse's last word is yakhruṣūn, and its meaning runs from guessing to lying. The root kh-r-ṣ originally means to estimate the yield of a date palm by eye, without counting, and from that sense of unverified estimation it came to mean speaking without knowledge and, finally, speaking falsely. The translation renders it as lying, and the harsher sense fits because a guess presented as certainty is a lie about one's own knowledge. The Qurʾān uses the same verb for the idolaters' fatalist argument in 6:148, where the identical sequence appears: they say had God willed, they are told they have no knowledge, and they are said to do nothing but guess. The word also appears in 51:10, perish the guessers, of those who speak about the Hereafter from conjecture. The idolaters' argument in this verse was a kharṣ in the exact original sense: an estimate of God's mind made by eye, from the outside, without counting anything, and then delivered as if it were a measured fact.

**Why the Excuse Fails on Its Own Terms**

There is a further problem with the argument that al-Rāzī and others draw out. If the idolaters truly believed that God's will justified whatever happened, they would have had to accept the Prophet's ﷺ mission on the same grounds, since it too was happening by God's will. They did not. They opposed it with everything they had. So the fatalist argument was not a principle they lived by but a shield raised for one purpose. The Qurʾān exposes this selectivity in 6:149 immediately after recording the argument: say, to God belongs the conclusive argument; had He willed He would have guided you all. God's will could have compelled belief, and He chose not to compel, because compelled belief is not belief. The space in which the idolaters worshipped angels was the same space in which they could have worshipped God alone, and the existence of the space proved nothing about which choice He approved. The verse closes the passage on the daughters of God with a verdict on the whole method behind the belief: no witness, no revelation, no knowledge, only guesswork. The next verse asks whether they had, perhaps, a book that said otherwise.
