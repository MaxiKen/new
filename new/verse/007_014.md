## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:14

> He appealed, “Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Request for Delay**

The verse reports the request that Iblīs made after he was sentenced, that his end be delayed until the day on which the dead are raised. The request does not ask for forgiveness, and the Book's other accounts of the scene present the same petition: the sūrah of the Rocky Tract reports the request to be delayed until the day of the appointed time (15:36), the sūrah of the Night Journey reports it as a request to be delayed until the day of resurrection (17:62), and the sūrah of Ṣād reports the same request with the same answer (38:79).

The Book's narrative places the petition after the sentence and before the vow, and the arrangement tells the reader what the granted time will be used for. The one who asks for delay does not intend to change his position; the verse that follows his vow makes his purpose explicit. The request in front of us is the request of an enemy for the opportunity to continue.

**What the Request Reveals**

The request reveals that Iblīs has no hope of restoration. The confession he could have made is not made, and the pardon he could have sought is not sought. The Book's accounts of the condemned in the Hereafter include the same refusal to ask for what would help them, and the sūrah of the Troops and the sūrah of the Family of Imran describe the despair of those who meet their reckoning with excuses instead of repentance.

The commentary tradition notes the connection between the name of Iblīs and the sense of despair that it may carry, and the narrative in front of us supports the connection. The request for time is not a request for time to amend; it is a request for time to work against the creature whose honour he resented. The verse states that request without commentary, and the reader is left to draw the conclusion from what follows.

**Adam's Response Compared**

The Book's account of the same events contains a second response to the divine command, and the comparison gives the passage its teaching. When Adam and his wife disobey, they turn immediately to their Lord with the admission that they have wronged themselves and with the prayer for forgiveness and mercy (7:23). Iblīs, when he disobeys, argues that his substance is nobler than Adam's and then asks for time to continue.

The two responses divide the sūrah's characters, and the division recurs in the sūrah's accounts of the prophets and their peoples. The people of Noah, ʿĀd, and Thamūd are given messengers and respond with mockery and demands for proof; the prophets themselves respond to rejection with patience and with continued warning. The verse in front of us states the reaction of the one who has resolved to remain what he is.

**The Day Named in the Request**

The day to which the delay is asked to extend is the day of the raising of the dead, and the Book uses several names for it in its accounts of the last things. The sūrah of the Heights itself will describe the weighing, the questioning, and the dialogue between the people of Paradise and the people of the Fire; the sūrah of the Clatterer, the sūrah of the Inevitable, and the sūrah of the Resurrection give the day its names and its scenes.

The request places the whole history of humanity between the sentence passed on Iblīs and the day he named. The Book's account of the enmity between the tempter and the human being therefore occupies that interval, and the sūrah's address to its own audience falls inside it. The verse in front of us states the length of the interval in the words of the one who asked for it.

**The Silence of the Verse About the Reason**

The verse gives the request without stating the motive, and the omission is filled by the verses that follow. The vow to lie in wait and the approach from every direction make the purpose clear, and the sūrah's later passages describe the methods that the tempter uses. The verse in front of us keeps to the petition, and the reader who continues finds the answer and the plan in sequence.

The Book's narrative style in such passages leaves the motives of the wicked to be inferred from their words and acts. The same restraint appears in the accounts of the peoples who demanded signs from their prophets, where the demand itself exposes what is behind it. The verse in front of us applies the method to Iblīs.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands fourteenth in the chapter, and it follows the sentence passed on Iblīs in the verse before it. The grant of the request is reported in the verse that follows, and the vow comes immediately after that. The passage in front of us is the hinge between the expulsion and the activity that the rest of the sūrah describes.

The reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening has been given the Book, the command, the warning, the reckoning, and the story of the first refusal. The scene in front of us adds the fact of the interval between the refusal and the end, and the chapter's warnings are addressed to the people who live inside that interval.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the answer given to Iblīs, that he is among those who are granted delay (7:15). The answer is short, and it establishes that the request was permitted and that the tempter's activity takes place within a term that has been set.

The two verses together state the terms on which the enmity operates. The petition is made and granted, and the reader of the sūrah knows from the verses that follow both the purpose of the one who asked and the end that awaits him. The sūrah then reports his plan in his own words, and the account of his approach from every direction follows.
