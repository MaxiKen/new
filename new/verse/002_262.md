## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:262

> Those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah and do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words—they will get their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Conditions**

The parable stated the harvest; this verse states the conditions under which the harvest stands, and it states them as a single sentence with two clauses joined. The first clause is the spending itself — *those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah* — the parable's own subject, the grain sown. The second is the negative condition that follows the sowing: *and do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words*. The two named spoilers — mann, the reminding, and adhā, the injury — are both post-gift phenomena: neither occurs in the giving but after it, in the speech that follows the transfer. The verse's syntax enacts the prohibition: the giving comes first, the following comes second, and the second undoes the first. Al-Rāzī states the mechanism the verse legislates against: exulting in one's generosity, or reminding the needy of their neediness, destroys the virtue in the charitable act — not partially but totally, the way the fiery whirlwind of the coming parable does not prune the garden but consumes it. The deed is completed at the handoff; everything said afterward either leaves it intact or burns it.

**Mann: The Reminder**

The first spoiler is the gift's afterlife in speech: the reminder. Its forms are catalogued by the commentators in the everyday register — the giver who mentions his generosity in company, who brings up the past gift in a present argument, who lets the recipient feel the weight of the favor at every meeting, who tells the poor man's neighbors who fed them. The reminder's operation is economic as well as moral: the gift transferred wealth, and the mann transfers it back — the recipient's dignity becomes the payment, collected installment by installment in every mentioning. What the giver keeps after the reminder is not the virtue of the charity but its receipt, and the receipt is precisely what the parable's planting forbade: the seed dug back up to be shown. The tradition's diagnosis is that mann converts the transaction's direction — the giving was for God's cause, and the reminding reveals it was for the giver's standing; the verse's condition thus tests the first clause, since spending *in the cause of Allah* and spending for the echo of one's own name cannot both describe the same completed act.

**Adhā: The Injury**

The second spoiler is broader and blunter: hurtful words — the rebuke of the asker, the contempt in the handing-over, the sigh that accompanies the gift, the characterization of the recipient in the act of relieving him. Al-Rāzī's commentary pairs it with mann as the two mouths of the same sin: the reminder wounds after the fact, the injury wounds during it. The verse's placement of both under one prohibition has a structural logic the section will confirm — the coming verse will prefer a kind word and forgiveness to a charity followed by injury (2:263), which means the giving without the wound is not the whole standard; the standard is the giving that carries the wound-free word inside it. The commentators connect the injury-prohibition to the recipients the section will later describe — the poor too constrained to beg, known by their mark (2:273): the people the verse protects are precisely those whose asking costs them most, and an adhā delivered to such a person taxes the very restraint that made them worthy of the gift.

**Their Reward With Their Lord**

The promised reward is phrased with a preposition that does the verse's theological work: *they will get their reward from their Lord* — ajruhum ʿinda rabbihim, their wage is *with* their Lord, deposited in a location the verse's two spoilers cannot reach. The commentators draw the contrast implicitly: the reward of the reminding-giver was collected on the spot, in the gratitude and deference of the recipients and the audience — which is why nothing remains with God. The reward of the un-reminding giver was never collected anywhere visible, and therefore stands entire *with* the Lord — the same custody the grain-parable assumed, the seed left in the earth of God's knowledge. The phrase also personalizes the transaction: the reward is not an amount but a relationship — *their* Lord, the رب who is theirs specifically, the guardian-language of the preceding verses (2:257) applied now to the ledger. The giver who refused to cash his gift in human acknowledgment has, the verse promises, exactly one creditor and exactly one payer, and the payment is kept by the One who watched the refusal.

**No Fear, No Grief**

The sentence's final clause is the Qur'an's formula of arrived security: *there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve*. The pair covers the two directions of the heart's disturbance — fear facing the future, grief facing the past — and its recurrence across the sūrah marks it as the language of final states: the believers addressed at the Garden's promise (2:25), the faithful of every revelation who believe and act rightly (2:62), the sūrah's closing portrait of the night-and-day givers (2:274). Applied here, the formula does precise work against the two spoilers: the reminder-giver lives in fear — of the gift forgotten, the gratitude expired, the standing unacknowledged — and the injuring-giver lives in grief's neighborhood, the enmity his words planted. The un-reminding giver is released from both economies: he has no future to fear because his reward is with his Lord, and no past to grieve because nothing he gave has been taken back in speech. The no-fear-no-grief clause is thus not a generic comfort but the spoilers' exact negation — the peace of the person whose generosity has no afterlife in anyone's memory but God's.

**The Verse as the Section's Rule**

Read as legislation, the verse is the spending-section's constitution in one sentence: the deed, its protection, its reward's location, and its reward's effect. The verses that follow are its commentary in both directions — the comparison that ranks the kind word above the wounded gift (2:263), the command that voids the charity of the preening giver and his rock (2:264), the garden that doubles its fruit for the sincere (2:265), the old man's ruined orchard (2:266), the command to give of the good (2:267), the secret giving that acquits sins (2:271). Every one of them elaborates a term this verse states: what following the charity means, what the cause of Allah requires, what with-their-Lord keeps, what the un-fearing and un-grieving do with their wealth. The tradition's summary of the whole is the verse's own shape — giving is easy, the commentaries say in effect, and the difficulty of the section is everything that happens after the hand opens: the words that follow, the audiences that watch, the memory that collects. The verse legislates the hand and the silence together, and promises, for both kept, the reward no reminder can reach and the peace no injury disturbs.
