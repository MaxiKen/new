## Sūrah an-Naml 27:1

> Ṭâ-Sĩn. These are the verses of the Quran; the clear Book.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Letters That Open a Chapter About Speech**

The sūrah begins with two Arabic letters pronounced separately: *ṭā*, *sīn*. They are not a word, and no reading turns them into one. Twenty-nine chapters of the Qur'an open in this way, and the letters used are always drawn from the same alphabet an Arab child learned to write. Al-Ṭabarī preserves several explanations — that each letter stands for a divine name, that the letters are an oath, that they are names of the sūrahs themselves — and he weighs none of them as certain. The most disciplined position is the one many Companions took: their meaning is known to God, and the gain in speculating is small.

What can be said with confidence is the rhetorical effect of placing them here. A chapter that will spend its length on remarkable speech — a call from a fire, the language of birds, an ant's warning, a hoopoe's report — opens with the raw materials of speech itself, uncombined. Before the reader hears Solomon understand a bird, he hears two letters that mean nothing on their own. The Book is built out of the same alphabet its first audience used every day, and the challenge implied is that they could not build this out of it.

**The Same Two Letters, a Different Neighbour**

Ṭā Sīn does not stand alone in the Qur'an. The chapter immediately before it opens *Ṭâ-Sĩn-Mĩm* (26:1), and so does the chapter immediately after it (28:1). An-Naml sits in the middle of that run with one letter removed. The three sūrahs are usually treated as a set, and they share their subject matter: each tells the story of Mūsā at length, each answers the charge that the revelation is poetry or sorcery, and each builds toward the same claim about who sent it.

The variation in the opening letters therefore tracks something real rather than decorating the page. Ash-Shuʿarāʾ and al-Qaṣaṣ frame an-Naml, and an-Naml is the shortest of the three and the only one that carries the Solomon material. Some later commentators, including al-Rāzī, read the missing *mīm* as a signal that this chapter handles the shared theme in a compressed form. That reading cannot be proved, but the pattern it notices is plain on the page: the same signature, slightly altered, on three consecutive chapters that do related work.

**"The Verses of the Quran" — Signs Before They Are Sentences**

The word translated *verses* is *āyāt*, and its ordinary Arabic sense is signs or marks. The Qur'an uses the same word for the alternation of night and day, for rain that revives dead ground, and for the units of its own text. Calling a sentence an *āyah* therefore makes a claim about what kind of thing it is: not a line of composition but a piece of evidence, pointing past itself to the one who placed it there.

This double use is deliberate and consistent. A person who watches the sky sees signs; a person who reads this Book reads signs. The verse tells the listener at the outset that what follows should be examined the way one examines evidence, not the way one enjoys poetry. That distinction matters in a chapter which will shortly report Pharaoh's court calling the miracles of Mūsā *“This is pure magic.”* (27:13) — a response that treats a sign as a performance. The opening word sets the correct category before the wrong one is dramatised.

**Reciting and Writing: The Quran and the Book Are Two Descriptions**

The verse names the same revelation twice, using two words that are not synonyms. *Qur'ān* comes from the root *q-r-ʾ*, to recite or read aloud; it is revelation as sound, delivered by a voice, carried in memory. *Kitāb* comes from *k-t-b*, to write; it is revelation as a fixed and recorded text. The two names describe one thing from two sides, and the Muslim community's handling of the revelation followed both from the start: memorisation in the chest and transcription on whatever material was available.

Exegetes split on the reference of *the clear Book*. Some, including al-Rāzī, take it as the Preserved Tablet, the heavenly record described as *“a glorious Quran, ˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet.”* (85:21–22), so the verse would mean that these recited signs come from a written original above. Others, al-Qurṭubī among them, take it as the Qur'an itself, the two names stacked for emphasis. The second reading fits the sentence better: both phrases are predicates of the same *these*, and the natural sense of *these are the verses of X, the clear Book* is one object described twice. The first reading remains theologically sound but imports a referent the sentence does not supply.

**Clear in Itself, or Making Things Clear?**

The adjective *mubīn* is built on a form that can be read two ways in Arabic, and both readings are grammatically available here. Taken intransitively, it means the Book is clear — its language is plain Arabic, its message is not encoded, its demands are legible. Taken transitively, it means the Book makes things clear — it clarifies what was disputed, exposes what was hidden, and settles what the earlier communities argued about.

Both senses have support elsewhere in the Qur'an, which is why the commentators generally accept the ambiguity rather than resolving it. A text that is itself clear can also clarify; the two readings do not compete. But the second is the more pointed one in context, because this chapter's business is exactly clarification: it will explain what the queen of Sheba could not work out on her own, what the hoopoe reported and Solomon tested, and what Pharaoh's people knew inwardly while denying outwardly. The irony of the opening is that a Book described as *clear* begins with two letters nobody can decode. Clarity, as the Qur'an uses the word, is not the absence of depth; it is the absence of evasion.

**Ten Chapters, One Formula, Sharpened Here**

The phrase *these are the verses of* opens several chapters in almost identical form: *“Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom.”* (10:1), and again *“These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom.”* (31:2). Yūsuf, ar-Raʿd and al-Ḥijr use the same construction with their own adjectives attached. The formula is fixed; the descriptive word attached to it changes from chapter to chapter, and that change is where the meaning lies.

Here the chosen word is *mubīn*, clear, rather than *ḥakīm*, wise. Luqmān and Yūnus emphasise the wisdom folded into the text; an-Naml emphasises its legibility. That choice suits what follows, since this chapter's stories turn on whether evidence gets read correctly. Mūsā's signs arrive *as a clear portent* and are named sorcery. The hoopoe brings news that is tested before it is believed. Bilqīs examines a palace floor and revises her judgment. A Book that opens by calling itself clear is announcing the standard by which every character in it will be measured.
