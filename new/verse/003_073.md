## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:73

> And only believe those who follow your religion.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely, ˹the only˺ true guidance is Allah’s guidance.” ˹They also said,˺ “Do not believe that someone will receive ˹revealed˺ knowledge similar to yours or argue against you before your Lord.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Indeed, all bounty is in the Hands of Allah— He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, AllKnowing.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Clause of the Same Plan**

The verse continues the report of what the group said among themselves, and the second part of their plan concerns who deserves to be believed at all. Their proposal is that only members of their own religion should be accepted as credible — a rule that closes the circle of trust around the community that made it.

The measure is presented as a matter of policy rather than conviction, and the sūrah's arrangement of the two elements is instructive. The first part of the plan was tactical: perform acceptance and then withdraw. The second is structural: hold that anyone outside our religion cannot be telling the truth about religion. The two together amount to a system in which the message can never be accepted on its merits and never accepted on testimony, because the only credible witnesses are those who have already refused it.

**The Interjection From God**

The sūrah breaks into the quotation with an instruction to the Prophet: say, guidance is God's guidance. The interruption splits the reported speech and answers it in the middle, and the commentators noticed the grammatical awkwardness and discussed several ways of reading the sentence.

However the syntax is resolved, the content is decisive. The claim that truth is confined to one community is met by the statement that guidance belongs to God, which places the matter outside the jurisdiction of any group. The sūrah has used the same principle earlier in a passage that described the well-grounded in knowledge accepting the whole of what was revealed, and it will return to it again when it states that bounty is in God's hands. What the remark does is to remove the question of credibility from the arena of communal authority and return it to the one who guides.

**The Fear Behind the Second Clause**

The group's second remark is reported in the same breath: do not believe that someone will receive revealed knowledge similar to yours, or that he will be able to argue against you before your Lord.

The reasoning is transparent, and it is not really a doctrine about revelation. It is a fear about standing: if someone outside the community can be given a book, then the community's possession of a book no longer guarantees its position in the argument. The sūrah reports the fear because it explains the resistance better than any doctrinal objection could, and the answer given to it is about God's freedom to give rather than about the merits of the recipients.

**"All Bounty Is in the Hands of Allah"**

The Prophet is told to say that bounty belongs to God, that He grants it to whoever He wills, and that He is All-Bountiful and All-Knowing. The sentence answers the objection at its root by placing the distribution of revelation in the same category as the distribution of everything else.

The Book uses the same argument elsewhere for the granting of prophethood itself, and the sūrah's opening verses made the same claim in their account of God giving authority and honour to whom He pleases. What is being asserted here is that no community holds a veto over God's generosity, and that the community which received a revelation earlier cannot determine who receives one later. The terms of the sentence also explain why the objection is misplaced: if the giver is all-knowing, then the choice of recipient is a matter of his knowledge rather than of the community's qualifications.

**The Names at the End of the Sentence**

The two descriptions chosen are bountiful and knowing, and each answers part of the objection. Bounty explains why gifts are given at all, since the giving proceeds from generosity rather than from the merit of the recipients; knowledge explains why the gifts specify the right recipients, since the one who grants them knows what each community can carry.

The pair recurs throughout the Book at the ends of verses about provision, prophecy, and mercy, and its presence here ties the distribution of revelation to the ordinary providence by which the world is sustained. The sūrah is telling its readers that the same hand which supplies food and rain decides where a book appears, which makes the jealousy in the previous clause unintelligible as a religious position and recognisable as what the Book calls it — envy.

**What the Verse Leaves With Its Readers**

The verse ends with a statement about God rather than with a rebuttal of the group, and the reader is left holding an argument that can be applied wherever the same reasoning appears.

Any community can arrive at the position described here: the true knowledge arrived with us, and anything arriving later or elsewhere is suspect. The sūrah's answer is that guidance and bounty are not community property, and it is an answer that applies to the community now reading the passage as much as to the one being quoted. The next verse continues the same thought, and it describes what the mercy of God does in the world.

**The Group and Its Representatives**

The sūrah does not say how many people held the position it reports, and the phrase it uses is a group among the People of the Book. That qualification has already appeared in the sūrah's accounts of the community, and it prevents the reader from converting a report about a faction into a verdict on a people.

The Book's practice in these passages is consistent. It names conduct, describes the reasoning behind it, answers that reasoning, and leaves the question of who else shares it open. The reader who closes the verse has been given a clear picture of a tactic and a clear statement of the principle that defeats it, and he has not been invited to assess the standing of anyone not mentioned in the text.
