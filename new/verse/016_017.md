## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:17

> **Can the One Who creates be equal to those who do not? Will you not then be mindful?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Answers Itself**

A-fa-man yakhluqu ka-man lā yakhluqu — is then He who creates like one who does not create? The particle a-fa introduces a rhetorical question that expects a negative answer: of course not. Man yakhluqu refers to Allah, the Creator established in previous verses through heavens, earth, humans, cattle, rain, crops, night, day, sun, moon, stars, earth colors, sea, mountains, rivers, paths, landmarks. Man lā yakhluqu refers to idols and false gods that create nothing. Al-Ṭabarī explains that the idols did not create anything, not even a fly, as 22:73 says: *Those you call upon besides Allah will never create a fly even if they gathered together for it* (22:73). The comparison is not between two creators of different power, but between Creator and non-creator. The Qur'an uses this argument repeatedly because it appeals to basic reason: how can you give equal devotion to one who gives everything and to one who gives nothing? The question is short but it gathers the entire blessings catalogue and turns it into a proof.

**The Logic Of Creation As Proof Of Divinity**

The argument from creation to Creator is central to al-Naḥl. Verses 3-16 listed creations; verse 17 draws the conclusion. If Allah alone creates, then Allah alone deserves worship. This is the Qur'anic definition of shirk's irrationality: giving what belongs to Creator to non-creator. Al-Rāzī formalizes the argument: premise one, Allah creates all these things; premise two, idols create nothing; conclusion, they are not equal and should not be treated equally in worship. The verse adds a-fa-lā tadhakkarūn — will you not then be mindful, remember, take heed? Tadhakkur, from dh-k-r, is to remember, to let knowledge affect heart. The question implies that the answer is already known but forgotten or ignored. The Makkans knew Allah was Creator; they said so when asked, as 29:61 says: *If you ask them who created heavens and earth, they will say Allah* (29:61). Their failure was not in knowing who creates but in not letting that knowledge lead to exclusive worship. The verse therefore calls not for new information but for remembrance that moves to action.

**Why The Verse Is Placed After Navigation**

The question comes right after landmarks and stars guide. The connection is deliberate: the One who creates landmarks and stars and makes them guide is Allah; idols do not create guidance nor do they guide. If you would not trust a stone to guide you in desert, why trust a stone to guide you to salvation? Al-Zamakhsharī notes that the verse's placement after a long list of blessings makes the rhetorical question more powerful. The listener has just heard about rain, crops, sea, mountains; his mind is full of images of creation. Then he is asked, can the One who creates all this be like those who create nothing? The answer is felt, not only reasoned. The question also prepares for verse 20-21 which will describe idols as dead, not alive, not knowing when they will be resurrected. The contrast is sharpened: Creator is Living, Knowing, Guiding; idols are dead, ignorant, unable.

**The Psychological Power Of Comparison**

The Qur'an often uses comparison to expose shirk's absurdity, as in 13:16: *Are the blind and seeing equal, or are darkness and light equal?* (13:16). Here the comparison is between creating and not creating. The human mind naturally values the maker over the non-maker. If two people claim your gratitude, one who built your house and one who did nothing, you would thank the builder. The verse applies this natural judgment to worship. Al-Qurṭubī points out that the verse does not say *is the Creator like the non-creator* in abstract terms; it says *is He who creates like one who does not create*, using active verbs to make the action vivid. Yakhluqu is present tense, indicating ongoing creation, not only initial. Allah continues to create: rain now, crops now, humans now. Idols continue to create nothing, now and ever. The contrast is present and continuous.

**From Mindfulness To Counting Blessings**

The call a-fa-lā tadhakkarūn — will you not be mindful — is answered in the next verse: *If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never number them* (16:18). Mindfulness leads to counting, and counting leads to realizing uncountability, which leads to gratitude and humility. The verse thus serves as a hinge between the catalogue and its summary. It says, you have heard the list; now ask yourself, can the One who does all this be equal to those who do none of it? If you are mindful, you will see they are not equal, and you will turn to the Creator alone. The verse's brevity is part of its rhetorical strategy. After many long descriptive verses, a short, sharp question wakes the listener. It is like a pause in a lecture where the speaker stops listing and asks, do you see what this means?

**Linguistic Note On Man And Tadhakkur**

Man here means who, the one who, referring to Allah and to idols. The use of man for idols, which are not persons, is intentional to include any false object of worship, whether idol, human, jinn, or desire. Yakhluqu vs lā yakhluqu is a stark binary: creates vs does not create, with no middle. Tadhakkarūn from dh-k-r means to remember, to take admonition. In Qur'anic usage, tadhakkur is not mere memory but memory that leads to action, as opposed to forgetting that leads to neglect. The verse ends with a question mark in translation, reflecting its interrogative form in Arabic. The question is not seeking information; it is seeking acknowledgment. It is the kind of question a teacher asks when the answer is obvious and the student needs to admit it.
