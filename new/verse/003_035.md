## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:35

> ˹Remember˺ when the wife of ’Imrân said, “My Lord! I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service, so accept it from me. You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The "When" That Opens a Scene**

The verse begins with the Arabic particle *idh*, which attaches a remembered event to an implied command to recollect it. The Book uses this construction to introduce scenes from the lives of earlier figures, and the effect is to place the reader inside the moment rather than to summarise it: a woman is speaking, and her words are still audible in the recitation.

The form matters for what follows. This is not a doctrinal statement or a legal ruling; it is a household scene from the distant past, preserved in revelation for a community that had no access to it, and the following verses will continue the same narration.

**A Vow Made by a Mother About a Child She Has Not Seen**

The words *what is in my womb* locate the vow before the birth, at the earliest point a human being can be spoken for. The Arabic term rendered "dedicated entirely to Your service" is *muḥarrar*, a word from the vocabulary of setting a person free — a slave released from every claim in order to serve a single purpose. Applied to an unborn child, it means that the mother is surrendering her own claim.

Nothing in the sentence suggests that the child was asked, and the Book does not present this as a problem. What is being described is a mother placing her offspring at God's disposal, an act the wider tradition of the region understood as the highest form of offering a household could make. The vow is the mother's, not the child's, and the narrative that follows will show what the child did with a life that began under that dedication.

**The Household Behind the Words**

The sūrah names the speaker as the wife of ʿImrān rather than by her own name, and the commentaries identify her as Ḥannah, the mother of Mary. They report that her husband died before the child was born, and that she had promised to devote the child to the service of the sanctuary, to learn from what was taught there and to be free of the ordinary responsibilities of a household.

The reports fill in a situation that the verse's six words already imply. A woman without a husband, carrying an unborn child, decides before the birth that the child will serve the sanctuary rather than support her in her old age. The vow is a surrender of her own security, and the prayer that follows it is the request that the surrender be accepted.

**"So Accept It From Me" — the Mother's Petition**

The sentence *so accept it from me* asks for something specific: the reception of the offering. In the Book, acceptance is a term of weight. Offerings, worship, and repentance are all described as things God accepts or does not, and the same vocabulary will return in the following verses when the mother's daughter is accepted graciously.

The request positions the mother as a suppliant at the moment of her greatest generosity. She has given up her claim and she knows that what she has offered still requires approval to count, which is the Book's understanding of every human act of worship. The person who brings a gift does not determine its value; the one who receives it does.

**The Two Names the Mother Invokes**

The prayer closes with *the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing*, the same two names that closed the previous verse about the chosen line. The fatherless household, the unborn child, and the vow spoken in private are enclosed by hearing and knowledge.

The choice is exact. A woman who speaks a vow about a child nobody else can see needs the one who hears her words and knows the child she is carrying. The names also answer the two forms of human uncertainty in her situation: whether a prayer spoken by an individual reaches anyone with the power to answer, and whether a life whose future cannot be predicted is already known to its maker. On both counts, the prayer says, the answer is the same.

**A Vow That Did Not Know What It Was Asking For**

The narrative will shortly report that the child was a girl, and the mother's response to that fact is preserved in the next verse. What is striking is the honesty with which the sūrah allows the story to develop. The vow was made in the expectation that a son would serve the sanctuary, and the child who arrived changed the calculation.

The Book does not present the mother as mistaken. It presents her as a person who made an unconditional offering and then had to place her expectation in God's hands along with the child. The acceptance that the following verse reports was given to the child herself, and the house that began with a vow about a boy ended up producing, through a girl, a prophet whose name carries the sūrah's narrative.

**Where a Long Story Starts**

An entire sequence of the sūrah's material depends on this sentence. The dedication of the child leads to her placement in the sanctuary, the sanctuary leads to Zachariah, Zachariah's prayer leads to John, and Mary's own story leads to the birth of Jesus.

The chain is important for how the passage is read. None of the famous events in it is presented as an isolated miracle. Each is an answer to a request made by an ordinary servant, and the first request in the sequence is a mother's, spoken quietly, before anyone knew what would come of it.
