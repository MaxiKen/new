## Sūrah an-Naml 27:44

> Then she was told, “Enter the palace.” But when she saw the hall, she thought it was a body of water, so she bared her legs. Solomon said. “It is just a palace paved with crystal.” ˹At last˺ she declared, “My Lord! I have certainly wronged my soul. Now I ˹fully˺ submit myself along with Solomon to Allah, the Lord of all worlds.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Floor Does What a Flying Throne Could Not**

The throne crossed a continent in an instant and produced from her a careful, measured answer about resemblance. A glass floor produces a declaration of faith. The chapter has arranged these two events in this order deliberately.

What changes between them is not the scale of the marvel but its relation to her. The throne was a wonder performed in front of her, impressive and external. The floor is a mistake she makes herself, in her own body, in public. She looks at a surface, concludes it is water, and lifts her skirts to wade — and is wrong. That is a different kind of evidence entirely. A person can admire a miracle and remain unchanged; a person who has just been shown that her own eyes reported something that was not there has learned something about herself rather than about the performer.

**The Physics of the Illusion**

The Arabic is *ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīr* — a structure paved smooth with glass. Commentators note that part of the effect came from fish swimming beneath the crystal, which al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī all mention, and which would make the reading of water not merely plausible but nearly forced.

The detail changes the character of her error. She was not being careless. Confronted with a transparent surface, moving shapes underneath, and a flat expanse stretching away, any competent observer would conclude there was water. Her inference was sound and her premises were wrong. The Qur'an is describing the most respectable kind of mistake — the one produced by reasoning correctly from what appears — which is precisely the mistake her whole nation had made about the sun. They looked at the greatest visible power and drew the obvious conclusion. Here, in miniature and in a single second, she is shown the shape of her error without anyone having to name it.

**Sulaymān Corrects Her Immediately**

The moment she acts on the mistake, he tells her the truth. *Innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīr* — it is a hall paved with glass. No pause, no enjoyment of the moment, no lesson drawn aloud.

The speed is the courtesy. A host who wanted to make a point would have let the misunderstanding run; Sulaymān ends it in the next breath and states the fact flatly, without adding an interpretation. The absence of any moral commentary from him is conspicuous. He does not say *as you were mistaken about this, so you were mistaken about the sun*, and he does not need to. She draws the conclusion herself in the same verse, which is the only way the conclusion could have been worth drawing. A realisation handed to someone is an assertion; a realisation reached is a conviction.

**The Folklore Around This Verse, and Why It Should Go**

A body of legend attached itself to this scene. The stories hold that Sulaymān's jinn told him the queen's mother was a jinn and that her legs were deformed, and that the glass floor was contrived to expose them; further tales have him marrying her afterwards. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī all record versions of this material.

Ibn Kathīr's judgment on it is the correct one. He sees little value in the folklore and traces it to Arab legend and to oral traditions carried in by converts from Judaism and Christianity — the category the scholars call *isrāʾīliyyāt*. Nothing in the Qur'an supports any of it. The text says she saw a surface, judged it water, and acted; it says nothing about her legs beyond the ordinary act of lifting a garment to cross water, and it reports no marriage. [UNVERIFIED: the stories about the queen's jinn ancestry, her legs and a marriage to Sulaymān have no basis in the Qur'an and no authenticated chain I could confirm; they are noted here only to be set aside.] The episode as the Qur'an tells it is about perception and it ends in a declaration of faith.

**"I Have Wronged My Soul"**

Her confession uses a formula the Qur'an gives to others at their turning points. *Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī* — my Lord, I have wronged myself. Mūsā says almost exactly this after the killing in Egypt: *“My Lord! I have definitely wronged my soul, so forgive me.”* (28:16).

The construction places the injury on herself. She does not say she was deceived by her people, misled by her advisors, or trapped by an inherited religion — all of which the previous verse had, in effect, said on her behalf. The Qur'an supplied her excuse and she declines to use it. That refusal is what makes the confession a real one. There is also something exact in the verb: *ẓulm* is putting a thing where it does not belong, and misdirected worship is precisely that, a right given to the wrong recipient. She names her error by its correct technical description without any prompting.

**Submitting "Along With Solomon," Not to Him**

Her final words are the most carefully constructed sentence anyone speaks in the chapter: *aslamtu maʿa Sulaymān li-Llāhi rabb al-ʿālamīn* — I submit, with Sulaymān, to God, the Lord of all worlds.

Three things are settled at once. She places herself beside him rather than beneath him, which the letter's wording had always permitted. She names the recipient of her submission as God, not the king who summoned her. And she uses the widest possible title, Lord of all worlds — the same phrase spoken at the fire in verse 8. Ibn ʿArabī drew the comparison that makes this land: her submission was superior to Pharaoh's, because she submitted to the Lord of all worlds without qualification, whereas he, at the moment the sea closed over him, submitted to the Lord of Mūsā and Hārūn. Pharaoh conceded a God attached to two men. She names God as He is. The chapter's two royal narratives end in the same act performed at two entirely different depths, and Sheba's story closes here, with the last word hers.
