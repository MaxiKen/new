## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:65

> Say, “He ˹alone˺ has the power to unleash upon you a torment from above or below you or split you into ˹conflicting˺ factions and make you taste the violence of one another.” See how We vary the signs, so perhaps they will comprehend.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Ways for a Community to Be Punished**

The verse lists three possibilities, and the order matters. The first two are punishments that come from outside: a torment from above, and one from below. The third is not an external punishment at all but a condition the community brings upon itself, in which it splits into factions and each group makes the others taste violence. The Book places the self-inflicted one last, which is where the emphasis falls.

Commentators interpreted the torment from above as stones or a storm, or as domination by a tyrant, and the torment from below as an earthquake, a sinking, or being swallowed by the earth. The Book's catalogue of destroyed peoples fits both categories: some were struck by a storm of stones, some were overtaken by a blast, some were made to sink into the earth, and some were drowned (29:40). The sūrah of Sabāʾ states the same power in a conditional: if God willed, He could cause the earth to swallow the people or cause deadly pieces of the sky to fall on them (34:9).

**The Third Punishment and Why It Is the Worst**

The sentence about factions describes a community tearing itself apart, and the Book's choice of image is exact: each faction is made to taste the violence of the others. Civil conflict gives a person the opportunity to be both victim and perpetrator, and it destroys the ordinary structures of trust that hold a society together.

The Book warns about this kind of trial elsewhere in a form that applies it to communities generally. It says that people should guard against a trial which will not strike only the wrongdoers among them (8:25), which is the Book's way of saying that disorder does not restrict itself to the guilty. The sūrah of al-Anʿām has already stated the same warning in the story of the earlier peoples it describes as having been split into groups, and it will return to the subject of divisions among the believers later, when it tells the Prophet that he bears no responsibility for those who split their faith into sects (6:159).

**A Warning Delivered to the Prophet's Own Community**

The force of the list is that it applies to the audience, and the Prophet is told to deliver it as a public statement. The Makkans were confident that they were the strong party, protected by their alliances and their numbers, and the verse tells them that their strength was itself vulnerable to the third scenario. The Book's history is full of communities whose destruction began as an internal quarrel.

The warning also served the Prophet as a boundary. He had been refused the power to bring the punishment, and here he is told that God can bring it in forms that no alliance can resist. His task remained the delivery of the warning, and the verse keeps him inside that task even while it describes what God is able to do.

**"See How We Vary the Signs"**

The verse closes with the same formula that appeared earlier in the sūrah, where the Prophet was asked to see how the signs were varied while the people still turned away (6:46), and where the explanation of the signs was said to make the way of the wicked distinct (6:55). The recurrence is deliberate. The sūrah keeps a running account of the methods being used on its audience, and this verse places the warning about punishment among the variations.

The different reactions attached to the same formula mark the progression across the sūrah. At the earlier point the complaint was that the audience turned away; here the purpose clause is that they might comprehend. The Book's expectation is stated as a hope, and the hope is what keeps the warning from being merely a sentence.

**The Verb That Asks for Understanding**

To comprehend in this context describes the act of grasping something with the mind and turning it over, and the Book uses it for the kind of understanding that changes behaviour. A person who comprehends the argument of this verse has understood that the power which rescued him at sea (6:63) is the same power that can reach him on land, and that the quarrel he is pursuing may itself be the form the punishment takes.

That is the practical use of the list. The divine power described in the third scenario does not need to be exercised directly, since a community left to its own factions will produce the outcome on its own. The verse states the possibility and lets the audience see where their divisions were leading.

**A Final Word for the Prophet**

The verse ends the section that began with the demand to hasten the punishment, and it ends it by transferring the whole question out of human hands. The Prophet does not control the timing, he does not control the form, and he cannot prevent the third scenario from happening to his own people. What he can do is deliver the warning and let the variation of the signs do its work on whoever is willing to comprehend.

The sūrah then turns from these public warnings to a different kind of instruction, addressing the Prophet about how to deal with the people who attack the message and telling him what his own position is in relation to those who reject it. The tone changes, and the audience is offered a portrait of the messenger's own conduct for the first time in several verses.
