## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:130

> ˹Allah will ask,˺ “O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?” They will say, “We confess against ourselves!” For they have been deluded by ˹their˺ worldly life. And they will testify against themselves that they were disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address to Both Assemblies**

O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours? The verse records the address given to the assembled orders of created beings, and its form is a question that contains its own answer. The Book's word for the assembly is the one used in the previous address to the jinn (6:128), and the two sentences together frame the scene: the partners are gathered, and both are asked about the warning they received.

The Book states the universality of the warning in several places. The sūrah of Fāṭir says that the Prophet was sent with the truth as a deliverer of good news and a warner and that there is no community which has not had a warner (35:24); the sūrah of the Bee says that God has sent a messenger to every community with the command to worship God and shun false gods (16:36); and the sūrah of the Women says that messengers were sent with good news and warnings so that humanity should have no excuse before God after the coming of the messengers (4:165).

**The Argument From the Messengers' Coming**

The question's purpose is to establish the justice of the judgement, and the Book builds its account of the reckoning on the prior delivery of the message. The sūrah of the Cattle itself records the words of the people of the fire, who will say that they had no messenger come to them, and the Book's answer anticipates that claim with the address in this verse.

The mention of messengers coming from among the people addresses a point the sūrah has been making about the prophetic office. The Book's messengers are drawn from the communities they address, and the sūrah of the Family of Imran states that God favoured the believers by raising a messenger from among them (3:164). The question in this verse therefore states that no community was left without a spokesman of its own kind, and it leaves the assembled people to acknowledge the fact.

**The Confession**

They will say, We confess against ourselves! The answer admits the truth of the accusation without argument, and the Book records similar admissions at other points in its accounts of the judgement. The sūrah of the Heights describes the cry of the people overwhelmed by the punishment, saying that they had been wrongdoers (7:5); the sūrah of the Kingdom says that they will confess their sins and that the residents of the blaze are far from mercy (67:11); and the sūrah of the Prophets records the declaration of those who had taken lords besides God, freeing God of any partnership and confessing that they had been the ones at fault (25:18).

The sūrah of the Cattle has already described an audience that would deny everything at the resurrection, with their only argument being an oath that they were never polytheists (6:23). The verse in front of us supplies the other response, which is the confession that the sūrah's account of the judgement requires for its justice to be visible.

**The Delusion of the Worldly Life**

For they have been deluded by their worldly life. The sentence gives the reason the confession comes so late, and the Book's word for the delusion describes being taken in by something that appears substantial. The sūrah's earlier passage used the same vocabulary for the people who took their religion as play and amusement and were deluded by the life of this world (6:70), and the two passages belong to the same account of what the world's attractions do to a person's judgement.

The Book's treatment of the worldly life is consistent across the sūrahs. It describes the life of the world as a pasture and a passing enjoyment, and it contrasts it with what is with God, without denying its reality. The verse in front of us states the effect of that life on the people who were addressed by the messengers and remained where their desires had placed them.

**The Testimony Against Themselves**

And they will testify against themselves that they were disbelievers. The verse closes with the completion of the admission, and the Book presents the record of a person's conduct as testimony that he gives against himself. The sūrah of the Night Journey states that every human being's destiny is bound to his neck and that a record will be brought out for him on the day of judgment, with the command to read it and the statement that the person is sufficient as an accountant against himself (17:13–14).

The sūrah has described the recording of deeds in its earlier passages, where the angels write down what the people do (6:61), and the verse in front of us shows the record being used. The testimony is not extracted by force; it is given by the people whose deeds it contains.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse establishes the terms on which the judgement of the gathered orders proceeds. Messengers came, revelations were proclaimed, warnings were delivered, and the people who were addressed were left without an argument. The confession that follows is the acknowledgement of that record, and the sūrah treats it as the final answer of the people who had argued throughout their lives.

The passage that continues from here returns to the theme of the Hereafter and the conduct of the people who denied it, and it moves toward the end of the sūrah's long middle section. The verse in front of us completes the sequence begun with the address to the jinn, and it states the principle on which the whole reckoning rests.
