## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:5

> **Should We then turn the ˹Quranic˺ Reminder away from you ˹simply˺ because you have been a transgressing people?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Expects the Answer No**

The verse is a rhetorical question, and the Arabic construction makes the expected answer clear. The particle is a-fa, "shall We then", and the form is that of a denial dressed as a question: shall We really do this? The expected reply is: no, We shall not. God is asking whether He should stop sending the Reminder to the Quraysh because they have been a people of isrāf, excess and transgression. The question is addressed to the disbelievers themselves, and it has a tone of rebuke wrapped in restraint. It does not say: you deserve to be abandoned. It asks whether abandoning them is what they imagine God would do, and then, by the shape of the sentence, refuses to do it. The verse thus sits between the description of the Book in verses 2–4 and the warning drawn from earlier nations in verses 6–8. The Book is exalted and wise; the people are transgressors; and yet the Book keeps coming to them.

**Ṣafḥan: Turning the Face Away**

The key word is ṣafḥan, from ṣafaḥa, whose root sense is the side or surface of something. To turn ṣafḥan from a person is to turn one's side to him, to present him with the flat of one's face rather than one's attention. The English "turn away" catches it. The word is used elsewhere in the Qurʾān in a gentler sense, where believers are told to pardon and overlook, faṣfaḥ, as in 15:85 and 5:13, because overlooking a fault is also a kind of turning aside from it. Here the same movement is threatened as a punishment: God turning aside from a people by no longer addressing them. The grammarians read ṣafḥan as an adverbial accusative describing how the striking-away would happen, or as a verbal noun standing in for the verb. Either way the picture is of a face turned to the wall. The verse asks whether the Quraysh's behaviour has earned that silence, and then declines to inflict it.

**Two Readings of the Reminder**

The exegetes differ on what al-dhikr means here. Al-Ṭabarī reads it as the reminder of punishment: shall We stop warning you of what is coming, and simply leave you to it? On this view the verse threatens the removal of warning, which would itself be the worst punishment, since a people no longer warned is a people already given up. Al-Jalālayn read al-dhikr as the Qurʾān itself, one of its regular names, so the question becomes: shall We stop sending the Qurʾān down to you? Ibn Kathīr develops the second reading in the direction of mercy. God continued to reveal the Qurʾān even after many had rejected it, and this continuation is an act of grace that the rejecters did nothing to earn. The two readings share a core. Whether al-dhikr is the warning or the whole Book, the point is that God's address to the Quraysh did not stop when they proved unworthy of it. The second reading is fuller, because the warning is part of the Qurʾān and the verse's immediate context, verses 2–4, has been about the Book as a whole.

**Isrāf: The Sin of Going Too Far**

The reason given for the possible withdrawal is that "you have been a transgressing people", qawman musrifīn. The root s-r-f means to exceed the proper limit, and a musrif is one who goes too far in anything: in spending, in eating, in killing, in claiming. The Qurʾān uses it for the man who wastes wealth (25:67), for the people of Lūṭ, and for Pharaoh, who is called musrif in 10:83 and 44:31. In the case of the Quraysh the excess was in worship and in speech: they worshipped what does not create, and they said of God what they had no knowledge of, both charges that this sūrah will lay out in the verses to come. The word does not name a single act but a habit of overreach, which is why the verse uses kuntum, "you have been", a past continuous. The Quraysh had a long history of it, stretching back through generations who inherited the idols and added to them. And yet the verb's tense also implies that the history could end. Isrāf is a way of behaving, not a nature, and a people who have been transgressors can stop being so, which is exactly why the Reminder is not withdrawn.

**Mercy That Outlasts Rejection**

The verse states, without any softening, a principle that runs through the whole sūrah. God's mercy in sending guidance is not conditional on the guided being worthy. A ruler among men, mocked and rejected by his subjects, would stop speaking to them. The verse takes that expected human response and refuses it in God's name. This is the same principle expressed in 21:107, where the Prophet ﷺ is sent as a mercy to all the worlds, including those who reject him, and it is the practical basis of the Prophet's own conduct in Makkah, where he went on reciting to people who had already mocked him. The rhetorical question also carries a warning inside the mercy. To ask whether the Reminder should be taken away is to remind the hearers that it could be. Guidance is a gift, not an entitlement, and the fact that it continued was a reason for the Quraysh to reconsider rather than a licence to go on. A gift that could have been withheld, and was not, places a debt on the one who receives it. The next verses supply the evidence that God's patience has a limit, by pointing to earlier peoples who exhausted it.
