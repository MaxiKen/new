## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:63

> When Joseph’s brothers returned to their father, they pleaded, “O our father! We have been denied ˹further˺ supplies. So send our brother with us so that we may receive our measure, and we will definitely watch over him.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**They Report the Condition Before the Money**

The order of the plea is worth examining. The brothers open with the loss — further supplies have been withheld from us — and only in verse 65, after the bags are opened, do they mention what was returned to them. Whether the chapter is reporting events in the order they occurred or arranging the argument for effect, the result is the same: Jacob first hears that his sons have come back short, and only later hears that they have come back with their money intact. The sequence shapes his response. Verse 64's refusal is given before the evidence of generosity is produced, which means Jacob rejects the request on grounds that have nothing to do with the minister's character.

**"The Measure Has Been Withheld From Us"**

The phrase denotes what they were refused rather than what they received. The commentators gloss it as the remainder of the food they had sought — specifically the eleventh camel-load requested for a dependant brother at home (12:59, 12:65). The brothers therefore report accurately: they came home with ten loads and without the eleventh. What they do not say in this verse is why. The condition attached to the refusal is stated only as a request — send our brother with us — and the reason behind it is left implicit. A father hearing this account learns that Egypt will not supply further grain unless a son travels there, and he is not told who imposed the condition or on what grounds.

**"Send Our Brother With Us"**

The request is direct and it names no one. The brother is identified only by relation, which is how the chapter refers to Benjamin throughout: never by name, always as the brother from their father (12:59), his brother (12:64), our brother (12:65), his full brother (12:77). The anonymity is a consistent feature of the Qur'anic account and it has an effect on the reader. A character without a name is defined entirely by his relations, and Benjamin's relation to Joseph is the one the narrative is building toward. The brothers use the same form of reference Joseph used, which means the request as they state it echoes the minister's wording exactly. The borrowing is deliberate and it does real work. By using the minister's own phrase they import his authority into their plea, so Jacob hears the request not as his sons' wish but as a condition imposed by the government that feeds them. What Joseph said to them in Egypt becomes their strongest argument in Canaan.

**"We Will Surely Be His Keepers"**

The assurance repeats the guarantee they gave about Joseph. In verses 12 to 14 they asked their father to send Joseph with them and undertook to protect him; the pledge failed, and the chapter recorded its failure in verse 16 with a false report and a bloodied shirt. The same undertaking is now offered for a second son, in the same emphatic form, by the same men, to the same father. The brothers do not acknowledge the earlier instance. The verse contains no reference to Joseph, no explanation of what has changed, and no admission that the previous guarantee was not kept. Jacob supplies all three in his answer. The claim is comprehensive, and that is its weakness. They undertake to protect him, to return him and to answer for him, in a household that has already watched them fail at precisely this. The verse lets the assurance stand at full strength so that the reply which follows can take it apart without needing to argue against anything weaker.

**The Verse Reports the Plea and Stops**

No reply is given here. Verse 63 ends with the brothers' assurance, and verse 64 opens with their father's response. The separation is a deliberate pause: a plea is stated in full, and the answer arrives in its own verse rather than inside the same sentence. The chapter frequently gives a single utterance its own verse when the utterance carries weight — verse 61 did the same with a promise of nine words. The effect is to let the assurance stand alone in front of the reader before Jacob dismantles it. A reader who has followed the story from verse 8 knows what is coming, and the pause allows the knowledge to arrive before the words do. Stopping there is a form of respect for the reader. The narrative does not need to rehearse the precedent, which has already been established, and the pause allows it to surface unaided. When Jacob speaks in the next verse the reader is ahead of the brothers, which is exactly the position the chapter wants for the scene that follows.

**The Rhetoric Is Structured, Not Spontaneous**

Three moves in three clauses: the loss, the remedy, the guarantee. First, we have been refused further supplies — establishing that a problem exists and that it is external. Second, send our brother with us so that we may obtain our measure — presenting the solution as a condition imposed by Egypt rather than a desire of theirs. Third, we will surely watch over him — anticipating the objection and answering it before it is raised. The structure is that of people who have rehearsed the conversation on the road home, and the chapter's silence about their rehearsal lets the reader infer it from the shape of the speech. Every element of the speech has a function and none is incidental. The provisions establish that the journey succeeded, the minister's condition explains why another son is needed, the pledge answers an objection before it is voiced. This is not a report delivered on arrival but a case prepared in advance and presented in order.

**What Is Left Out of the Report**

Several things the brothers know are absent from what they tell their father. They do not describe the minister — his age, his bearing, his treatment of them beyond the full measure. They do not mention the demand's peculiar form, a brother from their father, which ought to strike a parent as oddly specific. They do not report the refusal of access threatened in verse 60. And they say nothing about the money, which at this point may not yet have been discovered. The report is accurate and incomplete, and its incompleteness is what produces verse 64: Jacob answers the assurance offered and cannot answer arguments he has not been given. The omissions are the point. Nothing is said about the money returned, about the brother they lost, or about anything that might weaken the request; the speech contains only what supports it. Jacob is answering a curated account, and the chapter shows him answering the single part of it he can, because the rest has been withheld from him.

**The Famine Is the Unstated Premise of the Whole Speech**

Behind the three clauses lies a fact the brothers do not need to state because their father already lives inside it. Canaan is in the same scarcity as Egypt; the journey was necessary and the next one will be too. Verse 63 therefore does not argue that the family needs food. That is assumed. What it argues is that the food cannot be obtained again on the present terms, and that the change of terms requires Benjamin. The economy of the plea depends on the assumption, and the chapter relies on the reader having absorbed verses 47 to 49 well enough to supply it. A father hearing this speech is not weighing whether to feed his household; he is weighing whether feeding it costs him a son.
