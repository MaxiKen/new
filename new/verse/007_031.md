## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:31

> O Children of Adam! Dress properly whenever you are at worship. Eat and drink, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Wear Adornment at Worship**

The verse addresses the children of Adam and commands them to take their adornment at every place of worship, and the command follows the account of the first pair's exposure and the instruction about clothing that answered it. The adornment in question is understood by the commentary tradition as the clothing that covers the body, and the command therefore restates the provision that the previous passage announced. The Book's association of beauty with worship appears in its instructions about the pilgrimage and about the prayer, and the sūrah in front of us states the principle at the point where the audience's practice is being corrected.

The practice that the command corrects is described in the reports about the sanctuary. The Quraysh had established rules about the clothing and the food permitted within the sacred precinct, and those who came to perform the pilgrimage were required to use clothing belonging to the Quraysh or to go without. The verse in front of us ends the arrangement by commanding the people to be clothed at every place of worship, and it treats the covering and the adornment as the ordinary condition of prayer.

**Eating and Drinking Without Excess**

The second command instructs the people to eat and drink, and it places a limit on the conduct with a prohibition of excess. The permission is stated before the limitation, and the Book's instructions about food generally follow the same order, granting what has been made lawful and then naming the exceptions. The commentary tradition connects the command with the dietary restrictions that the audience had imposed on itself during the pilgrimage, and it reads the verse as a restoration of what had been forbidden without warrant.

The Book's treatment of lawful consumption appears throughout the sūrah's later passages about the animals and the offerings, and the sūrah of the Cattle contains a list of what has actually been forbidden. The verse in front of us states the permission in its broadest form, and the prohibition that follows it concerns quantity rather than kind.

**The Prohibition of Excess**

The verse states that God does not love the wasteful, and the commentary tradition gives two senses to the term. The first concerns the eating of what has been made lawful to the point of harm, and the second concerns the waste of what has been provided without any benefit from it. Both readings treat the prohibition as a limit on the use of the good things rather than as a restriction on their availability.

The Book's use of the same word for excess appears in the sūrah of the Cattle's account of the gardens and the fields, where the warning against waste accompanies the permission to eat of the produce. The sūrah of the Children of Israel states that the wasteful have been the brothers of the devils, and the verse in front of us places the same estimate of excess at the head of the passage on food.

**The Middle Way of the Passage**

The verse's combination of permission and limit gives the sūrah's teaching about the created goods its balance. The audience's practice had forbidden what God had not forbidden, and the ascetic impulse in any community tends toward the same error. The verse in front of us affirms the enjoyment of the good things and restrains it in the same sentence, and the commentary tradition cites reports in which the Prophet corrected those who proposed to abandon meat, perfume, marriage, and property in the name of devotion.

The Book's teaching about the goods of the world throughout the sūrah includes instructions about the harvest, the livestock, and the offerings, and the verse in front of us supplies the principle that governs all of them. What God has provided is to be used with gratitude, and what has been forbidden is to be avoided, and excess is excluded between the two.

**Clothing, Food, and the Worship**

The verse joins the two subjects of clothing and food under the heading of the places of worship, and the arrangement reflects the context from which the passage comes. The sūrah is addressed to an audience whose religion consisted largely of practices at a sanctuary, and the two matters that the practices regulated were what to wear and what to eat. The verse in front of us supplies the correct rule for both, and it refers the practices to the worship of God alone.

The Book's presentation of the pilgrimage and the prayer as acts of obedience appears in the sūrah of the Cow and the sūrah of the Pilgrimage, and the verse in front of us places the physical preparation for worship among the things that the Lord has commanded. The adornment is worn for the sake of the One before whom the person stands.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands thirty-first in the chapter, and it follows the account of the two groups and precedes the passage that asks who has forbidden the adornments that God has brought forth. The three verses on clothing, food, and the source of the prohibitions form a unit, and the command in front of us supplies the positive side of the matter.

The reader who has been told about the guidance and the straying is given the practical rule that distinguishes them. The provision is enjoyed within the measure that God has set, and the worship is performed in the clothing that He has given, and the excess that the audience practiced in both directions is excluded.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse gives the Prophet the command to ask who has forbidden the adornments and the lawful provisions that God has brought forth for His servants, and it states that they are for the believers in this world and exclusively theirs on the day of judgment (7:32). The command to wear the adornment and to eat and drink in front of us is followed by the exposure of those who forbid what God has provided.

The two verses together complete the passage's argument. The permission is stated, the limit is stated, and the claim of the audience to forbid what God has not forbidden is answered in the verse that follows.
