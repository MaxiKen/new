## Sūrah an-Naml 27:84

> When they ˹finally˺ come before their Lord, He will ask ˹them˺, “Did you deny My revelations without ˹even˺ comprehending them? Or what ˹exactly˺ did you do?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge Is Not Denial**

The question put to the assembled contingents is precise, and it is easy to misread. They are not asked why they denied. They are asked whether they denied *without encompassing the signs in knowledge*.

The grammatical weight falls on the second clause. Denial after investigation would be a different charge and would meet a different question; what is put to them is that they rejected without having examined. Al-Ṭabarī takes the phrase to mean they did not know or recognise the signs as they deserved to be known. The offence being identified is therefore procedural before it is doctrinal: a verdict reached without the evidence being looked at.

**"Encompassed in Knowledge"**

The verb is *aḥāṭa*, to encompass or surround, and this chapter has used it once before at a memorable moment. The hoopoe returned to Sulaymān and said *aḥaṭtu bi-mā lam tuḥiṭ bih* — I have encompassed what you have not encompassed.

That earlier use sets the standard the question here applies. A bird flew a long distance, observed a nation, identified its religion and its ruler, and returned with a report it described as certain news. The chapter presents that as the model of how a claim is established. Against it, the contingents at the judgment are asked whether they encompassed anything at all before rejecting. The word is the same and the comparison is unflattering. A hoopoe did the work; they did not.

**The Second Question**

After the charge comes an odd addition: *ammā dhā kuntum taʿmalūn* — or what was it that you used to do? The question is left open, and its openness is the point.

It functions as an invitation to supply an alternative account. If you did not reject out of ignorance, then tell us what you were doing. This is how a competent interrogation proceeds — the charge is stated and the accused is given room to answer it. What makes the moment devastating is not the severity of the question but its fairness. Nobody is being convicted by assertion. They are being asked to describe their own conduct, and the following verse records that they cannot speak.

**A Question That Assumes an Answer Existed**

There is something conditional in the phrasing that is worth drawing out. The question implies that encompassing the signs in knowledge *was* possible — that had they investigated, they would have found something.

The chapter has supported that implication throughout. Sheba's queen investigated and arrived somewhere. Pharaoh's court examined the nine signs closely enough to be certain of them in their souls. The Meccans were told to travel and look at the ruins, an instruction that assumes the looking would be informative. None of the chapter's evidence is presented as accessible only to the elect. The question at the judgment is answerable only because the inquiry was available, and the failure it names is a failure to use what was there.

**Speech Restored at the Worst Moment**

For the whole of this passage the deniers have been described as unable to hear. Now they are addressed directly by God and asked a question that requires an answer.

The reversal is pointed. In the world they had hearing they did not use; at the judgment they are given a hearing they cannot use, since what is asked of them is a defence they do not have. The chapter's account of the queen of Sheba ran the other way: she was spoken to by a letter, replied, was spoken to again, and ended by speaking for herself in the words of submission. Dialogue in an-Naml is consistently the mark of those who are getting somewhere. This exchange has one speaker and, as the next verse states, no reply.

**The Question Presumes a Standard of Inquiry**

Read the charge closely and it contains a claim about what people owe to evidence. To reject a thing is permissible; to reject it without having encompassed it in knowledge is what is being put to them as an offence.

That standard is high and it is applied at the judgment to ordinary people, not to scholars. The chapter has already illustrated what meeting it looks like, and none of the examples involve special training. Sheba's queen consulted her council, sent a probe, and travelled to see for herself. The hoopoe flew and observed. Ṣāliḥ's people were asked to wait and did not. What these have in common is the willingness to spend effort before reaching a conclusion, which is a disposition rather than a qualification. The question at the judgment assumes the disposition was available to everyone being asked.

**No Excuse Is Pre-empted**

Something the verse does not do is anticipate the defence. It does not say they had no excuse, does not list the warnings they received, and does not rehearse the evidence against them.

The restraint is consistent with the procedural character of the scene. A charge is put, an opening is left for a reply, and the text waits. Compare how the chapter handled Thamūd's conspirators: the plot was described, the counter-plot named, and the outcome given, with no speech assigned to the nine at all. Here the accused are addressed and given the floor. The difference is that this is the judgment rather than the world, and the judgment is where the parties are heard. That the hearing produces nothing is the subject of the verse that follows.

**Deniers, Not Disbelievers**

The charge names an act rather than an identity: *kadhdhabtum*, you denied, from a verb meaning to call something a lie. The assembled group was described in the previous verse by the same root.

This matters for how the passage should be read. The Qur'an has words for disbelief and for associating partners with God, and it does not use them here. What is under examination is a specific thing people did with specific evidence — they were shown signs and pronounced them false. The chapter's opponents did this in so many words. Pharaoh's court called the nine signs manifest sorcery; the Meccans called the account of the Hereafter tales of the ancients. The judgment scene asks about that pronouncement and about the inquiry that should have preceded it.
