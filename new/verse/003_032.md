## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:32

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Obey Allah and His Messenger.” If they still turn away, then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Two-Part Command in One Sentence**

The verse gives an instruction in the imperative and pairs two objects of obedience in a single phrase. The pairing is the point of the sentence. Obedience to God is not being divided or supplemented here; it is being located, so that a person who claims to obey God is told where that obedience is to be sought in practice.

Earlier in the sūrah, a test of love was set by the phrase *follow me*. This verse moves from following to obeying, and the two are not the same relation. Following describes a direction and a model; obedience describes a requirement with authority behind it. The Book uses both because a claim of devotion needs both: it must be a road, and it must be binding.

**Why the Messenger Is Named Beside God**

The command does not say *obey Allah* and stop. It names the Messenger as the second object, and the Book does this consistently — at 4:59, at 4:80, and in a series of verses that place obedience to the Prophet second only to obedience to God.

The doctrinal implication is the one the sūrah has been building toward since it addressed claims of love. If a person's religion is his private relationship with the divine, he can shape it from his own preferences and call the result spiritual. If the messenger is named in the same sentence as God, the shaping has been given to someone else, and the community's access to the Prophet's conduct becomes the practical content of the command. That is also why the transmitted record of his practice occupies the place it does in Islamic law.

**"If They Still Turn Away" — the Refusal That Repeats**

The clause assumes a specific reaction: not an argument, not a counter-command, but a turn away. The same verb was used of people invited to the Book of God to settle their disputes, and the Book uses it again here for those who hear the instruction to obey.

The turn is described as *still* turning, which implies a pattern. A single refusal might be hesitation, ignorance, or confusion; a persistent refusal in the face of a plain command is a settled position. The Book's response is correspondingly direct, and it does not take the form of an appeal.

**"Allah Does Not Like the Disbelievers"**

The verse's last clause names the consequence in the language of divine approval rather than of punishment: God does not love, or does not approve of, the disbelievers. The Book states this elsewhere with the same verb and a range of subjects: God does not like the wrongdoers, the ungrateful, the arrogant, those who spread corruption.

The choice of the word tells the reader what is at stake. The difference between a servant and God is not merely a difference of opinions or practices; it is the presence or absence of approval, which is the one relationship a person cannot create for himself. A person can arrange for a good reputation among people, and he can persuade himself of his own sincerity, but he cannot decide that God is pleased with him. The verse ends with the limit of human self-assessment, and it leaves the reader to draw the conclusion about what the earlier command is worth.

**What the Verse Answers in the Sūrah's Argument**

The passage has moved in a deliberate sequence. It taught a prayer that put all authority in God's hands, warned against alliances that replace the believers, described the knowledge that reaches into hearts and the day when every soul meets its record, moved from following the Prophet to obeying God and His Messenger, and now describes the refusal that settles a person's case.

Each step has narrowed the space in which a person can define his own religion. The command in this verse is the narrowest so far, and the clause that follows it states the result of declining. The reader is no longer being asked to consider evidence or to weigh claims; he is being told what obedience consists of, and what the refusal of it means.

**Disobedience and Disbelief**

The Book distinguishes between the sins of believers and the settled unbelief of those who refuse the message, and this verse belongs to the second category. Its subject is people who turn away from an explicit command, not people who fail at it.

The distinction matters for how a reader uses the sentence. A believer who struggles with a command has the whole apparatus of repentance, and the sūrah has already described the forgiveness promised to those who follow and ask. What removes a person from the category of the liked is not weakness but the decision that the command does not apply to him, which is what the turn away in this verse describes.

The verse also fixes the order in which the two obligations are met. Obedience to the messenger is not presented as a substitute for obedience to God, nor is it an optional layer added by a community to its religion. The Book places them in one phrase and one breath, and the sūrah has already made clear what it thinks of people who treat the messenger as a figure to be admired rather than a command to be followed.

**The Next Instruction and Its Consequence**

The following verse will name the families God chose and begin the long narrative section of the sūrah. The instruction to obey stands at the seam between the sūrah's arguments with its contemporaries and its account of earlier communities, and it applies to both: the same command was given to the people of the earlier scriptures, and the same refusal is described in their story.
