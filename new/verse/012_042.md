## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:42

> Then he said to the one he knew would survive, “Mention me in the presence of your master. ” But Satan made him forget to mention Joseph to his master, so he remained in prison for several years.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Thing Joseph Asks for Himself in the Whole Chapter**

Across a hundred verses Joseph makes few requests. He asks God to turn a scheme away from him (12:33), he asks to be put in charge of the storehouses (12:55), and he asks at the end to die as one who submits (12:101). Here he asks a fellow prisoner to say a word on his behalf. The request is small, human and entirely reasonable: a man cleared of every charge, held without a term, appeals to the only person leaving the building who has access to power. The chapter records it without criticism and without endorsement, and it records the consequence in the same breath. That consequence is the reason the verse exists. The sūrah is not interested in the propriety of the request; it is interested in what the request cost.

**"The One He Knew Would Survive"**

The description is precise and it restates the gift. Joseph knows which of the two men will live, and he addresses his appeal to that one. Nothing in the text suggests he chose him out of favour; the choice follows from the interpretation he has just delivered. The clause also has a narrative function. By identifying the man as the survivor, the verse forecloses the possibility that the message failed because it was given to the wrong person. The right man was selected, the right instruction was given, and the message still did not arrive. What defeated it was not incompetence but forgetfulness, and the verse assigns that forgetfulness to a specific agent. Naming Satan as the agent does something unexpected: it removes blame from the man who forgot and from Joseph who asked. Neither is shown acting badly. A palace servant whose position was owed to a prisoner's help had every reason to keep that debt quiet, and the verse records the resulting silence as an operation of the same force that has directed the chapter's opposition from the start.

**Satan Is Named as the Cause**

*Fa-ansāhu al-shayṭānu dhikra rabbih* — Satan made him forget the mention of his lord. The verb is causative: an act of forgetting produced in someone. The Qur'an attributes the failure to Satan rather than to the cupbearer's carelessness, which raises the stakes of the verse considerably. A man who simply forgot is a minor character in a minor lapse; a man made to forget by an adversary is part of a contested operation. The chapter has already established the terms of that contest, in the father's warning that Satan is a sworn enemy to humankind (12:5) and in the sūrah's use of *kayd* for both human and diabolic planning. The forgetfulness is therefore not an accident in the plot. It is the plot's continuation by other means. The brothers used a well and a lie; the women used a locked door and an accusation; here the instrument is memory. Each stage of the opposition adapts to the position Joseph happens to occupy, and none of them needs a villain to appear on the page. That economy is a large part of why the sūrah reads as a single sustained argument rather than as a set of episodes.

**Two Readings of Who Forgot**

The Arabic allows both, and the commentators preserve both. On the first reading, the object of the forgetting is the cupbearer: Satan made him forget to mention Joseph to his master. On the second, the object is Joseph himself — Satan made him forget the remembrance of his Lord, meaning that in the moment of asking a human being to intercede he neglected to rely on God. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have held the second reading, with the consequence that the years in prison were the price of that neglect. The first reading fits the sentence more naturally, since the man who is said to have forgotten is the one just addressed. The second is theologically heavier and the tradition kept it alive precisely because of that weight. Both readings agree on the fact that matters: a request was made and nothing came of it.

**"Several Years" Is Left Unquantified**

The phrase is *bidʿa sinīn*, a number of years — the word *bidʿ* covering the range from three to nine, deliberately imprecise. The commentators supply seven, and verse 45 confirms a long interval by describing the cupbearer's remembering as happening *after a period*. The Qur'an's refusal to give a figure is consistent with the way it handles Joseph's suffering generally: the length is stated as long, not as a number, because the number would invite calculation and the experience does not reduce to one. What the imprecision does accomplish is a change of register. The chapter has been counting things carefully — eleven stars, twenty dirhams, seven cows, seven ears — and at the point where Joseph's own endurance is at issue the counting stops. The silence has a clear effect whatever its intent. Estimates in the exegetical literature run from a few years to seven or to twelve, and the Qur'an commits to none of them. What survives the ambiguity is the shape of the sentence: one request, one lapse, and a long undated interval in which the reader is told of nothing happening at all.

**The Delay Places Him Where the Story Needs Him**

Read as arrangement rather than misfortune, the extra years do decisive work. Had the cupbearer remembered immediately, Joseph would have been released into a country with no famine and no need of him, a freed slave with a discredited record and no position. Instead he is still in the prison when the king dreams, and the only man at court who can connect the dream to an interpreter is the man who served time beside him. The chain that runs from verse 42 to verse 45 to verse 54 to verse 56 requires the delay at every link. This is the sūrah's consistent structure: a wrong or a failure produces the exact conditions a later good requires, and the narrator never explains the mechanism, only the sequence.

**The Tradition's Comment on the Sentence**

The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have commented on Joseph's conduct at the end of this period, in a narration preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī from Abū Hurayrah: after mentioning Abraham's request for reassurance and Lot's wish for a strong support, he said that if he had remained in prison as long as Joseph did, he would have answered the caller — that is, he would have accepted the offer of release without first insisting that his innocence be publicly established (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3372; the same wording is carried in the tafsīr book at Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4694 and in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The remark is a comparison in Joseph's favour, not a criticism. It identifies the request in verse 42 as the smaller matter and the insistence in verse 50 as the greater one. A separate report circulating in the tafsīr literature — that Joseph would not have stayed in prison so long had he not said what he said — is a different matter: Ibn Kathīr records it and rejects it, stating that every chain by which it has been transmitted is weak. The authenticated wording is the one that praises Joseph's patience rather than the one that faults his words.
