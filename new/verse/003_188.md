## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:188

> Do not let those who rejoice in their misdeeds and love to take credit for what they have not done think they will escape torment. They will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning About Self-Satisfaction**

The verse warns against supposing that people who exult in what they have brought about, and who want to be praised for what they have not done, are safe from punishment. The sentence describes a combination of satisfaction with oneself and a desire for credit that is not deserved.

The Book's warnings about hypocrisy usually concentrate on one of these elements, and the verse before the reader names both of them in a single sentence. The result is a portrait of people who are pleased with their own record and who also solicit approval for accomplishments that are not theirs, and the Book treats the combination as a single condition of the heart. The warning also addresses the community that recites it, since the desire for credit is not a condition confined to the group at which the verse was first aimed.

**The Repetition of the Prohibition**

The verse states its prohibition twice, at the beginning and in the middle of the sentence, and the repetition is a feature of the Book's rhetoric when a matter requires emphasis. The second occurrence interrupts the description of the people and resumes it after the warning has been restated.

The construction is unusual even by the standards of the Book, and the commentators noted it as a mark of the seriousness with which the conduct described is regarded. The effect is to make the sentence a warning about the people described rather than a statement about them, and the hearer is placed on guard before the description has been completed.

**What the Two Traits Involve**

Al-Rāzī explains the combination as a state in which a person takes his happiness from the goods and pleasures of the world while wishing to be regarded as sincere and devout. The two parts of the description belong together, since the desire for a reputation for piety is what makes the self-satisfaction dangerous.

The Book's warnings about the desire for praise run through its instructions about giving, praying, and fasting, and the sentence in this verse states the matter in its most compact form. A person who wants credit for what he has not done has made his reputation the object of his effort, and the verse places that condition among those that lead to punishment.

**The Reports About the Occasion**

The commentators record several occasions to which the verse has been connected. One report links it to hypocrites who stayed behind when the Prophet marched out, sought his pardon on his return, and then asked to be praised for conduct they had not engaged in. Others refer it to the hypocrites in general, who wanted credit for a belief they did not hold.

Al-Rāzī records further possibilities: that it refers to the People of the Book who distorted their scriptures and then expected to be honoured as devout, or that it refers to the concealment mentioned in the verse immediately before this one, where the concealing was combined with a claim to learning. All of the occasions describe the same pattern, and the verse covers it whichever report is preferred.

**Hypocrisy as Social Conduct**

The Book treats hypocrisy not only as a matter of belief but as a pattern of public behavior, and the passage describes people who were visible in the community and knew how to present themselves. The conduct described in this verse is social in character: a person seeks credit from others.

The Book's warnings about self-exaltation elsewhere make the same point. One passage asks whether those who elevate themselves have not noticed that God raises whom He wills, and another tells people not to claim purity for themselves since God knows best who is mindful. The verse before the reader states the social form of the same error.


The verse states that the people described are not outside the reach of punishment and that a painful punishment awaits them. The wording denies the assumption the people themselves were making, which is what the repeated prohibition is designed to address.

The phrase about not being delivered echoes the language used earlier in the sūrah for those who traded belief for disbelief. The Book's descriptions of punishment in this section are stated in almost identical words, and the repetition ties the various forms of the same conduct to the same outcome.

**Why the Sentence Is Repeated**

The double prohibition is the sentence's most distinctive feature, and its function is to leave no room for the assumption that the people described had nothing to fear. The Book uses repetition in this way when it addresses a condition that people do not recognise in themselves.

The community that recites the verse is asked to examine its own attitude toward reputation and toward the credit it expects for its conduct. The conduct described is common enough that the warning cannot be read as applying only to the hypocrites of the first generation, and the sentence's emphasis serves the later readers as much as the first ones.

**What the Community Learns**

The verse teaches the community to be suspicious of its own satisfaction and of its desire for the credit that comes with a good name. What the Book recommends instead is stated in the verses that follow, which describe people who remember God in every posture and reflect on the creation.

The sūrah is drawing toward the close of its long argument, and the sentences in this section deal with the difference between the people who use religion for their standing and the people whose conduct is aimed at God alone. The next verse states who owns what the first group is trying to secure.
