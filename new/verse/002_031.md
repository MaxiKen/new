## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:31

> He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, “Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true? ”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Arrives as a Curriculum**

The reply to the angels' question — *I know what you do not know* — is now unpacked, and the unpacking is not an argument but a lesson plan: *wa-ʿallama Ādama al-asmāʾa kullahā*, He taught Adam the names, all of them. The vicegerent's qualification is announced in the medium of instruction. Not power, not sinlessness, not seniority in creation — taught knowledge, comprehensive in scope, is what the office runs on. The verse's verb choice deserves weight: *ʿallama*, the intensive form of teaching, the same verb the angels will use of themselves in their confession — *what You have taught us* — so that the scene establishes one epistemology for every creature present: all knowledge in the room is taught knowledge, differing in scope, not in kind. And the scope given to Adam is total — *kullahā*, all of them. The demonstration to follow will show why the announcement of verse 30 was made in confidence: the being the angels questioned has just been equipped with something the questioners do not possess, and the equipment is public, examinable, and about to be examined.

**Which Names? The Exegetical Range**

The commentators debated the inventory, and the debate maps the verse's reach. Ibn ʿAbbās read the names as the vocabulary of human discourse — man, sea, mountain, down to the kettle, the whole working lexicon by which people name and address their world. Mujāhid took the widest scope: the names of everything, a complete nomenclature of existence. Others proposed narrower lists — the names of the angels, or of Adam's descendants. Al-Ṭabarī preferred the last, partly on a grammatical ground worth recording: the pronoun used for the presented things is *hum*, the plural reserved for persons, where non-persons normally take the feminine singular — though the commentators concede the rule is not absolute, citing counter-instances like the beasts referred to by *hum* in 24:45. Al-Rāzī drew the linguistic corollary: Adam was taught all the languages of the earth, and his descendants specialized into particular tongues over time. Weighing the readings, the grammatical argument for persons is strong but defeasible, and the safest construal keeps both poles — the names of things and of people, objects and offspring, the lexicon and the genealogy. The verse is claiming, at minimum, that the first man was given the complete naming-capacity: everything in his world could be called, classified, and addressed.

**Adam From the Earth's Whole Surface**

The exegetes attach to this scene a report about Adam's making that explains humanity's variety. His name is derived by some from *adīm*, the surface of the earth, and a prophetic saying preserved in the ḥadīth collections states the derivation as fact: Allah created Adam from a handful taken from the entire earth — and the children of Adam correspond to the earth, some red, some black, some white, some in between, and so in temperament: ease and difficulty, good and bad. The report does three things for the verse. It grounds the first human in the whole planet rather than a locality — the vicegerent is made of the estate he will administer, sampled from every province of it. It explains difference within the species as compositional rather than hierarchical: the handful was mixed at the source. And it ties Adam's creation to the earth of verses 22 and 29 — the soil furnished for humanity is the soil humanity is furnished from. The naming-knowledge of this verse is thus given to a creature whose body is already a catalogue of the earth, microcosm holding macrocosm, fitted to name what he is made of.

**The Presentation and the Demand**

The scene's staging is precise: *thumma ʿaraḍahum ʿalā al-malāʾikah* — He presented them to the angels. What was presented, the commentators note, is either the named objects themselves or the names — the exhibit is deliberately not itemized, and the omission is the point of the following clause: *anbiʾūnī bi-asmāʾi hāʾulāʾ*, tell Me the names of *these*. The demonstrative "these" presumes a display the angels are looking at and the reader is not shown; the narrative withholds the inventory from everyone except the parties being tested. The demand's closing condition — *in kuntum ṣādiqīn*, if you are truthful — imports the sūrah's idiom of testing: the same conditional closed the literary challenge of 2:23, *if what you say is true*. Truth-claims in the Qur'an are consistently cashed out in performance — produce a sūrah, name the exhibits — and the condition here is not a doubt of angelic honesty but the standing rule of the scene: what you claim to know, demonstrate. The angels made a claim in verse 30 about their glorifying and sanctifying, and about the corruption of the announced heir; the demonstration will sort the claim.

**The Test the Angels Cannot Pass — and Its Fairness**

The demand is not a trick. It measures a real difference the verse has already installed: Adam was taught *all* the names; the angels, as their coming confession will state, hold only what they were taught for their functions. Ibn ʿArabī's reading sharpens the difference into a metaphysics: the angels in relation to the world are like the faculties of a person — each fixed to its office, each limited to what it is, and each thereby veiled from what is above it; Adam alone was given the synthesis, the capacity to hold all the names together and through them to know the Namer in a way no single-faculty knower can. On that reading the test is fair because it asks each party only for what was given it: the angels cannot name the exhibits because their knowledge is functional, and functional knowledge is exactly what verse 30's question was built on — they reasoned from their office. The superiority being demonstrated is therefore not moral but structural: the vicegerent's rank rests on comprehensive naming, the capacity to classify the whole and address it — which is also, not coincidentally, a description of what science, law, and language do.

**Naming as the Root of the Human Estate**

The verse's largest claim is anthropological. What distinguishes the human being in the Qur'an's founding story is neither sinlessness — the angels hold that — nor strength, nor age in creation, but taught, comprehensive, articulable naming. Every human science extends this endowment: taxonomy, grammar, law, medicine, astronomy are unfoldings of *al-asmāʾ*, the capacity to fix a name to a thing and thereby to hold the thing in discourse, transmit it, deliberate about it. The Islamic tradition read the verse exactly so — the superiority of knowledge over worship-only, the scholar's rank above the merely devout — and the story's outcome institutionalized the lesson: the demonstration of naming settled the hierarchy of the scene without conflict, without a single act of power. Authority in this narrative is decided by curriculum. The vicegerency announced in verse 30 turns out to be an epistemic office — deputy of the One Who knows all things (2:29), equipped with the names, installed over the earth that was furnished for him — and the reader inherits the estate with the capacity that conveys it: whatever you can truly name, you can truly steward.
