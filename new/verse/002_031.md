## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:31

> He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, “Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true? ”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Teaching as the First Act in the Scene**

The proof that follows the angels' question is not a display of power. It is a lesson, and the lesson is given to the creature whose appointment was being questioned. The verse does not say that Adam was made stronger, holier or more obedient than the angels; it says he was taught, and it places that fact before the demonstration that follows.

The Book treats knowledge as a gift from God rather than an achievement of the knower, and it does so consistently. The Qur'an describes writing and instruction as among the earliest favours shown to humanity: *“Who taught by the pen— / taught humanity what they knew not.”* (96:4–5). Another sūrah opens by tying revelation itself to the same verb: *“The Most Compassionate / taught the Quran”* (55:1–2). In this verse the subject of the verb is God and the student is Adam, and the pattern of giving followed by receiving is the same one that governs the whole Book's account of knowledge.

**What Was Taught, and How the Commentators Read "Names"**

The Arabic is precise: the names, all of them. Commentators differed on the scope of the phrase. Some took it as the vocabulary human beings use among themselves for ordinary things — a man, a sea, a mountain, a pot. Others read it as the names of everything that exists. A third view restricted it to the names of the angels, and a fourth took it as the names of Adam's descendants, a reading al-Ṭabarī preferred because the pronoun attached to the presentation is the masculine plural, which in the Book's usage ordinarily refers to beings with reason.

Al-Rāzī drew a wider conclusion from the same evidence: if Adam was taught the names of all things, then he was taught the languages in which things are named, and the diversity of tongues among his descendants is a later development rather than an original condition. Whatever the scope of the teaching, the verse gives the first human being a competence the angels do not possess, and it presents that competence as the reason the question in the previous verse received the answer it did.

**The Presentation of the Named Things**

The verse says the things themselves were placed before the angels, and then the challenge was issued: give the names. Commentators noted an ambiguity in the Arabic — what was presented could be the names or the objects named — and the practical sense of the scene is unaffected, because the angels are being asked to demonstrate knowledge of the created world they have lived alongside.

The Book does not describe how the presentation looked. That silence matters for how the passage is read. The scene's meaning lies in the exchange — a question asked, a test set, an answer withheld — rather than in the visual arrangement, and the sūrah's next two verses complete the exchange. What is on display is not a spectacle but a difference in knowledge.

**"If What You Say Is True"**

The closing clause refers back to the words the angels had spoken in the previous verse. Their question about corruption and bloodshed amounted to a claim about the relative worth of the new creation, and the challenge here asks them to support it. The wording is the same style of challenge the sūrah has already used against the doubters of the revelation, who were invited to produce a sūrah like it if their position was sound.

The Book's argumentative habits are consistent in this regard. When a claim is made against God's arrangement, the Book asks for the evidence, and it does so in the language of ordinary dispute rather than of reproach. The angels are not accused of anything, and no punishment is mentioned. They are asked to substantiate the implication of their own question, and the verse ends before they answer.

**Knowledge as the Qualification for the Appointment**

The placement of this verse explains what the appointment of a *khalīfah* on the earth is to consist of. It is not a promotion over the angels or a licence to act without account; it is a responsibility carried by a creature able to recognise, name and therefore think about what he has been given. The Book elsewhere ties the human being's standing to faculties of this kind, and it holds those faculties accountable when they are neglected.

Sūrah al-Isrāʾ records the same scene and adds the command that the angels prostrate to Adam, which the sūrah will report in a few verses' time. The teaching in this verse is placed before the prostration, so that the honour given to Adam rests on the knowledge he was granted. That order is the sūrah's answer to the question about corruption: the creature being appointed is one who can understand what he is doing, and who can therefore be held to it.

**A Report About the Clay He Was Made From**

The tradition preserves a report from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī that the Prophet ﷺ described the making of Adam in related terms: God created him from a handful taken from the whole of the earth, so that human beings resemble the earth — some red, some white, some black, mixed, smooth and rough, good and bad (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4693, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī; the same report appears in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2955 with a ḥasan chain). The report connects the diversity of human temperaments and appearances to the material of the first creation, and the verse's claim that Adam was taught the names of all things fits a creature made from a mixture.
