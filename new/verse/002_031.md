## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:31

> He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, “Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true? ”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Teaching That Distinguishes the Khalīfah**

“He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, ‘Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true?’” (2:31). The scene follows directly from “I know what you do not know” (2:30). What God knows and has not yet shown includes a capacity given to Adam that the angels, in their excellence, do not possess in the same synthetic form: to be taught al-asmāʾ — the names — all of them.

“Taught” — ʿallama — repeats the verb that will anchor the Qur’an’s first revealed words: “He taught man what he knew not” (96:5). Knowledge here is not self-acquired but received. The khalīfah is a taught deputy, not an autonomous knower.

**What “Names” Means — Three Classical Readings**

The source lists three main views without forcing exclusivity:

- The names are those humans use in discourse — “man, sea, mountain, kettle” — the lexicon by which world is ordered and communicated, attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās. On this view, Adam is taught language itself as institution: the ability to name and thus to classify, call, and covenant.
- The names are the names of all things — a universal naming, in Mujāhid’s gloss. This widens the first view to every kind that falls under language.
- The names are the names of angels, or of Adam’s progeny, favored by al-Ṭabarī partly on pronoun grounds: hum, the masculine plural for persons (hum for angels and humans, hāʾ/kulluhā for things). The pronoun hum suggests the “presented” items include personal beings, not only objects, though Arabic usage is not rigid (cf. 24:45 where hum refers to beasts).

Al-Rāzī draws from the universality the inference that Adam was taught all languages, with descendants later preferring one tongue after dispersion; al-Ṭabrisī extends the thought to post-Flood diversification. Whether or not one adopts that particular inference, the core stands: Adam receives comprehensive naming capacity, the semiotic key to the world furnished “for you” (2:29).

**Laid Them Before the Angels — What Was Presented?**

“Then He presented them to the angels” (2:31) — ʿaraḍahum. What was laid before the angels is ambiguous by design: it may be the named things themselves displayed, or the names as intelligibles, or the persons designated by those names. The ambiguity serves the test: the angels are asked to name what they see, and their ability to name will disclose their scope.

**“If What You Say Is True?” — Condition of Claim, Not Threat**

“Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true” (2:31) — in kuntum ṣādiqīn — refers back to the angelic contrast in 2:30: that they glorify and sanctify without fitna. The condition is not menace but disclosure: if the standard is worship without corruption, and if the claim is that such worship suffices without a creature who can also name and thus steward, then show naming that matches that claim. The next verse will show the angels cannot.

**Why Naming Is Governance**

To name is to govern. Without names, there is no contract, no law, no measure, no promise kept or broken, no “ties Allah has ordered to be maintained” (2:27). The mosquito parable (2:26) and the register of corruption (2:27) and the biography of return (2:28) all rely on distinctions that only a naming creature can bear. Law descends to a namer; guidance is addressed to a comprehender; trust is laid upon one who can be called and can answer by call.

This also explains why the test is not praising — in which angels excel — but naming. Khalīfah requires more than unbroken tasbīḥ; it requires the capacity to articulate creation’s kinds, relate them, and act upon them with word-bound responsibility.

**Adam — A Name from Adīm**

A lexical connection noted in early commentary links the name Ādam to adīm of the earth — its outer face or soil. Related to this is a widely transmitted report — UNVERIFIED in this exact phrasing — stating that the first human was formed from dust gathered from diverse regions, which explains why his offspring differ: in complexion and in life-path, some finding ease and some meeting hardship, some inclined to good and some to other. The resonance between dust and spirit lies in the name itself: earthly adīm bearing divinely taught capacity. The wording appears often in tafsīr collections, yet specific chains vary and require caution before attributing the precise phrasing to the Prophet ﷺ. Its sense, however, aligns with Qur’anic motifs of clay and earth giving rise to a naming creature empowered by teaching.

**Ibn ʿArabī’s Angle — Synthesis vs. Specialization**

The source preserves Ibn ʿArabī’s reflection: angels, in their cosmic function, are like spiritual and sensory faculties in the human — each excellent within its domain and veiled beyond it. Each angel glorifies God through the names it possesses; the human, as synthesis (majmaʿ), bears capacity for the gamut. Thus angels “did not possess the synthesis possessed by Adam” and did not know to glorify God through that full range. The point spiritualizes without mythologizing: excellence in specialization is not the same as comprehensiveness. The khalīfah is not more luminous than an angel in purity; he is more gathering in scope.

**The Limits of Claim**

The angels’ implied claim — we are more noble than any creature God might create — is identified in the source’s gloss for the next verse’s “what you conceal” as a thought some traditions attribute to them. Whether or not one adopts that specific wording, the structure of 2:31 exposes a general rule: to claim superiority is to be asked to demonstrate superiority on the point claimed. They are asked to name, and naming will reveal rank on the exact point where rank matters for trust on earth.

**Living the Verse**

InFormation shapes re-formation. If Adam is distinguished by being taught names, then the contemporary khalīfah is repaired by being re-taught names: accurate names for things. Much corruption (2:27) begins by misnaming — calling usury “profit,” oppression “order,” waste “growth,” severance “independence.” To learn true names is already to begin repair. And in education and daʿwah, the model here is divine pedagogy before examination: teaching precedes testing. The angels are not tested on what they were not taught; the failure is real because the teaching to Adam was real.

**Hinge to the Next Scene**

2:31 stages a distinction without contempt. The angels are not shamed; they are shown the frontier of their knowledge. That frontier, once acknowledged, will become their own glorification in the next words: “Glory be to You” (2:32). The movement from “I know what you do not know” (2:30) to “Tell Me the names” (2:31) to “We have no knowledge except what You have taught us” (2:32) is a three-step pedagogy in recognizing divine teaching as the sole source of true naming.
