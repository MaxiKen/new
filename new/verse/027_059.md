## Sūrah an-Naml 27:59

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Praise be to Allah, and peace be upon the servants He has chosen.” ˹Ask the disbelievers,˺ “Which is better: Allah or whatever ˹gods˺ they associate ˹with Him˺?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Changes Direction**

Fifty-eight verses of narrative end, and this one opens with an instruction: *qul*, say. The Prophet is told what to recite, and what follows is a series of questions aimed at the living rather than reports about the dead.

The hinge is well placed. Four peoples have just been shown receiving evidence and deciding, three of them badly. The chapter now stops describing decisions and demands one. Everything in the remaining verses is either a question or an answer to a question, and the questions are addressed to people in Mecca who worship beside God what cannot hear them. The narratives were the argument's evidence; this section is the argument. What changes is not the subject but the grammar, from reports about others to questions put to the reader.

**Praise and Peace Before the Challenge**

Before the question is put, two things are said. Praise belongs to God, and peace be upon the servants He has chosen. Only then does the interrogation begin.

The order is not decorative. A polemical question that opens with praise of God establishes that the speaker's position is not primarily against anything, and the salutation on the chosen servants ties the challenge to the line of messengers the chapter has just recounted — Mūsā, Dāwūd, Sulaymān, Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ. Those five were the *chosen servants* of the preceding narratives, and greeting them here links the history to the argument that follows. There is also a formal effect. The section that is about to ask five times whether there is another god beside God begins by giving God what is owed to Him, which is the practical answer to its own question.

**The Question That Frames the Rest**

*Ā-Llāhu khayrun ammā yushrikūn* — is God better, or what they associate? This is the general form of which the following verses are instances, and the question is a strange one on its face.

Taken literally it invites a comparison, and a comparison implies commensurability — two items on one scale. The Qur'an does not usually permit that. What makes the question effective is that it accepts, for the sake of argument, the framework its opponents live by. They treat their deities as beneficial: sources of rain, intercession, protection, good fortune. Very well, says the question: compare. The verses that follow supply the terms of the comparison — creation, water, gardens, guidance, answering the distressed — and each one belongs entirely to one side. The argument is won not by refusing the comparison but by making it.

**"Better" Is a Deliberately Mild Word**

The comparative is *khayr*, better, and its mildness is calculated. The verse does not ask whether the idols are false, powerless or imaginary, though the Qur'an asserts all three elsewhere. It asks which is better.

That framing makes the question difficult to refuse. A Meccan could decline to debate whether his gods existed; declining to say which is better forces him either to name his gods as superior, which nobody in that religious system claimed, or to concede the point and keep worshipping anyway. The mildness also matches how the Qur'an characterises Meccan paganism generally. These were not people who denied God; they acknowledged Him as creator and attached intermediaries. A question about relative rank is therefore aimed precisely at the actual belief, which was never that the idols outranked God but that they were worth addressing alongside Him.

**A Section Built on Repetition**

What begins here runs for several verses with a fixed shape. Each states something God does — creating the heavens and earth, sending water, fixing the earth, answering distress, guiding through darkness, originating creation — and each ends with the same refrain asking whether there is a god beside Him.

The repetition is the method. A single instance could be argued with; six instances covering cosmology, agriculture, geography, prayer, navigation and provision leave no domain in which the associated deities have work to do. The construction is also grammatically pointed. Each question begins with a relative clause — *He who created*, *He who made* — of a kind that sets up a comparison and then does not complete it, arriving instead at the refrain. The implication carried by that incompleteness is that no explicit comparison should be offered at all. The first of the six arrives in the next verse and begins with the largest claim available.

**Who the Chosen Servants Are**

The greeting falls on *ʿibādihi alladhīna iṣṭafā* — His servants whom He has chosen. The verb *iṣṭafā* means to select out, to take the best part of something, and the Qur'an uses it of prophets and of certain communities.

Nothing in the phrase identifies which servants are meant, and the commentators reasonably read it as covering the messengers named in the chapter just concluded. That reading fits the placement exactly. Five prophets have been described in the preceding verses, and the salutation arrives at the moment their stories end. There is also a quiet argument in giving them peace at this point. The narratives showed three of them rejected, one of them threatened with expulsion, another accused of bringing bad luck; the greeting states the actual verdict on men whose own communities delivered a different one. Before the chapter asks the Meccans to compare God with their gods, it corrects the record on the people who first put the question to them. The men their own societies expelled, accused and plotted against are the ones on whom peace is pronounced here. The structure of the verse moves from a command to speak, through a statement of praise, to a question, and each step narrows the ground available to the audience.
