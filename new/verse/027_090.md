## Sūrah an-Naml 27:90

> And whoever comes with an evil deed will be hurled face-first into the Fire. Are you rewarded except for what you used to do?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Matching Half**

This verse completes the pair begun in the previous one. The same construction opens it — whoever comes bringing — and the object is reversed. One arrives with a good deed, one with an evil deed, and the two outcomes are set beside each other.

The parallel makes the difference visible. The good deed received something better than itself; the evil deed receives exactly itself, as the closing question states. Nothing is added on the negative side. That asymmetry is the Qur'an's consistent position on the two ledgers, stated elsewhere as a tenfold return for good and a like-for-like return for wrong, and this verse is where an-Naml records it.

**What the Evil Deed Is**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both identify the *sayyi'ah* here as *shirk* — associating partners with God. The identification is the mirror of the good deed in the previous verse, which they took as the testimony of God's oneness.

Reading the pair this way makes the two verses a summary of the chapter rather than a general statement about ethics. An-Naml opened by describing believers who pray, give alms and are certain of the Hereafter, and it has spent its length on one question: who is worshipped. Sheba prostrated to the sun. Pharaoh claimed a status he did not have. Thamūd and Lūṭ's people rejected the messengers sent to them. The final assessment scene sorts by the same criterion the whole sūrah has been applying.

**"Face-First"**

The phrase is *kubbat wujūhuhum fī al-nār* — their faces cast down into the Fire. The Qur'an uses similar imagery in several chapters, describing the guilty gathered upon their faces or dragged upon them.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both note that in Arabic idiom the face, like the head or the neck, can stand for the whole person, so that "their faces" may mean simply "they." On that reading the violence of the image is a figure of speech rather than a posture. The other reading takes the detail literally, matching the passages elsewhere that describe the guilty cast down inverted. Either way the connotation is the same in Arabic as in English: the face is where dignity resides, and to be put on it is to be humiliated rather than merely harmed.

**The Face in This Chapter**

There is a reason the detail lands where it does. The chapter has used the face for status throughout, and the contrast with its earlier scenes is sharp.

Sheba's queen entered a palace and was told to look at a floor; Sulaymān's response to the arrival of her throne was to lower himself in gratitude. The chapter's whole moral vocabulary has been about who bows and to what. A person who declined to lower his face voluntarily is described here as arriving at the same position involuntarily. That is not a flourish added for effect; it is the logic the sūrah has been building since the hoopoe reported a nation prostrating to the wrong object.

**The Closing Question**

The verse ends by turning to the reader: *hal tujzawna illā mā kuntum taʿmalūn* — are you repaid for anything other than what you used to do? The address shifts to the second person, and the shift is abrupt.

Up to this point the verse has described other people in the third person. The question breaks that frame and puts it to whoever is listening. Al-Rāzī records the alternative that the question is spoken to those being cast down, as they are cast down, which would keep it inside the scene. Both readings are available and the ambiguity may be intended: a question addressed to the condemned is overheard by the living, and its force is the same in either position.

**Nothing Is Added to the Penalty**

The question's content is a claim about justice and it is defensive in form. It anticipates an objection — that the outcome is excessive — and answers it by pointing out the exact correspondence between what was done and what is received.

The chapter has made this argument before in narrative. Thamūd's nine plotted and were destroyed by a plot; the word for their scheming and for what met it is identical. Lūṭ's people had been warned and received what the chapter calls the rain of those who were warned. In each case the penalty was described in the vocabulary of the offence. The closing question here states the principle those scenes illustrated: the return is the act, given back.

**The End of the Argument**

With this verse the chapter's case is complete. It has presented the Book, told four histories, asked six questions about the natural world, answered the demand for a date, described the record, staged the judgment, and now stated the rule by which the sorting is done.

What remains is three verses of instruction to the Prophet — what he was commanded, what he is to recite, and what to say to those who refuse. The argument does not end with a summary or an appeal. It ends with a man being told his job and a statement that the signs will yet be shown. That is a characteristic close for a chapter which has insisted throughout that evidence is available, the reading of it is the reader's own work, and the verdict belongs to someone else.

**A Rule, Not a Threat**

The second half of the verse is phrased as a question rather than as a warning, and the difference in register is deliberate. Threats describe what someone will do to you; this asks you to check an equation.

The Qur'an uses interrogatives throughout an-Naml for exactly this purpose, and the chapter has been full of them: is there a god besides God, have they not travelled, do they not see, did you deny without knowledge. Each puts the burden of the answer on the hearer. Ending the assessment scene the same way keeps the pattern intact to the last. A reader is not being told what will happen to him. He is being asked whether he can name any basis for the outcome other than his own conduct, and the question is rhetorical because there is none available.
