## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:47

> O Children of Israel! Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you and how I honoured you above the others.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Summons, Almost Word for Word**

The verse repeats the opening of the address given seven verses earlier, and the repetition is intentional. In the Qur'an, a command to remember that is issued twice marks the theme as central to the passage, and the intervening verses have in the meantime supplied content for the memory: the covenant, the confirmation of scripture, the prohibition on mixing truth with falsehood, the three commands, the rebuke about teaching, and the portrait of the humble.

So the reader reaches this verse with a filled-in picture of what remembering means. It is not nostalgia and it is not a compliment to ancestors. The Book's use of the verb here carries the sense of bringing something to mind in a way that changes present conduct, and the second issuance of the command makes the requirement unmistakable.

**"Above the Others," and What It Consisted Of**

The Book explains the favour in concrete terms rather than leaving it as an honorific. One verse lists the components: *“Indeed, We gave the Children of Israel the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood; granted them good, lawful provisions; and favoured them above the others.”* (45:16). Another summarises the same history from Moses' mouth: *“Remember Allah's favours upon you when He raised prophets from among you, made you sovereign, and gave you what He had never given anyone in the world.”* (5:20).

Elsewhere the Book states the choice in one line: *“And indeed, We chose the Israelites knowingly above the others.”* (44:32). The favour was therefore historical and specific. A people held in slavery received deliverance, a law, a land, and a continuous line of messengers, and the Book treats that record as the fact the community's identity rests on.

**Favour as a Trust Rather Than a Privilege**

The Book nowhere treats the election as an unconditional possession. It states the conditions attached to the favour in the same passage that commands the community to remember it, and it makes fidelity to the covenant the means by which the favour continues. The most striking statement of this appears in the description of the messenger awaited in their own scriptures: the one described in the Torah and the Gospel, who commands what is right and relieves burdens (7:157).

That description turns the favour into an examination. A community that has been given prophets and books is a community that has been given the means to recognise the next messenger when he arrives, and the Book's address to this audience assumes exactly that situation. What was granted as a gift functions in the argument of the sūrah as a responsibility that is now coming due.

**The Shift the Book Announces Elsewhere**

The Qur'an does not present the favour as a permanent entitlement that no later community can share. In a passage addressed to the believers, it declares: *“You are the best community ever raised for humanity—you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah.”* (3:110), and the same verse goes on to say that some among the People of the Book are faithful while most are rebellious. The favour in this verse is therefore a historical fact, not a claim that survives every generation unchanged.

The sūrah's audience was in a position to test this against its own situation. The community addressed in Madinah had the scripture, the learning and the standing that came with an old tradition, and the Book tells it that these assets are held on terms. The reminder of honour received is followed by the obligation to make one's conduct consistent with it.

**Why the Reminder Comes Before the Reckoning**

The verses that follow this one recount the favours one by one: the deliverance from Pharaoh, the parting of the sea, the appointment with Moses, and the forgiveness after the calf. They also record the failures in the same sequence, so that the section is a history with two columns. The Book begins with the good column, and it does so twice.

That order is not merely polite. The Book's purpose in the passage is to renew an invitation, and a recitation of failures that opened the section would have foreclosed the argument before it started. The reminder of favour is the ground on which the later questions rest, and the command to remember appears at the head of the passage because memory is what makes the rest of the address intelligible.

**Memory as Accountability**

The verb at the centre of this verse is used in the Book for a mental act with consequences. To remember a favour, in the Qur'an's usage, is to be aware of it in a way that shapes what one does next, and the Book's frequent pairing of memory with gratitude and with conduct follows from that. A person who forgets what he has received will read his own position as something he produced.

The community being addressed is therefore not asked to feel a particular way about its history. It is asked to keep in view the fact that its scripture, its teachers and its standing came from someone, and that the same someone is now speaking to it again. The verse names the favours and the honour in a single sentence, and it leaves the consequences of remembering to the verses that follow.

**How This Verse Sits in the Address**

The summons brackets a block of argument. The first occurrence opened the address with a command to remember the favours and honour the covenant; the verses between it and this one supplied the terms of that covenant, the instruction to believe what confirms the existing scripture, the prohibition on mixing and concealing, three commands, a rebuke about conduct, and a portrait of the people who find the requirements light. This second occurrence closes that block and turns it toward the historical record.

The repetition also prepares the reader for what follows. From the next verse onward the sūrah recites the favours in detail and then reports the failures that accompanied them, and the command to remember is what authorises the whole recitation. The audience is being asked to accept its own history as evidence, and the Book issues the summons twice so that nobody reaches the list of failures without having first been told what the list is for.
