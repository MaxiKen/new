## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:47

> O Children of Israel! Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you and how I honoured you above the others.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Bracket of the Address**

Seven verses after the first, the sūrah reopens the same envelope: *Yā Banī Isrāʾīl udh-kurū niʿmatiya* — O Children of Israel, remember My favor. Verse 40 spoke the address and the command to remember; verses 41–46 delivered the covenant's terms — believe, do not deny, do not sell, do not mix, do not conceal, pray, pay, bow, practice, seek help through patience and prayer, hold the certainty of return. Now verse 47 restates the opening in nearly the same breath, and the restatement marks a structural seam: the first unit of the Israel address closes and the second opens, the long recital of delivered history — Pharaoh, the sea, the calf, the forty nights, the manna, the rock — beginning under a fresh invocation of the favor. The Qur'an's method here is the method of a speaker who re-catches the listener's eye before each new section. The literal address "Children of Israel" occurs six times in this sūrah — at verses 40, 47, 83, 122, 211, and 246 — each occurrence a bracket around a unit of argument, and the word *remember* runs through seventeen of the verses between verse 40 and verse 122, the section's dominant verb. The repetition is not redundancy; it is the sūrah holding the addressee's attention across a prosecution that the addressee would otherwise interrupt.

**Niʿma: One Favor Containing Many**

The noun is singular — *niʿmatiya*, My favor — and the singular is deliberate. The favor recalled is not one item on a list but the whole endowment held as one: election, prophets, scripture, deliverance, provision, forgiveness. The enumerations follow because the singular must be cashed out — verse 49 opens the ledger with the deliverance from Pharaoh's people, and the sūrah will file entry after entry through the wilderness years. The grammatical possessive matters equally: *My* favor. The endowment is Allah's property conferred, not the people's attribute earned; the yāʾ of *niʿmatiya* keeps the direction of the gift visible in the word that names it. Remembering the favor is thus not nostalgia but acknowledgment — the debtor's act of holding the debt in mind — and the verse's placement shows what the acknowledgment is for: immediately after it comes the warning of verse 48, the day no soul will avail another. The favor remembered and the day announced are one argument: you were given everything, by Me, and the audit is coming, and nothing you were given transfers.

**Faḍḍala: A Preference Conferred, Not Possessed**

The second object of remembrance is the preference: *wa-annī faḍḍaltukum ʿalā al-ʿālamīn* — and that I preferred you above the peoples. The verb is Allah's act — *I* preferred — and the passive position of the people in the grammar enacts their position in the fact: recipients, not claimants. The word *ʿālamīn*, the peoples or worlds, sets the preference's scope wide, and the exegetes read its horizon historically: preferred above the peoples *of your time* — the generation among which the prophets, the book, and the deliverance were lodged. The preference is real and the verse does not soften it — the Qur'an files the election with the same stress — *"And indeed, We chose the Israelites knowingly above the others"* (44:32) — a choosing accompanied by full knowledge of what the chosen would do with it. But preference in the Qur'an is always functional and always conditioned: it designates a people for a trust, and the trust's tenure is the covenant. The same scripture that records the preference records its terms (5:12) and its revocation clauses — and verse 146 of Sūrah Āl ʿImrān and its neighbors will narrate how many a prophet's companions held on after great losses, the comparison doing quiet work: preference was offered to others too, and kept elsewhere.

**The Election and Its Bill**

The pairing inside the verse — favor *and* preference — sets up the unit that follows, and the setup is juridical. In covenant language, the recital of benefits is the preamble that grounds the obligations: *because* I favored you, the terms bind. The sūrah will exploit the structure in both directions. The blessings file the case for gratitude — and gratitude in the Qur'an is never sentiment but compliance, so every remembered favor becomes a question about behavior. And the preference files the case for accountability — the people raised above the peoples are audited above the peoples, which is why the coming recital is so unsparing: the calf, the refusal to enter, the killing of prophets, the price-selling of the signs. A reader who stops at verse 47 hears only the honor; the unit's design is that the honor cannot be stopped at, because the honor *is* the retainer. The verse thereby inoculates the sūrah's treatment of Israel against both distortions — the antisemitic reading that hears only indictment, and the triumphalist reading that hears only inheritance. What is recorded is a contract with a chosen party, its benefits itemized, its breaches itemized, and its renewal offered to whoever keeps its terms.

**Why the Address Uses the Father's Name**

*Banū Isrāʾīl* names the people by their ancestor — Isrāʾīl, the honorific of Yaʿqūb, servant of God — and the naming is itself part of the verse's courtesy and its argument. The Qur'an never addresses them as children of a failure; it recalls the patriarch whose wrestling earned him the title, and calls the descendants to the family resemblance. The prophets who will populate the recital — Mūsā, Hārūn, Dāwūd, Sulaymān, ʿĪsā — are all filed under this father's line, and the honorific keeps the line's nobility in the address even at the moments of severest charge. The exegetes note the rhetorical effect: a people reminded of *whose* children they are, are reminded of a standard that is theirs by genealogy — the standard of the one named Israel, who kept his wrestling faithful. The address thus performs what verse 44 demanded of its scholars: it begins with the good it asks for. Before the recital of breaches, before the warning of the day, the people are called by their noblest name and told to remember what was done *for* them. The indictment will be exhaustive; it is not the first word, and the first word is kept standing at the head of every unit — verses 40, 47, 122 — so that the whole prosecution is read in the register of a father's house, not an enemy's court.

**The Favor and the Ummah That Reads It**

Verse 47 has a second audience the sūrah is building toward. The preference "above the peoples" was historical and functional; al-Baqarah will soon announce its transfer — the community raised for the nations, commanding good and forbidding evil, believing in Allah (3:110), the *khayra ummah* whose qualification is not descent but direction. Madina's Muslims, listening to the address of Israel, are hearing the terms of their own election in draft: a people favored, covenanted, warned that the favor is a trust, and audited on its handling. That is why the recital runs to more than eighty verses — longer than any other sustained address in the sūrah — and why it is placed at the Book's head rather than buried in a late Meccan narrative. The new community is to learn covenant-consciousness from the fullest case file in revelation's possession: the people who received the most and the record of what they did with it. Verse 47 is the file's second cover page, and every reader of al-Baqarah opens it as an addressed party — reminded that favors are remembered or lost, and that preference, in Allah's arithmetic, is a debt before it is a distinction.
