## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:87

> And do not disgrace me on the Day all will be resurrected—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham Asks for Protection From Final Disgrace**

Abraham's prayer turns from his father's condition to his own standing on the Day of resurrection. He asks God not to disgrace him. The request does not suggest that Abraham expects disgrace as a necessary result. It expresses the humility of a servant who knows that the final day belongs to God's judgment and that honour before God cannot be secured by human reputation.

Disgrace is more than private pain. It is exposure before the gathered creation, the collapse of a person's claim to dignity, and the revelation that worldly status could not protect him. Abraham therefore asks for the opposite of being exposed as rejected. He wants to meet resurrection under God's acceptance.

**The Day Is Public in Scope**

The wording all will be resurrected gives the request a universal setting. No family, nation, or social rank will remain hidden behind a private world. Abraham's concern is not the opinion of one audience in one generation. It is his standing when every servant is gathered and the truth of every allegiance is made plain.

That universal setting makes worldly reputation small. A person may be honoured in a palace, praised by a community, or defended by descendants, but none of those judgments controls the resurrection. Abraham asks the Lord who governs that day to preserve him from the shame that follows rejection, hypocrisy, or false worship.

**Righteousness Does Not Produce Self-Satisfaction**

Abraham has just been described as one who rejects idols and seeks the Lord's wisdom. Yet he still asks not to be disgraced. His prayer shows that obedience does not create a right to boast. The servant may act faithfully and still depend on God's mercy for final acceptance.

This humility is not a denial of God's promises to His servants. It is the opposite of claiming security through one's own record. Abraham knows that good deeds are signs of loyalty, but God remains the Judge of hearts and outcomes. A believer can hope in God's acceptance without turning hope into a certificate written by the self.

**Disgrace Is Different From Every Form of Trial**

The request should not be read as a demand for a life without pain, loss, or difficulty. Abraham's story contains struggle, rejection, separation, and danger. The disgrace he fears belongs to the final meeting with God. A person may suffer in the world and still be honoured in the Hereafter; another may enjoy public success and still be exposed as spiritually ruined.

This distinction protects people from judging God's favour by comfort. Illness, poverty, exile, and opposition do not by themselves mean disgrace. Nor do wealth and applause prove acceptance. Abraham asks for final honour, the kind that survives the end of worldly appearances.

**The Prayer Recognizes the Seriousness of Accountability**

Some spiritual language becomes shallow when it treats judgment as a formality. Abraham's request restores its weight. Resurrection is not only a return to life; it is a return before the Lord. The servant's hopes, loyalties, and treatment of truth will matter there. Asking not to be disgraced means asking to be found among those whose allegiance was sound.

The prayer therefore calls the believer to examine the heart before that examination is imposed from outside. Are worship, repentance, and moral action directed toward God, or are they performed for an audience? Human beings can manage an appearance in this world. They cannot manage the final disclosure of what they truly served.

**God's Honour Is the Only Secure Honour**

Abraham's request places honour where it belongs. The Lord grants it, and the Lord can preserve it. A person who tries to secure dignity through idols, lineage, wealth, or power remains dependent on things that pass. The prayer turns the heart from borrowed status to the approval of the Creator.

That turn does not make a believer careless about good reputation. Trustworthiness and honour among people can serve useful purposes. But public respect must remain a means, not the final goal. Abraham asks for honour before God because every other form of honour ends at the grave or changes with the crowd.

**A Parallel Supplication Explains the Fear**

The believers' prayer in 3:192 also links being admitted to the Fire with disgrace and the absence of helpers. That parallel confirms that disgrace is a spiritual consequence of rejection, not simply an emotional embarrassment. Abraham's request is therefore a plea for God's protection from the final state in which no created support can repair the loss.

The connection also shows why his earlier prayer for forgiveness matters here. Mercy and honour belong together. The servant asks for pardon because he wants to meet the Day of resurrection as one accepted by God rather than as one whose public identity conceals a rejected heart.

The verse ends with a dash after naming the Day of resurrection. The pause holds the believer before the event rather than allowing the mind to move quickly past it. Abraham's prayer remains unfinished in the sense that every servant must carry it into his own life. The day is certain, but the personal outcome is not something to treat casually.

His words teach a disciplined hope. Work for truth, reject false worship, seek forgiveness, and ask God for final honour. The goal is not to escape every hardship or preserve a flattering image. It is to be raised among those whom the Lord does not disgrace. The prayer asks for a verdict that remains good when every temporary reputation has fallen away. It is a request for lasting dignity, not for escape from the honest consequences of a servant's choices.

That concern can shape the present without creating panic. The believer corrects the heart now because the final day will reveal what the heart became.
