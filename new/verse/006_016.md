## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:16

> Whoever is spared the torment of that Day will have certainly been shown Allah’s mercy. And that is the absolute triumph.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day Known by Its Torment Before Its Relief**

The verse names the Day by what people fear in it. Before it is described as anything else, it is the Day of the torment, and the one who escapes the torment is described as having been shown mercy. That is the Book's usual manner of speaking about the Hereafter when the audience is still undecided: the danger comes first, because the danger is what a person must take seriously in order to be saved from it.

There is no attempt to soften the picture or to suggest that the threat was exaggerated. The sūrah's argument depends on the audience believing that a real account is coming, and the mercy offered in this sentence would be meaningless if the torment it names were merely a figure of speech.

**Safety Described as Mercy, Not as Wages**

Notice the grammar of the escape. The one who is spared has been shown mercy; he has not been described as having earned his exemption. That is a significant choice of words in a Book that insists deed counts for something, and it reflects the Book's consistent position that the accounting is exact while the outcome is a gift. Even a person whose good deeds outweigh his bad ones arrives at the Day carrying a debt he cannot settle on his own.

The same framing appears when the righteous are described as praying for protection from the consequences of their evil deeds, since whoever is shielded from the evil of his actions on that Day has been shown mercy (40:9). The prayer of the best of people is therefore a request for mercy rather than a request for a fair wage, and this verse states the outcome of that request.

That wording also explains why the Book never lets its readers treat the Day as a negotiation. A wage can be argued about, disputed, or reduced by a clever advocate; a gift is either received or refused. By describing salvation as mercy shown, the verse puts the outcome in the category of something given rather than something owed, and it leaves the accountable person with no claim to press and nothing to plead except his need.

**"That Is the Absolute Triumph"**

In the four words at the end of the verse, the Book redefines winning. Escape from the Fire is not a rescue from a misfortune; it is an attainment, and the phrase is used elsewhere for a reward measured in Gardens and forgiveness of sins (61:12), and for the ultimate triumph of the saved (3:185). The scale of the language shows how the Book weighs the stakes it is describing.

For an audience that measured success in livestock, lineage, caravan profits, and victories in tribal skirmishes, the claim is a direct challenge. The Quraysh would have understood triumph as a matter of reputation and acquisitions in this world. The verse assigns the word to an outcome that happens after death, when none of those assets can be carried, and it does so without conceding any comparison between the two.

**Why Only This Victory Deserves the Word**

The Book is blunt about the alternative. Whoever is spared from the Fire and admitted into Paradise has truly triumphed, while the life of this world is nothing but the delusion of enjoyment (3:185). The contrast is drawn with a word that implies illusion rather than smallness: the pleasures are real, but the belief that they constitute a life's achievement is not.

Read in that light, the absolute triumph of this verse is not a supernatural version of a worldly win. It is the one achievement that survives the loss of everything the other achievements depend on. A man's victories in battle, his herds, and his reputation all end at the same border, and the verse states plainly what remains valuable when a person crosses it.

**The Sentence That Says Two Things at Once**

Placed directly after the announcement of the Prophet's own fear, the verse supplies the destination of that fear. The one who escapes has been shown mercy, and the escape is the greatest thing that could happen to a person. Fear and mercy are therefore not two competing portraits of God; they are the two halves of one transaction, with the accountable creature standing in the middle.

The word "that" carries the whole weight of the sentence. It points back to the torment, forward to the mercy, and settles on the triumph, and it treats the three as a single event viewed from three positions: the danger that was real, the gift that was given, and the deliverance that results from it. The verse's shortness is part of its force.

**What the Verse Asks Its Hearer to Prepare For**

No instruction follows the promise, and no list of deeds is attached. The verse is a statement about how the Day will be settled, and its absence of a to-do list is intentional in this place in the sūrah, where the audience has been arguing rather than asking for guidance. What it offers instead is a description of the only outcome worth arranging one's life around.

The arrangement itself has been described in the surrounding verses: submit, and do not join others with God (6:14); fear the Day if you disobey (6:15); and know that escape from it will come as mercy rather than as a settlement (6:16). The verse's quiet generosity depends on the reader having taken the earlier sentences seriously — mercy is offered, but only to those who needed to be spared.
