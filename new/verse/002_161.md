## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:161

> Surely those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers are condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condition Described**

The verse names those who disbelieve and die in that condition, and it states that the curse of God, of the angels, and of humanity will be upon them. The sentence's subject is not disbelief alone but disbelief that remains until death, and the wording identifies the point at which the matter is settled.

The Book's treatment of the previous verse has described a person who turns from his offence and is accepted, and the sentence here describes the person who does not. The contrast gives the reader the two outcomes, and the placement of the verses shows that what decides between them is not the offence but the response to it while life remains.

**The Meaning of the Curse**

The term rendered as curse is understood in the community's tradition to mean being driven away from God's mercy and distanced from His favour, and the commentators explained it in that sense rather than as an act of abuse. The curse of the angels and of the believers is their invocation of God's judgment on those who deserve it.

The Book uses the term in several contexts, and the community's scholars drew a distinction between the curse that is a statement about a condition and the curse that a person utters in anger. The sentence describes the first, and its subject is the condition of those who have removed themselves from mercy by their own choice.

**The Curse of Humanity**

The sentence states that all people share in the curse, and the commentators offered several readings. Some took the reference to humanity as covering everyone whose nature recoils from what is vile, others applied it to the believers whose judgment the Book describes, and others connected it with the Day of Judgment, when the followers of a misleader turn against him.

The variety of readings reflects the difficulty of the phrase, and it also shows how broadly the Book's statements about the hereafter apply. What the sentence establishes is that the condition of the person who dies in disbelief is recognised as what it is by God, by the angels, and by the creatures who know the truth, and the reader is not left with a private verdict.

**Al-Qurṭubī on Cursing the Dead**

The commentators discussed whether the community is commanded to curse such people, and al-Qurṭubī argued that the verse describes the hereafter rather than prescribing an action in this world. He noted that a curse functions as a warning and an expression of disapproval, which is of no use to a person who has died, and he connected the verse with a later passage in which the followers and the followed curse one another on the Day of Resurrection.

He also cited the Prophet's prohibition against abusing a person who is undergoing a legal punishment or one who has repented sincerely, and he concluded that a curse belongs only to the condition that deserves it. What the community's teachers drew from the discussion is that the verse is a statement about the state of those who die in disbelief rather than a licence for the living to abuse them.

**The Series of Curses in the Sūrah**

The verse belongs to a series in which the sūrah describes the consequences that follow certain offences. The Book has stated that those who conceal the truth are cursed by God and by the cursers, and it now states that those who die in disbelief have the curse of God, the angels, and humanity upon them.

The progression shows how the sūrah builds its account of accountability, and the reader is shown that the outcomes described in the passage are not arbitrary. Each sentence names the conduct and the condition that follows it, and the whole passage functions as a statement of the terms on which the community lives.

The Book’s use of the term in the sūrah runs from the refusal of the covenant to the account of those who die in their disbelief, and the community’s scholars read the passages as a graded series. What the sentence adds is the universality of the judgment, since the curse is attributed to God, to the angels, and to humanity together. The reader is shown that the condition described is recognised as such by every party that knows the truth, and the verse leaves him with no private standard by which to judge it.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Here**

The sentence follows the verse in which the road back is described, and its placement gives the reader the warning after the invitation. The Book's method in this passage is to state what is available before it states what is lost, and the reader is given the opportunity before the account of its forfeiture.

The verses that follow describe the permanence of the punishment and the oneness of God, and the passage closes with the call to worship the one whose signs fill the creation. The reader is thus carried from the account of concealment and its remedy to the description of the one against whom the offence was committed, and the movement gives the whole passage its shape.

The community’s teachers pointed to the placement when they explained the sūrah’s treatment of accountability, since the offer and the warning stand side by side. A reader who meets the sentence about repentance and then the sentence about those who die in disbelief is given the whole matter in the space of two verses, and the Book does not leave the outcome to inference. The passage therefore functions as a statement of what is available and what is at stake, and the following verses describe the condition of those who do not take the offer.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes that the fate of a person is decided by the condition in which his life ends, that the judgment on that condition is universal, and that the community's own verdicts are not what the passage is about. The reader is left with the seriousness of the matter and with the invitation of the preceding verse, and the passage continues with the account of the hereafter that gives the terms their weight.
