## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:161

> Surely those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers are condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Conditions and the Closed Door**

The verse states its subject with two joined conditions: those who disbelieve, *and die* as disbelievers. The second condition is what gives the verse its finality — and its justice. Disbelief alone, held by a living person, stands under the exception of the previous verse as long as breath remains; the door of repentance closes at death, not at sin. The sūrah will state the closure explicitly in its law of repentance: the return is not accepted from those who go on doing evil until death approaches (4:18), and the whole economy of 2:159–160 assumed a living audience. What this verse describes is the completed case — rejection carried through the whole span allotted for reconsideration and delivered, unrepented, to the far side. The conjunction of the two conditions also identifies the verse's true subject: not the disbeliever as a category of people walking the earth, but the fixed outcome of a life whose direction never turned. The community reading it is being shown the terminus of the road it was just offered the exit from.

**The Curse of God**

The penalty repeats the curse of 2:159 and completes its definition: *upon them is the curse of Allah* — the distance from His mercy made permanent. In the earlier verse the curse fell on an act, the hiding of clear proofs; here it falls on a settled state, disbelief consummated by death. The exegetes' definition governs both: to be cursed is to be driven away from God and excluded from His mercy. The permanence is new. A curse on an act can be lifted by the act's undoing — the repentance that clarifies — but a curse on a completed life has nothing left to undo it with; the material of repentance is time, and the verse's subjects have spent theirs. This is why the passage ordered the verses as it did: the act cursed (159), the door opened (160), the closed case sentenced (161). A reader who meets 161 without 160 will hear despair; a reader who meets both will hear the exact boundary — mercy is infinite in width and finite in duration, wide as any sin, long as any life.

**The Angels and the Curse They Voice**

The second curser is the angels. Al-Ṭabarī explains the sense: the curse of the angels and the believers is their invocation of God's curse upon those God has cursed — creatures ratifying the verdict of their Lord, echoing in the unseen what He has pronounced in the seen. The angels' participation is not additive to the divine curse but expressive of the order it establishes: the beings nearest the Throne, who know what the concealers and disbelievers refused to acknowledge, are aligned against the refusal. The sūrah of the Confederates states the same alignment in a curse-verse of its own — God curses the disbelievers and prepares for them a Blaze, and the angels' testimony belongs to that economy. A person rejected by heaven's court is not facing a private divine displeasure but a settled disposition of the entire unseen world, in which every conscious being that knows the truth he denied stands where the truth stands.

**All of Humanity**

The third curser is *al-nās* — mankind, all together — and al-Rāzī worked through its possible scopes with care. It may mean all people in the plain sense: humanity's collective verdict against the settled denier of its Lord. It may mean some people, since the definite plural in the Qur'an often covers a part. And it may mean something sharper: one's own allies and the people one misled, cursing him in the Hereafter for the misguidance he spread — the followers disowning the followed, the scene this same passage will stage two groups of verses later. Al-Rāzī's synthesis gathers the readings: since all human beings curse what is intrinsically vile, those who die disbelieving come to bear the curse of all in one way or another — directly from the righteous, retrospectively from the misled, universally from anyone who sees the case as it is. The verse that began with heaven's curse ends with earth's, and between them nothing stands for the cursed one: not God, not the angels, not mankind.

**A Verdict for the Hereafter, Not a License for the World**

Al-Qurṭubī drew a boundary around the verse that the tradition took seriously: the curse it describes belongs exclusively to the Hereafter. His reasoning was functional — a curse is a kind of prodding away from disbelief, a communication of disapproval meant to move its object; it is of no use directed at someone dead or insane, who cannot be moved by anything. The verse therefore describes what awaits, not what believers should perform; it is eschatology, not a command to action in this world. He grounded the limit in prophetic practice: the Prophet forbade cursing a person undergoing a legal punishment or one who had sincerely repented — a curse is leveled only at a state deserving of it, and one does not heap insult on a man already under sentence or already returned. The sūrah of the Spider supplies the Hereafter scene the verse points to: on the Day of Resurrection the allies of falsehood *"will disown and curse one another"* (29:25). The discipline this produces is precise: the believer may know that God's curse rests on settled rejection without being licensed to curse the living, among whom the door of 2:160 stands open until the last breath.

**The Full Range in Four Verses**

Read in its sequence, this verse completes a moral map drawn with unusual care. The crime: hiding what God clarified, cursed by God and every curser (159). The exception: repentance, mending, and disclosure, met by God's first-person acceptance (160). The closed case: disbelief carried through death, cursed by God, the angels, and mankind together (161). And in the next verse the sentence executed — abiding, unalleviated, unrespired (162). The map's two poles are separated by a single hinge, and the hinge is a life: while it lasts, the curse of 159 is escapable through 160; when it ends in rejection, the curse of 161 is total. The verses give no third region, no partial damnation for partial belief, no posthumous appeal. The clarity is itself a mercy — the boundary is published in advance, from every pulpit of the passage, so that no one can say at the end that the two outcomes were not shown side by side while there was still time to walk between them.
