## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:161

> Surely those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers are condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity.

**The Sentence Hangs on the State at Death**

The verse does not pronounce on the disbeliever in the middle of his life; it names the one who disbelieves and dies in that state. Al-Ṭabarī reads the participle *kāfirīn* as the condition on which everything else depends: the door that is open in this world closes at death, and the Book states elsewhere that repentance is not accepted from those who commit evil deeds until death arrives and they say that they now repent (4:18). The same ruling appears in the sūrah named for Muḥammad, where those who disbelieve and turn others from the way of God and then die in their disbelief are told that God will not forgive them (47:34). The condition is stated twice in the passage so that no reader mistakes the measure of his own case.

**The Curse of God**

*Laʿnah* is the removal of a person from the mercy of God and his exclusion from the good it carries, as Ibn Kathīr explains it, and the verdict is God's before it is anyone else's. Al-Rāzī notes the order in which the three parties are named: the Lord who owns the judgement, the angels who carry out His command, and the human order that follows the two. The first curse supplies the meaning of the other two, since the servant's curse is not and cannot be a verdict of its own; it is the recognition, on the part of the created order, of a sentence already passed by the One who owns mercy and withholds it.

**The Anger of the Angels**

Al-Qurṭubī places the angels in the sentence as the noble recorders, the servants described in the sūrah of the Gathering who write what the servants of God do and know the measure of it. Their curse is an entreaty against a person who has forfeited the mercy they announce, and the Book gives their office a second face: in Sūrah Ghāfir the angels who bear the Throne and those around it seek forgiveness for the believers (40:7), and in the Shūrā they ask pardon for those on earth who turn to God (42:5). The same order of servants who appeal for the faithful is named here as cursing those who die in denial, and the two statements describe one harmony rather than a change of temper.

**All Humanity, Including the Disbelievers**

The hardest phrase in the verse is the last, since the disbelievers are themselves part of mankind. Al-Rāzī takes up the difficulty and reads the sentence as the unanimity of the created order against a denial that has set itself outside it; al-Ṭabarī supports the reading with two passages in which the cursed turn on one another. In the sūrah of the Heights, the followers among the people of the Fire say to their leaders that they were their imitators in the world and ask whether the leaders will bear part of the burden; the leaders refuse, and the curse of each falls on the other (7:38–39). In this sūrah itself, the followed ones disown the followers on the Day of Judgement and the ties between them are cut (2:166–167). The verse's *all* is not a crowd standing together but a company turning on itself, each member cursing the others for the road they agreed to travel.

**Cursing a Sinner and Cursing a Disbeliever**

Ibn Kathīr cites a report in the Musnad of Aḥmad concerning a man brought repeatedly for drinking wine, until a bystander called down God's curse upon him and the Prophet ﷺ forbade it, saying that the man loved God and His Messenger. The scholars drew from the report the line that this verse draws: the curse pronounced here belongs to the one who dies in denial, not to every Muslim who falls into sin, and the tongue of the believer is not to be loosed against a person whose affair belongs to God's judgement. The verse names a class defined by its end, and the tradition keeps the community from applying its language to a man whose end is still unwritten.

**The Same Sentence in the Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān**

The Book states this verdict a second time in almost identical words. In the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān, those who disbelieve after having believed and after the clear proofs came to them are told that their recompense is the curse of God and of the angels and of all mankind, that they abide under it, and that their punishment is neither lightened nor delayed (3:86–88). Al-Rāzī sets the two passages side by side and observes that the same three parties are named in the same order, the same word for abiding is used, and the same two denials close the sentence, which shows the earlier passage to be an amplification of this one rather than a separate teaching. The audiences differ, since the concealers among the People of the Book stand in the foreground here while those who turned back after belief are addressed there, and the sentence pronounced is one.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The permanent condition is denial persisted in until death, and everything the verse pronounces is measured by that. The curse begins with God and is echoed by the angels and by the human order that answers to Him, so that the sentence is one verdict and not three. The disbelievers' presence inside the *all* of the last phrase shows the sentence turning on itself from within, the people of the Fire cursing one another for a road they chose together. And the tradition of the drunken man marks the boundary of the verse's use in the community: a warning addressed to unbelief, not a licence for the believer's tongue.
