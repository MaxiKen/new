## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:43

> the people of Abraham, the people of Lot,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Names From One Household**

The verse continues the catalogue of verse 42 with two more communities: the people of Abraham and the people of Lot. The pairing is deliberate, and it is not chronological.

Abraham and Lot were kinsmen — Lot was Abraham's nephew, and he is described in the Qur'an as one of those who emigrated with him and as one of those to whom God gave prophethood. Their two communities are therefore linked in the narrative, and their two destructions are reported in the same passages: the angels who came to announce the birth of Isḥāq went on from Abraham's household to Lot's town.

That is why the sūrah places them together. What is being listed is not a sequence of unrelated tribes but a cluster of stories that the audience already knew as one connected account.

**The Founder of This Sūrah's House Appears Among the Denied**

The inclusion of Abraham's people is the sharpest item on the list, and the sūrah makes the irony available to anyone who has read its first half.

Verses 26 and 27 of this very sūrah commissioned Abraham to purify the House and to call humanity to the pilgrimage. He is the founder of the sanctuary the sūrah is named for, the man whose prayer established the valley, and the one whose religion the Qur'an says the Muslims follow. His own people rejected him.

That is the point the Prophet is being consoled with, and it is a stronger one than the earlier names. If Abraham — the man whose House the Makkans venerated and whose rites they claimed to be performing — was denied by his own community, then denial is not a verdict on the messenger. The Makkans claimed Abraham as their ancestor while rejecting his successor, and the sūrah has already spent several verses showing that the rites they inherited from him had been emptied of what he was sent with.

**Lot's People and the Nature of Their Refusal**

Lot's community is the one item on the list whose specific crime is reported in detail elsewhere in the Qur'an, and it was not idolatry in the ordinary sense.

Their denial took the form of refusing a prohibition, assaulting the guests in their town, and treating the messenger who warned them as an offender. They are described as a people who *transgressed* rather than as a people who worshipped stones, and their destruction was correspondingly specific: the town was turned upside down and rained with stones of baked clay.

Including them widens what the catalogue covers. The list is not a catalogue of polytheists. It is a catalogue of communities that were told something by a messenger and refused it, whatever form the refusal took.

**Two Words in the Arabic, and Their Weight**

The verse is two names long. In the Arabic it is *wa-qawmu Ibrāhīma wa-qawmu Lūṭin* — and the people of Abraham and the people of Lot — no verbs, no predicates, nothing but the continuation of the sentence begun in verse 42.

The brevity is the rhetoric. A catalogue that paused to describe each community would become a set of stories; a catalogue that does nothing but accumulate names becomes an argument from sheer number. Nine communities in three verses, with the verdict reserved for the end, produces the sense of an accumulation too large to argue with.

**What "His People" Means for the Verdict**

The phrase *qawmu Ibrāhīma* — the people of Abraham — is the standard Qur'anic formula for the community a messenger was sent to, and its assumptions repay attention.

The assumption is that a messenger is sent *to* someone. No messenger in the Qur'an preaches to humanity in the abstract; each is sent to a specific people with a specific language. The universal address of verse 49 will come later in this passage, and it is the exception that proves the rule.

The consequence for the catalogue is that the rejection is always local and particular. These communities were not rejecting a distant message; they were rejecting a message delivered to them, in their language, by one of their own, and that is what makes the verdict on them intelligible.

**Every Messenger on This List Was an Insider**

One feature runs through the whole catalogue and is easy to miss because the verses are so compressed. Every messenger named was sent to *his own* people, and the Qur'an is careful to say so.

*“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ”* (7:73). *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd”* (11:50). The word translated "brother" is used repeatedly and with emphasis: Shuʿayb is called the brother of the people of Midian (7:85), and the pattern holds for every community on the list.

The significance for the consolation being offered is considerable. These communities were not rejecting foreigners with unfamiliar customs. They were rejecting a man they had grown up with, whose family they knew, whose honesty they had previously relied on. The denial was not a failure of information about the messenger; it was a refusal to accept a claim from someone whose ordinariness was the problem.

That is precisely the objection the Qur'an records against Muhammad ﷺ — that he was a man like them, that he ate and walked in the market, and that no angel had been sent down with him. The catalogue's answer is that this has always been the arrangement.

**Why the Kaʿbah's Neighbours Should Have Recognised This**

One further consideration. Abraham's people and Lot's people were the communities nearest to the audience's own geography, and their ruins were known. ʿĀd and Thamūd were in the peninsula; Lot's towns were on the trade route to Syria that Makkah's caravans used every year.

The Qur'an's argument here is not addressed to people who needed to be told these stories for the first time. It is addressed to people who passed the remains on their way to work, and who are being asked why the remains did not change their minds.
