## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:88

> They say, “Our hearts are unreceptive!” In fact, Allah has condemned them for their disbelief. They have but little faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Excuse Quoted**

The recital now lets the accused speak, and the speech is a single clause of defense: *they say, "Our hearts are unreceptive!"* The Arabic beneath the translation is *ghulf* — a word the exegetes open three ways, and each opening exposes a different posture of refusal. It means *covered* — the heart wrapped, unable to receive what is addressed to it; it means *sheathed*, as a sword is sheathed — the heart enclosed in its own casing, sealed against entry; and its semantic range allows the boastful reading the commentators mark first: *our hearts are vessels* — vessels full, holding all knowledge and requiring nothing further, or vessels empty in the other sense: nothing spoken from outside finds resonance within (Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Thaʿlabī). The three readings are not rival translations so much as the excuse's full psychology: my heart is already full; my heart is closed to you; my heart hears nothing it recognizes. Sūrah Fuṣṣilat records the same defense in plainer terms — *"Our hearts are veiled against what you are calling us to"* (41:5) — and Sūrah al-Nisāʾ will quote it again, with the same rebuttal attached (4:155). The excuse's antiquity is its interest: it is the oldest answer appetite gives guidance — not *you are wrong* but *I am constitutionally unable to hear you*, a defense that concedes the message's clarity while pleading the organ's incapacity.

**The Rebuttal: Condemned for Disbelief**

The verse's answer is immediate and inverts the excuse's causal order: *in fact, Allah has condemned them for their disbelief*. The covering is not the reason for the rejection but its result — the heart is not unreceptive and therefore unbelieving; it disbelieved and was therefore condemned to un-receptiveness. The same inversion stands in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ, where the claim *our hearts are unreceptive* is answered: *it is Allah Who has sealed their hearts for their disbelief, so they do not believe except for a few* (4:155) — the seal attached to the disbelief as consequence to cause, with the exception clause preserving the remnant the recital keeps sighting (83). This is the sūrah's consistent grammar of hardening, filed since its opening: the hearts sealed in verse 7, the hearts hardened like stone after the signs in verse 74 — divine action that follows, never precedes, the human turning. The rebuttal's structure deserves precision because it answers the excuse exactly where the excuse is strongest. The speakers claim a condition; the verse grants a condition but reassigns its authorship — the heart's state is real, and it is theirs: condemned *for their disbelief*, the covering earned term by term through every disliked message, every arrogant rejection, every killed messenger. An excuse that describes its own manufacture is not an excuse but a confession in the passive voice.

**The Responsibility Question**

The verse's claim — hearts covered, yet their owners condemned — raises the question the exegetes face directly: if the organ of faith and understanding is covered, is the person responsible for its contents? The discussion in the tradition runs with unusual candor. The verse here dismisses and rebukes the notion of hearts covered as a defense; yet 4:155 affirms the sealing. The reconciliation the commentators offer turns on a fact about self-knowledge: whether or not Allah seals hearts, a heart wholly sealed could not register its own sealing. The awareness of one's own covering is evidence against the covering's totality — a soul that grieves its closedness is not wholly closed, since the very sentence *my heart is covered* has two opposite meanings depending on who speaks it: on the cynic's tongue it is the excuse of verse 88, incapacity pleaded over refusal; spoken by a soul that longs for God and grieves the distance, the same words are a prayer, and no one perceives anything of Allah except by Allah's allowance (the discussion refers back to the sūrah's verse 7 and its treatment of sealing, responsibility, and free will). The verse's condemnation falls on the first utterance only, and the distinction it installs is permanent: the genuinely sealed do not litigate their sealing, and those who litigate it are still standing in the courtroom, which means the door they call boarded is the door they came through.

**“They Have but Little Faith”**

The verdict's final clause quantifies: *they have but little faith*. The exegetical readings run the idiom's two edges. On one, *little* is the Arabic understatement that means none — the negation clothed in diminution, the way a total absence is announced as a trifle. On the other, it is exact: a faith that exists but does not govern — belief in Allah held alongside the rejection of His messengers, acknowledgment of the Book alongside its rationing, the ransom paid while the expulsion continues. The second reading fits the passage's record: these are not atheists but partial believers, the community of verse 85 that believed in some of the Scripture and rejected the rest, and little faith is the name of that arithmetic. The clause also answers the excuse's boastful form — *our hearts are vessels full of knowledge* — with the measurement they would least expect: the full heart is assessed at almost nothing, because faith is not measured by contents held but by contents obeyed. A vessel storing the Torah while expelling its people holds little; the illiterate of verse 78 who conjecture hold less; and the few excepted at verse 83, whose faith governed their conduct at cost, hold the quantity the verse says is missing. The gradation is the sūrah's whole portrait of belief as weight rather than claim — and little faith, in the verse's economy, is a verdict on direction, not on information.

**The Ghulf of Every Age**

The excuse's modern forms are unrecognizable in wording and identical in structure. *I am simply not a religious person* — the sheath worn as identity. *I was raised differently; my heart was formed elsewhere* — the covering pleaded as history. *I have my own spirituality* — the vessel declared full, needing nothing from the messenger at the door. Each is *ghulf* in one of the exegetes' three senses: closed, sealed, or self-sufficiently complete. And each receives the verse's two-part answer. The condemnation-clause reassigns authorship: the condition presented as constitution is a record of decisions — every message disliked (87), every term rationed (85), every covenant outbid by an alliance (84–85) laid its layer, until the heart could truthfully describe itself as unreceptive and falsely conclude that the description was an alibi. The responsibility-clause leaves the door where the excuse itself proves it: whoever can say the sentence can hear the answer, and the answer is that the covering is liftable by the only party who ever put it on — the condemnation *for their disbelief* runs in reverse as absolution for their return, which is the width of 39:53's mercy measured against this verse's severity. The recital quotes the excuse not to foreclose it but to expose it while it is still a choice: *they say, "Our hearts are unreceptive"* — and the verse's own existence, audible to the hearts addressed, is the standing refutation that they hear.
