## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:88

> They say, “Our hearts are unreceptive!” In fact, Allah has condemned them for their disbelief. They have but little faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Excuse Offered in Their Own Words**

The sūrah quotes the community's explanation for its refusal, and the explanation is a claim about their own condition rather than an argument about the message. The messengers arrived, the evidence was presented, and the answer returned was that the hearts receiving it were closed. The Book reports the sentence without comment and then supplies the comment in the next clause.

Quoting an opponent's own words is a technique the sūrah has used several times in this passage. It lets the reader hear the position from inside, and it makes the correction that follows land on the speaker rather than on a description of the speaker. The Book's method is not to characterize the claim but to expose it.

**What the Word Behind the Translation Means**

The Arabic word in the quotation is one the commentators discussed at length, and it is usually explained as meaning covered, wrapped, or closed. Some readers connected it to the description of a heart that has been sealed, and others noted that the word shares a root with the idea of uncircumcision, which is how several older translations rendered it. Under every reading, the claim is that the community's hearts cannot receive what is being offered.

The same excuse appears elsewhere in the Book in the account of a people who told Moses that their hearts were wrapped, at a point where they had already broken their covenant and killed their prophets (4:155). The wording is repeated in both places, which suggests the sentence had become a standing formula in the community's vocabulary for declining a messenger.

**The Book's Answer**

The answer occupies the rest of the verse, and it rejects the claim at its root. God has condemned them for their disbelief. What the community presents as an inability is described as a consequence, and the Book reverses the order in which the two events happened. The hearts did not close first and produce the disbelief; the disbelief produced the closing.

That reversal is a recurring theme in the Book. It states elsewhere that God turns the hearts of those who do not believe, and that He leaves a person in the deviation he has chosen. The Qur'an's account of hardening is consistent: it follows the exercise of a person's own will, and it is described as a judgment that follows on conduct rather than as an arbitrary act that precedes it.

**A Small Amount of Faith**

The verse closes with a statement about the quantity of faith the community possessed. The translation renders it as very little. Commentators read the clause in two related ways, one taking it as a description of the actual belief held by some members of the community, and the other as an ironic statement about a faith that refuses to accept what its own scripture announced.

The reading that fits the surrounding verses is the second. A community that says its hearts cannot receive is not describing a small faith but a faith that has been made conditional on terms, and the sūrah continues to state that such belief does not satisfy the requirements of the covenant. The phrase is therefore not a measurement but a verdict on the quality of what the community retained.

**Why the Excuse Fails on Its Own Terms**

If the hearts really were closed by nature, the closing would not be a fault, and the Book would not hold the community responsible. The sūrah's entire passage on this people, however, treats them as responsible, and the previous verses described people who understood the scripture, distorted it knowingly, and killed men sent to them. The excuse contradicts the record the Book has just laid out.

That is why the reply in this verse takes the form of a judgment. The community is offering a description of itself as a defence, and the Book accepts the description and turns it into the charge. Their hearts are closed, and the verse states who closed them and why.

**The Believers and the Same Warning**

The sūrah applies this vocabulary to its own audience later, when it warns the believers not to become like those who forgot God and were made to forget themselves. Verse after verse in this Book describes the sequence of choice, consequence, and hardening, and the warning to the Muslims is always that the sequence is universal.

Read as a warning, the sentence has a practical edge. A person who habitually declines what he does not want to hear is not merely losing arguments; he is training the faculty that would have recognized the next truth that came to him. That is the mechanism the verse describes in three words, and the Book presents it as the most dangerous process a religious person can undergo.

**A Question About Hearts, Not Lineage**

The verse is often quoted as a description of one community, and the Book's use of the same language about its own followers rules out that reading. What is being described is a condition that anyone can enter, and the description is offered to the reader as a possibility rather than as a fact about someone else.

**How a Community Explains Itself to Itself**

The formula quoted in this verse has a function beyond refusing a messenger. It converts a history of choices into a statement about nature, and communities repeat that conversion. A group that has repeatedly declined what it did not want to hear will eventually describe its own condition as permanent, and the description begins to sound like humility rather than stubbornness. The reply in this verse refuses the conversion. What has been called a closed heart is named as a consequence, and the consequence is attributed to the conduct that produced it. That reversal makes the sentence useful far beyond its original occasion, since anyone can test it: a condition presented as incapacity is being described by someone who has not yet seriously attempted to act against it.

**When a Condition Becomes a Destiny**

The claim in this verse describes a permanent state, and permanence is the part the Book rejects. A heart that has hardened can be softened, which is why the Qur'an's warnings about hardened hearts are always accompanied by commands to remember God and to seek forgiveness. The community's formula removed that possibility at a single stroke by converting a consequence into a nature. What the Book answers with is not only a correction of the claim but a statement about responsibility: the condition was produced by choices, and choices are the kind of thing their maker can revisit. The verse is therefore a rebuke to despair as much as to stubbornness, since it refuses to let a person settle permanently into the position his own conduct has placed him in.
