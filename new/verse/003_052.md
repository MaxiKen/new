## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:52

> When Jesus sensed disbelief from his people, he asked, “Who will stand up with me for Allah?” The disciples replied, “We will stand up for Allah. We believe in Allah, so bear witness that we have submitted.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prophet Reading His Own People**

The verse begins with a prophet detecting something: when Jesus sensed disbelief from among his people, he asked a question. The word *kufr* here describes not a private opinion but a settled hostility, and the commentaries understood it as the point at which the rejection had become active — the hostility that made it necessary for him to look for support elsewhere.

The situation is the same one the sūrah's readers know from their own prophet's life, and the parallel is intended. The Book's messengers do not fail because their message is defective; they are refused by people who have already decided, and each of them reaches a moment where the question shifts from "will you believe?" to "who will stand with me?" The verse is therefore both a scene from the past and a pattern the sūrah has established.

**"Who Will Stand Up With Me for Allah?"**

The Arabic phrase has been read in several ways, and the differences are worth keeping. Some commentators took it to mean helpers alongside God — who will add his help to God's help — while others read it as helpers in the prophet's own movement toward God, describing the assistance of a man who is preparing to leave a hostile city.

Both readings agree on the essential structure: a servant asks other human beings to join a work that is not his own. The formula names the direction of the help — it is toward God — which is why the request does not become a bid for personal loyalty. Jesus is asking for allies in an enterprise he has already surrendered to the one who sent him, and the phrase's dependence on God is what makes it answerable by ordinary people.

**Ḥawāriyyūn: Who the Disciples Were**

The men who answer are called *ḥawāriyyūn*, and the word has been traced to a root meaning whiteness or purity, with some lexicographers connecting it to a term in another Semitic language for a messenger or one who steps forward. In the Book the word always refers to the closest followers of Jesus and appears in their support of his mission.

The tradition describes them as men of ordinary work — fishermen, tradesmen — rather than the learned class of the community. That is consistent with how the Book presents the message's reception in general: the people whose livelihood did not depend on the existing interpretation of scripture were usually the ones who answered. What the verse gives them is not a title earned by study but a name attached to an act of standing up.

**An Answer of Three Clauses**

The disciples answer with three short sentences that deserve separate attention. First, they commit themselves to the work: we will stand up for Allah. Then they state what they carry: we believe in Allah. Then they ask for the messenger to record it: so bear witness that we have submitted.

The order is the order of any serious commitment. A promise of support without belief would be a temporary alliance; a belief without the willingness to stand would be a private opinion. The Book's pattern in this verse is that the inner state and the outer act are spoken in the same breath, and the third clause asks the human messenger to witness a commitment made to God. What the disciples want is a record, kept by someone who can attest to it, of a decision taken in a moment when support was scarce.

**The Word They Use About Themselves**

The clause rendered "we have submitted" uses the participle, and the disciples thereby take the same word the sūrah used of everyone who has handed himself over to God. The Book uses it of Abraham and Jacob advising their children, and of the disciples again in another sūrah where their declaration is recorded with the word for belief and the request for witness (5:111). The word is not the property of any later community; it is what a person is when God's claim has been accepted.

The choice of the participle rather than a stronger statement of identity is also worth noticing. The disciples do not claim a rank or a station. They describe a state, and they ask that the state be witnessed. It is possible to hear in their sentence the tone of people who know that a commitment is only as good as what follows it, and who therefore put it on the record while it can still be tested.

**The Same Exchange in Another Sūrah**

The Book recounts the disciples' answer a second time as part of a longer passage. The believers are told to stand up for God as Jesus asked the disciples, quoting the question and the answer and adding the sequel: a group of the Children of Israel believed and another disbelieved, and God supported the believers against their enemies so that they prevailed (61:14).

The sequel tells the reader what the exchange was for. The disciples' commitment was not an end in itself; it entered a contest that the Book describes as having a verdict, and the verdict was on the side of those who stood up. Setting the shorter account beside the longer one gives this verse its context: a small group of men answered a question in a moment of danger, and the Book records their answer twice.

**What a Faithful Answer Costs**

The verse's ending is a request for witness, and the request implies a future that will require the answer to be repeated. Believing and standing up are not one-time announcements, and the disciples knew they were asking to be held to what they had said.

For the community reading the sūrah in Madinah, the scene offered a model that its own circumstances made urgent. A message that is not accepted by the powerful will need people willing to say publicly what they have decided privately, and the disciples are shown doing exactly that while their prophet is being refused. The next verse records what they asked for.
