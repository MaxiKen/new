## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:62

> conveying to you my Lord’s messages and giving you ˹sincere˺ advice. And I know from Allah what you do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Delivery of the Lord's Messages**

The verse states the messenger's work in two parts, the conveyance of his Lord's messages and the counsel he offers to his people, and it closes with the knowledge that he has been given. The Book describes the duty of the messengers as the delivery of what they were sent with, and the sūrah of the Family of Imran states that the messenger's duty is only to convey (3:20), while the sūrah of the Table repeats the same limit twice in a single passage. The verse in front of us places the delivery first, and the counsel follows it as the manner of the delivery.

The messages in the plural are the communications sent to the messenger over the course of his mission, and the Book's accounts of the prophets show the same pattern of successive revelation. The sūrah of Noah reports the messenger calling his people by night and by day and repeating his appeal in several forms, and the verse in front of us summarises his work under the two headings that the sūrah's other narratives also use.

**Sincere Advice to the People**

The second part of the messenger's work is counsel, and the Arabic term carries the sense of wishing well to the one advised without any mixture of self-interest. The sūrah's other narratives use the same word, and Hūd describes himself as the sincere adviser of his people (7:68), Ṣāliḥ says the same at the end of his account (7:79), and Shuʿayb repeats it when his people have been destroyed (7:93). The repetition places the term among the sūrah's fixed descriptions of the prophetic office.

The Book contrasts the counsel of the messengers with the concealment practiced by those who know the truth and hide it, and the sūrah of the Cow warns the people of the earlier books against covering what has been revealed. The counsel in front of us is offered to a people who have already dismissed the messenger, and the narrative presents the offer as continuing regardless of the response. The Book's teaching about the persistence of the messengers appears in its accounts of their repeated appeals to communities that refused them.

**What the Messenger Knows**

The verse closes with the statement that the messenger knows from God what the people do not know, and the knowledge refers to what has been revealed to him about the consequences of the people's conduct. The sūrah's narrative continues with the instruction to build the ark and with the news of the flood, and the knowledge in front of us is the content of that instruction. The Book states that the knowledge of the unseen belongs to God alone and that He discloses it only to a messenger whom He has approved (72:26–27).

The contrast in the verse is therefore between the messenger's knowledge of what is coming and the people's confidence in the permanence of their situation. The sūrah's accounts of the other communities repeat the contrast, and the sūrah of the Believers reports the people of Noah treating the messenger as a man possessed who should be endured (23:25). The verse in front of us states the knowledge that the people deny him, and the account that follows shows the arrival of what he knew.

**The Vocabulary of Counsel in the Sūrah**

The word for counsel recurs at the close of each of the sūrah's prophet narratives, and its position gives the series one of its fixed features. Hūd speaks it before his people are destroyed, Ṣāliḥ speaks it as he turns away from his, and Shuʿayb speaks it after the earthquake has taken his community. The repetition establishes that the messengers performed their duty completely and that the outcome belongs to the people who refused.

The Book's use of the term in its instructions to the believing community appears in its commands about advising one another, and the sūrah's narratives present the practice in its original form. The messenger in front of us advises a people who have accused him of error, and his counsel is stated in the same breath as the delivery of the message. The passage therefore pairs the two activities as a single work.

**The Messenger's Knowledge and Its Limits**

The verse's last clause attributes a knowledge to the messenger that his people lack, and the rest of the Book marks the limits of what a messenger knows. The sūrah of the Sand Dunes states that the Prophet is told to say that he does not know what will happen to him or to his people (46:9), and the sūrah of the Spider reports the demand for signs and the answer that signs belong to God and that the messenger is only a clear warner (29:50). The knowledge in front of us is therefore what has been revealed to him for the purpose of the warning.

The sūrah of the Family of Imran states that God does not leave the believers in their condition until He distinguishes the good from the evil, and it states that He does not disclose the unseen to anyone except a messenger whom He approves (3:179; 72:27). The verse in front of us states the arrangement in the messenger's own words, and the narrative that follows shows what the disclosure contained.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands sixty-second in the chapter, and it completes the answer that Noah gives to the charge of the notables. The verse that follows reports his appeal to the people's sense of wonder at the reminder that has come to them through a man of their own, and the narrative then proceeds to their denial and the flood (7:64). This verse states the work that the messenger performed and the knowledge that supported it.

The reader who heard the leader's charge and the messenger's denial now receives the account of what the messenger did among his people. He delivered the messages, he gave counsel, and he knew what the people did not; and the sūrah's next verse records the appeal he made on the basis of that knowledge.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse asks the people whether they find it astonishing that a reminder should come to them from their Lord through one of their own, warning them so that they may beware and perhaps receive mercy (7:63). The statement of the messenger's knowledge in front of us is followed by the appeal that addresses the people's astonishment, and the narrative then turns to their answer.

The two verses together state the messenger's position and his appeal. He knows what they do not know, and he asks them why the arrival of a warning through a man of their own community should surprise them; and the verses that follow record the refusal that ends the account.
