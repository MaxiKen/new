## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:186

> You ˹believers˺ will surely be tested in your wealth and yourselves, and you will certainly hear many hurtful words from those who were given the Scripture before you and ˹from˺ the polytheists. But if you are patient and mindful ˹of Allah˺—surely this is a resolve to aspire to.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Trial Announced**

The verse tells the believers that they will certainly be tested in their property and in themselves, and that they will hear painful words from two groups. The sentence is stated as a prediction, and its certainty is part of what it conveys.

The Book announces trials to its communities in several places, and one passage lists the forms they take: fear, hunger, loss of property, loss of life, and loss of crops. The verse before the reader names two of those categories and adds the specific form of harm that comes through speech, and the community is told all of it in advance.

**What the Reference to Souls Covers**

The phrase about being tried in one's soul can be read as referring to the person himself, and al-Ṭabarī records another reading on which the word means one's own kin and the members of one's household. On that second reading the verse predicts the loss and the suffering of the people closest to the believer.

Both readings describe what the community at Madinah had already experienced. Its members had lost relatives on the field, and the survivors had borne the strain of the losses in their own persons. The verse's phrasing gathers the different forms of the trial into two words, and the commentators' disagreement reflects how much those two words were expected to carry.

**The Hurt From the Two Groups**

The verse states that the community will hear much that is painful from those who were given the Book before it and from the idolaters, and the word used covers both physical harm and verbal attacks. Al-Ṭabarī explains that it includes mockery, slander, falsehoods, and blasphemous statements about God and His prophets.

The sūrah had already reported examples of such speech in its account of the taunt about God's poverty and the challenge about the burnt offering. The community was being prepared for what it would continue to meet, and the verse names the source of the hurt rather than describing the community's reaction to it.


The community's situation in Madinah involved constant dealings with both groups, and the taunts came in the course of ordinary conversation and argument rather than only in open hostility. The verse places the experience in the category of a trial rather than of an accident.

The Book's teaching here is that the words of opponents were among the things that the community was going to be tested by, and the test is described in the same sentence as the losses of property and life. What the community would need in order to pass it is named in the clause that follows.

**Patience and Reverence as the Prescription**

The verse states that if the community is patient and mindful of God, then its conduct belongs to the matters that require resolve. The two qualities named are the ones the sūrah has been recommending throughout its account of the community's difficulties.

Patience in this context covers endurance under provocation as well as under loss, and mindfulness covers the awareness that governs how a person responds. The verse places both of them in the same clause, and the Book's descriptions of steadfastness elsewhere use the same pairing when the affliction takes the form of insults rather than blows.

**The Course Worthy of Resolve**

The phrase that closes the verse appears again in the Book in the advice given to Luqmān's son about enduring what befalls him, and in the statement that whoever endures and forgives has undertaken something that requires determination. The expression describes a course of action that a person must decide to take, and the determination is part of what it requires.

The community is therefore told that the endurance of insult is not a passive condition but a course of conduct. The Book's treatment of the matter in all three passages is the same, and the word it uses places the conduct among the things that human beings choose rather than among the things that happen to them.

**The Book's Counsel of Forbearance**

The Book counsels restraint in the face of rejection in several passages. One records the instruction to Moses and Aaron to speak gently to Pharaoh in the hope that he might be reminded. Another tells the believers to respond to evil with what is better, and promises that the person with whom one is in a feud may become a close friend. A third tells the believers to forgive those who do not look for the days of God.

The verse before the reader belongs to this body of teaching, and the community's conduct at Madinah was meant to be shaped by it. The Book's instruction about the manner of conveying the message is as consistent as its instruction about the message itself.

**The Question of Abrogation**

Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭūbī considered the argument that this verse was set aside by the passages that permit fighting, and they rejected it. Their reasoning is that patience and leniency in the face of insult were the practice of the Prophet and his companions throughout their lives, including the periods in which fighting occurred.

The point is a useful one for the verse's later readers. The Book's permission to defend the community does not cancel the requirement to bear provocation patiently in the ordinary course of dealings with opponents, and the sūrah's placement of this instruction after its account of the battle makes the same point in its own way.

**The Prediction the Sūrah Documents in Its Own Pages**

What the verse announces as a future trial is not left to the reader's imagination, because the sūrah supplies instances of it on its way toward this point. The taunt about God's poverty and the challenge about a sacrifice consumed by fire are both reported in this chapter with the words of the people who spoke them, and the sentences that answer them belong to the same argument.

The two groups the verse names are also the two whose conduct the chapter has been tracing. The People of the Book appear in the disputes about Abraham, the covenant, and the concealment of knowledge; the idolaters appear in the account of Badr and Uḥud and in the mockery that followed the defeat. A reader who has come this far has already met the hurt the sentence predicts, which is why the instruction is phrased as a reminder of what perseverance means rather than as a warning about an unknown future.
