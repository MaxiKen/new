## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:77

> Do they not know that Allah is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Answers the Previous One**

The verse meets the private conversation of the preceding sentence with a single question, and it does not argue or explain. The speakers had asked whether their knowledge should be disclosed; the Book asks whether they understand who is listening. The two questions are put back to back, and the second one makes the first one irrelevant.

The construction is one the sūrah has used before and will use again. The Book's method with secrecy is never to threaten exposure in detail; it states the fact of knowledge and moves on. What makes the sentence effective is its position, arriving immediately after a reported conversation that its participants believed to be private.

**Concealment and Disclosure as a Pair**

The Book pairs these two words throughout, and it usually does so where conduct is being weighed. It tells the Prophet that whether a person conceals what is in his heart or reveals it, it is known to God, who knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth (3:29). It states that God is the Knower of the unseen of the heavens and the earth and knows best what is hidden in the breasts (35:38). It closes a chapter about speech and intention with the assurance that God knows the unseen and sees what people do (49:18).

The repetition of the pair across the Book is not redundancy. Each occurrence is attached to a different situation in which a person is managing appearances, and the vocabulary keeps the same shape so that the reader recognises the claim. In this verse the pair is attached to a specific conversation between people who had arranged their public and private positions differently.

**Why the Knowledge Matters More Than Exposure**

The sentence does not say that the concealed material will be published. It says that God is aware of it, which is a stronger statement and a more uncomfortable one. Exposure can be managed once the damage is done; awareness that cannot be evaded removes the concept of a private zone in which a person's real position is kept.

The Book draws the practical conclusion elsewhere when it tells the Prophet to announce that whether people hide or reveal what is in their hearts, God knows it and is capable of everything. The same awareness appears in the sūrah's earlier portrait of the hypocrites, whose mockery was reported in the text before any human being had detected it. What the verse states is not a threat of discovery but the impossibility of the arrangement the previous verse describes.

**The Question Form and Its Assumption**

The interrogative is the Book's way of stating that the answer is already known and that the people addressed are behaving as though it were not. It is not an appeal for information, and the Arabic construction expects assent. The question therefore functions as a correction of the assumption behind the conversation that had just been reported.

The sūrah uses the same device at the end of several portraits of this community. It asks whether a group that had distorted a text and misunderstood the consequences of its conduct fails to understand, and it asks the same of the community when it reads the Scripture without acting on it. In each case the question presumes that the answer is available to the addressee, which is what makes the passage a rebuke rather than an insult.

**What the Verse Assumes About Hiding**

A community that discusses which of its knowledge is safe to disclose has already divided its life into two accounts, and the Book addresses the division rather than the specific disclosure. The Qur'an reminds its readers throughout that no arrangement of public and private conduct escapes notice, and it states the point to the believers as well as to their opponents: some of the men who believed had concealed things they should have been forthright about.

The verse is therefore not a weapon to be used against one community. The Book places the same standard over its own audience in later passages and warns them in the same terms about what they reveal and what they conceal. What the sentence establishes is a fact about knowledge that applies to every party in the sūrah's disputes, and its brevity makes it final.

**A Correction Left Without a Consequence**

Nothing follows the question in this verse. The next verse moves to a different group among the same community, describing the illiterate who know the Scripture only by hearsay. The arrangement leaves the rebuke standing without a punishment attached, which is how the sūrah handles several of the charges in this passage.

That absence is part of the Book's method with people it is still inviting. A verdict would close the discussion; a question leaves it open, and the address to this community continues for many more verses with instructions, warnings and repeated invitations to accept what confirms their scripture. The verse states the fact of divine knowledge and leaves the readers to decide what to do with a fact they had already conceded in private.

**Knowledge as a Standing Fact**

A hearer may wonder why the Book returns to God's knowledge so often in this passage. The answer lies in the nature of the offence being described. Concealment, distortion, and selective belief all depend on a gap between what a person knows and what he shows, and every one of them becomes impossible once that gap is closed. The verse closes it. The sentence states a description of the world as it actually is rather than a threat of exposure, and the Book expects its hearers to draw the practical conclusion without being told. A person who arranges his conduct on the assumption that part of his life is unobserved has made an error about God rather than about his neighbours, and the correction offered here takes the form of a question because the answer was never in doubt.
