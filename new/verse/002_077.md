## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:77

> Do they not know that Allah is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Sentence, the Widest Surveillance**

Seven words in the translation close the scene the previous verse staged at length. The two rooms — the public confession, the private counsel — are collapsed into a single question: do they not know that Allah knows what they hide and what they show? The compression is the verse's method. After the conspirators' argument has been quoted in full, with its premises, its fear, and its internal rebuke, the recital does not answer it point by point; it asks whether the arguers have noticed the audience. Every calculation in the private room assumed a bounded disclosure — knowledge dangerous only if it reached the believers, a case lost only if the evidence surfaced before people. The verse removes the boundary: the disclosure they were managing has already happened, continuously, to the only party whose knowledge decides anything. A rhetorical question of this brevity functions as a verdict rather than an inquiry — the facts are not in dispute, only the defendants' awareness of the facts — and its placement makes it the hinge of the whole passage: verses 75–76 built the case of informed concealment, and verse 77 states the condition that makes concealment incoherent. The sūrah's longest argument with a community is here reduced to its smallest form, and the smallest form is the strongest.

**A Theme With No Exception in the Book**

The knowledge of hidden and manifest is the Qur'an's most ubiquitous divine attribute after mercy, and its ubiquity is the verse's warrant. Sūrah al-Naml states it in the same pairing: *"And surely your Lord knows what their hearts conceal and what they reveal"* (27:74) — hearts specified, the seat of the private room named as transparent. The same declaration recurs across the revelation — Allah knows what is in the souls, knows the secret and the more secret, knows what the breasts conceal — in verse after verse the recital could cite (21:110, 24:29, 28:69, 33:54, 36:76). The divine names gather it: Knower of the unseen and the witnessed, the Great, the Exalted — the pairing that closes the sūrah's own verse of the Throne (2:255). The cumulative effect is a cosmology without interior walls: there is no category of the concealed that functions as concealment before Allah, and every human institution built on that category — secrets, cabals, double scripts, the managed disclosure of verse 76 — is built on a local fiction. The verse's question form (*do they not know?*) presumes the answer is available knowledge: this is not esoteric doctrine but published revelation, stated so often that ignorance of it is itself a choice. The conspirators of the previous verse knew it as text and forgot it as fact — which is the precise failure the question exposes.

**The Practical Atheism of the Private Room**

The question's target is not unbelief but inoperative belief. The men of verse 76 affirmed the Lord, the revelation, and the Day — their counsel quoted all three. What their conduct denied was the bearing of those affirmations on the room they were sitting in: a plan built to keep knowledge from Allah's witnesses, as if the witnesses were the whole jury. Islamic moral theology has a name for the structure — living as though the unseen were absent, the practical atheism of hearts whose creed is sound and whose calculations exclude it. The sūrah's opening had diagnosed the same condition from the other side: the disbelievers warned that a seal has been set (2:7), the organ of reckoning closed; verse 77 describes its softer variant, where the reckoning is acknowledged verbally and omitted operationally. The distinction the verse installs is between knowledge-that and knowledge-in-force: they know *that* Allah knows, and the question asks whether they know it in the way that reorganizes behavior. The answer their counsel gave is on record — a disclosure policy with no line-item for divine awareness — and the policy is the verse's indictment. This is why the question is addressed to *them* in the third person rather than as a rebuke spoken to their faces: it is diagnostic speech, filed for the believers' education, describing a condition the recital's audience is being inoculated against.

**The Collapse of the Two Rooms**

The verse's two objects — what they conceal, what they reveal — name the distinction the private room lived by, and the single verb governing both abolishes it. Before people, concealment and disclosure are different acts with different consequences; the hidden word travels no further than its hearers, the public word goes on record. Before Allah the two stand in one field of knowledge, equally present, equally preserved — and the verse's grammar enacts the leveling: one knower, two objects, no difference in the knowing. The collapse has an immediate practical reading, which the tradition made into a virtue's definition: sincerity is the private room behaving like the public one, the believer whose concealed and revealed are the same substance because both are addressed to the same Witness. It also has a judicial reading, which the recital is running: the evidence the conspirators feared disclosing is not being gathered from informants but from the field itself — nothing needs to leak where nothing was ever sealed. The verse thus completes verse 76's exposure from inside its own logic. Their question was *will you tell them?* The recital's answer is that telling was never the mechanism: the knowledge is not moving toward Allah, because with Allah it never moved. Every reader inherits the architecture — the rooms everyone keeps, the words budgeted for privacy — and the verse states the only fact about them that finally matters: the budget is imaginary, the rooms are one, and the audit verse 74 announced — Allah's never being unaware of what we do — has been running the whole time.

**One Attribute, Two Readings of the Same News**

The knowledge verse 77 announces is the recital's most double-edged fact, and its two edges are filed in the sūrah itself. Against the concealer it is threat without remainder: no strategy survives contact with an omniscient opponent, and the litigation posture of verse 76 — managing evidence before the Lord — is revealed as the one unwinnable case. Toward the sincere it turns as comfort with the same remainder: the nearness the sūrah will state in its own terms — *I am truly near. I respond to one's prayer when they call upon Me* (2:186) — is the identical attribute experienced from the other side. The heart's concealed prayer, the unspoken grief, the goodness nobody witnessed: all of it is known, which means none of it is lost. The two readings are not different doctrines but one fact meeting two postures — the hidden contents of a soul that fears exposure, and the hidden contents of a soul that trusts. The verse's placement between the exposure of hypocrisy and the recital's continuing audit fixes its function as the passage's ground rule: everything filed after it — the ransom, the distortions, the claims about the Fire, the covenant breaches to come — is evidence in a court whose judge reads the sealed file as easily as the open one. *Do they not know?* The question is the recital's standing invitation to move from the first room to the second, and to discover that there was only ever one.
