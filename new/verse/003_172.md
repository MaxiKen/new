## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:172

> ˹As for˺ those who responded to the call of Allah and His Messenger after their injury, those of them who did good and were mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have a great reward.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Summons After the Wounds**

The verse describes those who responded to God and the Messenger after they had been wounded, and it promises a great reward to those among them who acted well and remained mindful of God. The phrase places the response after the suffering, and the order is the point of the sentence.

The incident is well known in the sūrah's history. On the day after the fighting, with the community's wounded still being tended and its dead not yet counted, the Prophet called the men who had marched the previous day to set out with him in pursuit of the Makkan army.

**The Call and the Place of Assembly**

The reports describe the community assembling despite its condition, with the Prophet himself among the wounded, and marching a few miles from Madinah to a place called Ḥamrāʾ al-Asad. The force was smaller than the one that had fought the day before and in worse condition. The reports note that the men who answered the call included those who had been injured the day before, and that the place they reached lay several miles out on the road the enemy army had taken.

The verse's phrase about responding after being wounded therefore describes something quite specific. The community was not summoned at a time when enthusiasm was easy; it was summoned when the cost of the previous engagement was visible to everyone, and the verse records that the call was answered. The sūrah mentions the wounds before it mentions the response, and that order has shaped the way the passage has been read ever since.

**The Arrangement That Prevented Another Battle**

The reports describe the steps the Prophet took to prevent the Makkans from turning back toward Madinah. A man from a tribe with which the Prophet had a treaty was sent to tell the Makkans that the Madinans were pursuing them in far greater numbers than before, now including all those who had stayed behind during the fighting.

The community also lit a great many campfires at night within sight of the Makkan camp, so that the enemy would judge its numbers from the fires. The Makkans abandoned their intention to return, and the sūrah reports the outcome in the verse that follows this one. The arrangement shows how much of the affair turned on what each side believed about the other, and the community's own uncertainty was answered by the success of the ruse.

**What the Phrase About Responding Means**

The phrase about responding to God and the Messenger identifies the merit of the people described, and it does so by naming the two directions of the summons. The community's response was not to a leader's preference but to a call that carried divine authority.

The sūrah has used this vocabulary throughout its account of the campaign, and it uses it here for the group that answered when the answer was difficult. The Book's descriptions of merit are frequently attached to the difficulty of the act rather than to its scale, and the verse before the reader places a march of a few miles in that category because of the condition of the men who made it.

**The Reading That Points to the Lesser Badr**

A minority of commentators connected these verses with the appointment for a return engagement that the two sides are said to have made after Uḥud, with the meeting set a year later at the site of Badr. On that account the believers went out at the appointed time, found no enemy there, and bought goods at a market before returning home.

The reading changes the occasion but not the lesson. Whether the community marched in pursuit of the army that had beaten it or out to a meeting that its opponents failed to keep, the verses praise people who answered a summons when their own condition made compliance costly.

**The Condition Attached to the Reward**

The verse promises a great reward to those among them who were virtuous and mindful of God, and the qualifier distinguishes within the group that went out. The sūrah has already shown that the community's ranks included people whose aims varied, and the sentence keeps the distinction in view.

The requirement is the combination the sūrah has named repeatedly: good conduct and the awareness of God. The men who answered the call were not all alike in their motives, and the reward named in this verse belongs to those whose action was accompanied by the quality the sūrah has been teaching throughout the passage. The conditional form keeps the community from supposing that the march itself, apart from its motive, secured the promise.

**The Encouragement the Book Gives to Pursuit**

The Book addresses the same situation in another passage, where it tells the believers not to falter in the pursuit of the enemy and reminds them that their opponents were also suffering, while the community had hopes that the others did not have.

The encouragement in that verse and the account in this one belong together. The Book treats the decision to keep pressing an opponent as a matter that follows from the community's hope, and the men described in this verse made that decision while their wounds were still fresh. The difference in prospects between the two sides is presented in that passage as a practical advantage, and not merely as a consolation for the hardships of the march.

**The Model the Verse Supplies**

The verse offers the community a picture of what recovery from a defeat looks like: the fighting ends, the losses are counted, and then the community answers the next call. The Book presents that response as something more than a military decision.

The sūrah's readers have been given an extended account of a failure followed by correction, and this verse begins the account of what the corrected community did. What the Book shows is a body of people who had been told the cause of their loss and who then acted in accordance with the correction rather than with their fear. The next verses describe the report that reached them on the road and the reply they made to it, and the passage closes with a sentence about the source of the fear they were being asked to feel.
