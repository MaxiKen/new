## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:49

> ˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ledger Opens With a Rescue**

The bracketed "remember" at the head of the verse is supplied by the grammar: *idh*, "when," hangs on the *udh-kurū* of verse 47 — remember My favors... *and when* We delivered you. The recital of favors has begun, and its first entry is not a gift of property or status but an act of extraction: deliverance from a tormentor. The choice determines the reading of everything that follows. Before the sūrah records a single one of this community's failures — and it will record many, beginning a few verses on — it files the rescue that made them a people. Israel's national existence in the Qur'an is dated from the Exodus: a delivered community, born in the act of being taken out. The ledger's order is also the order of gratitude's logic: the favor remembered first is the one no later merit contributed to, since a slaughtered infant and an enslaved mother had nothing to offer. Whatever the covenant will demand, it demands from people whose first line-item was pure rescue — and the verse states the rescue with Allah as its only actor: *We delivered you*, no intermediary named, no human verb in the sentence's main clause.

**The People of Pharaoh: Deliverance From a System**

The object of the deliverance is *āl Firʿawn* — the people, household, and following of Pharaoh, not Pharaoh alone. The word *āl* gathers a tyrant's apparatus: the court, the officers, the army, the overseers, the informants — the whole machine through which one man's will becomes a population's torment. The deliverance is thereby political in the full sense: it ends not a person but a regime, and the Qur'an's phrasing everywhere keeps this collective frame — Pharaoh and his soldiers pursuing at the sea (10:90), Pharaoh and his hosts drowned, the lands and honors left behind passing to the delivered people, as the wider recital in al-Shuʿarāʾ records. The same favor is rehearsed in Sūrah Ibrāhīm with Moses as the preacher, addressing the same people with nearly the same sentence: *"Remember Allah's favour upon you when He rescued you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment—slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord"* (14:6). The double filing — Moses reciting the favor to them in the wilderness, Allah reciting it to their descendants in Madina — shows the memory being instituted as worship: a delivered people are commanded to keep the deliverance in speech, generation after generation, because the favor operates only while it is remembered.

**The Two Torments: Slaughter and Keeping**

The torment is itemized in two participles. *Yudhabbiḥūna abnāʾakum* — slaughtering your sons: the systematic killing of male infants, the verb of ritual slaughter applied to children, which is the word's full horror. *Wa-yastaḥyūna nisāʾakum* — keeping your women alive: the deliberate sparing of the females, not mercy but policy — life reserved for servitude, the women kept as the instrument of a people's reduction. The pair is a demographic strategy: kill the future (the sons), own the present (the women), and the nation dissolves within a generation. The verse's summary verb above them — *yasūmūnakum sūʾa al-ʿadhāb*, they afflicted you with dreadful torment — carries the sense of *sām*: to make someone taste repeatedly, to impose and re-impose; the torment was not an episode but a regimen. And no reason is given. The Qur'an records Pharaoh's motives elsewhere — the fear of a boy who would end his rule, the arrogance of a king who styled himself his people's supreme lord — but this verse presents the torment in its bare form, unmotivated in the telling: power afflicting because it can. The gratuitousness is part of the indictment; a torment with a grievance invites argument, and the verse declines to let Pharaoh have one.

**Balāʾ: The Torment Was Also a Test**

The final sentence reassigns the event: *wa-fī dhālikum balāʾun min rabbikum ʿaẓīm* — and in that lay a trial, immense, from your Lord. The torment, in its full wickedness, is simultaneously filed as *balāʾ* — trial — and the filing is from Allah: *min rabbikum*, from your Lord, using the name of care. The two readings do not cancel: Pharaoh's crime remains wholly Pharaoh's, and the trial remains wholly Allah's — the Qur'an's standing structure for evil, in which the perpetrator acts freely and is judged for it, while the Event enters God's governance as examination. Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ states the principle universally: *"Every soul will taste death. And We test you ˹O humanity˺ with good and evil as a trial, then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned"* (21:35) — evil is test-material as much as good is, and the test of evil is the hardest form, because it asks faith to survive without evidence of care. The verse thus binds the Exodus to the sūrah's wider theology of trial — the tests of Adam in the garden, of Ibrāhīm at the altar, of the believers at Badr to come — and it quietly arms the reader for the recital's next movements: the manna and the quail will be a test of gratitude, the wilderness commands a test of obedience, and the sea itself a test of trust. Deliverance does not end the examination; it changes its syllabus.

**Why Egypt Comes First in the Recital**

The sequence of the coming verses — rescue, sea, calf, pardon, book, manna, water from rock — is not merely chronological; it is pedagogical. Egypt is filed first because it is the fact that every later failure aggravates. The people who build the calf (verse 51) are people who watched the sea drown an army; the people who demand to see Allah plainly (2:55) are people who were fed without sowing; the people who refuse the city (2:58) are people who were carried out of a furnace. Each breach in the recital is committed by witnesses of verse 49, and the verse's position at the head makes that witness permanent: no failure can be read here without the rescue already on file. The order also teaches the sūrah's readers the anatomy of ingratitude — kufr al-niʿma, covering the favor — by showing its precondition: a favor this complete, received this helplessly, forgotten this fast. The recital is thus structured as a single argument across eighty verses: here is what was given; here is what was done with it; here — in the covenant of verse 40 and the warning of verse 48 — is the frame that makes both matter.

**“You”: The Descendants as Parties**

The verbs are all second-person plural — *We delivered you, they tormented you* — spoken to people centuries after the events, and the grammar is the verse's deepest claim. The Madinan children of Israel are addressed as the direct objects of the Exodus because a community that inherits a covenant inherits its history as its own: the favors flow to the descendants through the same channel as the obligations, and the Qur'an will not let the inheritance be taken one-sided. The collective personhood at work here — the ummah across generations as a single moral subject — is the same structure that lets verse 47's favor and verse 48's audit bind people who never stood at the sea. It also extends beyond Israel by design: al-Baqarah recites the Exodus to Muslims who are not its biological descendants, files it in their scripture, and makes its memory their own — which is why the sūrah addresses *them* with the same "remember." Every reader of verse 49 inherits the ledger: the rescue was for you too, in the only sense the Qur'an finally cares about — that the God who extracted a slaughtered people from Pharaoh's machine is the God your covenant runs through, and the test that began in Egypt has not ended; it has only changed addressees.
