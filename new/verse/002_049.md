## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:49

> ˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Favour Named in Full**

The command to remember the favours, issued twice in the preceding verses, now begins to be answered with specifics. The first item on the list is a rescue, and the Book describes it by describing what was being escaped. The sentence gives the oppressor, the method of oppression, and the two categories of victim, so that the reader understands the scale of what the community was brought out of.

That order matters for the passage. Before the Book asks anything of this community, it recites what the community received, and it recites it in the vocabulary of an ordinary historical record rather than of a sermon. The deliverance is presented as an event the audience is expected to know, and the memory is being invoked as evidence of the relationship that the following commands will ask them to honour.

**The Killings and the Policy Behind Them**

The oppression is described with two clauses that belong to a single policy: the male children were killed and the women were kept alive. The Book states the same arrangement in the sūrah of Stories: *“Indeed, Pharaoh ˹arrogantly˺ elevated himself in the land and divided its people into ˹subservient˺ groups, one of which he persecuted, slaughtering their sons and keeping their women. He was truly one of the corruptors.”* (28:4). The purpose of such a measure is control, and it explains why the community could not grow out of its position.

The Qur'an does not supply a reason for the killing beyond that, and the commentator al-Rāzī noted the implications. Since Pharaoh threatened the same policy again when Moses had grown up, the murders are better understood as a standing instrument of domination than as the result of an astrologer's prediction about a child who would overthrow him. The Book's own account supports the broader reading by tying the persecution to the policy of dividing a subject people.

**"Keeping Your Women"**

The second half of the phrase is easy to pass over and is part of the same description of tyranny. To keep the women alive was to keep them without protection, and the arrangement ensured both that the community could not increase and that its members could be used at the will of the ruling house. The Book includes the phrase in each of the two passages where it describes these events, which means it is not incidental to its account of the suffering.

The Qur'an's concern with this kind of detail is consistent. When it describes the affliction of a people, it names the specific harms done to those who had no power to prevent them, and it treats the fate of the vulnerable as the measure of a regime. The verse's hearers are being shown their own history as the Book remembers it, and the memory emphasises the position they once occupied.

**"A Severe Test From Your Lord"**

The closing clause names the whole experience a trial, and the word carries the sense of testing that reveals something. The Book uses it for both hardship and prosperity, and it applies the term to what a person goes through rather than to what a person deserves. In this case the trial is the suffering itself, and the Book presents it as having come from the Lord of the community that endured it.

The phrasing therefore holds two facts together: the oppressor is responsible for the cruelty, and the event takes place within the government of God. The Book nowhere treats tyranny as an accident or an absence of providence, and the verse assigns the trial to God without diminishing the guilt of the ones inflicting it. That combination recurs throughout the Qur'an's accounts of the prophets, where the persecution of a messenger and his people is described as a test and the persecutors are held to account for every act.

**What the Memory Was For**

The verse is the first item in a list of favours that the sūrah recites to prepare its audience for a request, and the sequence is instructive. Deliverance from a murderous regime comes first, and the Book states the deliverance before it narrates the escape itself. The reader learns that a people was saved from its oppressor before he learns how the saving happened.

The sūrah then continues to the sea and to the appointment with Moses, so that the passage amounts to a recitation of the community's foundations. What the Book is doing is claiming the audience's own history and reading it as a series of gifts. When the request to accept the confirming messenger arrives, it arrives to people who have just been reminded that everything they are as a community began with an act of rescue.

**What the Book Does With Oppression**

The Book records the persecution of a people in specific terms, and it does so in a passage that has nothing to gain from embellishment. The pharaoh's policy, the killing of male children and the retention of the women, is named in two separate sūrahs with the same vocabulary, and another verse explains the policy as part of a systematic division of a subject population (28:4). The Qur'an does not present tyranny as an unfortunate accident of politics; it presents it as a crime with a method.

The detail about the vulnerable is part of the Book's manner throughout. When it describes the suffering of a community, it names the harm done to those who could not defend themselves, and it treats those specifics as the measure of the regime. The audience being addressed is told what their ancestors endured, and the memory is placed at the head of the favours because deliverance from it is the foundation of everything that follows.
