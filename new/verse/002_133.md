## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:133

> Or did you witness when death came to Jacob? He asked his children, “Who will you worship after my passing?” They replied, “We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers— Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Put to the Audience**

The verse opens by addressing the reader directly and asking whether he was present at a particular deathbed. The question is rhetorical, and its function is to introduce a scene that the audience had never witnessed but whose record the Book is now supplying.

The sūrah has been handling claims about the patriarchs, and it introduces the final testimony of Jacob in order to let the reader hear what the ancestor and his children actually said. The Book's method here is to answer a claim about ancestry with a report of the ancestor's own words, and the instruction comes from the one who knows the past. The address also involves the reader in the scene, since the question asks him to consider what he knows about the past and where his knowledge comes from. The Book's answer is a report, and the audience is invited to receive it as the record of what the patriarchs actually said.

**Jacob at the End of His Life**

The Book presents Jacob as a prophet whose later years were marked by the loss of his son and by patience, and the chapter devoted to Joseph's story describes his trust in God throughout. The scene in this verse concerns his last concern before death, and it is not his property or his standing but the worship of his children.

The choice of subject gives the reader a measure of what the patriarchs valued. What a man asks about at the end of his life indicates what he has been living for, and the question recorded here is about the object of worship rather than about any worldly arrangement, which matches the bequest recorded in the previous verse.

**"Who Will You Worship After My Passing?"**

The question is direct and addressed to the children as a group. It presupposes that the answer matters and that the answer will be given by them rather than assumed from their family background, and the Book presents the exchange in order to make that point for its own audience.

The sūrah's argument throughout this passage concerns the claims a community makes about its forebears, and the question in the verse demonstrates that the forebears themselves treated the matter as one of personal commitment. What was entrusted to the children was not a status to be inherited but a worship to be maintained, and the question asks them whether they will maintain it. The question also identifies what the dying man considered essential. In a scene that could have concerned property, blessings, or family arrangements, the Book records a question about worship, and the answer follows the same theme, which shows what the patriarchs treated as the family's real inheritance.

**The Answer of the Children**

The reply identifies the God of their forefathers and names the three patriarchs: Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac. The list is instructive, since Ishmael is the uncle of Jacob rather than his father, and the Book elsewhere uses the term for forefathers broadly, covering ancestors in the line as well as the immediate parent.

The children's answer also affirms the oneness of God and attaches their own submission to it. The sentence therefore repeats the content of the religion in the words of the descendants, so that the reader hears the confession from the same generation the sūrah has been discussing. The reply also serves the sūrah's argument by showing that the descendants of Jacob knew what the religion of their forefathers was. The Book presents the answer as an item of family memory, so that the claims later made about the patriarchs by their descendants are measured against the confession recorded here.

**The Point of the Verse in the Argument**

The passage has been answering claims that the religion of Abraham belonged to one community, and it now supplies the testimony of the patriarch's own household. If the descendants of Jacob bore witness to the worship of one God and to their own submission, then the communities that later disputed over them were arguing about a legacy whose content the Book has now stated.

The verse also serves the community receiving the revelation, since the confession it records is the same one the sūrah asks of its readers. The Book presents the patriarchs as belonging to the religion of submission, and the reader who accepts the account is placed in the line of those who gave the same answer.

**The Names of God in the Verse**

The Arabic of the children's reply uses the general word for a deity rather than the proper name of God, and the commentators noted the distinction, since the phrase is the God of your forefathers, one God, rather than a repetition of the divine name. The wording allows the reader to see the confession as a statement about the object of worship, which is what Jacob's question had asked about.

The verse ends the cycle of Abraham material in the sūrah and prepares for the passage that follows, where the community is invited to identify itself with the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not a polytheist. The narrative of the patriarchs concludes with their descendants confessing the worship of the One God, and the sūrah's next argument concerns the community that claims them.

**The Religion the Patriarchs Left**

The scene at Jacob's death completes the chain the sūrah has been constructing from Abraham's first answer to the command to submit. Four generations are represented in the passage, and each of them is shown holding the same position: Abraham answered the command, Isaac is named among the forefathers, Jacob passed the charge to his sons, and the sons answered with a confession of the One God. The Book's purpose in tracing the sequence is to establish that the religion it defends is the religion of the patriarchs, so that the claims made about them by later communities can be tested against what they themselves said. The community reading the sūrah is asked whether it was present at the deathbed, and the answer is that the record now supplies what the audience could not have witnessed. What the reader is given is the family's own testimony, and the passage that follows turns from the testimony to the question of who is entitled to claim it.
