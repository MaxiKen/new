## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:133

> Or did you witness when death came to Jacob? He asked his children, “Who will you worship after my passing?” They replied, “We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers— Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Witness Question**

The verse opens by putting its audience on the stand: *or did you witness when death came to Jacob?* The question — the recital's challenge-form, familiar from verse 80's pledge-demand and verse 94's death-wish — is addressed to the claimants of the patriarchs, and its first function is evidentiary. The disputants of Madina spoke for Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes; the verse asks whether they were present at the scene that defines the patriarchs' faith — the deathbed of Jacob — and the answer is known: they were not. Their claim rests on report, and the report they do not carry is being recited to them now, in dialogue, with the questions and answers intact. The verse thereby installs the recital's authority over the patriarchs' legacy: whatever the claimants assert about their ancestors' religion, the Qur'an files the founding scene itself — the actual question the dying father asked, the actual answer the twelve sons gave — and the filing is the testimony the absence of witnesses cannot rebut. The *or* of the opening continues a challenge-sequence, and its continuation is the verse's method: the counsel of verse 132 stated the bequest in summary; verse 133 stages it, so that the bequest is no longer reported but performed — the waṣiyya heard in the room where it was given, with the recitation itself as the only reliable witness present. The audience of every later age stands in the claimants' position: no one since has been present at that deathbed either, and everyone since receives the scene from the same testimony.

**The Dying Father's Question**

Jacob's question to his children is the verse's center, and its wording is a deathbed's whole priorities in one sentence: *what will you worship after me?* Not who will lead you, not how will the tribes be ordered, not what will become of the estate or the covenant's external marks — the object of worship, and its survival past the father's presence. The question assumes the danger the patriarchs' counsel has been legislating against: religion held in a father's presence can be held by proximity, and the deathbed is the test of whether the sons hold the God or the household. The interrogative *what* rather than *who* — *mā*, the thing-question — carries a theological precision the exegetes mark: the question asks after the object of worship as such, the reality to be worshipped, not yet its name; and the sons' answer supplies the identification. The scene also completes verse 132's counsel in dramatic form: the bequest commanded *do not die except in submission*, and the deathbed question examines the command's transmission — will the state survive me? Jacob asks what every bequeather actually asks, and the verse files the asking as the patriarchs' final office: not the transfer of property but the examination of orientation, conducted at the one moment when pretense is most expensive, with the sons' answer to be lived after the room empties. The recital's hundred-verses file of the tribes' subsequent history is, from this scene's vantage, the long record of the answer's keeping and losing — and the verse preserves the answer in its founding purity so that the record can always be measured against it.

**The Sons' Answer**

The reply is the verse's creed, and its structure moves in three steps. *We will worship your God* — the personal term first: the sons receive the faith as their father's God, the deity of a lived relationship they were raised inside, the continuity of the household's worship. *And the God of your forefathers — Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac* — the historical term second: the fathers named, the lineage's three patriarchs listed in an order the verse makes remarkable. Ishmael stands between Abraham and Isaac — the Arabian uncle named among the fathers, in a list the Israel-line's descendants speak, and the inclusion is the recital's genealogical reconciliation in the sons' own mouth: the House's two builders (125, 127) are both ancestral to the worship confessed here, the family's two lines joined in one God-list. The exegetes note the verse's noun-choice with matching care: *your God* translates *ilāh*, the common noun for deity, not the proper Name — the sons confess the object's oneness before they speak its name, and the confession's third step states the oneness: *the One God* — *ilāhun wāḥid*, the singularity that makes the three fathers' God one God rather than three household deities. The answer closes where the whole section has been heading: *and to Him we submit* — the islām-clause, the patriarchs' counsel (132) answered in the patriarch's own house, the submission-pose of verse 131 spoken by the twelve. Sūrah Yūsuf preserves the same confession from one son's mouth a lifetime later: Joseph in the Egyptian prison following *the faith of my fathers — Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob* (12:38), the list recurring, the submission restated — the deathbed creed become the tribes' standing formula in its best son.

**The Patriarchs Cannot Be Owned**

The verse is the recital's argument, in narrative form, against every exclusive claim on the patriarchs, and the argument's force is that the scene is the claimants' own. The Qur'an's general case runs through this section: Abraham was neither Jew nor Christian (3:67); his millah is submission, held by whoever answers its command (130–131); the covenant excludes the wrongdoer of any line (124). Verse 133 supplies the case's decisive exhibit — the deathbed itself, with the Israel-line's own father asking the worship-question and his own sons answering in the terms the recital legislates: one God, the fathers' God including Ishmael, and submission to Him. The answer the tribes' founder received is the answer the Qur'an preaches, which makes the Madinan claimants' rejection of the Messenger a rejection filed against their own founding scene: the patriarchs, in the tradition's reading, were followers of the pure religion of Abraham — primordial monotheism, which is to say islām in the verse's own closing word — and the communities that claim them while rejecting the submission-formula contradict, the recital asserts, the only witnessed testimony about what the patriarchs actually transmitted. The verse's witness-question thus inverts at its close: the claimants were not present at Jacob's deathbed, but the recital was — and what it reports is a creed with no exclusivity clause in it, no lineage-reservation, no millah-property: the God of all the fathers, one, submitted to. The patriarchs belong to whoever holds their sons' answer, and the answer's text is quoted here in full, so that the belonging is adjudicated by the scene rather than the claim.

**The Scene as the Section's Seal**

Verse 133 closes the recital's Abraham-cycle, and the closing scene gathers the cycle's whole argument into a room. The test and its fulfillment (124) end in the question every fulfillment faces: will it survive into the children? The imāmah conditioned on righteousness (124) stands confirmed: the office passed through the answer, not the blood. The House built and purified (125), the city prayed for (126), the building's acceptance requested (127), the submitting nation and its messenger asked (128–129) — every petition lands in this scene's confession: the nation submitting, in its patriarchal nucleus, answering at the deathbed of Israel's father. The millah beyond denomination (130), the one-word command answered in the perfect tense (131), the bequest commanding the submitted exit (132) — all three verified in the sons' closing clause: *to Him we submit*. The recital seals the section with the formula its next movement will legislate for the Muslim community in the same terms, and the seal's message to every claimant of every patriarch is the verse's standing challenge: the witness-question runs against all of them — you were not present, the testimony is recited, and the testimony contains the creed. Whoever holds the creed holds the fathers; whoever rejects it is rejected by the scene, whatever his lineage. The deathbed of Jacob remains the recital's examination-room for every child of the patriarchs, and the twelve sons' answer remains on the record — your God, the God of Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac, one God, submitted to — waiting at every generation's exit for the voice that will speak it as its own.
