## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:26

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ gives you life, then causes you to die, then will gather you ˹all˺ on the Day of Judgment, about which there is no doubt. But most people do not know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply Put in the Prophet's Mouth**

The verse is issued as a command: say. The Prophet is not told to argue, to rebut the challenge of the previous verse point by point, or to plead for a hearing. He is given a sentence to deliver, and the sentence contains the whole answer. This is the Qur'an's regular way of handling a hostile question. The messenger states what is true about God and lets the statement do its work, and the response of the audience is not his responsibility. A reader who has watched the demand for the ancestors to be produced might expect a defence of the possibility of resurrection. What arrives instead is an assertion about who gives life in the first place, and the shift is the answer.

**Life and Death as the Work of One Hand**

The two verbs are placed together as a single act of a single agent. This is the direct reply to the claim that nothing destroys people except the passage of time. If the same power that gives life also takes it, then time is not the destroyer; it is the interval, and the one who set the interval is also the one who ends the life inside it. The Book states the pairing in verse after verse, as when it says that God is the one who gives life and causes death (53:44), when it describes him as the one who gives life and causes death and who only has to say "Be!" for a matter to exist (40:68), and when it says that he created death and life as a test of who would act best (67:2). The denial of the resurrection begins with a misreading of the most ordinary fact available: that a person did not give himself life and cannot keep it.

**The Gathering After the Dying**

The third clause of the sentence moves past the point at which the deniers stopped. After life and death comes the gathering on the Day of Judgment, and the Book attaches the same certainty to it in other places: God has taken it upon himself to be merciful, and he will certainly gather all of humanity together on that Day about which there is no doubt (6:12). The order of the three clauses is the order of the argument. The first two are uncontested facts that the deniers themselves had admitted in different words; the third follows from the nature of the one who performs the first two. If a person's life is given and taken by a power outside himself, the question of what happens to him afterwards is not settled by the observation that his body lies in the ground.

**"About Which There Is No Doubt"**

The phrase is not a rhetorical flourish; it describes a property of the Day itself rather than the state of mind of the people hearing about it. The Hour is certain, and the uncertainty belongs entirely to those who doubt it. The Book holds this line consistently, describing the Hour as coming without warning and asking why people question it, and it treats the confidence of the believer as a share in a fact rather than a feeling of optimism. The sentence in this verse therefore answers the sceptic's posture rather than his objection. He had asked for proof that the dead can return. The reply informs him that the event has an appointed time, that its occurrence is not in question, and that his inability to schedule it does not affect the case.

**"But Most People Do Not Know"**

The clause appears throughout the Book in nearly the same wording, and it serves as a frame for a certain kind of fact: people do not know about the Hour (7:187), most people do not know that the Prophet was sent to all of humanity (34:28), and most do not know that God gives life and death. The repetition is not contempt for the human race; it is an accurate description of a majority condition on matters of the unseen, and the Book uses it to prepare the believer for being in the minority. The sentence is delivered to an audience that had just made a demand, and the clause tells them, in plain language, what their position amounts to. They are not the holders of a difficult truth. They are the people who do not know.

**The Answer to the Demand for a Sign**

The challenge was to bring the ancestors back so that belief could follow. The answer names the one who has them, and who gives and takes according to a schedule none of the challengers controls. No miracle is staged, and none is promised. What the verse offers instead is an ownership claim: the dead are not in the hands of the men who demand them, and the Day on which they will all appear has already been set. The Book's restraint here is the point. A revelation that produced a demonstration to order in the market would have made belief a function of spectacle, and the sūrah has spent its first half arguing that the evidence has already been provided in the created order.

**A Message the Majority Would Not Accept**

The three facts of the verse are simple, and the verse itself says most people will not know them. The sūrah never pretends otherwise, and the next few verses turn to the scene in which the matter is settled: a kingdom that already belongs to God, an Hour that will be established, and the loss of those who worked to make the truth look false. The Prophet is instructed to deliver the sentence and to leave the outcome alone. The chapter has no interest in winning an argument in the marketplace. It is interested in stating the situation clearly, and the situation is that life, death, and judgment are all in the hands of the one who made them.
