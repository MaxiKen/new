## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:26

> So Lot believed in him. And Abraham said, “I am emigrating ˹in obedience˺ to my Lord. He ˹alone˺ is indeed the Almighty, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Follower Named**

Lot's acceptance of Abraham's message is reported in three words, and the brevity is part of the point. Nothing is recorded about the arguments that convinced him or the doubts he weighed; the Book simply states that he believed the man standing beside him. Lot is identified elsewhere as a relative of Abraham, and the family connection makes the sentence more valuable rather than less, since the first person to accept a prophet is usually the one who has watched him most closely. The Book's habit of naming the early believers in its narratives — a wife, a nephew, a household — gives the reader a picture of the movement at its beginning: not a crowd persuaded by spectacle but a handful of people near enough to see what kind of man the messenger was. The verse's restraint is also a form of respect for Lot: he is given no speech here, only the act of believing, which is the whole of what the account requires of him at this point.

**A Departure Announced by the Founder**

The statement that follows belongs to Abraham rather than to Lot, and the Book gives it the form of a public declaration: I am emigrating in obedience to my Lord. A man who has just been told that his town intends to burn him announces a journey, and the announcement is made without haste and without apology. Nothing in the sentence suggests flight in the ordinary sense, and the Book never describes Abraham's departure as an escape from danger; it is a transfer of residence undertaken at his Lord's direction. The declaration also serves notice on the town. The argument is over, the man is leaving, and the initiative in the story passes from the council that condemned him to the God he says he is obeying.

The announcement is also the first step of the migration rather than a comment on it. Abraham states his intention in public before he moves, which gives his household and any believer within reach of his voice a chance to prepare, and the Book records the declaration because it made the departure a decision rather than an escape.

**Emigration as an Act of Worship**

The word the verse uses places the journey among the things a person does for God rather than among the things that are done to him. Commentators treat this as the first migration in the Book, and the pattern it establishes recurs in the lives of the prophets and in the experience of the first Muslim community in Makkah. Sūrah Maryam records the consequence in the same order the story requires: after he had left them and what they worshiped besides God, God granted him Isaac and Jacob, and made each of them a prophet (19:49). The reward in that verse is attached to the departure, not to the argument, and the connection is not accidental. Obedience that costs a person his city is the kind the Book treats as a test passed at a different level from the debates that preceded it.

The framing also gives the journey a purpose beyond survival. A family that moves only to avoid harm is driven by its enemies, while a family that moves for the sake of worship is carrying out an instruction, and the sūrah's own command to the believers in Makkah rests on the same distinction.

**The Names That Steady a Departure**

The two names at the end of the sentence answer the two fears that a migration produces. A person leaving home needs strength, because the road and the new country will test whatever he has; and he needs foresight, because the decisions ahead are unfamiliar and irreversible. Abraham names his Lord as the Almighty, able to protect what He has taken charge of, and the All-Wise, whose arrangements are not improvisations. The Book attaches these names to its promises of settlement elsewhere, and the pairing works as a receipt for the decision just announced. He is giving up a place whose protection he could see for a protector he cannot see, and the sentence states the terms on which he is content to do it.

The names he uses also tell the reader what a migrant needs to believe about the place he is going. A person who leaves home with nothing arranged needs to be convinced that the one who directed him is able to receive him, and the titles Abraham pronounces cover exactly that pair of needs.

**What the Book Promises the Migrant**

The Book generalizes the promise that Abraham's decision implies: whoever emigrates in the cause of God will find many safe havens and abundant resources across the earth, and the one who leaves his home and dies while emigrating has his reward already secured with God, who is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful (4:100). Sūrah al-Naḥl describes the same people as those who emigrated after being persecuted, and promises them a good home in this world and a better reward in the next, naming patient endurance and trust in their Lord as their distinguishing marks (16:41–42). The Book never presents departure as a solution available in every case; the earlier reports in this sūrah acknowledge believers who were genuinely unable to leave. What it presents is a decision with an attached guarantee, and Abraham is the first to make it. The terms are stated in the plainest things the Book has at its disposal — shelter, provision, a reward that cannot be lost — which is exactly what a person weighing such a step needs to hear before he takes it.

**The Relative Who Believed Without a Debate**

The Book records no argument between Abraham and Lot, and the silence is worth noticing in a chapter that has reported extended disputes. The nearest witnesses to a prophet are usually the hardest to convince, because familiarity makes them experts on the man rather than on the message, and the Book elsewhere supplies the counterexample: Abraham's own father heard the case and rejected it, and the argument he received was patient and detailed. Lot accepted, and he is named in the verse by his belief alone. The Book's habit of recording who believed early is a record of recognition, and it presents the first follower of this mission as a man who had watched his relative closely and drew the conclusion his neighbours would not.

The silence around Lot's acceptance also serves the account. The Book reports no argument, no hesitation, and no condition, and a reader who has watched the extended disputes in the surrounding verses notices the contrast, which suggests that a man living beside a prophet may have needed less persuasion than a stranger.

**What Was Carried and What Was Left**

A migration is always a subtraction as well. Abraham walked out of a city where he had a father, a household, and a place in the argument, and the Book elsewhere places the wife of Lot among the examples of a house divided on the question of belief, naming her alongside the wife of Noah as a case of betrayal inside a prophet's own family (66:10). The example establishes that kinship does not carry faith with it, and this verse shows the same rule from the side of the man who leaves. He carried out of the town a decision and a nephew; what he left behind included people who could not be brought with him, and the Book records the loss in the same narrative as the promise, without softening either one. A reader who wants the reward of obedience delivered without subtraction will not find that arrangement anywhere in this chapter.
