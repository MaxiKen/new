## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:104

> Do not falter in pursuit of the enemy—if you are suffering, they too are suffering. But you can hope to receive from Allah what they can never hope for. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Do Not Falter in Pursuit of the Enemy**

The verse opens *wa lā tahinū fī ibtighāʾi al-qawmi* — do not falter, do not weaken, do not flag in seeking, pursuing, going after the people — the enemy. *Tahinū* — to be weak, to waver, to lose heart — was explained as slackening after the strain of travel, fear prayer, and guarded worship just taught (4:101–103). *Ibtighāʾ* — seeking, pursuing — was read as following after, not merely defending where attacked but seeking the hostile that refuses peace (4:89–91) and that wishes you neglect for a sweep (4:102). The prohibition was understood as addressed to a community that had been told to go forth in groups or together (4:71), to fight for the oppressed who cry (4:75), and whose struggles had included Uḥud’s wound and the questioning *“why was fighting ordered now and not delayed a little?”* (4:77). To *not falter* was read as the sūrah’s steadiness: the same discipline that makes prayer a writ at fixed times (4:103) makes pursuit a writ where justice requires it. The sūrah elsewhere says *“do not be like those who left homes boastfully and hinder from God’s way”* (8:47), but here the command is internal: let not wound become weakness.

**If You Are Suffering, They Too Are Suffering**

The measure *in takūnū taʾlamūna fa innahum yaʾlamūna kamā taʾlamūna* — if you are suffering, feeling pain, they too are suffering as you suffer — was explained as the sūrah’s equalisation of pain. *Taʾlamūna* — you suffer, you feel hurt — was read as wounds, fatigue, hunger, fear — the same suffering the sūrah counted as shared: hunger and thirst are human weakness (4:28), and death overtakes even in fortified towers (4:78). To say they suffer as you do removes the illusion that the enemy’s side is without cost; the hostile who wish you to be as they are (4:89) and who plunge back into *fitnah* (4:91) also bear loss. The comparison was linked to *“if a wound touches you, a like wound touched the other people”* (3:140), revealed after Uḥud where both sides were wounded yet the believers faltered. The sūrah that taught that good and evil are both destined while evil from the self as means (4:78–79) here applies that symmetry to pain: pain is destined for both in conflict, but its meaning differs, as the next clause shows.

**But You Hope From God What They Never Can**

The distinction *wa tarjūna mina Allāhi mā lā yarjūn* — but you can hope to receive from God what they can never hope for — was explained as the sūrah’s asymmetry of hope. *Tarjūn* — you hope, expect, await — was read as hope for ranks, forgiveness, and mercy from Him (4:96), for many havens and secured reward even if death overtakes on hijrah (4:100), for the humiliating punishment prepared for disbelievers where you guarded prayer (4:102), and for the Gardens where the prayer writ is kept (4:103). That hope was explained as the believers’ exclusive because the disbeliever, by turning away from trust and referral to the Messenger (4:58–61), has no standing to expect it. The clause therefore does not deny the enemy’s pain but denies his hope, as the sūrah elsewhere says *“do not think those who disbelieve prolonging time is good; they increase in sin”* (3:178). The hope is not a claim of victory only; it is forgiveness for the mistaken, mercy for the helpless, and reward for the striver — all sealed *Ghafūr Raḥīm* (4:96, 4:100) or *ʿAfuww Ghafūr* (4:99). The sufferers on one side have a door that sufferers on the other do not: the same suffering, divergent destination.

**And God Is All-Knowing, All-Wise**

The seal *wa kāna Allāhu ʿAlīman Ḥakīmā* — And God is All-Knowing, All-Wise — was read as the knowledge that weighs faltering and wisdom that times pursuit. *ʿAlīm* — All-Knowing — was explained as knowing who falters from wound and who from desire to deviate (4:27), who verifies before fighting (4:94) and who seeks fleeting gain by denying peace (4:94), whose earth is wide yet unreachable (4:98) and whose heart can still seek pardon. *Ḥakīm* — All-Wise — was read as wisdom that commands pursuit where the oppressed cry (4:75), permits shortening where travel and fear constrict (4:101–102), establishes prayer at fixed time (4:103), and will send the Book to judge by what He shows (4:105). The pair *ʿAlīm Ḥakīm* has sealed the law for mistake by fasting (4:92) and for judging between people; here it seals the ethic of pursuit: He knows pain and knows who bears it for hope and who bears it without hope, and He judges with the wisdom that does not command beyond weakness yet does not excuse faltering where hope is offered.

**A Verse That Turns Shared Pain Into Divergent Hope**

Placed after the law that guards prayer under arms (4:102) and the writ that prayer is at appointed times (4:103), this verse turns the shared fact of suffering into divergent hope. The sūrah that began from a single soul and commanded mindfulness of ties (4:1), that promised lightening because humanity is weak (4:28), here acknowledges weakness — you suffer — then refuses to let suffering become faltering where the enemy still refuses peace (4:89–91). The suffering is equal as wound, unequal as meaning, because one side’s suffering can be met with hope for ranks and mercy, the other’s cannot. For the weak this is steadying: to pursue the hostile who would sweep upon neglect (4:102) is not to deny pain but to place pain inside hope, as the sūrah’s later command to be firm for justice though against yourselves (4:135) will place justice inside pain. The next passage will turn from pursuit to judgment, where the Book was sent to judge between people by what He has shown and not to be an advocate for the deceitful (4:105).
