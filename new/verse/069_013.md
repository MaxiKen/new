## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:13

> At last, when the Trumpet will be blown with one blast,

**Expanded Commentary**

**History Ends, the Trumpet Begins**

Verse 13 pivots the sūrah from precedent to apocalypse. Tribes, empires, and floods have testified across verses 4-12; now the trumpet sounds and the cosmos itself takes the stand. Fa-idhā — "then when" or "at last, when" — marks the hinge: given all those local hours, here comes the Hour. Escalation is total. Wind unmade a tribe, water unmade a world, but the trumpet unmakes the universe. Readers feel the floor drop: history's gallery exits directly into eschatology, with no intermission. The verse's incompleteness quickens the fall — a "when" clause dangling toward verse 14, grammar itself holding its breath. What follows will crush earth, tear sky, and station angels. All of it hangs on this blast. The trumpet is the sūrah's true subject; everything before it was prelude.

**One Blast, No Rehearsal**

Nafkhatun wāḥidah — "one blast" — stresses singularity. The trumpet sounds once, and once suffices; heaven needs no encore to end the world. Unity here conveys both ease and finality — creation that rose by command falls by breath. Other passages number two blasts: one felling all creatures, one raising them (39:68). This verse holds only the first in view, the blast of universal swooning. Singularity also answers delay-based doubt: deniers counted quiet years as proof of safety, but one morning's note will settle all accounts. No gradual decay, no negotiated surrender — a single sound, and silence after. The adjective wāḥidah will echo in verse 14's "one blow," doubling singularity into certainty. Twice-struck oneness leaves no room for survival. What one blast begins, one blow completes.

**First Blast or Second**

Which trumpeting does the verse mean? Most exegetes — al-Bayḍāwī, Qatādah, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī — hear the first blast, blown by Isrāfīl to end the world. A minority — Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn — hears the second, raising the dead for judgment. Grounds divide on sequence. The majority notes what follows: earth lifted and crushed (verse 14), sky torn (verse 16) — scenes of unmaking, not of rising. The minority notes the sūrah's theme: resurrection dominates, so the blast should raise. Context favors the majority decisively. Verses 14-17 stage demolition and angelic stationing, the world's end rather than its courtroom; the courtroom arrives at verse 18's exposure. The sūrah thus orders its apocalypse correctly — felling first, reckoning after. Both blasts are real; this verse sounds the first. The dead will rise later, when the ruins have finished falling.

**Isrāfīl Holds His Breath**

Tradition names the trumpeter: Isrāfīl, the archangel who stands horn to lips awaiting the command. Exegetes who identify the first blast place him here, poised between verses — breath drawn since creation, note approaching. The image concentrates dread exquisitely: the universe persists only because one angel has not yet exhaled. Such personification of the end serves immediacy; apocalypse is not mechanism but obedience, an angel's single act. The Book elsewhere multiplies trumpet scenes — the piercing call, the stunning cry, the day of the great shout (50:20; 74:8; 78:18) — each naming the same note from a new angle. Variety in naming matches unity in event. Whatever the blast is called, Isrāfīl blows it once. Between his readiness and his sounding stretches the whole of remaining history — mercy's last exhalation before his.

**The Blast That Ends All Noise**

Consider what one blast silences: markets mid-haggle, armies mid-march, orchestras mid-note, mockers mid-scoff. Every human sound stops inside heaven's single sound. The trumpet is thus creation's last word and judgment's first — punctuation ending history's sentence. Fa-idhā's suddenness forbids preparation: no countdown warns, no prelude softens. Suddenness is theologically essential. A scheduled end would breed last-minute bargains and deathbed theater; the unheralded blast finds every soul exactly as a life left it. Deniers who demanded dates (70:1's challenger among them) get instead a note without warning. Punctuality belongs to God alone. The verse withholds the blast's sound itself — no onomatopoeia, no description — letting dread imagine it. What cannot be pictured can still be expected. Expectation is the verse's whole demand.

**At Last Answers Every Delay**

"At last" in translation catches fa-idhā's flavor of long-awaited arrival. The overture named the Inevitable Hour; history certified lesser hours; now the Hour itself finally sounds. Delay mocked by deniers ends in dawn. Such vindication-after-waiting structures the Book's entire eschatology — Noah waited centuries, the prophets waited lifetimes, and every wait ended in morning. The verse's "when" is therefore promissory as well as predictive: it pledges arrival to the strained as much as it threatens arrival to the scornful. Both congregations hear the same blast coming; preparation divides their hearing. For believers the trumpet is wedding-music, longed for through affliction. For deniers it is the alarm they mocked. One note, two receptions — the Day's first duality, previewing the ledgers' greater one. Waiting ends for all; welcome ends for some.

**Mercy Hides Inside the Warning**

The trumpet threatens, yet the threat itself is mercy. Warnings unheeded still count as grace extended — every verse describing the blast gives hearers time the blast itself will not. Noah preached centuries before water rose; Lot warned till dawn before earth flipped; the trumpet's description preaches till its sounding. Such is heaven's habit: announce ruin long, execute ruin swiftly. The living should therefore receive this verse as gift rather than gloom — dread now purchases safety then. Only the Day's arrival ends mercy's announcements. Until that note sounds, every recitation reopens the offer: hear the trumpet described, and never hear it deserved. The verse terrifies the comfortable precisely to comfort the terrified. Warning is rescue wearing dread's clothing.

**The Sentence Spans Two Verses**

Verse 13 opens a "when" it never closes — the apodosis waits in verse 14's crushing and verse 15's befalling. Grammar enacts apocalypse: the blast sounds, and consequences cascade across verse boundaries without pause. Reciters feel the momentum bodily, breath carrying forward because syntax does. Such enjambment quickens dread — no full stop between trumpet and ruin. The sūrah used similar spanning for ʿĀd's wind and the challenger's dare; here the stakes are cosmic. Form follows content: just as the blast permits no interval, the sentence grants none. Readers should ride the momentum rather than resist it, letting verses 13-15 arrive as one avalanche. The trumpet sounds in this verse; the next verse crushes the earth beneath its note.
