## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:84

> So fight in the cause of Allah ˹O Prophet˺. You are accountable for none but yourself. And motivate the believers ˹to fight˺, so perhaps Allah will curb the disbelievers’ might. And Allah is far superior in might and in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**So Fight in the Cause of Allah**

The command *fa qātil fī sabīli Allāhi* — so fight in the cause of Allah — is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ alone after the diagnosis that many lag, that news of security or fear is publicised without referral (4:83), and that some were told *do not fight, establish prayer and give alms* then feared people as God when fighting was prescribed (4:77). *Fa* — so — ties the command to that diagnosis: though many hesitate, the cause remains God’s, the same *sabīl* that believers fight for while disbelievers fight for the Devil whose schemes are ever weak (4:76), and the same cause for which the oppressed cry for a guardian and helper (4:75). The sūrah has said to take precautions and go forth in groups or together (4:71) and to let those who sell this life for the next fight with a great reward whether martyred or victorious (4:74); here the Prophet ﷺ is told to fight even if others do not. The cause does not depend on the laggers’ measure — *blessed we were not there* at disaster (4:72) — nor on the fearful’s plea for delay; it is God’s path made clear (4:26) and the straight path to which doing what was advised would have guided (4:68).

**You Are Accountable for None but Yourself**

The clause *lā tukallafu illā nafsaka* — you are accountable for none but yourself — was read as both limit and liberation. The Prophet ﷺ is responsible for his own fighting, not for compelling the hypocrites who turn away stubbornly (4:61) and spend the night contradicting (4:81). The sūrah has said messengers are sent to be obeyed by God’s leave (4:64), that obedience to him is obedience to God (4:80), and that he is not a keeper over those who turn (4:80) but a warner and witness (4:41). To be accountable only for himself frees him from the burden that the laggers’ absence reflects on his mission; the mission remains to convey, to fight, and to motivate. The same clause was read as mercy for the community: each believer is accountable for his own referral to the Messenger and authorities (4:83) and for his own standing at *ḥidhr*. The sūrah elsewhere says *“no soul is charged beyond what it can bear”* and here that charging is individualized at the point of fighting, where fear of people can make a person feel responsible for all.

**Motivate the Believers**

The addition *wa ḥarriḍ al-muʾminīna* — and motivate, urge, rouse the believers — was explained as the Prophet’s second task beyond his own fighting: to stir the believers to fight with reminders of what the sūrah has shown — the Gardens with vast shade for believing and doing good (4:57), the great reward for selling this life for the next whether martyred or victorious (4:74), and the cry of the oppressed who ask for a guardian and helper from grace (4:75). *Taḥrīḍ* — to urge, to sharpen — was read as encouragement that makes the reluctant firm — *ashadda tathbītā* — the firmness that doing what was advised would have given (4:66). The sūrah has shown why urging is needed: some fear people as God (4:77), some lag and call absence a blessing (4:72), some publicise fear. Urging counters each with the sūrah’s measures — *Satan’s schemes are weak* (4:76), *death overtakes wherever you are even in towers* (4:78), *good from God, evil from self by what hands put forward* (4:79). To motivate is therefore not to push the weak beyond lightening (4:28) but to remind them that fighting after precautions, in God’s cause, is the form obedience takes where oppression exists.

**Perhaps Allah Will Curb the Disbelievers’ Might**

The hope *ʿasā Allāhu an yakuffa baʾsa alladhīna kafarū* — so perhaps Allah will curb, restrain, the might of the disbelievers — was read as the divine outcome of the Prophet’s fighting and urging. *Baʾs* — might, force, violence — was explained as the power by which the land of oppressors holds the weak — men, women, children — who cry for deliverance. To curb that *baʾs* is the sūrah’s answer to that cry; the guardian and helper appointed from grace (4:45, 4:75) curbs the might that made the *qaryah ẓalimi ahluhā* — the town of oppressors. The word *ʿasā* — perhaps — was explained as assurance from God, as elsewhere *“perhaps your Lord will...”* means He will, because the speaker is God. The sūrah has shown that curbing comes not by the believers’ numbers alone but by God’s might, as the next clause says He is superior in might and punishment. Early reports placed the verse when the Prophet ﷺ faced the Confederates with few, and God curbed them by wind and division; the type was read as every time believers fight with precautions after referral, God curbs.

**Allah Is Far Superior in Might and in Punishment**

The closing *wa Allāhu ashaddu baʾsan wa ashaddu tankīlā* — and Allah is far superior in might and in punishment — was read as the ground for *perhaps He will curb*. *Ashaddu baʾsan* — more intense in might — than any *baʾs* believers face; *ashaddu tankīlā* — more intense in punishment, in making an example — than any deterrent the disbelievers deploy. The pair matches the sūrah’s earlier *“Satan’s schemes are ever weak”* (4:76) and *“that is easy for Allah”* where deliberate devouring leads to Fire (4:30). The might that appoints heirs after debts (4:11), that renews skin for tasting (4:56), and that multiplies an atom’s weight (4:40) is the might that curbs. For the weak for whom burdens were lightened, the verse is the final lightening for fighting: you are accountable only for yourself, urge the believers, and perhaps God will curb the might that frightened you as God should be feared. The superior might and punishment are not threats to the believers who fight for the oppressed; they are guarantees that the cause they fight in — God’s — is supported by a might no *baʾs* can match and a punishment no *kayd* can withstand.
