## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:5

> **And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Blessing After The Rebuke**

After describing the human as a contentious challenger, the Qur'an immediately says: wa-l-anʿāma khalaqahā — and the cattle He created for you. The pronoun lakum, for you, is placed early to stress purpose and favor. Despite contention, provision continues. Anʿām in Qur'anic usage denotes grazing livestock: camels, cattle, sheep, and goats, as detailed in 6:142-144 where eight pairs are listed. These four kinds were the core wealth of Arabian society, providing transport, milk, meat, wool, leather, and social standing. By naming them right after the drop, the sūrah moves from origin to sustenance, showing that the One who created from a drop also sustains that creation with other creations. Al-Ṭabarī notes that khalaqahā emphasizes that even these familiar animals are not self-existing; they were created, assigned, and subjected. The human did not invent the camel's endurance or the sheep's wool.

**Warmth, Food, And More**

Lakum fīhā difʾun wa-manāfiʿu wa-minhā ta'kulūn — in them there is warmth and benefits and from them you eat. Difʾ, translated as warmth, comes from d-f-ʾ, to warm, to protect from cold. Classical commentators explain it as wool, hair, fur, and leather used for clothing, tents, and bedding. In a desert climate where nights are cold, this is not luxury but survival. Al-Qurṭubī adds that difʾ also includes the warmth of shelter made from animal products, not only clothing. Manāfiʿ, benefits, is plural and indefinite, indicating many other uses: milk, breeding, sale, dowry, blood-money, and manure for fields. The Qur'an often leaves manāfiʿ open to include what people know and what they discover later. Wa-minhā ta'kulūn — and from them you eat — is placed last though eating is immediate, to show that food is one benefit among many, not the only one. Al-Rāzī points out the order: first protection from cold, which is preservation of life, then general benefits, then specific food, moving from necessity to utility to enjoyment. The structure teaches gratitude in layers.

**A Blessing That Is Also A Sign**

In Sūrat al-Niʿam, blessings are never only blessings; they are signs pointing beyond themselves. Cattle are a sign of Allah's mercy because He created them with qualities perfectly suited to human need: camels store water and endure thirst, sheep produce wool that grows again, cattle provide strength for plowing. These qualities did not have to be together. Al-Rāzī argues that the convergence of many benefits in one animal is evidence of intentional design. If chance ruled, one might find an animal with wool but no milk, or meat but no ability to carry loads. Instead, the same animal offers several. Moreover, the verse says khalaqahā lakum — for you. This purposeful assignment implies a purposeful Assigner. The Qur'an elsewhere asks, *Did they create them or did We create them?* The answer is obvious to anyone who reflects on a newborn calf that stands within minutes, guided to its mother's milk without teaching. The blessing thus becomes an argument against the partners mentioned in verses 1 and 3. Idols did not create cattle nor do they sustain them, yet a share of gratitude is given to them.

**The Social World Of Anʿām**

For the first Makkan audience, anʿām were not abstract. They were currency, status, and survival. A man's wealth was counted in camels. A tribe's strength was measured by its herds. When the Qur'an says these were created for you, it touches the core of that economy and says, your wealth is a gift, not an achievement. This is why the sūrah later mentions that cattle bear burdens to lands you could not reach except with hardship (16:7). The same animal that gives warmth also makes trade possible, connecting distant markets. The mention of difʾ first also subtly refutes the idea that the world is purely for the strong. The weak, the cold, the hungry are considered first: warmth for those who shiver, food for those who hunger. In a society where the strong often seized grazing lands, this emphasis matters. The One who created cattle assigned them for all of you, not only for chiefs.

**Why The Verse Ends Without A Seal**

Unlike verses 1, 3, and 7, verse 5 does not end with a divine name or transcendence formula. It ends with benefits and eating, leaving the thought open. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that this openness invites the reader to continue to the next verses where beauty, transport, and mercy are added. The catalogue is cumulative. Each verse adds a layer: warmth and food, then beauty when returning and grazing, then burden-bearing, then riding and adornment, then unknown creations. The absence of a seal here is therefore rhetorical; it keeps the list moving. The reader is not allowed to stop at one blessing and say enough; he is led through an inventory that will later be summarized as uncountable (16:18). The verse also introduces a pattern that will dominate the sūrah: lakum, for you, repeated to stress that creation is oriented toward human need, not indifferent.

**Lexical Note On Difʾ And Manāfiʿ**

Difʾ is an uncommon word in modern Arabic but common in classical texts for anything that provides warmth, including clothing, tents, and even fire from dung in some interpretations. Lisān al-ʿArab cites difʾ as what protects from cold, from the verb dafaʾa, to repel cold. Manāfiʿ is the plural of manfaʿah, from n-f-ʿ, to benefit. Its indefinite form suggests abundance. The bracketed ˹many other˺ in translation reflects that manāfiʿ is general, covering benefits beyond warmth and food. The translators add it to avoid the impression that benefits are limited. In tafsīr, al-Ṭabarī lists milk, offspring, and price among manāfiʿ, while al-Qurṭubī adds use of skins for water containers and wool for furnishing. The point is that the Qur'an leaves the category open so that each generation finds its own known benefits inside it, while acknowledging that full enumeration is impossible.
