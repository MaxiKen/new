## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:82

> And He is ˹the One˺ Who, I hope, will forgive my flaws on Judgment Day.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham's Hope Faces the Day of Judgment**

Abraham's description of God ends with forgiveness and the Day of Judgment. The future day gives his hope a definite setting. He is not asking for a vague feeling that everything will be fine. He is looking toward the day when deeds are exposed and the servant stands before the Lord. Hope becomes serious when it is attached to accountability.

The wording keeps both truths together. Abraham trusts God's mercy, yet he does not treat forgiveness as something he can claim by right. He hopes for it. That posture is not weakness. It is the humility of a servant who knows the greatness of the Lord and the limits of his own record. Confidence belongs to God's promise and mercy, not to the servant's ability to declare himself safe.

**Hope Is Not Presumption**

A person may hope for forgiveness while still fearing the consequences of wrongdoing. Hope moves the heart toward God; presumption makes the heart careless. Abraham's words model the first kind. He names the Lord as the One who may forgive, so the request rests on divine generosity rather than on a denial that flaws exist.

The distinction matters because religious language can be used to excuse sin. If forgiveness is assumed without repentance, hope has been distorted into permission. Abraham's hope is spoken by someone who has already separated himself from idols and placed his life under God's guidance. The heart that hopes in mercy also takes the judgment seriously.

**The Language of Flaws Invites Humility**

The translation uses flaws for what Abraham attributes to himself. The source tradition records several ways of understanding this difficult confession. Some interpreters treat it as the language of aspiration and servant humility, not an admission of ordinary moral corruption. Others connect it to episodes that appeared outwardly problematic and explain them within the protection granted to prophets.

Whatever interpretation one prefers, the verse does not turn Abraham into a person who trusts his own perfection. He speaks as a servant seeking God's pardon. That is the point believers can receive without making careless claims about prophetic error. A righteous person can be sincere, obedient, and still stand in need of divine forgiveness in the language of dependence.

**Forgiveness Belongs to the Lord's Character**

Abraham does not seek forgiveness from an idol, an ancestor, or an intermediary. He identifies God as the One who forgives. The earlier verses showed that God gives life, guidance, provision, and healing; this verse shows that divine care also addresses moral failure. The Lord's authority reaches the conscience as fully as it reaches the body.

That relation protects the servant from two opposite mistakes. He must not treat guilt as so small that it needs no pardon, and he must not treat guilt as so great that God's mercy cannot reach it. The Lord is both judge and forgiver. Hope becomes truthful when it bows before judgment without despairing of mercy.

**The Day Gives Hope a Moral Direction**

Hope for forgiveness on Judgment Day is not an escape from moral seriousness. It directs the present life toward repentance, repair, and obedience. A person who expects to meet God cannot be satisfied with the appearance of goodness. He asks whether the heart is turning toward the Lord and whether wrongs are being abandoned rather than merely renamed.

The Day also prevents worldly approval from becoming the final measure. A person may be praised by people and still need forgiveness; another may be criticized by people and still be accepted by God. Abraham's hope relocates the decisive verdict. It will not be issued by an idol, a family tradition, or a crowd, but by the Lord before whom all deeds are known.

**The Prayer Is an Example for Believers**

The exegetical discussion about Abraham's hope also explains why the verse can guide ordinary believers. A person who knows he is answerable should ask God for forgiveness rather than rely on self-congratulation. The prayer teaches a proper balance: hope keeps repentance alive, fear keeps hope from becoming careless, and trust keeps fear from becoming despair.

Abraham's language gives believers permission to speak honestly about their need for mercy. They do not have to pretend that spiritual maturity means never needing pardon. Nor should they use their need as an excuse to remain unchanged. The request is a movement toward God, and movement toward God includes leaving the flaws that obstruct obedience.

**A Parallel Prayer Confirms the Pattern**

In the prayer recorded in 14:41, Abraham asks God to forgive him, his parents, and the believers when judgment is established. That parallel shows that forgiveness is not a marginal concern in Abraham's devotion. It appears within his public hopes for his family and the believing community. Mercy is sought personally and shared with others.

The two prayers also show that hope can be specific without becoming presumptuous. Abraham knows the day of judgment will come, knows that forgiveness belongs to God, and still asks with humility. His certainty concerns God's authority and mercy; the servant's own standing remains a matter for prayer.

**Mercy Completes the Account of Divine Care**

The Lord who feeds the body and heals illness also forgives the servant's flaws. These forms of care are not unrelated. Physical need reveals dependence, and moral need reveals a deeper dependence that no meal, remedy, or social honor can solve. Forgiveness restores the relation that sin damages; it is a mercy more precious than worldly security.

Abraham's hope therefore closes his description of the Lord without closing the door of humility. He will die and be raised, and he will stand before God needing mercy. The proper response is neither pride in one's record nor despair over one's weakness. It is hope directed to the One who alone can forgive on the Day when every false refuge has ended.
