## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:91

> There is no blame on the weak, the sick, or those lacking the means ˹if they stay behind˺, as long as they are true to Allah and His Messenger. There is no blame on the good-doers. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Weakness Does Not Cancel Dignity**

The verse removes blame from the weak, the sick, and those without the means to participate. It names physical limitation and financial inability as real conditions, not as moral failures. The elderly, the chronically ill, the disabled, and the person who cannot afford the needed equipment are not required to pretend that capacity exists. Allah's law is not built on humiliating people for what they cannot do.

This protection also corrects a culture that praises visible sacrifice so loudly that hidden service disappears. A person who cannot travel may care for dependents, advise others, give prayer, or offer whatever resources remain. His worth is not measured by physical presence alone. The community must not turn an exemption into a mark of lesser faith.

**Truthfulness Is the Condition of Exemption**

The verse adds that they must be true to Allah and His Messenger. Exemption protects the sincere person, not the person who uses weakness as a costume. Truthfulness means that the person acknowledges the actual limit, remains loyal to the mission, and does not turn absence into contempt for those who serve.

A sick believer may grieve that he cannot go and still be free of blame. A healthy person who invents an illness to avoid sacrifice is in a different position. The outward result is the same, but the inward direction differs. Leaders therefore ask for honest information without demanding that the weak prove their worth through unnecessary pain.

**Naṣīḥah Is Sincere Loyalty**

The Arabic idea behind being true includes sincere counsel and faithful concern. It is more than a claim that one believes. A servant can be unable to participate physically and still show loyalty by wanting good for the community, warning it about danger, and supporting the work according to ability.

This makes sincerity active even in absence. The disabled person who shares knowledge, the sick person who prays, and the poor person who gives a small service are not outside the moral community. Their contribution may be less visible but still truthful. The verse broadens the meaning of service so that no person mistakes physical limitation for spiritual exclusion.

**There Is No Blame on the Good-Doers**

The phrase about good-doers emphasizes that a person who acts within Allah's limits is not blameworthy for what lies beyond capacity. Goodness includes accepting the exemption without resentment, serving in another way, and refusing to use the exemption as a reason to neglect every remaining duty.

The community also has a duty not to blame such people. It should provide access, transport when possible, and roles suited to ability. The strong are not allowed to define goodness only by what their own bodies can do. Allah's mercy is shown through a law that recognizes different capacities and through believers who make those differences easier to live with.

**Ability Is Measured Honestly**

This verse does not encourage every person to declare himself weak. It teaches accurate self-knowledge. A servant should ask what he can do safely, what support would make participation possible, and what duty remains if participation is not possible. Honest assessment is closer to worship than either boastful overexertion or comfortable exaggeration.

Leaders should make the same assessment. They should not compel a person into harm to prove loyalty, and they should not accept vague excuses without asking what alternative service can be offered. Fairness listens to the body, the resources, and the intention together. Allah knows the true limit even when human beings disagree about it.

**Mercy Makes Room for Good Work**

The verse ends with Allah's forgiveness and mercy. The wording turns an exemption into a place of hope. People can serve Allah without carrying a guilt that He did not place on them. They can grieve their limitation without treating grief as proof that they have failed.

The believer receives this mercy with gratitude and uses his remaining ability well. The healthy person thanks Allah for capacity rather than boasting over the weak. The weak person thanks Allah for a law that knows his condition rather than envying another's role. Goodness is not one shape. Allah's mercy includes every sincere servant who stands within his ability and remains faithful.

The verse also protects the community from a culture of forced heroism. When people must hide pain to prove loyalty, they may worsen an illness, injure dependents, or create a crisis that serves nobody. Honest capacity allows leaders to plan and allows the person to serve without pretending. Allah's mercy is not weakness in the law; it is wisdom about bodies, resources, and the different forms of sincere action. The able should be grateful rather than proud, and the unable should be grateful rather than ashamed. Both are tested by how they receive their condition and by whether they remain true to Allah within it.

Sincerity also means accepting the community's decision about what can be done. The weak person does not demand praise for every limitation, and the able person does not shame him for it. Both can ask how to preserve the purpose of the duty in another form. A sick person may advise, organize, or pray; a poor person may give time; a person with no transport may help those preparing to travel. Allah's mercy is expansive because His wisdom knows the many ways good can be carried. No person is blamed for lacking what he truly lacks, but every person is invited to use what he truly has.
