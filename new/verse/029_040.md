## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40

> So We seized each ˹people˺ for their sin: against some of them We sent a storm of stones, some were overtaken by a ˹mighty˺ blast, some We caused the earth to swallow, and some We drowned. Allah would not have wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Verdict, Four Methods**

The verse gathers the whole catalogue of destroyed nations into a single sentence and reports four methods of seizure. The variation is the striking feature. No two communities were ended in exactly the same way, and yet each is described as having been taken for its own sin. The Book has been building to this summary for many verses, and its purpose is to prevent the reader from attaching the lesson to a particular disaster. Fire from the sky, a cry that overwhelms, the ground opening, and water closing over a host are different events, but they are described here as one act performed by one authority, distributed according to circumstances that the verse says are known.

The list also shows that the Book does not treat the manner of destruction as the point of the account. What the four cases share is the verdict, and the variety of instruments—water, wind, sound, and a swallowing earth—keeps a reader from attaching the lesson to any one of them.

**Which People Met Which End**

The identifications are set out by the commentators. The storm of stones belongs to the people of Lot, whose end is recorded in the sūrah's own narrative and in the parallel accounts of the cities turned upside down. The blast, rendered elsewhere as a cry or a shout, is assigned to the tribes of Thamūd and Midian, both of whom the Book describes as being found lifeless in their homes after the sound came. The opening of the earth is Korah's end, swallowed with his house. The drowning belongs to the people of Noah and to Pharaoh's army, whose destruction in the sea is one of the Book's most familiar scenes. Each identification fits the pattern the sūrah has laid out, and the four together cover the entire list of communities named in the chapter.

**The Elements as Instruments of Judgment**

Commentators observe that the four methods fall out across the traditional elements: water in the drowning, earth in the swallowing, air in the wind that carried the stones, and fire in the blast understood as lightning or as the thunderbolt that accompanied it. The observation is not a claim about alchemy; it names the point the summary is making. Every part of the created order stands available to its maker as an instrument, and the communities that trusted in the stability of that order are ended by it. A scene this comprehensive leaves no place for a person to imagine that some corner of the world is exempt, and the sūrah has already told its audience in plain words that there is no escape on earth or in heaven. The four methods also dispel any notion that the judgments of the past were regional accidents. A merchant in a walled town, a nomad in open country, a farmer on good soil, and an admiral at sea each find their own environment among the instruments listed, and none of the four environments affords protection.

**"Each for Their Sin"**

The distributive phrase keeps the account honest, since it rules out collective punishment in the sense that a careless reader might fear. Each people was seized for what it had done, and the differences in their crimes correspond to differences in their reports. The Book never treats these destructions as the accidents of geography or as the misfortunes of a region that happened to be in the way of a flood. The word used for the cause covers error and wrongdoing alike, and it is applied to communities that had been warned by a messenger drawn from among them. What the sentence refuses to grant is the possibility that a people could be destroyed without a reason, and the reason is located in their own conduct.

The phrase assigns responsibility one by one rather than in a lump. Nations are discussed as bodies in the historical passages of the sūrah, while the sentence about judgment returns to the individual, and the shift matters for a reader tempted to hide inside a crowd, whether for blame or for safety.

**The Disclaimer That Closes the Ledger**

The final clause states the principle that the entire chapter has been demonstrating: God did not wrong them, but they wronged themselves. The formula recurs throughout the Book whenever the destruction of a nation is described, and the repetition is deliberate. It protects the reader from the portrait of a deity who acts on appetite, and it protects the record from the accusation that the punishments were disproportionate. The wrong in the sentence is reflexive, which is the Book's consistent understanding of sin: it damages the one who commits it before it damages anyone else, and the communities in this chapter are presented as having completed that damage on a scale that ended their existence. The verse's wording also denies any gap between the offence and the answer: the seizure is described as following the sin directly, with no intervening accident to blame.

**The Refrain That Repeats Across the Book**

The Book attaches the same disclaimer to nearly every account of national destruction, and its recurrence is a deliberate protection of the reader's understanding of God. The same sentence closes the story of the people in the wilderness, who were told that the evildoers did not wrong God but wronged themselves (2:57), and it appears in the punishment of the tribes and the overturned cities, where the Book states that God would have never wronged them but that it was they who wronged themselves (9:70). Five nations, five methods, one explanation, repeated in identical words. The repetition is the Book's way of ensuring that no reader draws the conclusion the disbelievers were accused of drawing elsewhere in the sūrah — that the disaster was arbitrary.

The repetition also serves a teaching purpose, since a community that hears the same clause at the end of story after story will eventually attach it to its own situation. The Book's habit of repeating a verdict in identical words makes the lesson portable, and this sūrah uses the technique at the point where the list of nations finally stops.

**What the Summary Does for the Chapter**

With this verse the sūrah closes the long historical survey that began with the story of Abraham and Lot. The verdict is stated as a general rule, and the reader is now in possession of the principle that the chapter's opening verses asserted about the testing of believers. The two halves of the sūrah fit together: the first half said that claims of faith would be examined, and the second has shown what happened to the communities that failed the examination. What follows in the chapter turns from history to comparison, and the sūrah chooses an image from the animal world to make its next point about where people place their trust. The transition is the chapter's way of applying the lesson. Having established that the consequences are real, the Book asks its readers to examine what they are relying on, and it offers them a picture so ordinary that no one can claim to have missed the point. The historical survey supplied the evidence; the comparison that follows supplies the test of whether a reader has understood the evidence or merely heard it.
