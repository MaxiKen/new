## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:25

> But how ˹horrible˺ will it be when We gather them together on the Day about which there is no doubt—when every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Exclamation Instead of a Scene**

The verse opens with a rhetorical question — how will it be when We gather them — and the question carries the whole weight of what follows. The Qur'an is describing a moment of immense consequence, and instead of painting a picture, it asks the reader to imagine the position of people who confidently expected a short punishment and now stand inside the gathering.

The choice is deliberate. A detailed description of the Day would occupy pages and invite arguments about its imagery; an exclamation focuses the reader on the contrast between the claim of verse 24 and the reality that answers it. The question is left hanging and then completed by the sentence about accounts that follow: the answer to "how will it be" is that every soul will be settled with, without exception.

**The Day About Which There Is No Doubt**

The phrase attached to the Day has already appeared in this sūrah, in the prayer of the well-grounded who asked God to gather all humanity on a Day about which there is no doubt (3:9). It recurs across the Book with the same formula: at 4:87 God swears by His own truthfulness that He will gather all of humanity, at 6:12 He has taken mercy upon Himself with the gathering as a settled fact, and at 45:26 the gathering is announced to a people who do not know.

The repetition is a strategy. A statement that no one can successfully doubt, repeated in different contexts, removes the possibility of treating the Day as a speculative item that some groups believe in strongly. The Book treats the gathering as part of the structure of reality, and it uses the same formula whenever it wants a warning to land without the padding of an argument.

**"Every Soul Paid in Full"**

The sentence about payment uses the vocabulary of debts and wages, and the Book applies it consistently to the judgment. *“Every soul will be paid in full for its deeds, for Allah knows best what they have done.”* (39:70). *“˹Consider˺ the Day ˹when˺ every soul will come pleading for itself, and each will be paid in full for what it did, and none will be wronged.”* (16:111). The same formula closes the last of the legislative verses of the sūrah of the Cow: *“Be mindful of the Day when you will ˹all˺ be returned to Allah, then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged.”* (2:281).

The metaphor of payment is exact rather than decorative. It tells the reader that the settlement is not a gesture, that it is owed and will be rendered, and it keeps the terms of the transaction where everyone can follow them: what was done, by whom, settled in full. The one who is paid may find the amount greater than expected; the one who pays will find nothing missing from the bill.

**"None Will Be Wronged" — the Guarantee Inside the Threat**

The verse's final clause is an assurance, and it is attached to a scene that has just been described as horrifying. The Book joins the two repeatedly: the scales of justice will be set up and no soul will be wronged in the least (21:47), and the record laid open will not leave any deed, large or small, unlisted (18:49), and God never wrongs anyone even by the weight of an atom (4:40).

For the reader who had reason to fear the account, the clause is a strange comfort and a real one. The horror of the Day, as the Qur'an presents it, is not that the judgment is arbitrary. It is that it is exact. Nothing hidden is lost, nothing forgotten is forgiven by default, and no one stands in a queue behind the powerful. The same guarantee that terrifies the man who hoped his community's claim would cover him protects the man who has no advocate at all.

**The Answer to a Borrowed Certainty**

Set beside verse 24, this one dismantles the doctrine without arguing about numbers. The people who had calculated that the Fire would touch them for a few days are now shown in the position of being paid in full. The calculation is not corrected in detail; it is simply irrelevant, because the day it concerned has arrived and its terms are set by someone else.

That is how the Book habitually disposes of comfortable theology. It does not spend pages refuting the arithmetic of privilege. It announces the appointment and describes the accounting, and leaves the reader to notice which version of the future he has been living in.

**What the Verse Asks Its Reader to Do**

Nothing in the sentence is directed at the reader, and that is the reason it makes its demand so effectively. The reader is shown a scene, told that it is not doubtful, and left to place himself in it. Every clause — the gathering, the payment, the guarantee of fairness — is a fact about him, stated in the third person, waiting for the moment he answers it.

The sūrah will continue to address nations and communities. This verse remains the floor under the whole discussion: one gathering, one account per soul, and a settlement made without favour.

There is a further consequence for the way a person reads his own life. A man who expects an account cannot spend his time on the theory that the account will be lenient because of who he is, and a community that recites this verse cannot build its confidence on the arithmetic of the previous one. Everything the Book says afterwards about prayer, charity, courage, and patience is the behaviour of people who have accepted the floor this verse lays down. Everything it says about the arrogance of the comfortable is a description of people who have not.
