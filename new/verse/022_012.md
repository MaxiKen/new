## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:12

> They call besides Allah what can neither harm nor benefit them. That is ˹truly˺ the farthest one can stray.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Prayer Directed at the Wrong Address**

The verb is *yadʿū* — he calls upon, he invokes, he prays to. The Qur'an uses the same verb for calling on God, and that shared vocabulary is what makes the verse an indictment rather than a sociological note. This is not a man with an unusual hobby. He is doing the thing a human being does when he needs help, and he is doing it at the wrong number.

The object of the verb is *min dūni Allāh* — from below God, short of God, in place of God. The phrase is standard and mild-sounding, and its frequency in the Qur'an is part of how the Qur'an argues: it treats the substitution rather than the existence of the substitutes. The worshipper is not being mocked for having recourse. He is being asked what he expects to happen next.

**"Neither Harms nor Benefits": the Qur'an's Working Test for a God**

The criterion stated here is functional, and it is stated in the same words across the Qur'an. Ibrāhīm puts it to his people in the form of a question: *“Do you then worship—instead of Allah—what can neither benefit nor harm you in any way?”* (21:66). The same formula is put into the mouth of the Messenger as a test his own community should apply to themselves: *“Should we invoke, other than Allah, those ˹idols˺ which cannot benefit or harm us, and turn back to disbelief after Allah has guided us?”* (6:71). It recurs as a flat description of the practice: *“Yet they worship besides Allah what can neither benefit nor harm them”* (25:55). Ibrāhīm's audience is asked the same question in another form: *“Or can they benefit or harm you?”* (26:73).

The repetition across the whole Qur'an is deliberate, and the reason repays attention. The test is not philosophical. It does not ask whether the idol is eternal, whether it has a mind, or whether it is worthy. It asks a question any worshipper can answer from experience: has this thing ever done anything for you? A criterion that anyone can apply, applied to a claim anyone can check, is the kind of argument that does not require a scholar to adjudicate.

**They Were Not Thought to Be Creators**

The obvious objection is that nobody in Makkah believed a carved stone had made the sky. The Qur'an knows this, and it says so: *“They worship besides Allah others who can neither harm nor benefit them, and say, ‘These are our intercessors with Allah’”* (10:18).

That verse is the key to this one. The role assigned to the idols was intercession — influence with the real power, a word put in with someone who can act. The Qur'an's reply in 10:18 is withering precisely because it takes the claim seriously: are you informing God of something He does not know?

Verse 12 of this sūrah dismantles the arrangement from the other end. An intercessor who can neither harm nor benefit has nothing to intercede with. If he cannot move a single atom on his own, his access to the One who moves atoms is worthless, since the whole point of the access was to produce an effect. A patron with no capacity is not a weaker patron; he is no patron at all.

**"That Is the Farthest Straying"**

The verdict is *wa-huwa al-ḍalālu al-baʿīd* — and that is the far straying, the straying at a distance.

The metaphor is distance, not depth. The Qur'an could have called it the deepest error or the gravest; it chose the word for remoteness, and the choice fits everything the sūrah has been building. The man of verse 11 was on the brink, in position but not committed. The man here has travelled. He is not near the edge of the truth having wandered slightly; he is at a measurable distance from it, and the distance was covered by steps that each seemed reasonable — recourse, intercession, a little help from a friend closer to the throne than you are.

The definite article matters too. *The* far straying, as though there were a recognized extreme and this were it. The superlative is not a rhetorical flourish but a classification: of all the ways a person can be wrong about God, taking a powerless thing as a route to the Powerful is the furthest out.

**Why the Criterion Is Benefit, Not Power**

Two things are worth drawing out about the test itself.

The first is that it is mercifully low. The Qur'an does not open its case against idolatry with metaphysics or with a demand for moral purity. It asks whether the thing has ever helped anyone. Every person who has ever prayed to an idol and received nothing already knows the answer, which means the argument is not being imported from outside the worshipper's experience. It is being drawn out of it.

The second is that the test cuts in the positive direction as well. If the mark of a false god is that it cannot harm or benefit, then the mark of the true one is that He can — and the whole of this sūrah, from the embryo in the womb to the rain on the dead field to the raising of the graves, has been establishing exactly that about God. The criterion of verse 12 is the criterion the sūrah's first eleven verses were written to satisfy.

**Two Ways of Getting This Wrong**

The verse's economy also protects it against two opposite misreadings, and both were live in the audience that first heard it.

The first is the view that the objects of worship are dangerous powers — real, malevolent, requiring appeasement. Verse 12 rules that out directly: a thing that can neither harm nor benefit is not a threat, and treating it as one is the superstition the Qur'an is dismantling. Fear of an idol is as misplaced as hope in it.

The second is the view that idolatry is harmless because the idols are nothing — that a person can keep his stones and his monotheism at the same time, since the stones do nothing either way. That is precisely the position verse 13 will confront, and the sūrah's answer there is that the worship itself does damage regardless of whether the object is a nullity. Futility in the object does not produce neutrality in the act.

Between the two, the Qur'an holds a narrow line that is easy to state and hard to keep: the idol is nothing, and worshipping it is a catastrophe.
