## Sūrah an-Naml 27:75

> For there is nothing hidden in the heavens or the earth without being ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Human Secrets to Everything Concealed**

The previous verse said God knows what hearts conceal. This one removes the restriction to hearts. Nothing hidden anywhere in the heavens or the earth falls outside a written record.

The widening is deliberate and it changes the scale of the claim. What was an assertion about the transparency of human intention becomes an assertion about the structure of reality: concealment is a relation between creatures and things, not a property of the things themselves. Something can be hidden from everyone and still be recorded. That is a stronger statement than omniscience alone, because it adds the element of writing — the hidden is not merely known but entered.

**The Word for Hidden**

The verse says *ghā'ibah*, from the same root as *ghayb*, the unseen category the chapter discussed at verse 65. The connection is direct and the two verses should be read together.

There the point was a limit: nobody in the heavens or the earth knows the unseen except God, and the resurrection was named as the case in hand. Here the same category is described from the other side — everything in it is written down. Put together, the two verses describe a single arrangement. There is a domain creatures cannot reach, and it is not chaotic or unformed; it is registered. The ignorance announced in the earlier verse is therefore not a statement about the world being partly indeterminate. It is a statement about who has access to a record that exists.

**"A Perfect Record"**

The phrase is *kitābin mubīn*, a clear or manifest Book. Al-Ṭabarī identifies it with what the Qur'an elsewhere calls the Mother of the Book — the archetypal record mentioned in Āl ʿImrān and az-Zukhruf, and connected to the Preserved Tablet described at the end of al-Burūj.

The adjective is the chapter's own. Verse 1 called the Qur'an *al-kitāb al-mubīn*, the clear Book, and here the same two words name something else. The overlap is not confusion; it is a claim about relation. What is recited in Arabic and what is written in the heavenly register are given one description, and the reader who has come from the chapter's opening will hear the echo. A Book called clear at the start of the sūrah is matched by a Book called clear at its close, and the sūrah's whole argument runs between them.

**Why Writing Rather Than Knowing**

The verse could have said God knows everything hidden, which is what verse 74 said about hearts. It says instead that the hidden is in a record, and the shift from knowing to writing carries an implication.

Knowledge can be complete and still private. A written record is the form knowledge takes when it is going to be produced — entered, kept, and brought out later. The Qur'an uses this image throughout its account of the judgment, where deeds are described as recorded, books are handed to their owners, and nothing small or large is left uncounted. What this verse establishes is the existence of the register before any of that machinery is described. The accounting the mockers of verse 71 were demanding a date for is, on this claim, already documented; only the session has not been convened.

**The Argument Against a Private Life**

Draw the last four verses together and they make one case. God is bountiful to all. He knows what the chest conceals. Nothing hidden anywhere is outside a written record. And the reckoning may be closer than the questioners assume.

That sequence removes, step by step, the conditions a person needs in order to live one way in public and another in private. Thamūd's nine had precisely that assumption when they swore an oath at night and drafted an alibi; the chapter showed what became of it. What makes this section's version of the argument different is that it is addressed to people who have not yet done anything of the sort. The nine were shown after the fact. These verses are put to an audience before, which is the position every reader occupies. The chapter now turns from what is recorded to what has been revealed, and to the disputes it was sent to settle.

**Nothing Small Enough to Escape**

The negative construction is absolute: *mā min ghā'ibatin... illā fī kitābin mubīn* — there is no hidden thing except that it is in a clear Book. Arabic builds this with a double negative that admits no exceptions, and the Qur'an uses the same pattern elsewhere when it says no leaf falls without His knowledge.

The absoluteness is what gives the claim its practical weight. A record that captured only significant matters would leave everyone free to calculate what counted, and the calculation would be made in their own favour. What this verse describes leaves no room for that estimate. It also has a consoling side that the surrounding polemic can obscure. A wrong done in private to someone with no power to protest is, on this claim, entered — which is precisely what the report about the supplication of the oppressed asserted from the other direction. The same comprehensiveness that threatens the plotter is the guarantee held by the person nobody was listening to.

**Two Books at the Chapter's Two Ends**

An-Naml opens by calling the Qur'an *the clear Book* and closes this section by giving the same title to a heavenly register. Between them the chapter has used the word *kitāb* twice more: for the letter Sulaymān sent to Sheba, and for the source of the knowledge held by the man who brought the throne.

Four books, then, in one chapter: a recitation, a diplomatic dispatch, a source of power, and a record of everything hidden. The recurrence is not likely to be accidental in a text this carefully arranged. What the four share is that each is a fixed, written thing that produces an effect at a distance — a letter that changes a kingdom, a knowledge that moves a throne, a record that will be produced at the reckoning, and a recitation that has outlasted everyone who first heard it. The chapter's first sentence and its last theological claim are held together by one word.
