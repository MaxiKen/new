## Sūrah an-Nūr 24:3

> A male fornicator would only marry a female fornicator or idolatress. And a female fornicator would only be married to a fornicator or idolater. This is ˹all˺ forbidden to the believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Moral Alignment of Intimacy**

The verse establishes an unsettling correspondence between inner moral character and external matrimonial union: *al-zānī lā yankiḥu illā zāniyatan aw mushrikatan*. The primary linguistic inquiry turns upon the verb *yankiḥu*, which in classical Arabic lexicography denotes both contractual marriage and physical cohabitation. If understood as descriptive of sexual misconduct, the text affirms that illicit intimacy is an act of mutual participation where neither partner can claim moral innocence; the male transgressor unites only with someone equally willing to transgress divine limits. Illicit relations are fundamentally symmetrical in their spiritual liability, binding both participants in shared guilt before God.

If understood in its normative legal sense of matrimonial alliance, the verse describes a profound psychological and spiritual reality: individuals characterized by persistent, unrepentant licentiousness find compatibility only with those who share their disregard for moral boundaries. A person who despises divine prohibitions naturally gravitates toward companionship that validates rather than reproaches their chosen lifestyle. The verse exposes the intuitive affinity that draws like-minded transgressors together in social and domestic spheres, warning that corrupt habits inevitably shape one's intimate choices.

**The Asymmetry of Faith and Corruption**

The text pairs the unrepentant fornicator directly with the idolater: *aw mushrikatan* and *aw mushrik*. This startling juxtaposition generated intense scrutiny among classical exegetes across generations. Idolatry represents an outright rejection of divine unity, whereas fornication is a major behavioral transgression committed by someone who may still profess faith. Pairing them together does not mean that sexual sin expels a person from the religion; rather, it highlights the severe spiritual dissonance between habitual unchastity and genuine submission to God.

An idolater feels no allegiance to divine law, while a habitual fornicator acts with open contempt toward divine statutes. By placing the two categories in parallel, the revelation emphasizes that a lifestyle founded upon unbridled desire shares an essential defiance with pagan disregard for divine sovereignty, making both completely unsuited for holy union with a devout believer. The comparison serves as a devastating rhetorical indictment of moral laxity within the community, warning believers against minimizing the gravity of persistent unchastity.

**Legislative Prohibition versus Descriptive Reality**

Scholars historically divided over whether this verse constitutes an enforceable legal statute or a stern moral condemnation. Imām Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal and early jurists from the Ḥadīth school interpreted the text as a binding legislative ban (*taḥrīm*). In their view, a Muslim convicted of fornication cannot lawfully marry a chaste believer unless the individual demonstrates verifiable repentance and undergoes a period of moral purification. They treated the contract as null and void if contracted prior to repentance, holding that the textual prohibition admits no procedural exceptions.

In contrast, the majority of jurists, including al-Shāfiʿī, Mālik, and Abū Ḥanīfah, maintained that the verse functions primarily as a profound moral censure and descriptive observation of customary behavior. They noted that a believer guilty of a major sin remains within the fold of the community, and therefore their marriage to another Muslim remains legally valid, even if highly discouraged. Both groups agreed that the spiritual ideal demands that believers actively seek partners whose devotion matches their own, preserving their lineage and household from moral compromise.

**The Historical Occasion: Protecting the Vulnerable Community**

The context of revelation illuminates the concrete social tensions that occasioned this ruling in early Madinah. Following the migration from Makkah, many impoverished emigrants, including several of the destitute companions residing at the Prophet's mosque (*Aṣḥāb al-Ṣuffah*), faced severe financial deprivation. In the city, certain wealthy women had acquired considerable fortunes through the practice of prostitution during the pre-Islamic era and continued to maintain lavish households.

Facing extreme hunger and homelessness, a number of destitute believers contemplated marrying these affluent women in order to secure food and shelter, rationalizing that financial security would alleviate their distress. This verse descended as an absolute prohibition against entering into such compromising alliances. The revelation made it unmistakably clear that physical survival and economic security cannot be purchased at the expense of moral dignity, forbidding believers from establishing households financed by illicit trade or compromised honor.

**The Inviolability of the Believer's Matrimonial Covenant**

The verse culminates in an unambiguous legislative decree: *wa-ḥurrima dhālika ʿalā al-muʾminīn*, and that has been forbidden to the believers. Commentators analyzed the referent of the demonstrative pronoun *dhālika*. One major interpretation holds that it refers to contracting marriage with unrepentant fornicators, establishing a protective boundary around the households of the faithful to prevent the normalization of moral corruption within the family unit.

Another recognized reading maintains that *dhālika* points directly to the act of fornication itself, reiterating its absolute illegality after describing its degenerative social effects. Under both readings, marriage in Islam is elevated far above a mere transaction of mutual convenience or legal cohabitation. It is a sanctified institution intended to cultivate tranquility, mutual respect, and the upright nurturing of future generations, objectives that are entirely subverted when a household is founded upon unrepentant moral corruption.

**Repentance and the Restoration of Matrimonial Eligibility**

The restrictive categorization established in this verse is not an inescapable life sentence. Classical commentators unanimously affirmed that sincere repentance (*tawbah*) radically alters an individual's legal and moral status. When a transgressor abandons illicit conduct, expresses genuine remorse before God, and commits to an upright life, the active descriptor of *zānī* no longer applies to them in the eyes of divine law.

Divine grace dissolves the spiritual impediment that previously disqualified them from honorable union. The Qur'an consistently rejects the permanent caste-like stigmatization of human beings based upon past errors. Once a soul turns sincerely to the divine threshold, their moral integrity is restored, and they re-enter the company of chaste believers, eligible once more to participate fully in the sacred covenants of marriage and communal leadership. Redemption remains perpetually open to all who seek genuine reform.
