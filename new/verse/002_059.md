## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:59

> But the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say. So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their rebelliousness.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Changed Word**

The verse's first clause reports one of the smallest sins in the Qur'an and one of the most heavily answered. The command had been a posture and a word (verse 58); the response was a substitution — *the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say*. The verb *baddala* is the Qur'an's technical term for replacement: not mispronouncing, not forgetting, but swapping the commanded thing for another thing in its place. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf files the same clause with the same verb and adds the qualifier that sharpens it: *"But the wrongdoers among them changed the words they were commanded to say"* (7:162) — *among them*. The substitution was not the whole camp's act but a faction's, and the naming matters: the recital indicts precisely and does not smear. What the qualifier also exposes is the social anatomy of the sin — a liturgy commanded to everyone, corrupted by some, and not corrected by the rest, since the punishment that follows lands on the community's file as a whole. The changed word is the smallest possible act of covenant-breaking, and that is why the verse treats it as the largest kind of sign: what a people does with one commanded syllable discloses what it will do with a scripture.

**What They Said Instead**

The reports supply the substituted words, and they supply two versions. The famous reading has them replacing *ḥiṭṭah* — absolve us — with *ḥinṭah*, wheat: the petition for forgiveness turned into a request for grain, the liturgy of the gate converted into shopping. Al-Zamakhsharī pressed the obvious objection: these people did not speak Arabic, so a wordplay between two Arabic nouns cannot be what happened, and the report's wit does not survive translation into the scene. The objection is sound as far as it goes, and the hadith literature preserves the Prophet's ﷺ own version of the substitution, which is different in wording and identical in kind. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī records it: the Children of Israel were told to enter the gate in prostration and say *ḥiṭṭah*, *"but they changed (Allah's Order) and entered, dragging themselves on their buttocks and said, 'Habatun (a grain) in a Sha'ratin (hair)'"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4641). The substitution in the ḥadīth is still a food-word — a grain, in a hair — but the mechanism the Prophet ﷺ emphasizes is the change itself, whatever the syllables: the commanded petition was replaced by a trivial phrase, and the commanded posture by its inverse. The stronger reading holds both reports together at the level they agree on: the word was changed because the word was the test, and a people unwilling to ask for absolution will always find a smaller sentence to say in its place.

**Entering on Their Backsides**

The ḥadīth's postural detail completes the picture. The gate required a bow or prostration — heads lowered, entering in humility (verse 58). What the Bukhārī report describes instead is a people entering *dragging themselves on their buttocks*, the body's inversion of the commanded reverence performed in a way that could only be deliberate: one does not accidentally slide through a gate seated. The two changes mirror the two commands exactly — posture reversed, word replaced — which converts the incident from lapse into liturgy-of-mockery, a counter-rite performed at the threshold of the gift. The verse's own summary word names its character: *fāsuq*-conduct, rebelliousness, the root that carries leaving-the-bounds. Mockery of a commanded form is not a lighter version of disobedience but its concentrated form, because it adds to the refusal a statement about the Refuser: that the command is a joke. The Qur'an treats that statement as decisive everywhere it appears, and the punishment clause of this verse — descending, as the mockery did, on the whole scene — is filed under the same heading the gate-posture was meant to prevent: a people who would not lower themselves at a gift's threshold were answered from above.

**A Punishment From the Heavens**

The response arrives as *rijz min al-samāʾ* — punishment from the heavens. *Rijz* is the Qur'an's word for penal affliction: the same noun names the plagues sent upon Pharaoh's people when they broke each promise (7:134), the affliction sent on the people of Lūṭ's cities (29:34), and — in its other register — the whispering pollution of Satan (8:11). The commentators read the heaven-sent rijz here as a plague, the identification supported by the word's use for the Egyptian plagues and by al-Rāzī's related reasoning that this punishment belongs to the period before any entry into the Holy Land. The clause's direction — *from the heavens* — answers the scene's geography: the sky had been this community's benefactor for a generation, dropping shade (verse 57) and dropping food; the same sky now drops the penalty, and the verse states the symmetry without comment because the symmetry is the comment. Nothing on earth mediated the punishment; no army, no famine-cycle, no natural cause is named. A liturgy mocked at a gate is answered by a dispatch from the direction the gift came, and the answer's precision — *upon them for their rebelliousness*, the cause stated in the sentence — keeps the punishment inside the covenant's own logic: not rage but consequence, filed against a named act.

**Why a Word Carries This Weight**

The disproportion between the sin's size and the punishment's weight is the verse's deliberate lesson, and the sūrah explains it by pairing this scene with a later charge against the same community's scholars: *"a group of them would hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it"* (2:75). The gate-substitution and the text-corruption are one crime at two scales — the single commanded word changed at the threshold, the received Book changed in the study — and the Qur'an's insistence on filing both says that the handling of words is this recital's central diagnostic. Words are the covenant's medium: promises are words, liturgy is words, scripture is words, and the demand to *say* ḥiṭṭah was a demand to let the mouth ratify what the heart knew — that the city was gift, the table was provision, and the guests were burdened sinners at a merciful Lord's door. To change that word was to change the covenant's terms from the human side, an act small in syllables and total in direction. Verse 42 had already legislated the principle for the scholars — do not mix truth with falsehood, do not conceal it knowingly — and verse 59 shows the principle's wilderness origin: the first corruption of a divine word on this community's record was committed with the mouth, at a gate, in a single substituted sentence.

**The Sky Keeps Both Ledgers**

Read inside the recital, the verse closes the entry-scene with the sūrah's governing arithmetic on display. The sky's ledger for this people now holds shade and manna on the credit side (verse 57) and rijz on the debit side (verse 59), with one community drawing from both columns. That is the verse's pastoral severity: proximity to grace does not immunize against penalty, and the penalty arrives through the same channel as the grace, which is the covenant's way of saying that the channel was never the point — the handling was. The wrongdoers are distinguished *among them*, the punished act is named, the cause is stated, and the file moves on: the recital will next record necessity rather than luxury answered — a thirsting camp and a prophet's prayer — because the covenant's dealings track need with the same precision they track insolence. For the sūrah's second audience, the verse's edge is unchanged. Every community lives at gates of given things, and every liturgy of entry — the word said over a meal, the prayer at a threshold, the confession that keeps a gift assigned to its Giver — has its substituted syllables. The changed word is never only a word; it is the heart's verdict spoken aloud, and verse 59 records what the heavens do with verdicts.
