## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:59

> But the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say. So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their rebelliousness.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What Was Changed, According to the Reports**

The verse states that the command was altered and does not say how. The reports that supply the detail describe a substitution of one word for a similar one, so that the sentence spoken at the gate was a pun on the sentence required, and they add that the posture was changed as well. The explanations differ in detail and agree on the character of the act: the community performed the command in form while mocking its content.

The Book treats this kind of substitution as a distinct offence. Its wording elsewhere describes those who take words out of their proper context and distort them, and it quotes the mockery that accompanied the distortion: *“Some Jews take words out of context and say, ‘We listen and we disobey,’ ‘Hear! May you never hear,’ and ‘Râ’ina!’—playing with words and discrediting the faith.”* (4:46). The verse in al-Baqarah reports the same behaviour at the gate of a city.

**Why a Changed Word Is a Grave Matter**

The requirement at the gate was a single word asking for relief, and the community's answer was to make a joke of it. The Book's charge is therefore not that the command was disobeyed, since disobedience would have been the straightforward refusal to enter or to speak; the charge is that the words were bent into a mockery, which means the act was performed and the meaning was refused.

The distinction matters for how the passage reads. A person who declines a command leaves the arrangement intact; a person who recites the command while ridiculing it has used the ritual itself to express contempt. The Book treats words as the material of the relationship, and it does not accept the performance of a formula as a substitute for the sense that the formula carries.

**Punishment From Above**

The consequence is described as coming down from the heaven upon the wrongdoers, and the same event is recorded in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf with more detail about its nature (7:162). The Book's judgments on this community frequently take the form of something descending, which matches the form the community's gifts took: bread came down from the sky, and so did the penalty. The two movements belong to the same arrangement.

The Book uses this kind of correspondence throughout its narratives. A people who receive their provision from above are corrected from above when they mock what has been given, and the passage makes no attempt to describe the punishment further. What is named is its source and its cause, which is the information the reader needs in order to understand the sequence.

**The Word for the Offence**

The closing phrase names the offence as a deliberate departure from obedience, and the term is the same one the sūrah has used for people who break out of a boundary. It is applied in the Book to those who violate a covenant, to those whose hearts are described as diseased, and to the men described in the earlier portraits of this sūrah. The vocabulary keeps this community's conduct inside a category the reader already understands.

The Book's usage also keeps the offence personal. Nobody is accused of misunderstanding the command or of arriving at it late; the term describes a choice to step outside the limit that was set. The verse states the cause as the reason for the punishment, and it leaves the reader with a picture of a community at the door of a promised land refusing, in the smallest possible way, to take God at his word.

**The Pattern Completed in Three Verses**

Read with the two verses around it, this sentence completes a unit. A gift is offered with an instruction; the instruction is trivial in its demands; the community recites it in mockery; the punishment follows. The Book has told the same story three times in this section already, with the calf, with the demand to see God, and now with the word at the gate.

The sūrah's address to the descendants of these people continues past the account of what their ancestors did. That continuation is the important fact. The Book records the failures of a generation that has been punished and then keeps speaking to their children, and the passage that follows returns at once to a gift, which means the arrangement is not treated as finished.

**What the Passage Never Claims**

Nothing in this verse says that the community was punished for an opinion, a question or a doubt. The offence is located in an act of verbal mockery performed at a moment of instruction, and the Book's objection is to the contempt expressed rather than to the vocabulary used. That precision keeps the charge against Israel in this passage consistent with everything else the sūrah says about this people: they were corrected for what they did, and their gifts and offences are recorded side by side.

**What Mockery Does to a Command**

A community that mocks a command while performing it has done something no refusal can do. Refusal leaves the command standing and the relationship intact; mockery uses the command itself as the material of contempt, so that the act and its rejection occupy the same breath. The Book treats this as a distinct category of offence, and it reports it in the case of the men who distorted words in greeting and in the case of the men who ridiculed the giving of small amounts in charity.

The punishment named here is therefore not a response to a minor slip. The phrase changed by the community was an acknowledgement of need, which is the one thing a person who wishes to protect his standing will not say. The verse records both halves of the exchange, the change and its consequence, and it leaves the reader to see why the smallest alteration carried the largest result.
