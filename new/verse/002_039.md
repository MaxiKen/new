## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:39

> But those who disbelieve and deny Our signs will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict Standing Where a Warning Was Expected**

The sentence states an outcome rather than issuing an instruction, and it completes the pair begun in the previous verse. A promise was made to those who follow; here the opposite case is stated, in a form that identifies the people involved and their destination. Nothing in between is left undecided, and the sūrah then moves on to its lengthy address to a particular community.

This way of putting matters together is a pattern in the Book. Even where the surrounding passage is narrative, the Qur'an places the two results side by side, so that the reader is never left with a promise whose alternative is unstated. The verse is short, and its brevity is part of the effect: the destination is named, the duration is named, and no description is offered.

**Two Verbs, and the Difference Between Them**

The people in this verse are described with two words. The first describes covering over what one knows, and the Book's usage is consistently about concealment rather than mere absence of information. The second describes calling something a lie, and it applies to the signs themselves: revelation, evidence, the marks of God's work in the world.

The pair recurs together in the Qur'an, and it appears in the same order in a later sūrah where the same judgment is passed: *“As for those who disbelieve and deny Our signs, they are the residents of the Hellfire.”* (5:10). The Book tends to list the two together because they describe two stages of one movement: first the covering over of what was recognised, then the public designation of it as false. The verse in al-Baqarah states the end of that movement.

**What "Our Signs" Includes**

The Arabic word for signs covers everything that points to its maker: the verses of the revelation, the phenomena of nature, and the events of a person's own life. The Book states the range in a single sentence: *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth.”* (41:53). Denial in the Qur'an is therefore not limited to rejecting a book; it can be directed at the world, at the self, or at both.

That breadth explains why the sūrah's earlier portraits of rejection were not confined to people who argued about revelation. A man who calls the evidence around him a product of chance has denied a sign in the same sense this verse uses, and the Book treats the two denials as one act of the same will.

**Residents, and the Word the Book Uses**

The term rendered residents describes people who belong to a place, in the way a householder belongs to his house. The Book uses it for both destinations, so it speaks of the companions of the Garden and the companions of the Fire, and the choice of the word implies permanence rather than a period of detention in an unfamiliar setting.

That permanence is confirmed in the closing clause, which states the duration. The Book repeats the phrase often enough that it becomes one of the recognisable marks of the descriptions of the Fire, and the sūrah will use it again later in its own text. The effect of repeating it is to prevent the reader from reading the sentence as a threat whose scale is unclear. The Book tells him exactly what it means.

**Forever, in the Book's Vocabulary**

The Arabic word for abiding carries the sense of remaining where one is, and the Book sometimes strengthens it with explicit language of endlessness and sometimes qualifies it with a statement of God's will. The Qur'an applies the same vocabulary to the reward of the righteous in the sūrah describing the Paradise promised to them: *“eternal is its fruit as well as its shade”* (13:35).

The Book uses the term consistently for both destinies, and it does not soften either. What varies in the passages is not the duration but the condition of the people concerned, and the commentary tradition spent considerable effort distinguishing those who enter the Fire permanently from those Muslims who enter it temporarily for purification. What the verse states is the case of those who die in the position it describes.

**Why This Sentence Precedes a Long Address**

The verse closes the account of Adam and the fall, and immediately after it the sūrah turns to the Children of Israel and speaks to them for the better part of a hundred verses. The transition is abrupt, and the placement is instructive: the address that follows is delivered to a community that already knows the difference between the two destinations outlined here.

The Book's order is to state consequences before it issues commands, which is the opposite of how a legal code is usually arranged. The reader encounters the two ends of the road first, and everything the sūrah goes on to request is then heard in the light of what has just been said about where the two responses lead. The command to fulfil a covenant comes after the reader has seen what is promised to those who keep one and what belongs to those who call the evidence lies.

**A Verdict That Closes a Unit**

This sentence ends the passage on Adam, and the sūrah moves at once to a lengthy address to a particular people. The placement is deliberate: the reader has been shown the two destinations, stated in consecutive verses, before a single command of the detailed teaching has been issued. When the address to the Children of Israel begins, everything it asks for arrives in the light of what the reader already knows about the two roads.

The Book's habit of ordering its material this way is visible throughout the sūrah. It states outcomes, then grounds them in history, then issues instructions, and it does not leave the reader to guess what follows from either response. The two verses about the guided and the deniers are the frame on which the rest of the chapter is hung.
