## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149

> to carve homes in the mountains with great skill?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mountain Homes Display Mastery**

Ṣāliḥ's question reaches the most striking expression of Thamûd's skill: homes cut into mountains. The rock itself becomes a building material, and the settlement appears protected by the permanence of the landscape. Such work requires planning, labor, and technical knowledge. The verse recognizes ability while questioning the pride that can grow around it.

A home is a legitimate need, and a secure dwelling can be a mercy. The problem is not shelter. It is the assumption that mastery over stone gives a people mastery over its future. A mountain dwelling may resist weather and enemies, but it cannot make its occupants independent of God or immune to death.


The Arabic description has been read as skilled or nimble, while another reading can carry the sense of exuberance or exultation. The difference affects the shade of the question. Ṣāliḥ may be challenging technical confidence, or the delight and pride with which the people display it. Both meanings converge on the danger of turning ability into self-admiration.

Skill is honorable when it serves life and is joined to gratitude. It becomes spiritually dangerous when the maker begins to love the evidence of his own power more than the Lord who gave him hands, minds, materials, and time. The issue is not whether Thamûd can build, but what its building teaches the people to believe about themselves.

**The Mountain Does Not Guarantee Permanence**

Rock seems more lasting than wood or cloth, and a carved home can make the future look solid. Yet permanence belongs to God alone. The people who inhabit a mountain pass away, and even the mountain remains under the command of its Creator. A durable structure is still a temporary residence for temporary lives.

Quran 89:9 recalls Thamûd as a people who carved rocks in a valley. That parallel confirms that their mountain work was part of their remembered identity. It also places the achievement in a warning about the fate of powerful peoples. A famous technique can survive in memory while its boastful society does not.


A dwelling protects the body, but it can also protect the heart from correction. Those who feel secure behind thick walls may stop noticing their dependence on neighbors, workers, water, and God. The house becomes a private world in which the occupant imagines that outside claims do not apply.

Ṣāliḥ's warning crosses that boundary. No home can conceal the purpose for which it was built or the actions carried out by those inside it. A mountain can hide a doorway from human eyes, but it cannot hide a people from divine knowledge. Security is sound only when it remains joined to accountability.

**Craft Does Not Excuse Corruption**

Thamûd's skill may have produced genuine benefits. Strong homes can protect families and preserve life. But useful work cannot erase injustice elsewhere. A society may be advanced in construction while being primitive in its treatment of the weak. Technical achievement and moral achievement are different measures.

This distinction matters in every age. A bridge, palace, machine, or institution should be judged not only by what it can do but by whom it serves and what it costs. A people that carves beautiful homes while abusing power has used one gift without learning the responsibility that should accompany it.


If the alternate shade of the word points to exuberance, it helps explain why Ṣāliḥ asks the question rather than simply praising the work. Joy in a blessing is not wrong, but exultation can make the self the center of the scene. The builders begin to celebrate not God's generosity but their own supposed greatness.

A grateful craftsperson can take pleasure in excellence and still say that the ability is received. An exultant society turns excellence into a claim over others. It expects admiration, obedience, and fear because it has shaped the landscape. Ṣāliḥ calls that expectation back under God's judgment.

**The Home Is a Trust, Not a Proof of Invulnerability**

People need places of rest, family life, and protection. The Qur'anic criticism should not produce contempt for care or good workmanship. It should produce humility about what a home can provide. A house can guard the body for a time; it cannot guard the soul from its Lord.

A responsible home therefore carries duties outward. It should shelter dependents, respect neighbors, and use resources without waste. The greater the skill and wealth invested in it, the more reason there is to ask whether it serves mercy or merely magnifies the owners.


By carving into a mountain, Thamûd may have thought it had conquered a difficult part of creation. Yet the mountain places human work in a larger frame. The builders cut a small space within a vast world they did not make. Their success is real, but it is not ultimate.

That perspective can turn pride into worship. See the dwelling as a sign of God's provision of material and intelligence, then use it to protect life and practice gratitude. If the same dwelling becomes a monument to self-sufficiency, its strength only makes the illusion more visible.


A visitor can admire a carved home and still miss Ṣāliḥ's point. The question is not whether the work is impressive. It is whether impressive work has made the people forget death, judgment, and the source of their ability. The moral value of a structure cannot be read from its surface alone.

Thamûd is invited to keep the skill while abandoning the boast. Build well, live responsibly, and remember that no mountain can shelter a people from the command of the Lord who created it. Mastery becomes a blessing when it leads to humility; otherwise, the carved home becomes a witness to pride.
