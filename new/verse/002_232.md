## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:232

> When you have divorced women and they have reached the end of their waiting period, do not prevent them from re-marrying their exhusbands if they come to an honourable agreement. This is enjoined on whoever has faith in Allah and the Last Day. This is purer and more dignifying for you. Allah knows and you do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Obstruction Forbidden**

The verse legislates the waiting-period's other end: when the divorced women have fulfilled their term — the ʿiddah complete, the marriage fully dissolved — do not hinder them from marrying their husbands, when the two have consented honorably. The prohibition is addressed to the people with power to hinder: the guardians, the families, the brothers and fathers whose consent the custom required and whose refusal could block the marriage. The case the verse answers is not the couple's — the couple's consent is assumed and named as honorable — but the third parties': the divorce is complete, the woman is free, the former spouses wish to remarry each other lawfully, and the verse forbids anyone from standing between them. The ruling's scope is precise within the code: it governs remarriage after a completed legal divorce — the single or twice-counted declaration whose waiting has ended — and not the thrice-divorced couple, whose bar the previous verse legislated until another marriage intervenes. Within its scope the verse is total: the family's objection, however felt, has no legal standing against the couple's honorable consent, and the hindrance the verse forbids includes every instrument of family pressure — refusal, delay, threat, the withdrawal of support.

**The Sister's Case**

The occasion the tradition reports gives the ruling its human shape: the verse was revealed, al-Ṭabarī records, when a man balked at the idea of his sister — divorced and single — remarrying the man who had divorced her. The brother's balk is the verse's whole subject in miniature. His motives are the family's familiar ones: the divorce was an injury to the household's honor, and the remarriage reopens it; the sister deserves better, or the former husband proved himself unworthy, or the family has begun arranging another match; the children of the first marriage complicate a second. The verse answers every such motive with a single prohibition: the brother's judgment about his sister's former husband is not a legal fact — the honorable consent of the two adults is the only fact the law recognizes at the waiting-period's end. The case also shows the prohibition's addressee in his social reality: the guardian is not a villain by definition but a family member exercising what custom gave him, and the verse legislates against the custom itself — transferring the marriage's decision from the household's honor-politics to the couple's consent, and making the transfer binding on the believers precisely as believers.

**Enjoined on Whoever Believes**

The verse's second sentence states the prohibition's addressee and its ground: therewith is counseled — admonished, enjoined — whoever among you believes in God and the Last Day. The formula is the scripture's weightiest mode of address: the same construction the Qur'an uses where a ruling is bound to the faith's foundations rather than to legal technicality. The obstruction of the honorable remarriage is thereby made a faith-question — not merely a violation of family law but a failure of belief in God and the Last Day. The two beliefs named do specific work. Belief in God: the consent-legislation is His, the couple's honorable agreement stands under His law, and the guardian who overrides it overrides a divine ruling by a family preference. Belief in the Last Day: the hindrance will be audited — the sister kept from her lawful marriage, the couple driven to secrecy or sin by a brother's balk, the harm done under color of family honor — all of it carried into the reckoning where the motives custom excused will be weighed as they are. The verse thus recruits the family's own piety against the family's own politics: the guardian is not threatened with a court but counseled as a believer, and the counsel's seriousness is the Day's.

**Purer and More Dignifying**

The third sentence gives the ruling's social wisdom: that is purer for you, and cleaner — azkā and aṭhar, the vocabulary of growth and purity. The comparative is measured against the alternative the obstruction produces, and the commentators' reading of the marriage-system supplies the calculation: when the honorable remarriage is blocked, the couple's consent does not disappear — it is driven underground. The correspondence continues, the meetings become secret, the arrangement that the law would have conducted in the open with witnesses and a contract becomes exactly what the family's honor-politics feared: a liaison without the law's protection. The lawful path blocked, the unlawful path opens; the purity the obstruction claims to defend is the first casualty of the obstruction. The verse names the reversal in its own terms: permitting the remarriage is the purer course and the cleaner — for the woman, whose freedom is real rather than nominal; for the former husband, whose honorable consent is honored; for the family, whose sister is married in daylight rather than compromised in secret; and for the community, whose law is not made an obstacle its members route around. The dignity-clause completes the wisdom: the ruling elevates rather than merely permits — a community whose families release their daughters to their own honorable choices is a community whose law is trusted, and the trust is the azkā, the growth the verse promises.

**God Knows, and You Know Not**

The verse ends with the knowledge-formula that closed the fighting-prescription (2:216): God knows, and you know not. The formula's return at the household-legislation's turn is structural — the two clauses bracket the whole passage from the war's obligation to the marriage's remarriage, and the bracket is the passage's epistemology: at both of the community's hardest legislations, the human judgment is submitted to the divine knowledge. Here the submission is specific: the brother who balks believes he knows what protects his sister — the character of the former husband, the scandal of the remarriage, the family's standing; the verse tells him that the knowledge operative in the case is not his. God knows the couple's consent and its honor, knows the harm the obstruction will produce, knows the purity of the lawful path and the corruption of the blocked one — and the guardian's knowledge is the family's interest, dressed as judgment. The formula is not a rebuke of the brother's love but a relocation of the decision: the love may remain, the counsel may be given, the concern may be voiced — but the hindrance is forbidden because the case belongs to a knowledge the family does not possess. The same relocation ran through the fighting's prescription: the community's dislike overruled by the divine knowing of the good (2:216); here the family's objection overruled by the divine knowing of the pure. Both legislations end in the same posture — obedience inside admitted ignorance — and the posture is the passage's formed heart.

**The Consent-Centered Household**

Within the divorce-code this verse completes the legislation's transfer of the marriage's center from the households to the spouses. The count and the bar (2:229–230) bounded the husband's power; the harm-prohibition (2:231) policed its exercise; and this verse establishes the couple's consent as the fact the whole system serves — honored at the marriage's founding (the gate of 2:221), protected at its dissolution (the release in grace of 2:229), and sovereign at its renewal (the honorable agreement of this verse). The remarriage-clause is the code's most complete statement of that sovereignty: two people who have been married, divorced, and freed may marry again on their own honorable consent, and every third party — guardian, brother, family, custom — is legislated out of the decision. The verse's closing counsel binds the arrangement to faith rather than to force: it is the believer in God and the Last Day who is enjoined, and the community's families are reformed not by the court's compulsion but by the counsel addressed to their belief. Between the war-legislation's God knows and this verse's God knows, the sūrah has legislated the community's two most contested domains — its violence and its households — under one epistemic rule: human judgments submitted, divine knowledge sovereign, and the purest path the one the Lawgiver named.
