## Sūrah an-Naml 27:52

> So their homes are there, ˹but completely˺ ruined because of their wrongdoing. Surely in this is a lesson for people of knowledge.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Evidence Is Still Standing**

The demonstrative is present tense and pointing: *fa-tilka buyūtuhum* — those are their houses. Not *those were*. The Qur'an directs its first audience toward structures they could go and look at.

Thamūd's dwellings survive in northwestern Arabia, monumental façades cut directly into sandstone outcrops, and the site lies on the caravan route between Mecca and Syria that the Quraysh used for trade. Merchants passing north saw them. That geography converts the preceding verse's claim from a story into a checkable assertion, which is exactly what the imperative in verse 51 demanded — look at the outcome. The chapter is not asking for trust in a report about ancient peoples. It is pointing out of the window at a ruin whose emptiness is the whole argument.

**Houses That Outlasted Their Builders**

There is a specific irony in what survived. Thamūd are remembered in the Qur'an for carving their homes out of rock, and elsewhere the Book records them doing it with confidence, as people securing themselves. The stone held. The people did not.

This is the point the ruins make without any commentary. A civilisation that invests in permanence is making a bet about what lasts, and the bet was answered in the most literal way available: the architecture is intact and the nation is gone. The Qur'an makes the same observation about other peoples who built to endure. What distinguishes Thamūd's case is that the evidence is unusually durable, since rock-cut tombs do not collapse the way mud-brick cities do. Two and a half thousand years after the fact, the houses are still there and still empty, which is the condition the verse describes.

**"Because of Their Wrongdoing"**

The verse assigns a cause: *bimā ẓalamū*, on account of the wrong they did. Al-Ṭabarī identifies the wrong as their idolatry and their denial of their prophet.

Naming the cause blocks the natural alternative explanation. Ruins invite the reading that a civilisation failed for the reasons civilisations usually fail — drought, invasion, trade routes shifting, a dynasty exhausting itself. The verse forecloses that reading for this case and states a moral cause instead. Whether a modern reader accepts the claim is a separate question from what the text is doing, which is refusing to let the evidence be absorbed into a purely material account. The word chosen is *ẓulm*, putting something where it does not belong, which covers both halves of al-Ṭabarī's gloss precisely: worship given to the wrong recipient, and a messenger given the wrong reception.

**The Lesson Is Restricted to Those Who Know**

The verse ends by limiting its audience: *inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yaʿlamūn* — in this is a sign for a people who know. Not for everyone who passes, and not for everyone who looks.

The restriction recalls the chapter's second verse, where the Book was called guidance and good news specifically for the believers. The same logic applies here. A ruin is publicly available and the sign in it is not, because reading a sign requires bringing something to it. Caravans passed these façades for centuries and most travellers saw architecture. Extracting a lesson from an empty city requires already believing that outcomes carry meaning, which is the disposition the chapter has been arguing for since it named the Book's verses *āyāt*, signs. The word used here is the same one. Thamūd's houses and the Qur'an's verses are placed in one category.

**An Inward Reading of the Ruin**

Alongside the plain sense, a strand of commentary read the desolate houses as an image of the people rather than of their property. The interpretation takes the hardened, emptied dwelling as a figure for a heart made rigid by disobedience — the structure intact, the life gone.

This is allegory, and it should be labelled as such rather than presented as the verse's meaning. The sentence is about buildings, and its evidential force depends on the buildings being real and locatable. But the extension is not arbitrary, because it works from something the plain sense already contains: the mismatch between a durable exterior and an absent interior. A person can maintain every outward form of a functioning life while the thing the forms existed for has departed, and there is no visible difference from outside. Whether the commentators were right to find that in this verse, the observation stands on its own, and it belongs to a chapter that has spent fifty verses on people who looked at surfaces and drew the wrong conclusion. Those who were not destroyed are accounted for next.

**A Sign Placed in the Landscape**

The word for what the ruins constitute is *āyah*, the same word the chapter's first verse used for the verses of the Qur'an. Two very different things are put in one category: sentences that are recited and stones that are passed on a trade road.

That shared term is a claim about how evidence works. A verse and a ruin both point beyond themselves to something that placed them there, and both require a reader willing to follow the pointing. Neither compels. The Qur'an uses *āyah* in a third sense as well, for the natural world — rain, the alternation of night and day, the growth of a seed — so the category covers scripture, history and creation together. Thamūd's empty houses belong to the middle term, history preserved in stone. They are what happened, preserved, available for inspection, and carrying a meaning that the chapter has just spelled out for anyone who would otherwise miss it. Nothing in the description asks the reader to take the outcome on trust; the evidence is placed where anyone travelling that road could inspect it.
