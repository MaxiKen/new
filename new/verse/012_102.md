## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:102

> That is from the stories of the unseen which We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not present when they ˹all˺ made up their minds, and when they plotted ˹against Joseph˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Story Ends, Address Begins**

Verse 101 closed with a prayer spoken by Joseph. Verse 102 turns away from the narrative entirely and addresses the person recounting it. The shift is abrupt and complete: no transition, no summary, no closing formula for the story. The chapter has told a continuous account for a hundred verses and now steps outside it to say where the account came from. The sūrah's structure is therefore framed — it opens with a statement about revelation (12:1–3) and closes its narrative with a statement about revelation — and the framing is what tells the reader that the story was never the chapter's only subject. The shift is abrupt and unsignposted. A hundred verses of continuous narrative close with a prayer, and the next verse turns to a reader who has been invisible until now. The chapter's structure claims that the narrative existed for the sake of the address, and the absence of any transition is what makes the claim without having to argue for it.

**"Accounts of the Unseen"**

The phrase denotes reports of matters absent from the recipient's experience: events he did not witness, in a country he has not visited, involving a family that lived centuries before him. The category is one the Qur'an uses repeatedly for its narratives, and its function is evidential. An account of the unseen is not a legend retold or a tradition collected; on the Qur'an's own claim it is information transmitted. The verse places the story of Joseph inside that category explicitly, which means the chapter is asking to be read as a report rather than as an edifying tale, and is asking the reader to consider how the report reached the person delivering it.

**"You Were Not Present When They Decided"**

The argument is stated as a fact about the recipient's absence. He was not with Joseph's brothers when they settled on their course and plotted. The commentators draw the conclusion directly: the knowledge contained in this chapter was given to the Prophet ﷺ without his having been present at the events, without his having learned it from human teachers, and without his having read it in a book. The verse does not elaborate the argument. It states the absence and leaves the inference — that a detailed account of a private council held centuries earlier cannot have been obtained by the person now reporting it. The argument targets a specific kind of knowledge. Not the general outline of a famous story, which many people possessed, but the content of a private conspiracy among eleven men in a desert, including what they said to one another. The verse asks where that came from and leaves no candidate except revelation, which is the chapter's claim about itself in a single sentence.

**A Council That Was Private**

The moment specified is not a public event. The brothers' deliberation is described in verses 8 to 10 as a conversation among them — a proposal to kill Joseph or cast him into a land where he would be forgotten, a counter-proposal to throw him into a well, an agreement. Nobody outside the group was present, and the chapter has already noted that the plan was concealed from the father, executed with a false report and supported by a bloodied shirt (12:16–18). Verse 102 selects that scene as the test case. A private conspiracy, hidden from the only adult in the family and never disclosed until decades later, is the kind of thing whose detailed narration requires a source other than observation.

**The Unlettered Prophet**

The Qur'an describes its recipient in terms that bear directly on this verse: the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description people find in the Torah and the Gospel (7:157). The description is relevant to the argument in verse 102 because it removes the ordinary explanations for detailed knowledge of the past. A person who does not read cannot have acquired the account from scripture; a person who was not present cannot have acquired it from the participants; a person who lived centuries later cannot have acquired it from witnesses. The verse states the absence and the Qur'an states elsewhere the literacy; together they are the chapter's claim about its own origin. The two statements work as one argument. A narrative containing details unavailable to any living source, delivered by a person without access to written accounts, leaves the audience with a question the chapter does not need to answer because it has already framed it. What is asserted is not the truth of the story but the impossibility of its having been assembled.

**Addressed to the Prophet and Through Him**

The address is in the second person singular and it names the recipient. The chapter has spoken in the narrator's voice throughout, and it now speaks directly to the man reciting it. The effect is to place the recipient inside the verse rather than outside it: he is not a transmitter mentioned in passing but the person to whom the accounts of the unseen are being revealed, at the moment of the revelation. The reader is thereby positioned as a third party overhearing a communication, which is the stance the sūrah's opening verses asked for when they described the Qur'an as a clear recitation sent down so that people might understand. The double address is what makes the story usable. A revelation given only to its recipient would have ended with him; a recitation sent down for people to understand is addressed to everyone who reads it, including whoever is holding this verse. The chapter tells its audience that the hundred verses behind them were meant for them, in the voice of a description rather than an appeal.

**Why This Verse Belongs to This Chapter**

Verse 102 could have closed any of the Qur'an's narrative sūrahs, and it closes this one. The reason is that no other chapter contains a comparable concentration of private, unwitnessed material: a family council, a concealed plot, a false report, a woman's locked room, a prison conversation, a search conducted in a calculated order, a cup planted in a bag, a disclosure made to one brother and withheld from nine. Almost nothing in the story was public at the time it happened. The chapter therefore has the strongest internal reason to make the argument in verse 102, and it makes it once, at the end, after the reader has encountered the private material a hundred times.

**Story and Claim Held Together**

The verse does not detach the narrative from its purpose. It says *that* — referring back to the account just completed — is from the accounts of the unseen. The demonstration and the claim occupy the same verse, which means the chapter expects the story it has told to function as the evidence for the claim it now makes. A reader unconvinced by the reasoning is being pointed back at the material: the detail, the coherence, the fulfilment of the dream, the fifteen-year agricultural calendar, the private council. The sūrah's method is not to argue for its origin in the abstract but to supply a narrative and then ask where it came from. The method distinguishes the chapter's argument from a doctrinal one. No premise is stated in advance, no authority is invoked at the opening, and no conclusion is drawn until the narrative has run its full length. The reader is given the evidence first and the question afterwards, which is the only order in which that question can be asked seriously.
