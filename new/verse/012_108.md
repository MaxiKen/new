## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:108

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “This is my way. I invite to Allah with insight—I and those who follow me. Glory be to Allah, and I am not one of the polytheists.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Declare**

The verse opens with *qul*, say — the imperative the Qur'an uses whenever a statement is to be made publicly in the Prophet's own name rather than in the narrator's. The command is significant after three verses of diagnosis. Verses 105 to 107 described an audience in the third person: they pass by signs, they associate others with God, they feel secure from an overwhelming punishment and from the Hour. Verse 108 changes the grammatical person and puts words into the messenger's mouth. The chapter's closing argument therefore ends not with an analysis of its audience but with a declaration by the person addressing it, and the declaration is ordered before the analysis has been resolved or answered by anyone. The order of the two acts is deliberate. A messenger who waited until his message had been accepted would never declare it, and the verse places the declaration first, unconditionally, with the results described afterwards and left where they belong. The chapter has just watched Joseph do exactly this: state the plan, request the office, and let the outcome rest with God.

**"This Is My Way"**

The word denotes a path, a road, a manner of proceeding. The commentators read it in two directions: the Prophet's customary practice and character, or the religion he brought. The Qur'an uses the same noun when instructing him to invite to the way of his Lord with wisdom and good exhortation, and the two usages belong together rather than competing. The declaration is therefore about a method as much as a doctrine — not merely what is believed but how the believing is arrived at, held and communicated to other people. Verse 108 states the method in the clause that follows, and the chapter's own narrative has spent a hundred verses demonstrating it in the career of a prophet who was sold, imprisoned and then given a country. The demonstration is what the declaration points at. A way described in the abstract could mean almost anything; a way illustrated across a hundred verses — through a well, a slave household, a locked door, a prison, a famine and a reunion — has a specific content the reader has just witnessed. The verse names the story behind it and calls the story a path.

**"With Insight"**

The commentators gloss the term as that by which a person distinguishes with certainty between truth and falsehood — insight in the sense of settled conviction rather than rhetorical skill or persuasive technique. The specification answers verses 105 and 106 directly. People pass by signs without reflecting on them; belief remains mixed because nothing has been sorted out and no distinction has been drawn. The invitation declared here is made on the basis of certainty about which is which, and it offers the hearer the same clarity rather than a competing claim requiring adjudication. The chapter has just shown what insight looks like in practice: a prisoner reading a dream nobody else in Egypt could read, and reading it as a fifteen-year agricultural programme with a year of relief attached.

**"I and Those Who Follow Me"**

The declaration includes the followers inside the method. This is not a claim to a personal distinction that others admire from a distance, nor a description of a unique office that cannot be shared. It states that the same way of inviting, on the same basis of insight, belongs to everyone who follows. The clause has a practical consequence for the chapter's audience. A message delivered with insight by one person and received uncritically by many would reproduce the mixed belief verse 106 describes, since the followers would hold a conviction they had never examined. A message whose followers are described as inviting on the same basis produces something different, and the verse states the second arrangement as the actual one rather than as an ideal. Stating it as actual makes the verse a description rather than an aspiration. The messenger is neither alone nor claiming a private revelation; he names a group sharing his path and its evidence, and he places himself inside that group rather than above it. The chapter closes its argument with the first person singular joined to a plural in a single declaration.

**"Glory Be to God"**

The exclamation declares God beyond whatever is falsely said of Him, and it functions as the pivot of the verse. Having described a way and a method, the speaker turns from his own activity to the object of that activity and removes from it whatever the audience has attached to it. The commentators connect this directly to the preceding verse's diagnosis: association is a statement about God, and the correction of association must therefore also be a statement about God rather than merely about the people making it. The exclamation is the shortest possible form of that correction — three words in Arabic — and it precedes the final denial so that the denial rests on a clarified object rather than on a contested one. Clarification is what the exclamation performs. Rather than simply rejecting a claim, the verse first removes God from the category of things that could be associated with anything, so the denial that follows concerns a settled matter rather than an open one. The chapter ends its long narrative with the move Joseph made in prison: establish who God is before saying anything else.

**"I Am Not One of the Polytheists"**

The verse ends with a dissociation stated in the first person. The speaker does not accuse his audience of association in this clause; he separates himself from it and says what he is not. The form is significant. After three verses describing what other people do, the declaration closes with a statement about the speaker's own position rather than about their failure. The chapter's final words of invitation are therefore self-describing before they are accusatory, which is consistent with the method named two clauses earlier: an invitation made on the basis of clarity about one's own standing, offered by a person who has sorted out his own belief before asking anyone else to sort out theirs. The dissociation also answers verse 106 in the only way the chapter considers effective — not by argument but by a stated position.

**The Verse Answers the Whole Closing Section**

Verses 102 to 108 form a single movement: an account of where the story came from, a statement that most people will not believe however keen the messenger is, a declaration that no reward is asked and that the message is a reminder to the whole world, an exclamation about signs passed by, a diagnosis of mixed belief, two questions about false security, and finally a way declared publicly in the first person. Verse 108 is the response to all of it, and it is the only verse in the sequence that offers something rather than describing something. The chapter's structure at the end mirrors its narrative: a long account of what happened to one prophet, followed by a short statement of what is now being offered by another.
