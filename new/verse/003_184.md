## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:184

> If you are rejected by them, so too were messengers before you who came with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Consolation Repeated**

The verse tells the Prophet that if the people deny him, they had already denied messengers who came before him with the proofs their Lord had given them. The sentence is the sūrah's fourth address to the Prophet's grief, and it uses the same remedy as the others.

The argument is addressed to the experience of rejection rather than to the arguments of the rejecters. The Prophet is told that the response he was receiving had a history, and that the men who preceded him had been met with the same treatment from the people they came to. The sentence's force comes from the fact that the Prophet is not being told to expect anything unusual.

**The Clear Proofs**

The commentators describe the clear proofs as the various kinds of demonstration that accompanied the messengers: the signs they performed, the testimonies they produced, and the arguments they brought. The term is a broad one, and the Book uses it for several kinds of evidence.

The phrase in this verse gathers all of them into a single category, and the point is that the messengers had not come to their peoples without support. The denial described in the sentence was a response to something that had been supplied, and the sūrah has been making the same point about its own community's opponents through its account of the arguments it was meeting.

**The Word for Scriptures**

The term rendered as scriptures is the plural of a word meaning something written, and the commentators note that it refers to books containing wisdom rather than to one specific volume. One of them observes that the term is distinct from the word for the Psalms of David, which has a different form and a narrower reference.

The Book uses the broader word for the revelations given to a range of messengers, and one of the later sūrahs refers to the sheets of Abraham and Moses in describing the teachings of the earlier revelations. The verse before the reader gathers the written revelation of the earlier messengers under one heading.

**The Luminous Book**

The phrase about the luminous Book has been explained as a reference to the Torah and the Gospel, and the commentators discussed whether it is a separate item from the scriptures mentioned before it or the same thing described again. Al-Rāzī read the conjunction as indicating that the two phrases describe a single reality.

The term for luminous appears in the Book's descriptions of the revelation in other places as well, and the quality it names is the light by which a text makes matters clear to the people who receive it. The verse thus lists what the messengers brought in three parts: the proofs that were shown, the books that were written, and the light they carried.

**The Earlier Messengers' Experience**

The Book describes the treatment of the earlier messengers in several passages. One states that every time a messenger came to the people of Israel with what they did not want, they rejected some and killed others. Another describes the covenant that was taken from them and the messengers who were sent and rejected.

The verse before the reader places the Prophet's own experience within that record, and it asks the community to read its own difficulties as a repetition of a pattern that runs through the history of revelation. The community at Madinah had seen its message dismissed by groups with every reason to accept it, and the sentence tells it that such a reception is not evidence that the message was false. What the community was facing had been faced before, and the messengers who faced it had been vindicated in the end.

**What the Community Gains From the Sentence**

The consolation in the verse extends beyond the Prophet. Any community that carries a message meets the same treatment, and the sentence supplies the response in advance: the denial of the present does not distinguish the messenger from those who came before him.

The sūrah had already told the believers not to be grieved by those who rush into disbelief. This verse adds the historical frame to the same instruction, and the community is given a way of reading its own difficulties that does not depend on the reaction of the people it addresses. What has been refused is not a new message but the latest in a long series, and the series has a record that the community can examine.

**The Book's Names for the Earlier Revelations**

The Book refers to the revelations that preceded it by several names, and it treats them as a single stream of guidance given to successive messengers. One passage lists the prophets who received revelation and mentions the Psalms given to David along with the books given to others.

Another passage asks the believers to declare their faith in what was revealed to them and in what was revealed to the earlier prophets without distinguishing among them. The verse before the reader belongs to that treatment of revelation as one continuous work, and it uses the general language of the Book's own vocabulary.


The verse closes the sūrah's answer to the arguments of those who demanded signs and mocked the message, and it prepares the reader for the statement about death and reward that follows. The two movements are connected by the subject of what endures.

The community had been told that its messenger would not be treated differently from his predecessors. The next verse states what every soul will experience and where the final account is settled, and the passage's concerns are gathered there in a single sentence that the community has recited ever since.
