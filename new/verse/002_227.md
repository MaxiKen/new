## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:227

> But if they settle on divorce, then Allah is indeed All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Exit**

The verse is one of the Qur'an's shortest legislative units — a single conditional and a pair of names — and its brevity is its function. The previous verse set the īlāʾ-oath's term: four months, then reconciliation or continuation of the harm. This verse names the alternative to reconciliation: but if they resolve on divorce — the second exit, stated without elaboration because the elaboration is coming: the waiting-periods, the counts, the honorable retention and graceful release of the verses that follow. What the verse does here is close the option-set. At the four months' end there are exactly two doors — return, under the Forgiving and Merciful of the previous verse, or divorce, under this verse's Hearing and Knowing — and the third state, the pre-Islamic limbo of the suspended wife, has no door at all. The legislation's completeness is in the closure: every exit of the estranged marriage is provided, named, and assigned its divine names, and the exit that harms without term — the husband's indefinite withholding — is simply absent from the architecture. A law that leaves no room for the weaponized suspense has legislated exactly what the īlāʾ-verses exist to legislate.

**Resolve, Not Drift**

The verse's verb deserves its weight: they *resolve* — ʿazamū, the word of firm decision, settled intention, the knotting of the will. Divorce at the deadline is not to be a drifting, not a continuation-by-default of the estrangement that expired, not a sullen letting-the-marriage-lapse; it is a decision, taken with the same finality the word carries elsewhere in the Qur'an — the resolve of matters, the determination that precedes great acts. The requirement transforms the exit's moral quality. The husband who lets the suspended state slide into permanent abandonment has decided nothing and harmed endlessly; the husband who resolves has looked at his marriage, concluded that its reconciliation is not willed by him, and taken the lawful step that releases both parties into the dissolution-legislation's care. The ʿazm also answers the wife's position: a resolved divorce begins the waiting-period whose end she can see and after which her freedom is complete — the resolve is what converts her open-ended captivity into a bounded process. The verse thereby makes decision itself a duty of justice: where reconciliation is not chosen, the choosing of divorce is owed, and the owing is enforced by the names that follow.

**Hearing, Knowing**

The names presiding over the second exit differ from the names that presided over the first, and the difference is the verse's theology. Reconciliation was received by the Forgiving, the Merciful — names of welcome over the marriage resumed. Divorce is received by the Hearing, the Knowing — names of audit over the marriage ended. The Hearing attends to the divorce's word: the pronouncement is a speech-act, uttered and heard, with legal consequences that attach to its utterance; God hears the ṭalāq as the community's courts must, and the name guarantees that the word spoken in the household's privacy is spoken in the presence of the Hearer. The Knowing attends to the divorce's interior: the resolve behind the word — whether it is the settled decision the verb requires or the masked continuation of harm, whether the estrangement's history carried injustice into the dissolution, whether the wife's rights will survive the husband's decision. The two names together install the divorce inside the same surveillance the whole domestic series has run under: the guardian's intentions known (2:220), the oaths' hearts earned (2:225), the womb's secrets legible to belief in the Last Day (2:228 as it will legislate) — and now the marriage's ending heard and known. Nothing about the exit escapes the audit, which is why the exit can be left so brief: the One who hears and knows requires no elaboration here to protect the departing wife.

**The Pair as One Law**

The two verses are read in the tradition as a single legislation — the commentators treat 226–227 together, and their joint architecture repays the reading. One institution is abolished: the oath-suspension without term. One diagnostic is installed: four months as the harm-line. Two exits are opened: return with pardon, divorce with resolve. Two name-pairs preside: Forgiving-Merciful over the reconciliation, Hearing-Knowing over the dissolution. And one state is left without existence: the marriage that is neither lived nor ended. The pair's economy — a long verse and a short one, the machinery and the exit — enacts the law's proportion: the reconciliation, which the legislation prefers and facilitates, receives the fuller treatment and the warmer names; the divorce, which the legislation permits and polices, receives the brieest clause and the names of audit. The preference is legible in the structure without being stated: the return is made easy, cheap, and welcomed; the divorce is made deliberate, audited, and consequential — and between the two the suspended woman's release is guaranteed either way, since both doors open from the inside at the four months' end.

**The Divorce-Legislation's Threshold**

This verse is the threshold of the sūrah's divorce-code, and its position determines everything that follows. The waiting-period legislation (2:228) presupposes a divorce properly declared — resolved, heard, known — and its rules about the waiting, the return-right, and the womb's truthfulness all operate downstream of this threshold. The count-legislation (2:229) presupposes divorces that are decisions rather than harassments: twice, then retention in honor or release in grace. The harm-prohibition (2:231) exists because the threshold can be abused — the resolve reversed and re-declared as sport — and the Knowing at this verse is the abuse's standing witness. Read as the code's entrance, the verse performs the entrance's whole office: it establishes that divorce in this legislation is a grave, deliberate, divinely audited act — not a husband's casual instrument, not a drifting default, not a weapon of suspense. The tradition's famous description of divorce as the most hateful of permitted things to God finds its textual ground in exactly this architecture: permitted at the threshold, audited by the names, bounded by the counts, and preferred at every point by the reconciliation the previous verse welcomed — the permission real, and surrounded on every side by the machinery that makes its use deliberate.

**The Household Under the Names**

The verse leaves the estranged marriage — and the reader — inside the divine names, and the whole domestic series is now visibly a legislation of named presences. The marriage's gate stands under the clarity that produces mindfulness (2:221); the body's cycle under the love of the repentant and the purifying (2:222); the tilth under the meeting with God and the glad tidings (2:223); the oaths under the Hearing and Knowing of speech and the Forgiving and Clement of habit (2:224–225); the īlāʾ's return under Forgiving and Merciful (2:226); and its divorce under Hearing and Knowing (2:227). No chamber of the household — its founding, its bed, its quarrels, its estrangements, its dissolutions — is left outside a named relation to God; every act, from the sworn word to the resolved divorce, occurs in the presence of attributes that see its interior and answer its need. The brevity of this verse is thus not thinness but density: two names doing the work that lesser legislation would need paragraphs to do, because the Hearing requires no procedure to hear and the Knowing requires no evidence to know. The husband who resolves on divorce speaks his word into that hearing; the wife whose release the resolve begins stands under that knowing; and the code that follows will legislate their separation in the same light — heard, known, and bounded by the One who does both.
