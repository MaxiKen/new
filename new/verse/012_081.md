## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:81

> Return to your father and say, ‘O our father! Your son committed theft. We testify only to what we know. We could not guard against the unforeseen.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Eldest Finishes the Speech He Began**

Verse 80 ended with a vow not to leave Egypt. Verse 81 continues the same speech and turns to the nine who are going home, instructing them what to say. The instruction is quoted at length: an address to Jacob composed by one brother for the others to deliver, beginning with the words they are to use and ending with the limits of what they may claim. The chapter's arrangement gives the eldest two verses of uninterrupted speech, which is more narrative space than any of the brothers receives anywhere else in the sūrah. The content justifies the space. What he composes is not a cover story, not a mitigation and not an excuse. It is an accurate account of a disaster, in the plainest language available, and the accuracy is the reason for his having composed it at all rather than leaving the report to chance.

**"Your Son Committed Theft"**

The central statement is given in the harshest available terms and it names nobody. *Your son* — not Benjamin, not our brother, not the boy you made us swear about. The formulation forces Jacob to identify the person himself, and it places the loss where it falls: between a father and a child rather than among brothers. The charge is not softened. The brothers are not to say that a cup was found, that a search was conducted, that a penalty was applied under their own stated law, or that ten of them offered themselves in substitution. They are to say their brother stole. The eldest chooses the sentence that will cause his father the most pain and does not attempt to make it bearable, which is a decision about honesty rather than about cruelty.

**Testimony Limited to What Was Seen**

The two qualifying clauses work together. The first restricts the evidence: we testify only to what we know. The brothers watched the search, saw ten bags opened and empty, and saw the object produced from the eleventh. They did not see it placed, and the chapter has told the reader who placed it (12:70). Their testimony goes to what happened in their presence and no further. The second clause denies a capacity rather than a duty: we were not keepers of the unseen. The commentators gloss this as meaning they could not have foreseen what would happen in Egypt when they made their pledge, and they connect it to the exception Jacob wrote into the oath itself — unless you are totally overpowered (12:66). The plea is placed inside the covenant's own terms rather than outside them.

**An Accurate Report for the Second Time**

The chapter has now shown these men deliver two reports to their father about two lost sons, and the difference in kind is complete. The first was composed before the event, supported by a bloodied shirt, delivered in the evening with weeping, and accompanied by the remark that their father would not believe them however truthful they were (12:16–18). The second is composed after the event, supported by named witnesses, delivered without tears, and confined to what was observed. Both are addressed to the same man, both concern a son he will not see for years, and both receive the same answer (12:18, 12:83). The sūrah does not comment on the difference in truthfulness. It presents the two reports and lets the reader hold them together, which is the method it has used for every moral comparison in the chapter.

**The Brothers Are Not Defending Themselves**

Nothing in the speech argues that they acted well. They do not claim to have protected Benjamin, do not explain why they failed to secure his release, and do not mention the offer of substitution that all ten of them made in verse 78 — an offer that would have counted for something with a father looking for evidence of good faith. The report is a bare statement of fact followed by a denial of foreknowledge, and it stops there. The absence of self-defence is consistent with what the eldest has just said to his brothers in private: that their father took an oath from them in God's name, and that they failed him once before regarding Joseph (12:80). A man who has made that judgement cannot compose a report that argues against it.

**Why the Report Names No Culprit Beyond the Brother**

The speech assigns responsibility for the theft to Benjamin alone and assigns responsibility for the loss to nobody. The brothers do not explain why they did not secure his release, do not describe the official who refused their offer of substitution, and do not characterise the Egyptian procedure that detained him. The report is therefore complete about the fact and silent about the agents. That silence has an effect on the father who receives it. Jacob is given a theft, a penalty and a detention, with no person to hold accountable except a son he cannot reach and ten sons standing in front of him. Verse 83 shows what he does with the gap: he fills it with the only agents he knows. The composition of the report, honest as it is, leaves room for a conclusion its authors did not intend.

**A Message Sent to a Man Who Will Not Believe It**

Jacob's answer in verse 83 rejects the report, not because he has found it inaccurate but because he has heard a similar report before from the same mouths. The eldest presumably knew this. He composed the speech anyway, added the limits on testimony, supplied witnesses in the following verse, and stayed in Egypt rather than deliver it himself. The chapter therefore shows a man taking responsibility for a message he expects to be disbelieved, and choosing absence over the position of being the one who says it. Verse 81 is the brothers' most honest act in the sūrah, and it is performed by nine of them at the instruction of a tenth who will not be present to defend it. That arrangement is what makes the report both credible and, in the event, useless.
