## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:68

> Then when they entered as their father had instructed them, this did not help them against ˹the Will of˺ Allah whatsoever. It was just a desire in Jacob’s heart which he satisfied. He was truly blessed with ˹great˺ knowledge because of what We had taught him, but most people have no knowledge.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Precaution Is Performed and Then Evaluated**

The verse reports compliance first: they entered as their father had commanded them. The obedience is complete and unremarked on, which is how the chapter records the brothers' conduct when they are not being tested. Then comes the narrator's evaluation, and it is blunt — it would not have availed them anything against God. The two statements occupy a single verse, so the reader is not allowed to hold the precaution as a success or as a failure. It was performed, and it changed nothing. The evaluation is not a criticism of Jacob; verse 67 had already contained the same judgement in his own mouth. What verse 68 adds is the narrator's confirmation, given before the events that will demonstrate it.

**"But It Fulfilled a Need in Jacob's Soul"**

The second half of the evaluation is the verse's real contribution. The instruction was useless as protection and valuable as an act. The commentators gloss the phrase as meaning that Jacob's advice, together with his sons' acceptance of it, served to assuage his fear of losing his children. A father who cannot prevent a danger can still do something about it, and the doing matters to him even when it cannot matter to the outcome. The chapter records the psychological fact without sentimentality and without dismissing it. The precaution is honoured precisely because it did nothing — it existed for the person who imposed it, and it was accepted by the people it concerned. That is an unusual economy of value and the verse states it plainly. An action whose entire benefit accrues to the person performing it, and whose failure costs nobody else anything, is still worth performing if it expresses something true about the one who performs it. Jacob's instruction is honoured as an act of fatherhood rather than assessed as a security measure.

**The Trial Arrives Anyway**

The verse's judgement is confirmed within two verses. The brothers enter Egypt, are received, are fed, and then a cup is placed in a saddlebag and a theft is announced (12:70). Benjamin is detained under a law the brothers themselves supplied (12:75–76), and the eldest refuses to return home (12:80). Nothing about entering by separate gates delayed or softened any of it. The chapter is describing a trial that has been arranged independently of the travellers' arrangements, and it states that fact in advance so that the reader does not look for the failure of the precaution as the cause of what follows. The cause is elsewhere, and verse 76 will name it. The deferral is exact. Nothing here explains why the brothers' arrangement fails, and the explanation is withheld for eight verses, until the moment it can be given in full. The chapter is willing to let the reader sit inside an unexplained reversal because it intends that reversal to be understood as part of an arrangement rather than as an accident.

**"He Was a Possessor of Knowledge Because We Taught Him"**

The clause is a statement about the source of Jacob's understanding. He is described as *dhū ʿilm*, a possessor of knowledge, and the possession is immediately attributed to divine teaching: *li-mā ʿallamnāhu*. The same construction governs Joseph's gifts. God established him in Egypt *so that We might teach him the interpretation of dreams* (12:21), and gave him wisdom and knowledge at maturity (12:22). Father and son are described in the chapter's terms as taught rather than as clever, and the attribution matters for how the reader is to take Jacob's conduct. His trust, his precaution and his limits on both are not the product of experience alone. The attribution rules out the reading that Jacob's care is merely accumulated grief. A man taught by God knows something about decree that experience cannot supply, and the verse uses that knowledge to explain how he can impose a precaution and declare it insufficient in the same breath. Both statements come from the same instruction, and the chapter credits the instruction rather than the years.

**"But Most People Do Not Know"**

The coda is one the sūrah returns to. Joseph used it at the end of his sermon on divine authority: *“That is the upright faith, but most people do not know”* (12:40), and the narrator attached it to the statement that God's will prevails over the affair of Joseph's sale (12:21). Here it qualifies Jacob's knowledge: he possessed knowledge because God taught him, and most people lack it. The repetition ties three moments together — a betrayal, a theological argument and a father's parting instruction — under a single claim about the rarity of understanding. The verse does not say what most people fail to know, and the omission leaves the claim general enough to cover every instance the chapter has produced. The instances the chapter has produced are specific enough to fill the gap. Most people do not know that a father's instruction about separate gates will avail nothing, that a planted cup will yield a lawful detention, or that knowledge can be taught rather than accumulated; each of these has been shown, and none of them was known by the people living through it. The generalisation summarises what the reader has just watched rather than adding a new claim.

**Two Kinds of Knowledge Distinguished**

The verse holds a distinction that the rest of the chapter depends on. Jacob knows something about God's decree — that no human precaution can avert it, that judgment belongs to God alone, that trust is the only adequate response. He does not know what the reader will soon learn: that Joseph is alive, that the minister of Egypt is his son, and that the cup is about to be planted. His knowledge is theological and certain; his information is incomplete. The chapter's irony depends on the gap between the two. A man with real knowledge of God sends his sons into a situation he cannot read, and the situation turns out to be governed by the very knowledge he has.

**No Rebuke, and That Is the Verse's Point**

A reader expecting the precaution to be exposed as superstition or as a failure of trust will not find that here. The verse says the instruction did not avail them and that it satisfied a need in Jacob's soul, then calls him a possessor of knowledge taught by God. All three statements stand together. The chapter thereby refuses two easy positions: that human measures work, and that they are pointless. Measures do not determine outcomes, and they are not on that account worthless, because the person who imposes them is doing something other than determining an outcome. The sūrah leaves the reader with a father's care described in the same verse as its ineffectiveness and his knowledge. The absence of rebuke is what completes the description. Had the verse corrected Jacob for relying on gates it would have taught that precautions are worthless; had it shown them working it would have taught that they are sufficient. Holding both facts together without comment is the only formulation that keeps reliance on God intact while still taking the means seriously.

**A Verse About a Man Who Is Not Present**

Jacob does not travel to Egypt in this section, and the verse keeps him in Canaan while narrating an event in an Egyptian city. The chapter is unusual in this: it reports the sons' entry, evaluates the father's instruction, and assesses the father's knowledge, all while the father is hundreds of miles away and unaware that his sons have arrived. The effect is to hold both locations in a single frame. Canaan contains a grieving man with real knowledge and no information; Egypt contains a ruler with full information and a plan. Verse 68 belongs to both, and its judgement about the uselessness of the precaution is spoken from the side that has neither the information nor the power to act on it. That is also the reader's position. Nobody reading this verse knows what Joseph is doing in Egypt or why he has detained Benjamin, and the judgement passed on Jacob's precaution is made with exactly the information the family possesses. The chapter asks its audience to occupy the same standpoint as the people it describes, and then to notice that the standpoint is not the whole of what is true.
