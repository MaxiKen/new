## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:13

> They invoke those whose worship leads to harm, not benefit. What an evil patron and what an evil associate!

**Expanded Commentary**

**From "Neither" to "Harm"**

Verse 12 said the object of worship can neither harm nor benefit. Verse 13 moves the ground: he calls on what harms him rather than benefits him. The escalation is small in wording and large in implication, and it is the sort of movement the Qur'an makes often within a pair of verses.

The first verse established the idol's nullity — nothing comes of it either way. That is the sceptical point, and it is sufficient to show the futility of the practice. Verse 13 goes further and says the practice is not merely futile but costly. A neutral object would at least waste a person's time; this one inflicts damage.

Read in sequence, the two verses describe a progression the worshipper himself would not accept. He would agree that his god does him no good — every devotee has had unanswered prayers, and the Qur'an's question in verse 12 is designed to be unanswerable. What he would not agree to is that the worship is actively harming him. That is the claim the sūrah makes, and it is the claim that explains the double exclamation at the end.

**What the Harm Consists In**

The commentators were careful here, and their caution is instructive. The harm is not magical. No idol emits a curse; the stone does nothing because it can do nothing, which verse 12 has just established. Al-Rāzī's reading is the one to follow: the worship of something other than God is harmful *in itself*, from the religious point of view, because it redirects the worship a human being owes and thereby dismantles the relationship that makes him whole.

On that account the damage is constitutional rather than punitive. A person is built to orient himself toward his source, and misdirecting that orientation is not a harmless hobby with an unfortunate ending; it is the deformation of the thing he is. The harm happens at the moment of the prostration, not afterwards.

There is a second, plainer harm in the social register, and it is the one the two closing nouns point to: a man has put his trust in a patron who cannot deliver. The damage is what happens to a person who discovers, at the moment he needs help, that his insurance was never underwritten.

**Two Words for a Bad Friend**

The verse ends by naming the relationship twice: *labi'sa al-mawlā wa-labi'sa al-ʿashīr* — what an evil *mawlā*, and what an evil *ʿashīr*.

Both terms are institutions before they are insults, and the choice of these two in particular is exact. A *mawlā* is a patron or client, the party to a bond of protection — the arrangement by which a person without a tribe attached himself to one, answered for by it and answerable to it. An *ʿashīr* is a close companion, the person you mix with, the associate of your daily life and, in the tribal vocabulary, a kinsman of your own generation.

Between them they cover the two relationships in which a pre-Islamic Arab located his safety: formal protection and informal company. The verse declares both of them evil in this instance. The god he took as a *mawlā* cannot protect him; the god he took as a daily companion, the one whose company he keeps, turns out to be worse than nobody.

**Why the Verdict Is Shouted Rather Than Argued**

The form is *labi'sa*, a fixed Arabic construction of censure — how evil is such-and-such. The Qur'an reserves it for a small set of judgements, and it always closes an argument rather than opening one.

That placement is the point. Verses 12 and 13 have already done the reasoning: the criterion was stated, the criterion was applied, the nullity was established, and the harm was named. What remains is not a further premise but a verdict, and the verse gives it as a verdict — an exclamation, not a syllogism.

The rhetorical effect is worth noticing because it is unusual in the Qur'an, which so often argues patiently with its opponents. Here the patience ends. A person who has been shown that his patron is powerless and still keeps the patron is not going to be moved by another argument, and the verse does not offer him one. It pronounces.

**The Verse Is Also a Warning About Company**

*ʿAshīr* deserves a second look. A *mawlā* is chosen deliberately, with formalities. An *ʿashīr* is acquired almost without noticing — the people you sit with, whose opinions become weather. The verse names both because the damage enters by both routes.

This is the practical edge of the passage. A person can renounce every idol he can name and still keep company that quietly reorients him, because companionship works by habit rather than by decision. The Qur'an treats association as a substantive act, one of the few things that shapes a person more than his stated beliefs do, and it will say so explicitly elsewhere when it describes a man biting his hands on the Day of Resurrection over the friend he chose.

Naming the *ʿashīr* as evil therefore completes the diagnosis. The idolater's error is not only that he bowed to a stone; it is that he built a life around things that could not help him, and he did it in company, which is how such lives are always built.

**The Damage Is Available to Be Seen Now**

One last feature of the pairing. Both nouns describe relationships in this world: a patron and a companion are things you have while you are alive. The harm the verse names is therefore not deferred to a judgment day, though the sūrah will return to that judgment soon enough.

The man in verse 11 lost both worlds, and the man here is losing this one already — his protection is fictional, his companion is worthless, and the discovery can be made by anyone who asks what his patron has ever actually done. The sūrah's next verse will turn immediately to the people whose patron is real.
