## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:2

> **By the clear Book!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Swears by His Own Speech**

The particle wa here is the wāw of oath. God swears by the Book, and the thing sworn is stated in the next verse. An oath in Arabic raises the value of what follows; a speaker swears by what is great in order to make the listener take the claim seriously. God swears by many created things in the Qurʾān: the dawn, the fig, the sun, the star, the horses that charge. Here He swears by the Qurʾān itself. This is a striking choice, because the object of the oath is also the subject of the passage. The Book vouches for the Book. That is not a circular argument in the way it first sounds. The oath does not ask the listener to accept the Qurʾān because the Qurʾān says so. It puts the text on the table as evidence: listen to it, weigh its language and its content, and judge whether such speech could come from the man reciting it. The Makkans had heard poets and soothsayers all their lives. They were being asked to compare, and the oath dares them to.

**Clear in Words, Clear in Meaning**

The adjective is mubīn, from the root b-y-n, which carries the sense of separation and distinctness. Something bayyin is set apart from what surrounds it, so that its edges can be seen. Applied to speech, it means speech whose sounds are plain and whose sense is plain. Ibn Kathīr takes both together: the Qurʾān is clear in its wording and clear in its meaning. Its Arabic is not obscure, its rulings are not hidden in riddles, and its central claims, that God is one, that the dead are raised, that the Prophet ﷺ is sent, are stated in plain sentences that a caravan driver could repeat. This has a polemical edge. The Quraysh sometimes said the Qurʾān was the jumbled dreams of a poet or the mutterings of a possessed man. Both accusations imply confusion. The oath answers that the text before them is the least confused speech they know. The same title is given to the Qurʾān in 5:15, 12:1, 26:2, 27:1, 28:2, and again in 44:2, the sūrah that immediately follows this one and opens with the identical words.

**Mubīn as Active: The Book That Makes Things Clear**

The grammarians noticed that mubīn is the active participle of the fourth-form verb abāna, and that this verb can be transitive as well as intransitive. Intransitively, abāna means to be clear; transitively, it means to make something clear. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī both allow the second sense here. On that reading the Book is not only clear in itself but a clarifier: it separates guidance from error, truth from falsehood, lawful from forbidden. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Jalālayn add that it makes clear what a person needs in order to practise the religion properly. The two senses do not compete. A text that is itself plain is the kind of text that can make other things plain, and a text that claims to sort truth from error must be readable if the claim is to mean anything. The transitive reading is worth preferring as the fuller one, because it explains why the oath is chosen for this sūrah in particular. Al-Zukhruf is a chapter about false measures of truth: ancestry, wealth, custom, the number of one's followers. Swearing by the clarifier at the outset announces that a better measure is available.

**Al-Ṭabarī: Clarity as a Sign of Origin**

Al-Ṭabarī reads the oath as an argument about origin. The Book is clear, and that clarity is evidence that it was sent down from God rather than composed by a human being. His reasoning is that the Qurʾān's clarity extends to matters no one in Makkah could have known or explained: the histories of earlier prophets, the fate of past nations, the nature of the Hereafter. Speech that is plain about what its human reciter had no way of knowing points beyond him. There is a further line of reflection that some exegetes bring in from other verses. The Qurʾān speaks of a clear Book in which everything in heaven and earth is recorded (10:61, 27:75, 34:3). A few have wondered whether the oath here is by that heavenly register rather than by the recited Qurʾān. The next verse, which speaks of it being made an Arabic Qurʾān, settles the matter: the thing sworn by is the text the Makkans were hearing. But the overlap in wording is not accidental. The recited Book and the register of all things share the name because they share the source.

**Why Swear at All to People Who Reject the Speaker?**

An objection could be raised. If the Quraysh did not believe the Qurʾān was from God, what force could an oath sworn by that same Qurʾān have on them? The answer lies in what oaths do in Arabic rhetoric. An oath is not a proof; it is a mark of seriousness and a summons to attention. When a speaker swears by something, he tells his hearers what he holds sacred and asks them to weigh his next words with that in mind. God swearing by the Book tells the audience where authority lies before the sūrah begins to dismantle the authorities they rely on. It also does something for the believing listener that it cannot do for the rejecting one. To a Muslim reciting this verse, the oath is a reminder that the text in his hands is the very thing God chose to swear by, and that it deserves to be read with the care such an object demands. The verse therefore speaks to two audiences at once, and it is complete without them agreeing: the challenge to one is the honour of the other.
