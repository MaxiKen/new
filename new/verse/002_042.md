## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:42

> Do not mix truth with falsehood or hide the truth knowingly.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Hands of the Same Wrong**

“Do not mix truth with falsehood or hide the truth knowingly” (2:42). Wa lā talbisū al-ḥaqqa bil-bāṭil wa taktumū al-ḥaqqa wa antum taʿlamūn. Two prohibitions, one ethic: do not confound, do not conceal. To mix (labisa) is to clothe, to obscure — the same root noted in the source as “to dress” truth in falsehood until it cannot be seen. To conceal (katama) is to tuck truth out of sight while knowing it. One soils clarity; the other withholds it. Both make guidance unrecognizable when it arrives “from Me” (2:38) as confirmation of what you have (2:41).

The source links labisa to sister uses — “why do you clothe truth with falsehood?” (3:71), “they obscured…” (6:65; 6:82; 6:137) — where clothing and confounding overlap. The image helps: falsehood is not stitched beside truth but over it, like a garment that hides shape. Mixing does not merely juxtapose two claims; it makes the true claim look entangled and thus ignorable.

**Mixing — How Truth Is Made Unrecognizable**

Mixing happens in degrees. At the low end, it is slanted phrasing that wraps a true statement in a false insinuation until the hearer cannot tell which is which. At the middle, it is citation without context, where true verses are clothed with false occasions. At the high end, it is the production of doctrine that formally affirms “we believe the Book” while the gloss cancels what the Book says about the confirming revelation. In each degree, the teller may keep words of truth present while their force is nullified by admixture.

The verse isolates knowledge as condition: “while you know” (2:42). This is not error from ignorance but adulteration with awareness. The same clause sharpens the previous warning not to sell signs for a paltry price (2:41): the merchant who mixes knows what he is diluting, and knows the buyer’s need for clarity.

**Concealing — Withholding What Would Oblige**

“Or hide the truth knowingly” (2:42) names the quieter wrong. Truth may be unmixed yet unspoken: a foretelling in Torah and Gospel not mentioned (7:157), a clear description not taught, a witness not borne though one recites (2:44). Concealment can be total — silence when speech is due — or selective — teaching what flatters, omitting what obligates. The sūrah soon illustrates selective religion in the call to “establish prayer, pay alms, bow with those who bow” (2:43) after addressing knowers who preached piety but forgot themselves (2:44).

**Why the Pair Matters for Confirmation**

The Qur’an’s claim to confirm what is with you (2:41) makes mixing and hiding particularly damning. If the confirming revelation coheres, then to obscure coherence is to block recognition that would otherwise be natural. The verse therefore sits as gatekeeping for guidance: “then when guidance comes from Me, whoever follows it…” (2:38) assumes guidance can be recognized. Labis and katm aim precisely at recognizability. To mix is to blur the face; to hide is to dim the lamp.

**From Marketplace to Classroom**

The progression 2:41 → 2:42 is from marketplace to classroom: do not sell (commerce), do not confound (thought), do not conceal (teaching). The price tempts mixing; mixing justifies concealment; concealment secures the price. The cycle breaks only by knowledge owned and confessed: “while you know” must become “while you show what you know.”

**Living the Verse**

- Audit speech for garments. Before publishing or teaching, ask: have I clothed a true claim in a false frame that will hide it? If so, strip the frame even at cost.
- Audit silence for hiding. Before withholding, ask: is this truth owed to this hearer at this time, and do I know it? If so, withholding is katm, not prudence.
- Separate correction from exposure. The verse forbids mixing truth with falsehood; it does not forbid distinguishing them with clarity, even firmly. Confusion is not kindness; clarity, when mercifully given, is.

**What the Verse Does Not Forbid**

It does not forbid analogy, summary, or arrangement that clarifies. Not every combination is mixing; to explain truth with illustration is not to clothe it with falsehood. The criterion is effect with knowledge: does the admixture make truth unrecognizable to one who needs to recognize it, and is that effect known to the speaker? If yes, it is what the verse forbids, however smooth the prose.

Lexical notes on labisa as “to clothe/confound” are sound Arabic observations widely cited (see 3:71; 6:82); reports about specific instances of Jewish leaders hiding descriptions should be cited as tafsīr historical illustration, UNVERIFIED as prophetic statements unless a Sahih chain is given. What is firm is the double prohibition — do not mix, do not hide while knowing — as the guard for recognition of confirming revelation (2:41–42).
