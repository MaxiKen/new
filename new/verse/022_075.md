## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:75

> Allah selects messengers from both angels and people, for Allah is truly All-Hearing, All-Seeing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb Is "Chooses"**

*Allāhu yaṣṭafī mina al-malāʾikati rusulan wa-mina al-nāsi* — God chooses messengers from the angels and from the people.

*Yaṣṭafī* is from the root meaning to select, to single out, to purify by choosing. It is the verb used for picking the best part of something, and it implies both a chooser with the right to choose and a selection made on the basis of what the chooser knows.

The emphatic claim here is about who does the selecting. The audience's objection, recorded throughout the Qur'an, was not that God sends messengers but that He sends *this* one — a man who eats, walks in the market and has no angel with him. The verse's answer is to assert the right of selection before addressing its form.

**From Both Categories**

The verse names two pools: angels and human beings.

The inclusion of angels is what makes the sentence work as an answer. Nobody in the audience doubted that God could send an angel; the whole objection was that He had not. By naming both categories in one breath, the verse makes the point that the choice between them is His, and that the existence of one category does not settle the question of who should be sent from it.

Al-Ṭabarī took the verse as a straightforward statement that God sends both — Gabriel and Michael among the angels, and men among humankind. Al-Qurṭubī read it as a reaffirmation that neither kind of sending is novel, which is the sense the sūrah needs, since the objection being answered was precisely that this sending was unprecedentedly lowly.

**Why a Human Messenger Is Fitting**

The sūrahs that answer this objection directly give two reasons, and both are worth having.

The first is that an angel would settle the matter by force of appearance. *“They say, ‘Why has no ˹visible˺ angel come with him?’ Had We sent down an angel, the matter would have certainly been settled ˹at once˺, and they would have never been given more time ˹to repent˺”* (6:8). Faith extracted by a visible angel is not faith, and the testing period would be over.

The second is that an angel would have to appear as a man anyway: *“And if We had sent an angel, We would have certainly made it ˹assume the form of˺ a man—leaving them more confused than they are”* (6:9). Sūrah al-Isrāʾ supplies the principle that governs the whole question: *“Had there been angels walking the earth, well settled, We would have surely sent down for them an angel from heaven as a messenger”* (17:95).

The logic is that a messenger must be able to be an example to the people he is sent to, and an example must be imitable. A being of another order cannot show a human being how to be a human being.

**The Objection's Own Incoherence**

The Qur'an records the objection in its own words, and the form it takes is worth noting: *“What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in marketplaces for a living?”* (25:7).

The taunt concedes the argument. A messenger who did not eat and did not work would be precisely the un- imitable being the objectors imagine wanting. The eating and the walking in the market are the qualifications, and the verse treats them as the evidence of fitness rather than as disqualifications.

**The Selection Is Not a Compromise**

One more point is worth drawing out, because it is the sting in the objection the verse answers.

The demand for an angelic messenger was not a request for more evidence. It was a claim that a human messenger is a second-best arrangement — that God, had He been able, would have done better.

The verse's grammar refuses that. God *chooses*, and choosing implies knowledge and freedom rather than constraint. To say that He would have sent an angel if He could is to say that the sending of a man was the best available option rather than the selected one, which is precisely the mismeasurement verse 74 described.

That is why the sūrah answers the objection the way it does — not by arguing that human messengers work, but by asserting the right of the One who sends them.

**Hearing and Seeing Close the Argument**

*Inna Allāha samīʿun baṣīr* — God is Hearing, Seeing.

The same pair closed verse 61, where the subject was a person wronged twice. Here the subject is the selection of messengers, and the attributes do different work.

*Samīʿ* covers what is said about the messengers — the mockery, the charge of sorcery, the taunts about eating and walking in markets. None of it is unheard. *Baṣīr* covers the selection itself: God sees whom He is choosing, and the choice is not made in the dark.

There is a quiet reassurance in the pairing as well. A messenger who is being mocked is told that the One who sent him hears what is said and sees what is done, which is the same assurance the sūrah gave the believers at verse 60.

**The Verse's Connection to Verse 74**

Read the two verses together and they form one argument. Verse 74 said that these people had not measured God correctly; verse 75 names one of the specific ways the mismeasurement shows itself.

To object to God's choice of messenger is to claim a better judgement about who should be sent than the One sending. That is what an underestimation looks like in practice, and the sūrah names it immediately after diagnosing it.

The two verses also close a loop that opened early in the chapter. At verse 31 the sūrah described the associator as a man who has fallen from the sky — a picture of helplessness rather than rebellion. What has been true of the objectors throughout is not that they aimed too high but that they aimed too low, both about God and about the people He sends.
