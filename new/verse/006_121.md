## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:121

> Do not eat of what is not slaughtered in Allah’s Name. For that would certainly be ˹an act of˺ disobedience. Surely the devils whisper to their ˹human˺ associates to argue with you. If you were to obey them, then you ˹too˺ would be polytheists.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prohibition Stated in the Negative**

Do not eat of what is not slaughtered in Allah's Name. The verse states the prohibition that corresponds to the command given a few verses earlier, and the Book uses the same form in the sūrah of the Table, where the list of forbidden foods includes what has been dedicated to other than God. The audience's herds produced both kinds of meat, and the rule tells the community which of them belongs to the food of the believers.

The commentators connected the rule to the practice of the Makkans, who ate animals that had died of themselves and animals over which the names of their gods had been invoked. The verse's prohibition covers both, and the reason given in the same sentence is that such food is disobedience.

**The Meaning of the Charge of Disobedience**

For that would certainly be an act of disobedience. The Book uses the same word for the person who leaves the bounds set by God, and the charge here attaches the eating to a departure from what has been commanded rather than to a defect in the food as a substance. The sūrah has been treating the audience's dietary rules as a system of invented restrictions, and this verse states that the eating they permitted was itself an offence.

The Book's usage attaches the term to the person who oversteps in a matter where God has given a rule, and the eating of what has not been consecrated is described in those terms because the Name was withheld deliberately. Where the invocation was not made, the person who killed the animal had chosen to omit it, and the food is the sign of that choice.

**The Whisperings of the Devils**

Surely the devils whisper to their associates to argue with you. The verse names the source of the arguments the community was facing, and the sūrah has described the same activity a few verses earlier, when it said that God made for every prophet enemies among devilish humans and jinn who whisper to one another with elegant words of deception (6:112). The whisperings in the two passages are the same activity directed at the same object.

The Book's account of the devils' associates appears elsewhere in its descriptions of the people who follow the promptings against the message. The sūrah of the Ornament describes God's placing a devil as a companion to the person who turns away from the Reminder of the Most Compassionate (43:36), and the sūrah of the Poets records the Book's denial that the devils brought the revelation down and its description of the ones on whom they actually descend (26:210; 26:221). The argument that the audience raised about food is presented here as something the devils inspired, and the association gives the dispute its place in the sūrah's larger account of how denial spreads.

**The Consequence of Obeying Them**

If you were to obey them, then you too would be polytheists. The sentence states the outcome of accepting the argument, and the identification of the outcome is exact. The audience's position was that the invocation of the names of their gods over an animal made its meat lawful, which is to say that beings other than God had a share in the authority over what may be eaten. To accept the reasoning would be to grant those beings the authority the sūrah has denied them throughout.

The Book's logic here is the same as in its treatment of obedience to any authority that contradicts the revelation. The sūrah of the Troops says that the worship of God is the only alternative to following the false, and the sūrah of the Cattle earlier said that whoever gives up the worship of God and follows the idols has strayed from the level path. The sentence in this verse applies that principle to the dinner table.

**What the Verse Says About Authority Over Conduct**

The section that runs from the dietary command to this prohibition states a single principle in several forms: what a person eats is determined by God's ruling, and the ruling is carried by the Name invoked over the animal. The Book's treatment is not a matter of ritual purity for its own sake but a statement about whose authority governs conduct. The invocation of the Name and the refusal to invoke anything else are the visible form of that statement.

That is why the verse ends with the identification of the alternative as polytheism. The argument the audience pressed was not an argument about hygiene or taste; it was a claim about who has the right to declare food lawful, and the sūrah treats the claim as an act of association with God.

**The Place of the Verse in the Section**

The passage that follows this verse turns to the details of the audience's system: the livestock they dedicated, the animals they set apart, the rules about who might eat what, and the shares assigned to God and to the idols (6:136–139; 6:143–144). The verse in front of us states the principle by which all of that is judged, and the sections that follow apply it.

The sūrah thus completes the first stage of its treatment of food. The believers are told what they may eat, why they may eat it, what is forbidden, and that the arguments offered against the rule come from the whisperings of the devils and their associates. The rest of the chapter takes the audience's own rules one by one and leaves the reader to measure them against the standard just supplied.
