## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:124

> ˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you said to the believers, “Is it not enough that your Lord will send down a reinforcement of three thousand angels for your aid?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Words Spoken to Steady a Community**

The verse recalls the Prophet's address to the believers, in which he asked them whether it was not enough that their Lord would send down three thousand angels to their aid. The question is put to people who were facing a force larger than their own.

The form of the sentence is a reassurance rather than an announcement. The Prophet is not reporting intelligence about the enemy; he is reminding his community of the resources that belong to the Lord they serve. The verse's recollection of the words places the community's encouragement inside the revelation, so that the promise is preserved along with the events it accompanied.

**Angels in the Accounts of Badr**

The Book's other account of angelic support at Badr states that the believers cried out to their Lord for help and were answered with the promise of a thousand angels sent in succession (8:9). The promise in the verse before the reader is of three thousand, and the difference belongs to the different occasions of the two passages.

The two accounts together show how the Book treats such aid. The numbers are stated, the condition of the community is described, and the outcome is attributed to God in the sentence that follows each promise. The assisted party remains a party of human beings who must do what is required of them, and the aid is described as a means of strengthening their hearts.

**The Form of the Question**

The question begins with the words "is it not enough", and the Book uses this construction when it addresses a doubt that has not been spoken. The community had been told the size of the force it must face, and the question supplies the answer to the unspoken fear.

The rhetorical question is a gentler form of instruction than a command. It does not accuse the believers of anything; it invites them to consider the adequacy of what has been promised, and the answer is meant to be supplied by the hearer himself. That the verse records the question rather than the answer suggests what the community's response was understood to be.

**Why Three Thousand**

The Book does not explain the number, and its significance lies in the contrast with the community's own strength. A force of three hundred and thirteen at Badr was told, in the sūrah's other account, of a thousand angels; here a promise of three thousand is given to a community that had recently been through a defeat.

The escalation matches the situation. The number is not presented as a calculation of what military assistance a battle requires; it is presented as evidence of the generosity of the one who gives it. The Book's descriptions of divine aid are consistently stated in this register, where the magnitude of the promised help answers the smallness of the community that receives it.

**What the Heavenly Aid Means in the Book's Framework**

The Book's mention of angels in the context of a battle has to be read with its own statements about the source of victory, which it states in the verses immediately following. The angels are described as sent for the sake of the believers' hearts, and the outcome is credited to God alone.

That is how the passage's language of aid works throughout. The promise of reinforcement tells the community that the resources of its Lord are not limited; the insistence that victory comes from God alone tells it not to locate its confidence in any particular means. Both statements are needed, and the sūrah provides both within three verses.

**The Prophet's Task of Encouragement**

The verse shows the Prophet performing a task that the Book assigns to leaders in difficult situations: keeping the community's resolve intact while the danger is stated openly. The sūrah's other passages contain the same function, where the Prophet is told to consult his companions and to encourage them.

What the verse records is therefore part of a pattern that the community was meant to learn from. The leader of a community at war does not conceal the odds and does not leave the people to face them without the reminder of what is promised to those who hold firm. The words in this verse are the model of that combination.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The verse stands after the reminder of Badr and before the promise of a larger reinforcement that is made conditional on the community's conduct. The three verses together form a single movement: the memory of a victory, the assurance of support, and the stipulation that the support accompanies endurance and awareness of God.

The community is therefore given the encouragement and its terms in the same breath. The reader who takes the promise without the condition has read a third of the passage, and the sūrah's habit is to state both so that the promise produces effort rather than complacency. The verses that follow draw the purpose of the whole arrangement together.

**The Words of Encouragement Preserved as Revelation**

The verse records a speech of the Prophet to his community, and the revelation preserves it as part of the Book that the community recites. That is not incidental to how the passage works.

A promise made in a moment of danger would otherwise be available only through the memory of those who heard it. By including the words in the sūrah, the Book makes the encouragement available to every later generation of readers, who face their own versions of the same anxiety. The verse also shows the Prophet's method in a crisis: he told the people what their Lord had undertaken to do, and he left the conduct of the battle to them.

**Where the Promise Belongs**

Al-Rāzī set out the disagreement over whether these words were spoken to the community in connection with the battle of Badr or the battle of Uḥud. If the promise belongs to Badr, the sentence joins the passage that recalls the community's weakness before the fighting and the help that followed it; if it belongs to Uḥud, the account runs on from the verse that describes the Prophet leaving his household on the morning of the battle. The commentators were equally divided over how the help of the angels was to be understood. Most held that the angels fought at Badr, where the Book's own account describes the rain, the sleep that came over the believers, and the hosts that were not seen (8:9–12). Some reports describe the participation in physical terms, with strange deaths in the enemy ranks and unknown fighters on the field. Others read the help as the strengthening of the believers' hearts, the sharpening of their senses, and the courage their belief in the promise gave them, which is how al-Rāzī treated the question.

**The Number and the Marks**

The promise of five thousand angels in the next verse carries a phrase that means the help would arrive at once, without delay. Al-Quṭūbī and al-Ṭabarī record the question whether the promise was fulfilled, since it was tied in the previous sentence to the community's firmness and its awareness of God, and some held that the conditions were not met and the reinforcement was never sent. The word describing the angels as bearing marks was read in two ways: as a statement about the angels' own appearance, and as a description of the sign they carried or the horses they rode, since the same root is used of an animal left to graze or taken into battle. The discussion shows how carefully the commentators treated the details of the promise, and it leaves the reader with the condition the verse had attached to it rather than with an account of what appeared in the sky.
