## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:6

> He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shaping in the Dark**

The verb is *yuṣawwiru*: He gives form, He fashions. The place is the hiding place: the wombs of mothers, where nothing human can see and where nothing human is doing the work. The clause is a claim about both capability and intimacy, and it is stated with the ordinary vocabulary of the workshop.

Other passages open the process up. The Qur'an says human beings are created *“from a single soul”* and that God *“creates you in the wombs of your mothers ˹in stages˺, one development after another, in three layers of darkness”* (39:6). At 22:5 the sequence is described in detail — dust, a drop, a clinging clot, a lump of flesh, formed or unformed — and the purpose is given explicitly: to demonstrate divine power. A related verse about formation uses the same imagery for the whole species: *“Who created you, fashioned you, and perfected your design, moulding you in whatever form He willed?”* (82:7–8), and 40:64 praises the same act: *“He shaped you ˹in the womb˺, perfecting your form.”*

Each of these verses treats the shaping as evidence rather than information. The making of a person is not offered to explain biology; it is offered as a reason to worship the maker.

**"As He Wills" — Order With No Template**

The phrase that closes the first sentence is the one worth the longest look. God shapes *as He wills*, and the result is not a uniform product. Two children of the same parents differ; every fingerprint in the record differs; every temperament differs. The formula holds variation and intention together in a way no mechanical account can.

The wording also blocks a conclusion that the reader might otherwise reach. If formation were described as a process with a fixed output, it could be credited to the process itself. The Qur'an attributes it to a will at work in each case, and then immediately names the will: *there is no god except Him*. The verse does not argue that a designer exists; it asserts His action and then names Him, which is the Qur'an's normal method for establishing identity rather than existence.

**The Shaping of Jesus as the Sūrah's Silent Case**

This sūrah will put a man who entered a womb and left it by way of a mother at the centre of a dispute about his nature. A reader who approaches that dispute carries the question this verse answers: could someone formed in a womb be God? The Book answers elsewhere in plain words: *“The Messiah, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger. ˹Many˺ messengers had ˹come and˺ gone before him. His mother was a woman of truth. They both ate food.”* (5:75).

The argument's logic is the logic of the verse: whoever is shaped is not the shaper, and whoever requires a hiding place does not hold the universe. The commentators noticed this quietly when they read the verse as an implicit answer to divinizing a man who was once an embryo. The verse does not mention him, and it does not need to; the principle covers the case before the case appears.

There is a second edge to the same principle, and the sūrah will use it. A community that claims privileged descent from a chosen ancestor is claiming something about formation — that ancestry can settle a person's standing before God. The verse answers that the shaping that produced the ancestor and the shaping that produced his critics were the same act of will, exercised in the same hidden place, with no lineage consulted.

**Power and Wisdom Named Together**

The verse ends with two names, *al-ʿAzīz* and *al-Ḥakīm*: Almighty and All-Wise. They are the pair that answers each half of the sentence just read. Power accounts for the fact that shaping happens at all; wisdom accounts for the fact that it happens "as He wills", which is to say according to something better than chance.

The Qur'an pairs them often, in verses about revelation, provision, creation, and judgment. The pairing is a warning against reading power alone, which produces dread of a force, and against reading wisdom alone, which produces comfort with a plan nobody can trust to be enforced. A universe shaped by a will that is both capable and wise is a place where an obscure life has a reason and a defence.

**What the Verse Asks the Reader to Notice**

The verse does not ask anyone to admire an argument. It asks him to look at his own body and notice that its particularity came from a decision made in darkness, before he had a say, and was carried out without his help. The doctrine of God begins, here, with a fact about the reader that he cannot dispute and did not arrange.

The two sentences are welded for that reason. Power produced him; wisdom placed him; and neither belongs to him. The rest of the sūrah will hold its disputants to this starting point — including the ones who think that their descent or their books settle who they are before God.

Take the claim away and the sūrah's later rebukes lose their footing; keep it, and the whole address gains its shape. A person is not being asked to accept a story about a distant event in a tent or a palace. He is being asked to accept the implications of a fact he lives inside: he was made somewhere he could not see, by a will he did not consult, and he has been carried ever since.

That is a low claim to build a doctrine on, and it is intended to be. The Qur'an habitually starts with the nearest evidence — the body, the rain, the leaf — before it argues about anything disputed. The womb is the nearest of all, and the verse that names it is the shortest sentence in this stretch of the sūrah.
