## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:47

> Mary wondered, “My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?” An angel replied, “So will it be. Allah creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mother's Question**

The announcement has been given, and Mary answers it with a question about how it can happen. The fuller account in the sūrah of Maryam records the objection in her own words, including her insistence that no man has touched her and that she is not unchaste (19:20).

The phrasing she uses is important for the way the Book treats her. She is not asking whether God can do it; she is asking how it is to be done, and she clears her own reputation in the same breath by ruling out the two possibilities that a listener would assume. The question belongs to the same family as Zachariah's, asked a few verses earlier by a man who had also been told something impossible, and it is answered in much the same terms.

**"So Will It Be" — the Same Answer Given Twice**

The reply begins with the same phrase used to Zachariah: so will it be. The Book uses one formula for two annunciations that have almost nothing else in common — an old man with a barren wife, and a young woman with no husband — because the point in both cases lies in the one who is speaking rather than in the circumstances of the recipients.

The repetition is not laziness; it is the sūrah's method of teaching a principle through a pair of cases. A reader who has followed the narrative from verse 35 to this point has now heard the same sentence applied to the limits of age and to the limits of nature, and each answer is followed by a statement about God's will. What the two cases establish together is that the ordinary order is a habit rather than a boundary.

**"Allah Creates What He Wills"**

The clause that follows the agreement is a statement about creation, not about reproduction. The Book does not say that God will use an unusual means; it locates the event in the category of things God creates because He wills them.

The parallel account in the sūrah of Maryam carries the same reply with an addition: the matter is already decreed (19:21). Elsewhere the Book states the principle without a birth attached to it, so that the reader meets it as a doctrine rather than as an exception. What is being taught is a view of creation in which the divine will is a sufficient cause, and the annunciation becomes one illustration of a rule that governs everything that exists.

**The Command "Be" and What It Means for a Creature**

The verse then supplies the mechanism, and the mechanism is a word. When God decrees a matter, He tells it to be, and it is. The Book uses the formula for creation generally: the Originator of the heavens and the earth says to a thing *Be*, and it is (2:117); the same words describe His action in the sūrah of the Bees, of the Bee's Lord who says to what He wills to exist, *Be*, and it is (16:40).

The sūrah of Maryam uses the formula in a passage that forbids the very conclusion the annunciation might invite: it is not for God to take a son, and when He decrees a matter He tells it to be (19:35). So the formula is a statement about power, and at the same time a statement about the distance between the one who commands and the thing that comes to be at the command. What is created does not become the creator by having been created directly.

**Placed Beside Adam**

The sūrah states the comparison outright a few verses later, where the example of Jesus before God is given as the example of Adam: created from dust, told to be, and he was (3:59). The pairing settles the question of what a miracle of origin proves.

Adam had no parents at all; Jesus had a mother and no father; the rest of humanity has both. If the absence of a father were a claim to divinity, the absence of both parents would be a stronger one, and the Book's handling of Adam makes clear that being created directly is a statement about God's power rather than about the creature's status. The same formula covers both cases, and both men are servants.

**Why the Answer Refuses to Explain**

A reader looking for an account of how the birth took place will not find one in this verse, and the Book's silence is consistent. What is supplied instead is a statement about the divine will and a command.

The refusal is theological rather than evasive. Once the event has been located in the creative word, a detailed mechanism would add nothing to the reader's obligation and would invite speculation in which the miracle is either explained away or made into a claim about the child's nature. The Book gives the fact, the cause, and the conclusion, and it leaves the mystery intact where the mystery is the point.

**What the Birth Signifies in the Sūrah's Argument**

The child is later described in this same conversation as a sign for humanity and a mercy from God (19:21). That description is the frame the sūrah wants for the whole episode: the birth is a mark of God's power and a gift, and it points away from itself to the one who decreed it.

Holding the sign in that position is what allows the sūrah to insist on what follows, that the child is a servant and a prophet. A sign that is itself a mercy, given to a woman who asked how it could happen, cannot be the deity it points to; and the Book, having told the story, goes on to name what the child did with the life he was given.
