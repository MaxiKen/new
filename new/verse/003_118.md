## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:118

> O believers! Do not associate closely with others who would not miss a chance to harm you. Their only desire is to see you suffer. Their prejudice has become evident from what they say—and what their hearts hide is far worse. We have made Our revelations clear to you, if only you understood.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Behind Intimacy**

The verse forbids the believers to take certain people as intimates, and the word it uses for that relationship is the one for the inner lining of a garment. It describes confidants, the people to whom a person tells what he does not tell others, and the metaphor pictures them as the layer of cloth closest to the body and hidden from view.

The choice of word tells the reader what is being regulated. The verse is not prohibiting dealings, speech, or neighbourliness; it is addressing the matter of trust, and it addresses the kind of closeness that exposes a person's inner affairs to someone else. The prohibition is therefore about the disposal of confidences rather than about the avoidance of a community.

**The Advice the Commentators Took From It**

The classical commentaries read the verse as a caution about relying on outsiders in matters where a disclosure could harm the community. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭūbī mention contracts, correspondence, and the conduct of important affairs, and al-Qurṭūbī adds a complaint from his own lifetime about the employment of Jews and Christians as scribes and secretaries.

The historical remark is a report about the society of that author rather than a rule of the verse. What the commentaries preserve is the practical sense of the instruction: a community at odds with a group should not place the details of its own affairs in the hands of that group, since the verse's own reason is that such people wish the community harm.

**The Limits of the Instruction**

The verse names a particular kind of relationship, and the Book's wider instructions show what it does not forbid. The same sūrah has just described upright people among the People of the Book and has promised that their good will not be lost, and it commands justice and kind treatment toward those who do not fight or drive people from their homes.

Read within the Book, the verse is a rule about the protection of the community rather than a permission for hostility. What it forbids is the extension of inner trust to people whose attitude toward the believers has been described in the preceding verse, and what it leaves in place is everything else that relations between people ordinarily consist of.

**"They Would Not Spare You Any Corruption"**

The second sentence of the verse states that such people will not miss a chance to harm, and their wish, the verse says, is that the believers should suffer. The wording describes a disposition rather than an incident.

The Book attaches the same disposition to the hypocrites elsewhere, whose speech changes with their company: they tell the believers that they believe, and when they are alone with their own people they say that they were only mocking (2:14). The verse before the reader assumes that condition as the reason for its instruction and describes what it produces when the opportunity arises.

**Hatred That Has Reached the Tongue**

The verse says that hatred has appeared from their mouths while their hearts conceal more. Speech is treated as the visible edge of something larger, and the sentence measures the depth of the hidden part by what has already escaped.

The Book uses the same approach in the sūrah of the Table, where it describes people who say that they believe with their tongues while their hearts are in disbelief (5:41). The point in both places is that a person's public statements are the least of what he carries, and the instruction in this verse is drawn from that fact: the community is being told what to expect from what it can already hear.

**What the Hearts Conceal**

The clause about the hidden part of the hostility links this verse with the one that follows, where the same theme appears in the description of what people say when they are alone. The sūrah returns to the knowledge of what hearts contain whenever it wants to close such a description.

The statement that God knows what is hidden occurs at the end of several verses in this passage, and it functions as the answer to a community that cannot see what is being planned against it. What the believers cannot penetrate is not hidden from God, and the instruction about intimacy is given in the knowledge that the calculation being concealed is not concealed from the one who settles accounts.

**"If Only You Understood"**

The verse ends with a call to understanding, and the phrase is the Book's way of saying that the instruction is obvious once its grounds are considered. The sūrah uses the formula in several places where a plain inference is available and has been missed.

Here the inference concerns the mismatch between what the community was doing and what it was dealing with. It was extending intimacy to people whose intentions had been made plain, and the verse names that mismatch as a failure to understand the situation rather than as a moral lapse in the abstract. The reading is confirmed by the verses that follow, which continue to describe how such people react to the community's fortunes.

**The Identification of the People Described**

Commentators differed about whether the people in view were the hypocrites of Madinah or a faction among the People of the Book, and both readings are supported by the passage's context.

The identification does not change the instruction. In either case the verse deals with people who are inside the community's reach, whose hostility is partly hidden and partly visible, and whose closeness to the believers' affairs is a risk. That is why the verse is addressed to the believers rather than to the people it describes, and why it speaks about trust rather than about enmity.
