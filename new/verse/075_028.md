## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:28

> And the dying person realizes it is ˹their˺ time to depart,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Certainty Arrives at Last**

The verb is the strongest available in Arabic for knowing: he became certain, with the settled knowledge that excludes doubt. After a sūrah full of people who mocked, supposed, reckoned, and asked when, the dying man arrives at *yaqīn*. The irony is complete and it is not gentle. Certainty was the one thing that would have changed everything, and it is granted at the exact moment when it can no longer be spent. The sūrah's whole diagnosis was that the obstacle lay in the will rather than in the evidence; here the evidence becomes unanswerable, and the will is given nothing left to do with it.

**What He Is Certain About**

The object of the certainty is *al-firāq*, the parting — the separation of what has been joined. The commentators took this in two directions, and both are true at once. The dying person knows that he is parting from the world: from the property he assembled, the people he loved, the body he lived in, and the version of himself that existed only among others. He also knows that the parting is the separation of soul from body, which the previous verse located at the collarbones. The word holds the two senses together without strain, and that is part of why the Qur'an chose it. Death is one event described from two sides, and the person undergoing it is the only one who experiences both. What makes the parting bitter is also stated plainly: the things being left are the things that were made to look desirable — *“The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people.”* (3:14). A person does not part easily from what he has spent his life being attracted to, and the Qur'an's remedy is not indifference to those things but the knowledge that the finest destination lies elsewhere.

**The Knowledge That Comes Too Late**

The Qur'an is unsparing about the timing, and the point is repeated often enough to be a principle. A person at the threshold knows, asks to be returned, promises to act differently, and is not returned: *“If only you could see when they will be detained before the Fire! They will cry, ‘Oh! If only we could be sent back, we would never deny the signs of our Lord.”* (6:27). The knowledge is real and the desire is real, and neither is of any use, because the thing that made them useful — a span of time in which to act — is precisely what has ended. The Qur'an's practical conclusion is obvious and it is stated elsewhere with equal plainness: whatever certainty a person is waiting for, he should get it while it can still be lived out.

**The Will and the Intellect Finally Agree**

The sūrah traced unbelief in v. 5 to what a person wants, and it has spent twenty-three verses describing the consequences of that wanting. At the deathbed, wanting and knowing coincide for the first time — the man no longer wants to deny, and he no longer can. That convergence is worth noticing, because it shows that the sūrah was never describing an intellectual failure that more evidence could have cured. Evidence arrives here in overwhelming quantity, and what it produces is regret rather than conversion. Faith, in the Qur'an's account, is a thing a person does with the interval he has been given, not a conclusion he reaches when the interval closes. The request made at that moment is always for a little more of it: *“My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.”* (63:10), and the answer is that no soul is delayed once its appointed time arrives (63:11). The man in this verse is not asking for more time because he has ceased to believe. He is asking because he believes perfectly well, and has discovered that belief without a remaining interval is not worth having.

**Clarity at the Threshold**

The scene also matches what the Qur'an says about the removal of the veil. *“You were totally heedless of this. Now We have lifted this veil of yours, so Today your sight is sharp!”* (50:22). The dying person's perception is described as sharpened rather than as newly informed, which means the clarity is not an addition of data but the removal of the interests that had been blurring it. Reports in the tafsīr literature describe the dying seeing the angels who have come for them, and these are cited in that literature rather than as authenticated narrations. What is safe to say from the Qur'an itself is that the moment is one of unveiled sight, and that the sight is not given as a gift. It is the absence of everything that had been blocking it.

**The Same Certainty, the Other Face**

Certainty at death is not always a terror, and the Qur'an gives the counterpart so that the reader does not take this verse as a statement about dying as such. To the tranquil soul the address runs: *“Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺.”* (89:28). And of those whom the angels take in a good state: *“Peace be upon you! Enter Paradise for what you used to do.”* (16:32). The same *yaqīn*, the same parting, the same threshold — and the content of the certainty is what a life has made it. The sūrah's next move is to follow the body from the bed to the grave and then to the driving, and it does so without pausing for comment.
