## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:168

> Lot responded, “I am truly one of those who despise your ˹shameful˺ practice.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lot Clarifies What He Rejects**

Lot responds to the threat by identifying himself among those who despise the practice. His words do not conceal his moral position to preserve his place in the community. He refuses to pretend that the conduct is acceptable when revelation has condemned it and when its public form is tied to wider corruption.

The object of his rejection is the practice, not a claim that every person involved has lost all human worth. That distinction matters. A messenger can condemn an act clearly while still wanting the people to repent. Lot's position is not indifference to the people; it is refusal to approve what is destroying them.


The word conveys strong moral revulsion. It should not be weakened into polite approval, because Lot is warning about a serious transgression. Yet moral revulsion must remain under God's law. It cannot become permission to insult, assault, expose, or deny basic justice to a person.

A believer may despise a sin and still hope for the sinner's return. This is the balance Lot embodies. He does not join the community's practice, but he also does not respond to its threat by inventing a private license for revenge. His anger is governed by revelation rather than by wounded pride.

**The Messenger Will Not Bargain Away Truth**

The people have offered Lot a choice between silence and expulsion. His reply shows that the threat has not made him uncertain about the command. He will not call shameful what God has forbidden merely to preserve social comfort.

This is an important test of prophetic integrity. A messenger who changes his moral judgment whenever the crowd threatens him is not trustworthy. Lot's firmness does not arise from confidence in his own strength. It arises from knowing that the Lord's approval matters more than the community's permission to remain.


Quran 29:28–30 describes Lot's rebuke of the men and also mentions their abuse of travelers and misconduct in their gatherings. That parallel helps explain why Lot's detestation reaches beyond a private preference. He is confronting a public culture in which sexual wrongdoing, aggression, and communal celebration of immorality reinforce one another.

The wider setting does not justify treating every individual as identical. It does show why a prophet must address the community's norms rather than only isolated acts. When a practice is protected by threats and imposed as a social ideal, silence would leave the vulnerable without a truthful defense.


Some people assume that compassion requires avoiding clear words. Lot shows otherwise. He speaks plainly because ambiguity would strengthen the community's self-deception. Compassion tells the truth about danger so that people can leave it; it does not rename the danger as harmless.

At the same time, clarity should not be used to enjoy another person's shame. Lot's words are directed toward correction and separation from wrongdoing. The believer must guard his own heart while speaking, because pride can imitate moral courage and turn hatred of sin into hatred of people.


The people threaten to expel him as if his objection were the offense. Lot refuses that inversion. He will not accept the idea that the person who warns is more blameworthy than the practice being warned against. His reply restores the proper order: wrongdoing is wrong even when a powerful group protects it.

This restoration is especially important where public language has been captured by the dominant culture. Words such as tolerance, freedom, or normality can be used to silence any appeal to revelation. Lot's response shows that a servant may respect people while rejecting the moral vocabulary used to defend disobedience.

**Detestation Must Be Joined to Personal Obedience**

Lot does not merely denounce the community while exempting himself from moral discipline. He stands among those who reject the practice, which means his position begins with his own obedience. A person cannot condemn an act in others while secretly cultivating the same disregard for God in his own life.

This is why prophetic warning has credibility. The messenger's disgust is not a performance to gain power. It is the public expression of a submission he practices himself. He does not ask the people to accept a burden that he refuses to carry.

**A Strong Reply to a Threat**

Lot's sentence is firm but not boastful. He does not claim that he can defeat the entire community by his own ability. He identifies where he stands and leaves the final judgment to God. Moral courage does not require theatrical anger; it requires steady refusal to call wrong right.

The believer can learn from that steadiness. When pressured to praise what God forbids, answer with truth without adding injustice. Do not bargain away conviction, and do not let the enemy's threat make you adopt the enemy's cruelty.


Lot despises the shameful practice, but the existence of a condemned act does not mean repentance is impossible. The prophetic call remains meaningful because people can stop, confess, and return. A society should not protect wrongdoing, yet it should not close the door of repentance for those who seek it.

That is the difference between moral opposition and dehumanization. Opposition says the act must end and the person must answer before God. Dehumanization says the person has no capacity for good and may be treated without justice. Lot's response supports the first and rejects the second.

His words also show that moral opposition can be sorrowful rather than triumphant. He does not relish being separated from his people or seek the humiliation of those he corrects. He detests the practice because it harms their relationship with God, and he continues to speak in the hope that the people will abandon it.
