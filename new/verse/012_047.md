## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:47

> Joseph replied, “You will plant ˹grain˺ for seven consecutive years, leaving in the ear whatever you will harvest, except for the little you will eat.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Opens With an Instruction**

Joseph does not begin by decoding the symbols. The first thing he says is what the country will be doing and what it should do with the result: you will sow for seven successive years, and what you reap you should leave standing in its ear. The interpretive work — cows are years, ears are harvests — is never spelled out, because it does not need to be. A reader holding verse 43 and verse 47 side by side performs the identification without assistance, and that is presumably the intention. The answer's form is that of an agricultural directive rather than an oracle, and the change of form is the change that matters: Egypt is not being told what its dream means so much as what its next fifteen years require.

**Seven Years Described as Successive**

The word attached to the seven years denotes continuation, one after another without interruption. The specification is not padding. An agricultural cycle with good years scattered through bad ones can be managed locally, by household storage and by trade between regions. Seven uninterrupted years of plenty followed by seven uninterrupted years of scarcity cannot. The consecutive character of the sequence is what makes central planning necessary, since no individual farmer and no single province can bridge that gap alone. Joseph's first contribution is therefore to identify the shape of the problem, and the shape is temporal rather than quantitative. Stress on sequence closes off the comforting readings available to a court. Were the years interchangeable, a bad harvest could be met from a good neighbour; because they run consecutively there is no neighbour to borrow from, and the entire famine must be provisioned out of the entire plenty. This is why the answer to the king's dream is a storage policy and not a prayer for rain.

**"Leave in the Ear" Is a Storage Technique**

The instruction is specific and agronomic. Grain kept in its ear and husk is protected from damp, from insects and from spoilage in ways that threshed grain is not, and it can be stored for long periods under ordinary conditions. The commentators take the phrase in exactly this sense: store the harvest as it comes off the field, rather than processing it immediately. That detail matters because it makes the plan executable by a society without specialised facilities. No new technology is required, no unusual construction, no imported expertise. What is required is a decision to withhold a large quantity of food from consumption and from threshing for seven years running, which is a decision only a government with real authority can enforce. The technique is also chosen for durability rather than for symbolism. Grain left standing on the stalk keeps longer and resists the damp and the pests that attack threshed stores, so the instruction fits an economy whose storage capacity was limited to what could be protected in bulk. It is the kind of detail that survives only in a plan meant to be carried out.

**"Except the Little You Eat" Imposes Rationing During Plenty**

The exception is the hardest part of the plan and Joseph states it in the same breath as the abundance. In seven years of exceptional fertility, the country is to eat little. The discipline is not a technicality. Every agricultural society facing surplus is subject to expansion of consumption in good years — more bread, more livestock, more feasting, more planting of luxury crops — and the expansion is precisely what destroys the margin needed for the bad years. The instruction therefore regulates appetite rather than logistics. The commentators note that the first phase is described as a time of great fertility, which makes the command to eat little counter-intuitive and, for that reason, the real test of the plan. Rationing during abundance demands an authority that scarcity never supplies by itself, because nothing visible justifies it at the time it is imposed. The plan therefore rests on belief in a projection nobody can yet see, and the chapter is candid that the discipline required is hardest precisely when it looks least necessary — in a year when the harvest is coming in fat.

**A Prediction and a Policy in One Sentence**

The verse contains a statement of fact and a statement of obligation, and does not separate them. You will sow for seven years is prediction; leave what you harvest in its ear is instruction. The two are joined because the prediction is only useful as the ground of the instruction. A ruler told that seven good years are coming receives news; a ruler told that seven good years are coming and that the harvest must be retained receives a policy. This is what distinguishes Joseph's answer from the one the court declined to give in verse 44 and from the kind of interpretation a diviner would supply. The knowledge is delivered in the form in which it can be acted on, and the acting is included in the delivery.

**The Plan Requires an Office, and the Chapter Knows It**

Nothing in this verse can be carried out by the person speaking it. A prisoner can describe a storage programme; he cannot enforce one across a country for seven years. The demand implied by the instruction will be met in verse 55, when Joseph asks to be placed over the storehouses of the land on the grounds that he is a reliable and skilled keeper. The sequence is worth noting because it reverses the usual order: the competence is demonstrated first, and the request for authority comes afterwards, grounded in the demonstration. The sūrah is describing a form of political legitimacy in which office follows from a plan that has been stated publicly and can be checked against events.

The plan also has a distributional consequence the verse leaves unstated. Retaining the harvest in the ear for seven years removes grain from ordinary markets, which raises prices in the very years when prices would otherwise be lowest. Egypt's farmers will therefore experience the programme as hardship during plenty and as rescue during scarcity, and only the second half of that experience will be visible to them at the time. Joseph's instruction asks a whole country to accept a cost it cannot yet justify, on the word of a prisoner. That is the political difficulty the following verses resolve, and it explains why the office requested later is not merely administrative but coercive.

**No Tax Is Imposed, and the Omission Is Notable**

The biblical account of the same consultation has Joseph propose that the crown take a fifth of the produce during the seven plentiful years and store it under royal authority (Genesis 41:34–36). The Qur'anic version contains no fraction, no levy and no reference to royal appropriation. What it prescribes is retention in the ear and restraint in eating, both of which fall on the producers themselves as much as on the state. Whether the difference reflects a different tradition or a deliberate choice cannot be settled from the text, but the effect is clear: the Qur'an's Joseph gives Egypt a plan for managing abundance rather than a plan for taxing it, and the authority he later asks for is over storehouses rather than over revenue.
