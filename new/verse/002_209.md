## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:209

> If you falter after receiving the clear proofs, then know that Allah is indeed Almighty, All-Wise. Waiting for Judgment?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Slip After the Proofs**

The verse states the community's danger-case in one conditional: if you slip — after the clear proofs have come to you. The verb zalla is precise: not the rebellion of the defiant but the slip of the foot on smooth ground, the stumble of one who was standing, the error of one who was upright. The sūrah's vocabulary keeps the distinction sharp — the disbeliever rejects, the hypocrite conceals, the transgressor exceeds; the one who zalla was on the path and left it by a loss of footing rather than a decision of enmity. And the timing is the clause's aggravator: after the clear proofs — the bayyināt — have come. Al-Ṭabarī lists what the proofs comprehensively denote: the Prophet himself, the Qur'an, Islam as the embodied way, or all of them together. The slip is therefore never for want of evidence; it happens inside full illumination, which is why the sūrah has spent two hundred verses supplying the proofs — the legislative detail, the portraits, the stories, the hajj just completed — and why this verse guards the whole apparatus with a warning: the clearest light does not make the footing automatic. The proofs have come; the slip remains possible; the verse exists because the possibility is real for the people the proofs have already convinced.

**Then Know That God Is Mighty and Wise**

The conditional's consequence is striking in what it is not: no immediate threat of punishment, no enumeration of penalties, but a command to know — then know that God is Mighty, Wise. The two names are the verse's whole response to the slip. ʿAzīz — the Almighty, the Prevailing, the One whose might cannot be resisted and whose decree cannot be outrun: the name tells the slipper that the defection has not placed him outside power, that the proofs' Sender remains the unchallengeable, and that a slip inside His reach is not an escape from His reach. Ḥakīm — the All-Wise, whose every act and ruling carries wisdom whether or not the wisdom is visible: the name tells him that the legislation he stumbled against — the complete entry commanded, the rites imposed, the limits drawn — is not arbitrary burden but ordered good, and that the slip was not a correction of the law but a failure to see its wisdom. The pairing is the verse's therapy and its warning in one breath: Mighty, so the slip cannot be held against Him; Wise, so the slip cannot be justified against His law. The knowledge commanded is knowledge of God in exactly the two attributes the slipping heart most needs — power it cannot flee and wisdom it cannot improve upon.

**The Verse Against Despair**

Read beside the spending-verse of the same passage, this conditional performs a specific pastoral work. The sūrah had already named one self-destruction as the sinner's counsel of doom — the reading of 2:195 recorded by the exegetes, in which a person who believes his sin unforgivable goes on sinning, surrendering to moral despair instead of repenting. The slip-clause here could feed exactly that despair: I was given the proofs and I stumbled, so my case is closed. The verse's answer is the knowledge-command: the slipper is directed not into self-assessment but into theology — know God's might and wisdom rather than measure your own ruin. The direction matters because the two names hold the door open from both sides: the Mighty is the One whose strength the sūrah has repeatedly made the refuge of the weak, and the Wise is the One whose wisdom includes the repentance-legislation the sūrah has spread everywhere — the Forgiving turned toward every returner (2:160), the Forgiving and Merciful named over the pilgrim's istighfār (2:199). The verse thus refuses the slipper both escapes: it does not soften the slip into insignificance (the proofs were clear; the fall was real; the Might is over you) and it does not harden it into finality (the Wisdom that legislated the proofs legislated the return). Know, it says — and the knowing is the standing-up.

**The Footing of the Community**

The address is plural, and the slip it warns of is communal as well as personal. The clear proofs came to a community — the Prophet among them, the Qur'an descending on them, the legislation arriving verse by verse into their actual cases — and the community can slip where individuals slip: the rite kept in form while its wisdom drains (the boast returning after the remembrance, the prayer of this world alone replacing the double-horizon petition), the complete entry of 2:208 decaying into the provincial practice, the diptych's eloquent impostor admired again after his pattern was published. The verse's placement after the entry-command is deliberate: total entry is the footing, and the slip is what happens to the feet that stop walking into the house. The knowledge-command then reads as the community's maintenance — the regular re-knowing of the Might and the Wisdom that keeps the proofs from becoming furniture, the familiarity that precedes every communal zalla. The sūrah's own method models the maintenance: it re-states its proofs at every station, returns its names at every verdict, repeats its portraits — because a community that has received the bayyināt needs them re-delivered, not once but at every footing-risk, and the verse is the shortest form of that re-delivery: know.

**"Waiting for Judgment?"**

The translation's closing question — Waiting for Judgment? — marks the verse's forward edge, the point where the conditional opens onto the scene the next movement will unfold. The question hangs over the slipper in both of its possible postures. The one who slips after the proofs may still imagine time: the judgment deferred, the return postponed, the footing recoverable at leisure — and the question asks whether he knows what he is waiting for. And the community that holds its footing may still be tempted to treat the Might and the Wisdom as abstractions: powerful, yes; wise, yes — but at a distance, in a horizon not yet arrived — and the question reminds it that the horizon is the arrival, that the names it has just been commanded to know are the names of the One whose coming settles everything at once. The verse thus ends its conditional with a suspended interrogative, the two names named and the waiting named, and between them the reader's own posture: slipping or standing, the knowledge of the Mighty and Wise is knowledge of the One the question points toward — and the answer to the question is the scene that follows, in which the waiting ends.

**The Knowledge That Stands**

The verse's total demand reduces to its verb: know. Not perform more, not fear harder, not recover the lost ground by effort — know God's might and wisdom. The command identifies knowledge as the footing itself: the clear proofs came as knowledge, the slip happened despite knowledge, and the recovery is a knowledge deeper than the proofs' reception — the knowledge of who sent them and what His attributes guarantee. This is the same knowledge the hajj-legislation ended on: know that to Him you will be gathered (2:203); and the same knowledge the war-code ran on: know that God is with the mindful (2:194), severe in retribution (2:196). The sūrah's legislative stations all close in the commanded knowing, and this verse stations the knowing at the danger-point — the slip after clarity — because the knowledge of the Mighty and Wise is precisely what a slipping person has stopped having. The proofs inform the mind; this knowledge re-forms the posture: the heart that knows the Might cannot treat the slip as escape, and the heart that knows the Wisdom cannot treat the law as mistake. Between those two impossibilities the standing is rebuilt — and the verse, having rebuilt it, turns toward the horizon where all standing and slipping arrive.
