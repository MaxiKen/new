## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:71

> Yet they worship besides Allah that for which He has sent down no authority, and of which they have no knowledge. The wrongdoers will have no helper.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Grounds, and Neither Is Present**

*Wa-yaʿbudūna min dūni Allāhi mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānan wa-mā laysa lahum bihi ʿilm* — and they worship, apart from God, that for which He has sent down no authority and of which they have no knowledge.

The verse names two things the worshippers lack, and the two are exactly the pair the sūrah named at verse 8 for the disputant. There the three absences were knowledge, guidance and an illuminating scripture. Here the two are authority and knowledge. The argument has not changed; it has been tightened.

The object of worship is described as *mā*, "that which" — the pronoun used for what is not a person. The grammar itself is part of the case: they are worshipping a *thing*, and the things in question have had no warrant sent down for them.

The two absences are also cumulative rather than alternative. The first is external: nothing has come down from God authorising this. The second is internal: the worshippers do not know what they are dealing with. A person might admit the first and still claim the second, which is why the verse names both — inherited practice produces confidence without warrant, and confidence can feel like knowledge. The relative clause is indefinite in both halves, so the description is not a list of particular idols but a rule: whatever lacks the warrant and the knowledge is what they are worshipping.

**What "Sulṭān" Means Here**

*Sulṭān* is the word for authority, warrant, proof, and the power by which one thing prevails over another. Its range is wide and all of it is in play.

In one sense it means a revelation: God has sent down nothing authorising this worship. In another it means intellectual demonstration — the argument by which a claim is established — and al-Rāzī took it in that sense here, linking it to the second half of the verse. In a third it means sheer power, the sense in which Satan says at the judgment: *“I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me”* (14:22).

The three senses combine into one requirement. For a claim about God to be acted on, something must have come down from God establishing it, or something must be demonstrable by reason, or something must actually have power. The verse says the idols have none of the three.

**The Burden of Proof Is Placed on the Worshipper**

This is the structural move of the verse, and it is the one that matters most.

The verse does not argue that the idols do not exist, or that they are powerless in some absolute sense. It says that no warrant has come down for worshipping them and that the worshippers have no knowledge of them. The claim being made is not about the object; it is about the justification.

That relocates the argument. Instead of the messenger having to disprove the idols, the worshipper has to produce the authorisation — and the sūrah's whole case is that he cannot, because no such authorisation exists and because the worship of these things was inherited rather than established.

The Qur'an makes the inheritance explicit when it wants to, and the wording it uses closes the remaining exit. Of the idols it says that the worshippers have *"mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized"* (53:23), and of the people it says that *"most of them follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions. ˹And˺ surely assumptions can in no way replace the truth"* (10:36). The verse is not demanding that the worshippers produce a demonstration of metaphysics. It is asking what they are relying on, and naming the answer before they give it.

**The Claim Is Falsifiable, and That Is the Point**

One of the more striking features of this verse is that it invites a test. If a warrant has been sent down, produce it. If the worshipper has knowledge of what he worships, state it.

The Qur'an makes this same challenge in the same form elsewhere, and the challenge is what makes the argument an argument rather than a denunciation: *“Call upon those you claim ˹to be divine˺ besides Allah. They do not possess ˹even˺ an atom’s weight either in the heavens or the earth, nor do they have any share in ˹governing˺ them”* (34:22).

The test is never met in the Qur'an's narratives. What happens instead is what happened at verse 72's recitations and what happened at verse 15's rope: rage, evasion, or an appeal to inheritance. The absence of a warrant is the sūrah's case, and it is a case that could in principle be answered.

**"The Wrongdoers Will Have No Helper"**

*Wa-mā li-l-ẓālimīna min naṣīr* — and the wrongdoers have no helper.

The word *naṣīr* is the one used for an ally who comes when called, the party to a pact of mutual defence. In the tribal vocabulary of the sūrah's audience, it was the word for the people who show up when there is fighting.

The claim is therefore concrete. On the day these people need their allies, none will come — and the irony is that the objects they worshipped were precisely the allies they had been counting on. Verse 12 established that those objects can neither harm nor benefit; verse 13 said they are worse than nothing; and this verse says that at the moment of need, there is no one.

The same sentence is put into Abraham's mouth in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt, addressed to exactly this arrangement, and it adds what the absence will feel like from the inside: *"You have taken idols ˹for worship˺ instead of Allah, only to keep ˹the bond of˺ harmony among yourselves in this worldly life. But on the Day of Judgment you will disown and curse one another. Your home will be the Fire, and you will have no helper!"* (29:25). The alliance dissolves in both directions: the gods do not come, and the worshippers turn on each other.

**Why "Wrongdoers" Rather Than "Disbelievers"**

The closing noun is *ẓālimīn*, those who do wrong — not *kāfirīn*, those who disbelieve.

The choice fits the argument the verse has just made. The charge was never that these people hold a false opinion about metaphysics. The charge is that they transferred to something else what belongs to God, which is an act of misplacement — the definition of *ẓulm*.

The sūrah used the same word at verse 25 for those who bar people from the Sacred Mosque, and it will use it at verse 72 for the rage on the disbelievers' faces. In every case the wrong is something done rather than something merely believed.

The Qur'an names the same act as the worst instance of the same category, and the naming is what settles the question of why *ẓālimīn* and not something milder: *"Never associate ˹anything˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺, for associating ˹others with Him˺ is truly the worst of all wrongs"* (31:13). The verse is not softening the charge by calling it wrongdoing. It is using the word the Qur'an reserves for the gravest misplacement there is.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Cumulative Case**

By this point in the sūrah the case against idolatry has been made in four different registers: the functional test of verse 12, the escalating harm of verse 13, the parable of the falling man in verse 31, and now the demand for a warrant.

The four are not repetitions. Each addresses a different excuse — that the object delivers benefits, that the worship is harmless, that the practice is inherited, and now that the worshipper has grounds. Verse 71 closes the last of them.

What follows confirms the reading. Having established that no warrant exists, the sūrah stops arguing about what the idols can do and starts describing what they are: the fly of verse 73, which cannot recover what it loses, and the mismeasurement of God in verse 74. Those are not further arguments; they are illustrations of a conclusion already reached. The warrant was never produced, and the two verses that follow are written for a reader who has accepted that it never will be.
