## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:85

> And let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this world, and ˹then˺ their souls will depart while they are disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Repeated Warning Corrects a Strong Attraction**

The warning about wealth and children is repeated because these are among the strongest attractions in worldly life. People easily treat prosperity, family size, and social influence as proof of divine favor. The repetition breaks that reflex. A possession can be good in itself while still becoming a means of punishment for the person who uses it against Allah.

The Prophet is told not to be impressed, not because wealth has no value, but because appearance cannot reveal the final account. A wealthy person may be grateful and just; a poor person may be proud and cruel. Children may strengthen a family in worship, or they may become objects of vanity. The meaning of the gift lies in its moral use.

**The Gift Can Become a Chain**

Wealth and children can punish a person through the fear of losing them, the pressure of maintaining status, and the duties he refuses to perform. A person may become a servant of his possessions. He works without rest, protects his image, and compromises truth because he believes that security depends on keeping what Allah gave him.

This is not a condemnation of family care or lawful earning. A parent should provide, and a worker should seek a lawful livelihood. The danger begins when the gift commands the heart. The person no longer asks what Allah wants him to do with the wealth; he asks only how to preserve it. The blessing has become a chain because it directs obedience away from Allah.

**This World Can Contain Punishment Before the Hereafter**

The verse places the torment in this world and then mentions the departure of the souls while disbelieving. Worldly punishment need not appear as visible poverty or constant sorrow. It may be anxiety inside comfort, rivalry within a family, or the inability to enjoy what one has because fear rules every pleasure.

The believer should not diagnose every prosperous unbeliever as visibly punished. Allah's judgments are not all open to human sight. The verse teaches a principle: possession can carry an inward cost when it deepens disbelief. Increase may be a chance for gratitude, or it may give a rebellious heart more material with which to defend itself. The servant asks for beneficial provision, not merely larger provision.

**Death Ends the Illusion of Ownership**

The soul eventually departs while the possessions remain. This exposes the weakness of worldly ownership. A person may spend years building a fortune and still leave it at the moment he most needs it to speak for him. Children may mourn, inherit, or continue the work, but they cannot replace faith in the account of the deceased.

The warning therefore teaches urgency in the present. Use wealth for obligations and charity before it becomes an accusation. Teach children gratitude rather than making them trophies. Repair relationships before death makes repair impossible. The safest possession is the one already converted into obedience, because that portion accompanies the servant in meaning even when the physical object stays behind.

**Do Not Envy a Burden**

A believer may look at a powerful family and imagine that it has escaped the uncertainty of ordinary life. The verse tells him to see beyond the surface. The burden may be greater than the apparent privilege. Envy is weakened when the servant remembers that every gift creates a question: did the owner thank Allah, fulfill rights, and protect the heart?

This perspective does not make the believer hostile to prosperity. He can ask Allah for wealth that serves people and children who grow in faith. He simply refuses to admire a blessing detached from obedience. The right request is for provision that helps him remember Allah, not for the possessions that make another person impressive.

**Measure the Gift by Its Direction**

The final test is direction. Does wealth move toward justice, family care, learning, and charity, or toward pride and fear? Do children become a reason for prayer and patience, or a reason to abandon truth? Does public influence make a person humble, or does it protect him from correction?

Allah alone knows the complete answer, so the believer avoids shallow judgments. He also takes the warning personally. A gift is safer when the servant keeps asking Allah to purify its use. The repeated verse teaches that the danger is powerful precisely because the attraction is powerful. Wealth and children can be means of mercy, but only when they remain means rather than masters.

The repetition also speaks to people who think a warning applied only to an earlier group. Wealth and children remain powerful tests in every generation. A person may inherit a business, gain public influence, or become proud of a successful family and assume that increase confirms his wisdom. The verse asks him to inspect the effect instead. If the gift makes prayer harder, charity more painful, and correction more offensive, it is already carrying a punishment within it. If the gift produces gratitude and service, the servant should thank Allah and remain watchful, because even a good use of wealth requires continued humility.

The believer therefore receives prosperity with two prayers: that Allah bless the gift and that Allah protect him from being ruled by it. He teaches his children that provision is a trust before it is a status symbol. He lets wealth fund useful work, lawful enjoyment, and charity rather than using it to purchase immunity from correction. If family responsibilities become difficult, he seeks help instead of blaming the family for the burden. The verse is not asking him to reject what Allah has made attractive. It is asking him to keep the attractive thing in its place. Wealth and children can carry a servant toward mercy when they remain servants of faith.
