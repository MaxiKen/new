## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:24

> See how they will lie about themselves and how those ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Order to Look at a Specific Moment**

The verse begins with a command to observe: see how they will lie about themselves. It is the same instruction the sūrah gives later when it describes those arraigned before the Fire (6:27) and when it reports what the disbelievers will say when they stand before their Lord (6:30). The reader is not asked to form an opinion about the Hereafter in general. He is placed at a precise point in a scene and told which detail to notice.

The device appears throughout the Book as a way of settling an argument that a general statement cannot settle. Denial in this life is cheap, because the denier holds the initiative and can keep changing the subject. The verse asks its hearer to watch what denial looks like when the initiatives have all been taken away.

**The Third Target of Their Lying**

Earlier in the passage the lies were aimed outward. They fabricated against God and they denied His signs (6:21), so their untruth was directed at their Lord and at the evidence He had supplied. Here the lie turns and strikes the speaker: they lie about themselves. The sentence they swore in the previous verse was intended to protect their standing, and it does the opposite, because a denial spoken in the presence of full knowledge is a verdict on the person who speaks it rather than on the case.

That movement is worth tracking, since the Book maps the damage of lying in concentric circles. A lie against God corrupts the religion, and a lie against the signs corrupts the evidence, while a lie about oneself corrupts the only thing a person carries out of this world.

**Why the Damage Lands on the Liar**

Commentators explain the phrase as a realization that arrives in the Hereafter, when the consequences of deeds are displayed and the truth about the person becomes visible to him. The Book names that day the day when the secrets are tested (86:9), which is precisely the function of the scene here: the hidden thing is produced, and the false story collapses against it.

No one has to expose these people and no witness has to accuse them. Their own record is the refutation, and their oath is the proof that they knew what they were doing. The verse's verb of lying is not a comment about their ignorance but about their choice to speak against what they could see.

**The Fabrications That Walk Away**

The second half of the verse states the consequence: the gods they fabricated fail them. The Book uses the same expression repeatedly at the end of scenes like this one. Coming back to God all alone as He created the first time, a person finds that his intercessors have vanished, his ties broken, and his claims brought to nothing (6:94); every soul faces what it has done, and whatever gods they fabricated fail them (10:30).

The failure is not described as an act of punishment. It is described as absence. The systems that were built to carry a person through the judgment simply do not arrive, and the worshippers who had spent their lives maintaining them are left carrying nothing. The sūrah of the Heights uses the identical closing formula in the same setting: those who ignored the warning ask for intercessors or for a second term, and the report ends by noting that they have ruined themselves and that whatever gods they fabricated have failed them (7:53).

What disappears with the gods is the entire chain of dependence that had been arranged around them. No patron steps forward, no offering is remembered as a credit, and no lineage carries weight. The verse that reports the collapse at the beginning of the Hereafter, when people come back alone as they were created the first time and every tie is broken (6:94), describes the same vacuum from a different angle.

**Idolatry's Ordinary Disappointments in This Life**

The Makkans did not need to wait for the Hereafter to test the reliability of their gods. The Book points out that the objects of their worship neither respond nor protect nor benefit (20:89), and it asks how anyone can worship, besides God, what can neither harm nor benefit him (5:76). The complaint is not that the idols are weaker than another deity. It is that they are not participants in the transaction at all.

Read that way, the verse's demonstration has already been running in the audience's own experience. A stone that fails in a famine will fail at the judgment; the difference is only that at the judgment nobody can leave and go home.

**What the Reader Is Meant to Take Away**

The instruction to look is not aimed at the men in the scene. It is aimed at the person holding the text, who is still alive, still able to choose, and still in a position to examine what he relies on. The sūrah will go on to describe people who listen without hearing (6:25) and leaders who block the way (6:26), and this verse stands before them as the preview of where that road ends.

Two things are being shown: an accusation that convicts by its own utterance, and a support that stops supporting. The reader who has understood both has understood the case the sūrah intends to make before it moves to anything else.

There is also a difference between regret and change, and this verse marks the border between them. The men in the scene have understood their position completely, and their understanding produces nothing but a denial. That distinction between knowing the truth and letting the truth redirect a life is what the sūrah examines next, in a passage about people who hear the recitation and leave it still deciding about the recitation rather than about themselves.
