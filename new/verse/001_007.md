## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Company Named Rather Than a Quality Described**

Verse 6 asked for the straight path; this verse answers by naming who walks it. The path is not defined abstractly but socially: the path of those You have blessed. Some transmissions count this clause as a separate verse, which highlights its function as a direct reply to the petition that precedes it. The Qur'an elsewhere gives the membership of that company: those who obey Allah and the Messenger are gathered with the prophets, the truthful, the witnesses and the righteous, whom Allah has blessed, and excellent is that companionship (4:69). Other commentators describe the blessed as those God has purified for nearness to Himself, or those He has enabled to keep the Law’s limits while also tasting its inward realities without breaching either side. In each case the verb matters. *Anʿamta* is perfect and active with God as subject — You have blessed — so the status is conferred, not achieved by autonomous effort. To ask for the path of the blessed is therefore to ask for incorporation into a fellowship already constituted by a prior act of favor. The road sought is not a technique to be mastered in isolation but a communion to be joined.

**The Syntax That Separates Two Negations**

The exclusion is phrased *ghayr al-maghḍūb ʿalayhim wa-lā al-ḍāllīn* — not the ones who incur Your displeasure, nor those who wander astray (1:7). Grammarians note that *ghayr* can be read as modifying “path” or as modifying “those,” but the practical effect converges: the way requested is declared to be other than the two rejected ways. The repetition of negation — first *ghayr*, then *lā* — keeps the two rejections distinct rather than merging them into a single undifferentiated error. Early exegesis offers several mappings. One links those who incur wrath to idolaters and those astray to hypocrites; another distinguishes grades of offense — willful rejection that draws displeasure versus misguidance that reflects confusion or lesser fault; another speaks generally of moral states — heedlessness and profligacy — that together span the varieties of distraction by worldly share. The common insight is that the verse separates two modes of leaving the road: one by provoking displeasure through deliberate breach, the other by drifting through failure to follow guidance. Both are excluded, and the double particle ensures neither is softened into the other.

**Two Different Modes of Leaving the Road**

Scripture treats wrath and straying with different vocabularies, and the Fātiḥah preserves that distinction. Wrath is tied to an act of opening the heart to disbelief after guidance has been recognized; the Qur'an speaks of a wrath that settles upon such people (16:106), of the anger that rests on those who dispute about God after having responded to Him (42:16), and of the divine displeasure prepared for hypocrites and idolaters (48:6); the taking of a believer’s life without right is placed under the same severity (4:93). Straying appears more widely and is attributed to a broader set of causes: exchanging faith for disbelief (2:108), denying Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers and the Last Day (4:136), and following mere caprice without guidance (28:50). Wrath names culpable defiance; straying names misdirection that can stem from defiance, neglect, or confusion. By excluding both, the Fātiḥah admits two vulnerabilities in the supplicant: the will can reject what it knows, and the mind can lose its way while meaning no defiance. The petition seeks protection on both fronts.

**A Completed Favor Against a Still-Open Warning**

A tense difference carries pastoral weight. “Those You have blessed” is perfect — the blessing has been bestowed, definitively settled, giving finality and certitude. The other two descriptions are participial, indicating a state or liability rather than an executed sentence. They convey exposure to wrath and the condition of wandering, not that final retribution has already fallen. Traditional reflection draws the implication: because wrath is presented as incurred but not yet finally imposed, repentance remains possible, since *“Allah guides to Himself whoever turns”* (13:27) and the Qur'anic maxim *“Allah does not wrong people in the least, but it is people who wrong themselves”* (10:44) places responsibility with the agent while leaving the door open. The wandering described can result from deliberate choice or from error, which the Qur'an illustrates by placing the same term *ḍāll* in very different mouths: Mūsā before his commission describes himself as among the astray (26:20), and the Prophet is reminded *“Did He not find you astray and guide you?”* (93:7). Straying therefore covers a continuum from a messenger before revelation to one who barters faith for denial (2:108). The request not to be among the astray becomes a plea for continual remembrance — to be guided at each step rather than a claim to have arrived.

**Vertical, Descending, and Horizontal: Three Ways Cast as Space**

When the straight path is imagined as the vertical ascent toward God, the alternatives compose a spatial diagram. The blessed ascend; those who incur displeasure descend away; those astray meander horizontally off the line. The three correspond to ascent, descent, and lateral drift, and together they map every possible orientation of the person. The Qur'an presents the underlying movement as universal: human beings are laboring toward their Lord and will meet Him (84:6), and *“to Him is the journey’s end”* (5:18); see also (2:285), (3:28), (24:42). What varies is not whether one moves but how. *“Is he who walks fallen on his face more guided, or he who walks upright on a straight path?”* (67:22), with echoes at (3:83) and (13:15), poses the question as one of posture. The Fātiḥah’s final petition, read against this, does not ask for immobility; it asks for upright movement — to proceed toward God with honor rather than to be dragged prone or diverted sideways. The inevitability is given; the dignity is requested.

**A Reported Identification and Its Proper Bound**

A well-known report, widely cited though not graded among the most rigorously authenticated, associates those who incur wrath with the Jews and those astray with the Christians. Commentators who mention it often connect the first to verses where divine anger is said to have come upon the Children of Israel (2:61), (2:90), (3:112), (5:60), and the second to the caution *“Do not follow the caprices of a people who went astray before, led many astray, and themselves strayed from the right way”* (5:77), taken as a reference to followers of Mūsā and ʿĪsā who exceeded limits. Even those who transmit this identification preserve the wider Qur'anic usage: wrath and straying are conditions, not proper names of peoples, and can appear in any community, including the one reciting the prayer. The more careful glosses therefore hear the verse primarily as a supplication for the speaker himself — to follow those whom God has *“raised in degrees”* (6:83), *“those who believe and those given knowledge”* (58:11), those *“who believe and do not obscure their belief with wrongdoing”* (6:82) — rather than as a verdict upon others. The path requested is the companionship described at (4:69); the paths declined are every way that solicits divine displeasure or aimless wandering away from guidance.

**The Seal Placed After the Prayer**

In congregational prayer it is customary to follow the Fātiḥah with *āmīn* after a brief silence that marks this utterance as distinct from the Qur'anic text itself. The practice rests on a prophetic report that when the imam says “not those who incur wrath, nor those astray” and the worshippers’ *āmīn* coincides with that of the angels, previous sins are forgiven. Jurists note it is not obligatory and not part of the recitation, yet strongly recommended and traced to the Prophet, with views differing on whether it should be voiced aloud or quietly. Its meaning is rendered as “O Allah, answer,” or “so be it,” akin to the Hebrew *amen*, and is described as the seal upon a supplication. Another gloss takes it as one of the divine Names, but the seal-image predominates: a prayer that began with the Name that consecrates every act ends with a word that entrusts the prayer itself to the One whose Name began it, and the angels’ concurrence signals that the request is not heard alone.

**The Company That Makes the Path Concrete**

The list at (4:69) — prophets, truthful ones, witnesses, righteous — gives the straight path a human shape. To ask for the path is to ask for those companions whose walk already constitutes the path, and the Qur'an’s closing “what excellent companions they are” (4:69) presents the attractiveness of the way as part of its evidence.

**Wrath and Errancy as Distinct Exclusions**

The Fātiḥah excludes both a culpable provocation of divine displeasure and a wandering that may be willful or inadvertent. The distinct Qur'anic treatments — wrath for opening the heart to disbelief (16:106), straying for bartering faith for denial (2:108) — demonstrate that two separate safeguards are requested, not one.
