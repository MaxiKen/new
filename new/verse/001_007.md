## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Who the Blessed Are**

The path requested in the previous verse is specified here by the company travelling it. Guidance is never offered in the Quran as an abstract state; it is a road, and roads have people on them. The verse therefore does two things at once: it names the destination's travellers, and it names two groups whose way must be avoided.

The Quran supplies the roll of the first group elsewhere: *whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company!* (4:69). Blessing is described in the past tense — *those You have blessed* (1:7) — and the tense is doing work. The favour is treated as already fixed and settled, not as something pending review, so that the worshipper is asking to be joined to a company whose standing is not in doubt.

Two further descriptions in the Quran indicate what qualifies people for that company. It belongs to *those who are faithful and do not tarnish their faith with falsehood* (6:82), and to those God *elevate[s]… in rank* by faith and knowledge (58:11). Obedience is the entry; knowledge and unclouded faith are the degrees within it.

**The Best-Known Identification, and How Far It Goes**

Commentators proposed several ways of filling in the two negative categories, and the proposals are not all compatible. Some said those who incur displeasure are the idolaters and those astray are the hypocrites. Others mapped the pair onto kinds of wrongdoing — disbelief drawing wrath, lesser sin producing error. Others again read them as moral types: the heedless and the profligate.

By far the most cited identification traces to a report of ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, who said that the Prophet said, *The Jews are those who Allah is wrath with, and the Christians have strayed* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2954, graded ḥasan). The reading has support in the Quran's own usage, since wrath is repeatedly attached to the Children of Israel in their history (2:61; 2:90; 3:112; 5:60) and straying is warned against in language applied to earlier communities (5:77).

The identification should nonetheless be held as a specification, not a definition. It names the clearest historical instances available to the first hearers; it does not restrict the categories to those instances, since the Quran applies both terms elsewhere to idolaters, hypocrites, and murderers (48:6; 4:93; 16:106). What survives the identification is the moral distinction behind it, and that is the substance of the verse.

**Two Ways of Going Wrong**

The two categories are not two intensities of one failure. The tradition distinguishes them by what the person had. Wrath attaches to those who knew and turned away — the failure of a will that has been informed. Straying attaches to those who lack the knowledge in the first place, and it can therefore be involuntary.

The Quran shows this by using the second term of people who are not condemned. Mūsā, describing the blow he struck before his mission, says of himself that he acted when he was among those astray (26:20) — the Arabic is from the very root used here, though most English renderings soften it to "lacking guidance." The same root is used of the Prophet's own pre-revelatory state: *Did He not find you unguided then guided you?* (93:7). Neither passage is a condemnation, and that is the point. Straying can describe a condition someone is in rather than a choice someone has made, which is why the Quran pairs it with the possibility of being found.

The contrast with wrath is sharp. Those who *trades belief for disbelief* has *truly strayed from the Right Way* (2:108) and those who *denies Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, and the Last Day has clearly gone far astray* (4:136); but the specific gravity of the first category lies in knowledge resisted. Where guidance has arrived and been refused, the Quran speaks of wrath, and it speaks of it as earned: *They have invited the displeasure of Allah* (3:112).

**The Grammar Leaves a Door Open**

A detail of construction has drawn the commentators' attention. The blessing is stated as an act with God as its agent, in the past tense. The displeasure is stated in the passive, with no agent named and no assertion that it has actually fallen. The difference is not stylistic.

The Quran's own principle explains why: *Allah does not wrong people in the least, but it is people who wrong themselves* (10:44). Attributing the outcome to the sufferer rather than to God is consistent with the refusal to say that He has acted against them, and the passive form keeps the sentence from foreclosing what has not yet happened. Someone described as subject to wrath may still not have reached it. *Allah… guides to Himself whoever turns to Him* (13:27), and the verse asks to be kept away from the path of those under displeasure while that turning is still available.

The particle *ghayr* — "not" — can be read as attached to the path or to the people, giving either "a path other than theirs" or "the path of those You have blessed, who are not those under displeasure." Both readings were defended, and both arrive at the same meaning, which is why the disagreement has no practical consequence.

**Three Directions**

Read geometrically, the three groups describe three motions. The blessed ascend toward God. Those under wrath descend away from Him. Those astray move sideways, wandering without arriving anywhere. The three exhaust the possibilities of a creature's direction, and the Quran poses the choice as a question about posture: *Who is ˹rightly˺ guided: the one who crawls facedown or the one who walks upright on the Straight Path?* (67:22).

Every person is moving, and the question is only which way. The sūrah that opened with God as the owner of every world and every day ends by sorting the inhabitants of those worlds into three, then asking to be placed with the first.

**Āmīn, the Worshipper's Seal**

The sūrah ends on a request, and the worshipper answers it with a word that is not part of the Quran. The Prophet is reported to have said: *Say Amen when the Imam says, 'not the path of those who earn Your Anger, nor of those who go astray'; all the past sins of the person whose saying of Amin coincides with that of the angels will be forgiven* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 782). A brief pause separates the recitation from the response, marking the word as the worshipper's and not the text's.

The word is usually understood as a supplication — *O God, answer* — and some took it to be one of the divine names. Its position is the point. Having been taught what to ask for, the worshipper adds nothing new, asks for no change, and simply asks that what was said be accepted. Seven verses begin with God and end with a request; the response of the one reciting is to ratify the whole of it.
