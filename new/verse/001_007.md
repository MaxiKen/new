## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Blessing Spoken as a Completed Act**

The first group is described by an act of God that has already happened. The Arabic relative clause puts the blessing in the past, which gives the phrase the firmness of something settled rather than hoped for. The Qur'an names those people elsewhere: *“And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company!”* (4:69). The four ranks are not achievements registered by the travellers; they are the company of the blessed, listed by the one who blessed them.

That the verse attaches the blessing to God rather than to the blessed is the whole point. The reciter does not say "the path of the successful" or "the path of the deserving", which would make the road a prize won by its walkers. He says the path of those God has blessed, and the model that follows in the Book confirms the pattern: Abraham is described as *“a model of excellence: devoted to Allah, ˹perfectly˺ upright—not a polytheist—”* (16:120), a description of what God made of him rather than of what he made of himself.

**Displeasure Stated Without a Delivered Blow**

The second group is handled differently. God remains the agent — the verse reads *those You are displeased with* — but the sentence names an attitude and stops. It does not say that the anger has arrived, that a punishment has been executed, or that a final verdict has been entered. Classical translations rendered the phrase as those who incurred wrath, which preserves the same restraint: a condition is described, and its outcome is left open.

The commentators drew a practical conclusion from that wording. A door of repentance remains ajar while displeasure is stated as displeasure, and the Qur'an reports cases where the anger did come down — a people *“stricken with disgrace and misery”* who *“invited the displeasure of Allah”* (2:61), and another described in the words *“They have invited the displeasure of Allah and have been branded with misery”* (3:112). Between a state that is described and a sentence that is executed lies the whole space in which a person can still turn back.

**Astray Is a Condition, Not Necessarily a Crime**

The third group is described with a single intransitive word. Being astray, *ḍāll*, is a state into which one may wander rather than an act of hostility, and the Book applies it across a wide range of people. Moses says of his own past, *“I did it then, lacking guidance”* (26:20), and God reminds the Prophet, *“Did He not find you unguided then guided you?”* (93:7). At the other end stands the deliberate wanderer: *“who could be more astray than those who follow their desires with no guidance from Allah?”* (28:50), and the verse that goes further still, *“They are only like cattle—no, more than that, they are astray from the ˹Right˺ Way!”* (25:44).

The range matters for a prayer that asks to be spared. If being astray meant only wickedness, the request would be a request to be good; since it means losing the road, the request is to be kept from a condition that can overtake anyone who stops consulting the guidance he has been given.

**A Report That Named Two Communities**

A report from ʿAdiyy ibn Ḥāṭim in the Book of Tafsīr of Jāmiʿ at-Tirmidhī, graded ḥasan, records that the Prophet ﷺ identified those under displeasure as the Jews and those astray as the Christians. The identification answers the question the two descriptions raise, and the Qur'an itself supplies the language it depends on: it speaks of a people who *“earned Allah's condemnation and displeasure”* (5:60) and warns against *“those who went astray before ˹you˺”* (5:77).

The verse's own words nevertheless name no community, and the commentators handled it as a description of two ways rather than of two nations. The distinction holds in practice as well. A person may belong to a community and not follow its worst example, and the report itself was given concerning two tendencies that the Book identifies wherever they appear: knowing the truth and turning from it, and seeking the truth and missing the road.

**A Prayer Sealed by a Word Not in the Book**

The sūrah closes with a petition rather than a pronouncement. The reciter has asked to be kept from two conditions and placed in a third, and the community answers the ending with the word *āmīn*. A report of Abū Hurayrah in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 780 attaches a promise to it: *Say Āmīn when the imam says it, and if the Āmīn of any one of you coincides with that of the angels, his past sins will be forgiven.*

*Āmīn* is not part of the sūrah; it is the worshipper's seal on it, which is why the schools differ over whether it is said aloud and whether it belongs to the recitation at all. The distinction is precise. The Book supplies the request, and the human voice adds agreement without adding a verse. What ends the greatest of sūrahs is not a statement about God but a request addressed to him, and the last word the community speaks is a request to be answered.

**Where the Prayer Leaves the Servant**

Read from beginning to end, the Fātiḥah moves through praise, mercy, ownership, worship, reliance and guidance, and it ends without the servant having claimed any of them as his own. Mercy was named twice at the beginning; the ending asks that the reciter be placed among the blessed and kept from the two ruined ways. Nothing in the sūrah allows the one who recites it to conclude that he has arrived.

The Qur'an's own prayer of the servants has the same shape: *“My Lord! Forgive and have mercy, for You are the best of those who show mercy”* (23:118). The Fātiḥah, recited in every unit of prayer, keeps the worshipper in that position permanently — asking, with the words God gave, for a road he has not finished walking. Muslims recite it as the opening of the Book; the sūrah itself refuses to let anyone read it as a certificate.
