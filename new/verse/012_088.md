## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:88

> When they entered Joseph’s presence, they pleaded, “O Chief Minister! We and our family have been touched with hardship, and we have brought only a few worthless coins, but ˹please˺ give us our supplies in full and be charitable to us. Indeed, Allah rewards the charitable.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Journey Has a Different Purpose**

The commentators note that the brothers travelled to Egypt this time to inquire about Joseph and Benjamin, in accordance with their father's instruction (12:87), rather than only to buy grain. The verse does not report that purpose in their speech. What they say to the official concerns hardship, inadequate goods and a request for full measure. The search commissioned by Jacob is therefore conducted silently, in the same audience as the commercial plea, and the chapter keeps it out of the dialogue. The reader knows both purposes are in the room. The man being addressed knows one of them, since he arranged the other.

That silence has a consequence for the scene. Because the brothers do not mention Joseph, nothing in their speech prompts the question they receive in verse 89. They ask about grain and charity; they are answered about a brother they believe to be dead. The chapter thus makes the disclosure wholly unforced by the visitors. It arises from the person addressed rather than from anything said to him, which is what allows verse 89 to land as a revelation instead of as a reply.

**"Hardship Has Touched Us and Our Family"**

The word denotes affliction that has made contact — hardship that has touched them, not merely threatened them. The commentators gloss it as intense hunger, and the verse extends it beyond the travellers to their household. This is the third or fourth year of a seven-year famine (12:48), the stores carried home on earlier journeys have been consumed, and the family in Canaan is described in the same terms as the family in Egypt. The plea is therefore not a bargaining position. It is a statement of condition, and the condition is what makes the request in the second half of the verse both necessary and difficult to make.

The extension to the family is also a rhetorical move. The brothers are not describing their own discomfort on a long journey; they are describing a household that includes an ageing father whose sight has failed from weeping and whose youngest son is detained in this same country. Everything the officials might need in order to be moved is contained in the phrase, and the brothers do not elaborate on any of it. Verse 89 will show what the official actually does with a plea of this kind: he ignores its content entirely and asks about a well.

**"A Few Worthless Coins"**

The brothers describe what they have brought: meagre merchandise, goods of little value. The commentators understand this as the small amount of trade goods or money they possessed to exchange for food. The admission is remarkable in a negotiation. A buyer normally conceals the weakness of his position; these men state it in their opening address, before making their request. The verse is describing people who have nothing left to bargain with and who know it. What they offer instead is a description of their need and an appeal to the official's character, which is the only currency remaining to them in a famine.

The admission also has a history in this chapter. On the first journey they arrived with eleven camels and requested eleven loads (12:59). On the second they had their money returned to them unasked and argued from it at home (12:62, 12:65). Now they bring little and say so. The three journeys trace the exhaustion of a household, and the verse records the endpoint in the brothers' own words rather than in the narrator's. Nothing about their appearance, their numbers or their camels is described; what the reader is given is their own assessment of what they are worth as customers.

**"Give Us Our Supplies in Full"**

The request uses the same standard Joseph set for himself in verse 59, when he asked the brothers whether they could not see that he gave full measure. They have watched him meet that standard twice, and they now ask for it a third time while admitting they cannot pay for it. The request is therefore grounded in his own published conduct rather than in their entitlement. The chapter is showing the brothers arguing from what they have observed — the same method they used in Egypt when denying the theft (12:73) — and the observation happens to be accurate. The man they are addressing is the one who established the standard.

**"And Be Charitable to Us"**

The commentators understand this clause as a request for the return of Benjamin, which means the plea has two objects: grain for the family and the release of a brother. The verse does not distinguish them, and the wording allows both readings at once. *Taṣaddaq ʿalaynā* — be charitable toward us, give us something beyond what is owed. In a rationing economy the phrase covers a supplementary measure of food; in the situation these brothers are actually in, it covers a detained son. The ambiguity is the verse's device. The brothers ask for charity without specifying which of the two things they need, and the answer they receive is neither.

**"Allah Rewards the Charitable"**

The closing clause invokes a religious maxim to an Egyptian official. The brothers have used the same method before, appealing to what their interlocutors already know (12:73), and here they appeal to a principle of recompense. The irony is complete and the chapter does not mark it. They are asking for charity from the most charitable person in the country — a man who fed them at full measure twice, returned their money into their bags unasked (12:62), and has just disclosed himself to the brother they are pleading for (12:69). The maxim they quote is true, and its fulfilment is standing in front of them, and none of them knows it.

**From Eleven Camel-Loads to a Few Coins**

The verse measures the family's decline in the chapter's own units. In verse 59 ten brothers arrived with eleven camels and requested eleven loads. In verse 65 they argued for an additional load as something easily secured. Here they bring a small quantity of low-value goods and ask for full measure as a favour. The distance between those positions is the famine, and the famine is the thing Joseph predicted and administered. The chapter does not draw the connection, but the arithmetic is visible: the men who once requested an extra camel-load are now asking a stranger for charity, and the stranger is the brother whose sale funded nothing and whose foresight is feeding the region that sold him.

**What the Brothers Do Not Ask For**

The plea is confined to food and to charity, and its omissions are as informative as its contents. The brothers do not ask for Benjamin's release by name, although one reading of the request for charity covers it. They do not mention their eldest brother, who has stayed in Egypt under a vow and whom they have now joined. They do not refer to the oath their father took from them, or to the penalty they themselves stated in verse 75, or to the substitution they offered in verse 78 and had refused. They do not mention Joseph at all. What they bring to the minister of Egypt is a household's hunger and a small purse, presented without leverage and without history, to a man who has the whole of that history and has just been told by his own father's sons nothing at all.
