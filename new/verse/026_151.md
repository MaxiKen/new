## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:151

> And do not follow the command of the transgressors,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning Turns Toward Leadership**

After describing Thamûd's wealth and skill, Ṣāliḥ identifies the danger of following the commands of the transgressors. Prosperity does not remove the need for guidance; it makes the choice of leadership more consequential. A people can possess good resources and still be led into ruin by those who treat power as permission.

The verse does not ask Thamûd to withdraw from society or refuse every instruction from another person. Communities need cooperation and ordered responsibility. Ṣāliḥ warns against obeying commands that cross God's limits, especially when the command comes from people whose conduct has already shown a disregard for justice.


The people may admire leaders who act decisively, spend freely, and silence opposition. The Qur'anic warning separates courage from transgression. A bold act can serve truth, while an apparently strong act can be driven by appetite and contempt for limits. The measure is not how forceful the leader appears but whether he remains answerable to God.

A transgressor is dangerous because he turns excess into a norm. He makes greed look like ambition, cruelty look like security, and waste look like celebration. Once that language governs a society, ordinary people may follow harmful commands while telling themselves that they are merely being loyal.

**The Arabic Sense Includes Excess**

The word used for the transgressors is associated with crossing bounds and, in the source discussion, with prodigality or wasteful excess. That range is important. Corruption is not limited to a single violent act. It can include a pattern of wanting more than justice permits and encouraging others to pursue worldly advantage without restraint.

This meaning connects the command to Thamûd's material confidence. The people are surrounded by crops, homes, and security, so leaders who promise ever greater consumption can easily win them. Ṣāliḥ tells them not to let abundance turn into a reason to obey those who have forgotten moderation.


A transgressor may command a following because he knows how to speak to fear and desire. He offers the crowd a sense of belonging and promises that no limit should restrain its strength. Popular agreement does not make the command lawful. A whole society can be carried toward harm when it rewards the loudest appetite.

Following revelation requires the courage to pause before the crowd. Ask what the command does to the weak, the land, the truth, and the worship of God. If it produces humiliation, waste, or aggression, obedience to a leader becomes disobedience to the Lord.


Leadership is not condemned simply because it directs others. A just leader can organize work, protect people, settle disputes, and distribute responsibility. The danger arises when authority is separated from accountability. The transgressors want people to follow the command while ignoring the consequences borne by others.

Ṣāliḥ's warning gives every person a duty to judge the moral direction of an order. “I was only obeying” cannot transform wrongdoing into righteousness. A servant may have limited power, but he still has a conscience and remains responsible for the choices he makes within the limits of his ability.

**Moderation Protects the Community**

Quran 7:31 commands people to eat and drink without waste and says that God does not love the wasteful. That parallel clarifies one dimension of Ṣāliḥ's warning. Excess is not harmless when it consumes resources, teaches vanity, and leaves others to bear scarcity. Moderation is a form of obedience, not a refusal of lawful enjoyment.

A community that practices moderation is harder for transgressors to manipulate. Its people are less willing to defend a ruler's extravagance as their own success. They can recognize that a spectacle funded by injustice is not prosperity in the moral sense.


At first, a person may follow a transgressor for convenience or fear. Repetition then changes the person's sense of normality. What once seemed excessive becomes expected, and resistance begins to look unreasonable. Ṣāliḥ intervenes before the habit becomes the people's identity.

The warning is therefore preventative. Thamûd still has a choice about whom it will imitate and whose commands it will treat as authoritative. A society does not need to wait until corruption fills every institution before refusing its leaders. It can stop following the pattern while correction remains possible.

**Obedience to God Includes Refusal**

Religious obedience is not passive submission to whoever holds power. Sometimes it requires saying no. Refusal can be respectful and orderly, but it must be real when a command asks for injustice. Ṣāliḥ gives Thamûd permission to distinguish between the messenger's call and the transgressors' demands.

That distinction protects the community from the claim that dissent is always rebellion. A person who refuses a corrupt command may be preserving the moral order that makes legitimate authority possible. Fear of Allah gives him a higher loyalty and keeps refusal from becoming mere personal defiance.


The transgressors may be skilled, wealthy, or admired. None of those qualities makes their command safe. The key question is whether their direction leads toward justice and submission or toward excess and corruption. Thamûd must choose its examples carefully because people become like the authorities they imitate.

For the reader, the warning reaches every sphere in which influence operates. Do not follow a person simply because he is successful or forceful. Follow what accords with God's limits, and refuse the command that turns a blessing into a weapon. Ṣāliḥ's short prohibition is a defense of conscience against corrupt leadership.

The prohibition also preserves the possibility of reform inside the community. People who refuse a corrupt command are not necessarily rejecting society; they may be refusing to let society destroy its own moral foundations. A calm refusal, joined to truth and responsibility, can protect neighbors who have no public voice and can remind leaders that authority is borrowed.
