## Sūrah an-Naml 27:41

> ˹Then˺ Solomon said, “Disguise her throne for her so we may see whether she will recognize ˹it˺ or she will not be able to.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Throne Is Altered Before She Arrives**

Having obtained the throne, Sulaymān does not display it. He orders it changed. The verb is *nakkirū*, from the root for making something unrecognisable — the same root that gives Arabic its word for the unknown and for denial.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the instruction as changing the throne by adding some things and removing others. Al-Qurṭubī records a simpler version: turning it upside down. Either way the object remains hers and stops looking like hers, which is the precise condition a test of recognition requires. Too small a change and the question is trivial; too large and it is unanswerable. What is being prepared is an examination with a correct answer, and the design of it tells us something about the examiner. Sulaymān is not staging a spectacle. He has taken the most impressive object he could obtain and deliberately made it less impressive in order to learn something.

**What Exactly Is Being Tested**

The stated purpose is to see whether she is guided or not — *a-tahtadī am takūnu min alladhīna lā yahtadūn*. The verb is the same one the Qur'an uses for religious guidance throughout, including in the hoopoe's report three verses earlier, where Sheba's people were described as left unguided.

That shared vocabulary creates a deliberate double meaning that the Arabic carries better than English. On the surface the question is whether she can identify a piece of furniture. Underneath, it is whether she can be guided at all. The two senses are connected by a real inference: recognising a familiar thing in unfamiliar form is exactly the cognitive act that religious guidance requires of her, since she is being asked to recognise the God she has been worshipping in the sun under a description she has not met before. Al-Ṭabarī reads the clause as waiting to see whether she recognises the throne, and Ibn Kathīr calls it a test set for her. Both are right at their own level.

**A Test Designed to Be Passed**

There is a reading of this episode in which Sulaymān is humiliating her — seizing the seat of her power and then playing games with it. The design of the test argues against it.

He does not ask her to identify the throne cold. He asks whether it is *like* hers, which supplies the comparison and invites a judgment rather than demanding certainty. He alters it partially rather than beyond recognition. And he asks before revealing what he has done, so that her answer is her own. Every choice here lowers the difficulty and preserves her standing. A ruler intending to shame a rival would have unveiled the throne in its original state and let the silence do the work. What Sulaymān constructs is closer to a question a good teacher asks: hard enough to be informative, fair enough to be answerable, and phrased so that a wrong answer costs the answerer little.

**The King Keeps Testing**

This is the third time in the chapter that Sulaymān responds to a situation by devising a test. He tested the hoopoe's report by sending a letter and watching the reply. He tested his court's capability by asking who could bring the throne. Now he tests the queen's judgment by altering it.

Three tests, three different subjects, one method. What the pattern establishes is that this king does not form conclusions from impressions, about a bird's honesty, his subordinates' powers, or a foreign monarch's capacity for truth. He builds situations that produce evidence. The chapter opened by describing people whose deeds are made to look good to them so that they wander blindly, and every one of Sulaymān's tests is a defence against exactly that condition — the condition of a person whose beliefs are never checked against anything outside himself. His kingdom includes jinn who can cross continents, and his characteristic act is still to ask a question and wait.

**Both Rulers Now Have Tested the Other**

The symmetry with what came before is complete. She sent a gift to discover what kind of man he was; he alters a throne to discover what kind of woman she is. Neither acted on assumption, and neither relied on the reports of others.

That mutual testing is what makes the meeting that follows a genuine encounter rather than a submission scene. Two rulers have each independently gathered evidence about the other, and each has run an experiment capable of returning an unwelcome result. Her test found a man who could not be bought; his is about to find a woman who can tell the truth under pressure. The Qur'an has spent fifteen verses on this exchange without either party seeing the other's face, and the whole of it has been conducted by inference from behaviour. She arrives in the next verse, and the question is put to her immediately.

**Recognition Requires Something to Recognise With**

There is an assumption behind the test worth making explicit. To identify an altered object you must hold a reliable memory of the original and be able to compare against it under interference. A person with no clear idea of their own throne would fail regardless of intelligence.

Sulaymān is therefore testing two things at once: whether she pays attention to what is hers, and whether she can hold that attention when the object in front of her contradicts it. Both are transferable capacities. The religious question she faces requires exactly the same operation — she has an inherited account of how the world is governed, and she is being asked to hold it against evidence that does not fit. A ruler who cannot tell an altered throne from an unaltered one is unlikely to manage the larger comparison. The test is practical, and its result is directly informative about the thing Sulaymān actually wants to know.
