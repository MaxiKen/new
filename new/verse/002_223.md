## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:223

> Your wives are like farmland for you, so approach them ˹consensually˺ as you please. And send forth something good for yourselves. Be mindful of Allah, and know that you will meet Him. And give good news to the believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tilth**

The verse's central image is agricultural and its meaning generative: your wives are a tilth — harth, cultivated land, sown field — for you; come to your tilth as you will. The commentators read the symbolism straightforwardly: the tilth is the place of sowing, and the sowing's harvest is the child; the image locates the marriage's intimacy inside the same order as the field's — seed, soil, season, fruit — and by that locating, bounds it. A tilth is approached for what a tilth is for; the field's meaning governs the coming to it. Some exegetes took the verse as giving permission for any manner of vaginal intercourse, the symbolism referring to procreation as the act's natural end (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī). The image also carries the whole ethic of cultivation the sūrah has been building: the hypocrite strives to destroy tillage and offspring (2:205) — the same two words, harth and nasl — while the believer comes to his tilth as a cultivator, and the verse that destroys and the verse that cultivates stand twelve verses apart, the field appearing in both as the measure of the heart.

**The Occasion: Two Customs, One Ruling**

The reports behind the verse preserve a clash of marital customs in the young community. The Emigrants from Mecca — Quraysh and their fellows — practiced intercourse in various positions; the Helpers of Medina held to a single manner and considered the others reprehensible. When Emigrant men married Helper women, the customs collided: one account names a Muhājir who married an Anṣārī woman and attempted his customary manner, and she refused him — the tradition preserves her objection in substance: we are approached in one way; do that, or keep away from me. The matter came to the Prophet, and this verse was revealed: Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records it from Jābir (1435) — the ruling permitting the positions, from the front or from behind, so long as it is in the one orifice; and in some narrations the Prophet summarized the verse in a phrase: a single orifice (al-Ṭabarī). The occasion is the verse's social theology: the woman's objection was not overruled by the husband's right but carried to revelation, and revelation answered with a ruling that bounded both customs — the Medinan restriction lifted, the Meccan license fenced by the orifice's singularity. Her refusal had legal standing; her case reached the Prophet; the household's dispute became scripture.

**The Grammar of Direction and Place**

The exegetes pressed the verse's grammar with care, and their conclusions bound the permission they found in it. Al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Ṭabrisī note that the Arabic construction — come to your tilth *annā*, whence or however you will — denotes direction and place, and they devote their commentary chiefly to the two questions the wording governs: which part of the body, and from which direction. They are emphatic about what the verse does not address: not when, not how in the sense of manner's morality, and not at whose behest the act takes place. The jurists' unanimity supplies the boundaries the metaphor implies: intercourse during menstruation forbidden by the previous verse, anal intercourse forbidden at all times — the permission of *as you will* operates entirely within the tilth's single sowing-place. The translation's rendering — approach them consensually as you please — draws from the occasion its governing spirit: the will of the verse is a bounded freedom inside a marriage whose disputes revelation takes seriously, the freedom of direction and place fenced by the field's own nature. The permission is real — the customs' narrowness overruled — and its fences are equally real, drawn by the same sentence that opens it.

**Send Forth for Your Souls**

The verse's second command turns the intimacy outward in time: send forth for yourselves — qaddimū, put forward, as one sends goods ahead to a destination. The exegetes read the sending as the performance of good deeds that will benefit the doer in the Hereafter, the same forwarding the Qur'an describes elsewhere: the day a soul finds what it did of good present (3:30 in substance), the provision sent ahead in the sūrah of the Cleaving and the Resurrection passages the commentators cross-reference. Within the marriage-context the command carries its immediate senses too: the child is the sending-forth par excellence, the soul's deposit into the future; and every act of the household — the affection kept lawful, the rights rendered, the kindness stored — is provision forwarded to the meeting the next clause names. The imperative transforms the verse's subject: the intimacy legislated is not a closed circuit of pleasure but a field whose harvest is forwarded — seed sown in the tilth of this world reaped in the soil of the next. The marriage, like the charity-verse's spending and the hajj's provisions, becomes an instrument of the two-worlds reflection the passage commanded (2:220): the household runs in this world and ships its yield to the other.

**Know That You Will Meet Him**

The verse's closing triad — be mindful of God, know that you will meet Him, give glad tidings to the believers — stations the whole ruling in eschatology. The meeting is the horizon the sending-forwards travels toward: the deeds forwarded arrive, and the spouses arrive, and the One met is the One whose tilth-legislation governed the household. The mindfulness commanded is the daily form of the meeting's knowledge — taqwā as the consciousness that the private is presided over, that the marriage's most intimate chamber is inside God's jurisdiction, that the permission of *as you will* is held under the audit of the meeting. And the glad tidings close the domestic series' first movement on its characteristic note of mercy: the believers — the ones who keep the gates, the boundaries, the forwardings — are given good news, the bashārah that runs through the sūrah from its opening pages (the glad tidings to the patient, 2:155) to its end. The bedroom verse ends in the evangelium: intimacy legislated, harvest forwarded, meeting certain, and over all of it the good news — the household's private law enclosed inside the public promise.

**The Verse Between Its Neighbors**

The verse's placement completes a pair with the menstruation-ruling and opens the series that follows. Before it: the question about menstruation answered with bounded restriction and beloved purification (2:222). After it: the oaths not made into hindrances (2:224), the oath against the wife legislated to four months (2:226–227), and the whole divorce-architecture (2:228–233). The pair 222–223 thus legislates the marriage's body in its two states — the cycle's suspension and the intimacy's permission — and both end where the series will keep ending: in the names and the meeting. The tilth-image holds the whole domestic legislation together: a field is a thing of seasons (the cycle), of bounded cultivation (the orifice), of forwarded harvest (the sending), and of the owner's audit (the meeting) — and the marriage that follows, through its oaths and estrangements and divorces and nursings, will be legislated as the care of that field in every weather. The verse's last word — the believers given glad tidings — leaves the household under the promise: the field cultivated in mindfulness, the harvest sent ahead, and the cultivators met, at the season's end, by the Giver of the news.
