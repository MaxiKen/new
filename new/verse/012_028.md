## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:28

> So when her husband saw that Joseph’s shirt was torn from the back, he said ˹to her˺, “This must be ˹an example˺ of the cunning of you ˹women˺! Indeed, your cunning is so shrewd!

**Expanded Commentary**

**He Looks for Himself Before He Speaks**

The verse opens with an act of verification. *Fa-lammā raʾā* — when he saw. The husband does not accept the witness's report at second hand, does not ask a servant to examine the garment and does not defer to anyone's opinion. He inspects the shirt with his own eyes and only then pronounces. That sequence is the reverse of verse 25, where his wife had already delivered a verdict before any inspection took place. The chapter is contrasting two ways of reaching a conclusion in a household dispute: one that begins with a claim and looks for support, and one that begins with an object and ends with a claim. Which of the two the text endorses is not stated, because it does not need to be. The man who verifies is the man who reaches the right answer.

**Who Speaks Is Genuinely Uncertain**

The pronoun in *he saw* can refer to the ʿAzīz or to the witness from her family, and the commentators preserve both readings. If the speaker is the witness, then the remark is the closing line of his own testimony, addressed to the husband as a conclusion from the evidence he proposed. If the speaker is the husband, it is the reaction of a man who has just discovered that his wife attempted a serious crime and prosecuted an innocent person for it. The second reading is the more natural, since the following verse continues with instructions to Joseph and to the wife that only the master of the house would give. The first reading has a certain force: it would make the witness the person who names the cunning, and therefore the only member of the family to say so out loud. The ambiguity is small and the content of the speech is unaffected.

**Kayd Is the Word This Chapter Thinks With**

The term rendered *cunning* here is *kayd*, and it is one of the sūrah's organising words. It names the brothers' plot against Joseph (12:5), the women's stratagem in this verse, Joseph's prayer that God turn their cunning away (12:33) and God's answer that He did so (12:34), and Joseph's later reference to his Lord's knowledge of their cunning (12:50). The chapter closes with the same vocabulary applied to the brothers' original conspiracy (12:102). Kayd is not a moral evaluation in itself; it is a description of concealed planning, and the sūrah uses it for the malicious and the protective alike. What the word tracks is the fact that every party in this story operates by arrangement rather than by openness, and that the arrangements are visible to God in every case.

**From One Woman to a Class**

The husband's exclamation generalises immediately: this is of the cunning of *you women*, and your cunning is great. A single act by a single person becomes a statement about a category. The move is rhetorically ordinary and analytically empty, and the chapter does not let it stand unexamined. The women of the city who gossip about her in verse 30 are not cunning in this sense; they are merely indiscreet. The women of verse 51 testify against her and in Joseph's favour, saying *“Allah forbid! We know nothing indecent about him.”* (12:51) The class accusation of verse 28 is therefore contradicted by the behaviour of the same class later in the sūrah, and the contradiction is left in the text. What the verse records is how a man in that position speaks, not how things are.

**He Admires the Method Instead of Condemning the Act**

*Inna kaydakunna ʿaẓīm* — your cunning is immense. The adjective is one of scale, not of blame. He does not say the act was vile, does not mention the indecency she attempted, and does not refer to the false accusation she made against a man in his own house. He remarks on the sophistication. That choice of emphasis tells the reader what this household values: competence in manipulation ranks above honesty in conduct. The observation is not a caricature. Verse 29 will show the same priorities in action, when the response to a proven attempt at seduction and a proven false accusation is an instruction to keep quiet and a suggestion that the offender seek forgiveness privately.

**What the Verse Does Not Contain**

No punishment is announced. No vindication of Joseph is offered to anyone outside the room. No inquiry is opened into how the situation arose, why the doors were locked, or why a purchased slave was left alone with the wife of the house. The husband's speech ends with an exclamation and verse 29 begins with damage control. The absence is the verse's most informative feature, because it explains everything that follows: the gossip of verse 30 could not be suppressed by a settlement that never addressed the facts, and the imprisonment of verse 35 could be decided by people who had already seen the proofs and preferred not to act on them. A household that responds to a crime with admiration for its technique will not protect the victim of it.

**The Reader Is Given the Verdict the Household Withholds**

Joseph is acquitted inside this verse and never acquitted publicly. The shirt has proved his account, the witness has stated it, and the master of the house has accepted it — all within four verses, in a room nobody else enters. Yet no announcement is made, no record is kept, and the man cleared of the charge remains a slave in the house of the woman who made it. The chapter therefore separates two things that legal systems usually fuse: the finding of fact and the consequence that follows from it. The finding is complete and correct. The consequence is deferred for ten more verses, and when it arrives it is imprisonment. That gap between being proven innocent and being treated as innocent is the precise injustice the sūrah is documenting.
