## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:20

> But no! In fact, you love this fleeting world,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Swings Back**

Four verses were spent instructing the Prophet about how to receive revelation, and now, with the particle *kallā*, the sūrah turns away from him and speaks to people in the plural. The shift is abrupt and it is meaningful. The parenthesis established that God guarantees His text; the accusation that follows is that the hearers do not want what the text offers. The two belong together. A people who loved what the revelation promised would not need to be told that they love something else, and the sūrah's structure puts the guarantee and the refusal side by side so that the refusal cannot be blamed on obscurity. The message has been made clear. The objection is not that it is unclear. The particle *kallā* appears three times in this short sūrah, and each appearance marks a turn in the argument rather than a simple negation. In v. 11 it closed the cry for escape; here it dismisses the whole posture of the person who receives the revelation and ignores it; later it will introduce the moment of death. The recurrence gives the sūrah its rhythm: a claim stated, a refusal marked, and the argument moved forward over the objection rather than around it.

**What the Word al-ʿĀjila Carries**

The word translated as fleeting is *al-ʿājila*, from a root meaning to hurry. The present world is named here for its speed rather than for its wickedness, and that is the whole diagnosis in a single adjective. What defines this life in the Qur'an's vocabulary is not that it is corrupt but that it is passing — it arrives quickly, it presses, it demands an answer now, and then it is gone. The opposite term, used throughout the Book for the next life, is *al-ākhira*, the later, the last. The pairing is temporal rather than moral. One is the thing in front of you that will not last; the other is the thing coming that will. To prefer the first is to prefer what is near over what is permanent, and the sūrah is accusing its hearers of exactly that calculation.

**The Charge Is Love, Not Preference**

The verb is *tuḥibbūna* — you love. Not you prefer, not you choose, not you overestimate. Love is the strongest word available, and it relocates the problem from judgement to affection. A person who miscalculates can be corrected with better information; a person who loves the wrong thing cannot, because the attachment is the obstacle rather than a consequence of it. This is consistent with the sūrah's earlier diagnosis. In v. 5 unbelief was traced to what a person *wants*, and here it is traced to what he loves. The argument of the sūrah runs through the will rather than through the intellect, which is why its appeals are almost entirely to what a person should fear and hope for rather than to what he should infer.

**The World Is Not Condemned Here**

Nothing in this verse, or in the Qur'an generally, requires contempt for created things. The lawful provisions and adornments God brought forth are declared to be for His servants in this life (7:32), and the sūrah of the Poets warns against the opposite error of withholding them. What is condemned is the ordering: *“Surely those ˹pagans˺ love this fleeting world, ˹totally˺ neglecting a weighty Day ahead of them.”* (76:27). The parallel verse in Sūrah al-Insān makes the structure explicit — loving the hasty thing and neglecting the weighty thing are two descriptions of one movement. A person does not have to hate the world to be ruined by it. He has to love it in the wrong position, ahead of the thing that outlasts it.

**Why Haste Is Fatal**

The danger in the adjective is that the hasty world does not feel temporary. It presses with the urgency of things that need deciding today, and the Hereafter, being absent, never presses at all. That asymmetry is the test. It is not a fair contest between two objects of equal presence, and the Qur'an's remedy is to make the absent thing present through remembrance, revelation, and the constant reminder of death. The sūrah has been doing precisely that for twenty verses — collapsing the sky, opening the record, closing the exits — so that the weighty Day acquires some of the urgency the hasty world has by default. The warning here is the conclusion of that effort: you are being hurried by a thing that is itself hurrying away. The Qur'an states the comparison in the plainest terms available: *“But you ˹deniers only˺ prefer the life of this world, even though the Hereafter is far better and more lasting.”* (87:16–17). Better and more lasting — the two adjectives are the argument. A person is not being asked to choose a worse thing out of duty. He is being told that he is choosing the smaller and the shorter, and the sūrah of Iron spells out why the near thing cannot bear the weight put on it: *“this worldly life is no more than the delusion of enjoyment.”* (57:20).

**Reordering, Not Renunciation**

If the disease is misordered love, the cure is not the destruction of the object but the reordering of the heart, and the Qur'an's prescriptions take that shape. Wealth is not abolished; it is put to work — *“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish.”* (3:92) — and the test is precisely that the thing given is the thing loved. Time is not abolished; it is filled with remembrance at its two ends. The world is turned from a rival into a resource by being spent rather than accumulated. That is why the sūrah's complaint is followed immediately by a description of two faces: the ordering a person lives by now is the ordering that will be visible on him then.
