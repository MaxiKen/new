## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:115

> They will never be denied the reward for any good they have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of those mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise That No Good Is Lost**

The verse states that whatever good such people do will not be denied to them, and it adds that God has full knowledge of those who are mindful of Him. It closes the three-verse description of the upright group with an assurance about their reward.

The assurance is stated in the strongest form the language provides, since the verb of denial carries the sense of refusing something that is due. The Book uses the same idea in other places when it assures people that their charity and their vows are known to God, and that nothing given has gone unrecorded (2:270). The sentence in this verse is the shortest version of that reassurance.

**What the Word for Denial Implies**

The word translated as denied shares its root with the Book's word for ingratitude, which is the refusal to acknowledge a benefit that has been received. Applying that word to the reward means that the good done by such people will not be treated as if it had never happened.

The choice is precise in a passage about people whose standing was doubted by others. The sūrah has just described a group whose worship and conduct the receiving community might have been tempted to discount, and the sentence removes the possibility of discount. What is promised is not a reward of a particular size but the acknowledgement that the deed occurred and belongs to the person who did it.

**The Reassurance for People Who Feared Their Deeds Were Wasted**

Commentators connected the verse with the situation of people from among the People of the Book who had accepted the message and were then taunted by their former coreligionists with the claim that everything they had done before was now worthless.

Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī both report this connection, and the verse's wording fits it. The sentence tells such people that the good they performed in their former community is not lost, and it does so without making the reward depend on the label they once carried. Some commentators read the verb in the second person, so that the verse addresses the believers directly and says that they will not be denied what they have done, which produces the same assurance in a different voice.

**God's Knowledge of the Mindful**

The second sentence shifts from the reward to the knowledge of the one who gives it. The Book's statement that God knows those who are mindful of Him explains how the first sentence can be true of deeds that nobody witnessed.

The group described in the preceding verses prayed at night and worked quietly at their good, and the Book has just described practices that are hidden from public view. The sentence about knowledge is what makes the promise about reward coherent: the deeds that no one saw are recorded by the one who saw them, and the standard for the reward is the mindfulness that accompanied the act rather than its visibility.

**Reward as Knowledge Rather Than Arithmetic**

The verse does not describe a scale or a measure, and it places the reward in the same sentence as God's knowledge. The Book's other statements about recompense often multiply or specify, and this one simply says that nothing is refused.

That way of putting the matter suits the passage's purpose, which is to settle a question about standing rather than to publish a tariff. A reader is left with the assurance that what he does is not lost and with the reminder that the one who receives the deed knows the state of the person who performed it. Both halves matter for anyone whose good deeds are not recognised in the world.

**How the Sentence Closes the Description**

The three verses about the upright group end with this promise, and the sequence is worth noticing. The group is described first by their worship, then by their beliefs and practices, and finally by the assurance that their reward is secure.

The order moves from the observable to the promised, which is the Book's usual direction in passages of this kind. Nothing in the description is drawn from claims the group makes about itself; everything is conduct, and the outcome is stated by God. The verse thus completes the portrait that began with the phrase about the People of the Book not being all alike, and it does so by attaching the reward to the conduct rather than to the community.

**The Balance the Passage Has Held**

The stretch of verses that ends here began with an address to the believers about being drawn away from their faith and continued through commands about unity and the warning against division. It then described the community's rank, the limits of what its opponents can do, the condition of those who fight against the message, and the upright minority among the People of the Book.

The passage's balance is the sūrah's method in miniature. Warnings are followed by commands, judgments on communities are followed by qualifications, and descriptions of conduct are closed with statements about reward and knowledge. This verse is the last element in that sequence, and the sentences that follow take up the theme of wealth and children and the fate of those who disbelieve, returning the reader to the contrast with which the sūrah's earlier passage began.

**What the Verse Does Not Measure**

The sentence promises that no good is denied, and it says nothing about how much a good deed is worth. The Book's statements about recompense elsewhere give the impression of abundance rather than of an exact scale, and this verse keeps the emphasis on the fact of the accounting rather than on its size.

The criterion the verse does name is in its final clause, where the reward is connected with mindfulness of God. That is the Book's consistent position on the value of acts: the deed's worth is tied to the state of the person who performs it, and the state is known to God alone. The passage's closing sentence therefore leaves the reader with what the Book considers sufficient, which is the assurance that nothing good is lost and that the one who sees the heart is the one who settles the account.
