## Sūrah an-Naṣr 110:2

> and you ˹O Prophet˺ see the people embracing Allah’s Way in crowds,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sign That Has to Be Seen With the Eyes**

The second condition is perceptual. *Raʾayta* — you saw — is a verb of sight, and the verse puts the Prophet ﷺ in the position of a witness rather than of someone receiving information. God could have said simply that the people have entered. Instead the sentence waits for the sight of it: when you *see* them entering.

That verb has a wider use in Arabic. *Raʾā* can mean to know or to judge, as when a speaker says "I see that this is so," and some commentators read it that way here, taking the verse to describe realization rather than observation. The dispute matters less than it looks, because both readings keep the weight on evidence. What the sūrah waits for is a state of affairs that can be checked in the world: crowds, visible, arriving. Faith at this scale leaves a social trace — delegations on the road, oaths taken in public, whole districts changing allegiance — and the verse treats the trace as the sign.

Sight as the criterion also fixes the promise inside a lifetime. A revelation that waits for something to be seen has to arrive while the one who is to see it is still alive.

**Afwājā Is the Qur'an's Word for a Procession**

The last word of the verse is *afwājā*, plural of *fawj*: a company, a troop, a group travelling together rather than singly. "In crowds" keeps the number but loses the movement — a *fawj* is a body of people on the road as one.

The Qur'an reserves this word for processions of consequence. The damned are driven to Hell *“in ˹successive˺ groups”* (39:71), and the righteous are led to Paradise under the same term (39:73); when the Trumpet is blown, humanity *“will ˹all˺ come forth in crowds”* (78:18). Each time *afwāj* describes a multitude moving toward a destination it cannot leave, company after company. Used here, the word gives the spread of Islam the shape of an eschatological procession: not scattered conversions but an ordered arrival.

The choice has a second effect. A crowd is anonymous. The verse lists none of the tribes that came, names no convert, and does not sort those who believed from those who merely submitted. What is recorded is the movement itself, which is why the sentence stays true long after the particular companies have been buried.

**What Changed When Mecca Fell**

For eight years after the Hijrah, Islam grew in Arabia one person or one household at a time. The obstacle was political as much as theological: while Quraysh held Mecca and the Kaʿbah, the religion looked like a rebel faction, and the tribes of the peninsula waited to see which side would win. The Prophet's own city standing outside his dominion was the last argument his opponents possessed.

The conquest ended it. In Ramadan 8 AH — January 630 CE — Mecca opened with almost no bloodshed, the idols around the Kaʿbah were broken, and a general amnesty covered the population that had driven the Muslims out eight years earlier. The next year is known in the sources as the Year of Delegations, *sanat al-wufūd*. Ibn Saʿd's Ṭabaqāt lists seventy-one tribal embassies reaching Medina in the ninth and tenth years, most of them to announce that a whole tribe had accepted Islam and to ask for teachers. That is the scene the verse describes: not the old trickle but companies arriving.

**"The People" Is Left Unqualified**

The subject is *al-nās* — the people — with no adjective, no tribe, no region attached. Two attempts to narrow it are recorded. Some exegetes take it to mean the Arabs in general, since they were the ones entering in companies; others identify a large body from Yemen that had recently accepted Islam, on the strength of reports about Yemeni delegations in Medina.

Neither narrowing is required by the text, and both weaken it. The general noun is what lets the verse be read as a standing description rather than a single census. Every later mass movement into the religion — in Persia, in Anatolia, along the Swahili coast, across the Indonesian archipelago — falls under the same phrase, because the phrase names nobody. Those who specify Yemen or the Arabs are answering a historical question about which crowds the Prophet ﷺ actually saw with his eyes; the reading that keeps the noun open answers the question the sūrah is asking. The unqualified word is the reason a chapter this short has been recited for fourteen centuries as a description of something still going on.

**Entering God's Way, Not Joining a Man's Party**

What the people enter is *dīn Allāh* — Allah's Way. They are not said to enter the Prophet's following, his party, or his state; the thing entered belongs to God. The possessive does real work. A movement that grows because its leader has won can always be read as allegiance to the leader, and the grammar forecloses that reading.

The same phrase carries the Qur'an's own definition: *“Certainly, Allah's only Way is Islam.”* (3:19). And this claim has to be held together with another Madinan verse, which states that faith cannot be forced: *“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood.”* (2:256). The two are reconciled by the amnesty at Mecca. The city was taken, the population was pardoned, and the entry that followed was chosen by people who had been given the option of remaining exactly as they were. That is why the Qur'an can describe mass conversion without embarrassment: the crowd arrived after the sword had been sheathed, not because of it.

**Why Triumph Is Made the Trigger for Worship**

The verse reporting the largest influx of the Prophet's mission sets up an order to praise God and ask His forgiveness. Success is the moment at which a movement stops examining itself. Victory produces credit, and credit is claimed — by generals, by tribes, by the winning side's own account of why it won. This sūrah answers that risk before it forms, by making the sight of the crowds a signal for worship rather than celebration.

The pattern holds wherever triumph appears in the Qur'an. Joseph, at the height of his power in Egypt, asks to die a believer rather than to go on reigning. Solomon, given the throne of a distant queen in the blink of an eye, asks whether he will prove grateful or ungrateful. The arrival of a good thing is treated as a test of a particular kind — the test of attribution. Will the recipient trace the gift to its giver or to himself? This verse puts the question in the plainest terms available: everything you asked for has arrived; now say whose it was.

**The Promise Fulfilled in Truth**

Two earlier revelations described this scene before it happened, and the verse reads as their discharge. During the truce year the Prophet ﷺ had been shown in a dream that he would enter the Sacred Mosque; the Qur'an confirmed the dream and explained the delay: *“Indeed, Allah will fulfil His Messenger's vision in all truth: Allah willing, you will surely enter the Sacred Mosque, in security... He knew what you did not know, so He first granted you the triumph at hand.”* (48:27). What the sūrah of Victory announced as coming, this sūrah reports as arrived.

The other promise was broader, and it carried a condition: *“Allah has promised those of you who believe and do good that He will certainly make them successors in the land, as He did with those before them; and will surely establish for them their faith which He has chosen for them; and will indeed change their fear into security—˹provided that˺ they worship Me, associating nothing with Me.”* (24:55). The condition and the crowds meet in the verse that follows.
