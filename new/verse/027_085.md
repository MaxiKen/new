## Sūrah an-Naml 27:85

> And the decree ˹of torment˺ will be justified against them for their wrongdoing, leaving them speechless.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Falls After the Question**

Verse 84 put a question to the assembled deniers. This verse records what follows: the word falls against them, and they do not speak.

The order of events is the substance of the passage. The charge was stated, an opportunity to answer was given, and only then does the decree take effect. That sequence is what distinguishes a judgment from a punishment. The Qur'an could have described the outcome without the intervening question and lost nothing in severity; including it establishes that the verdict was reached through a procedure in which the accused were heard. What the verse reports is that they had nothing to say when the moment came.

**"For Their Having Done Wrong"**

The cause is named: *bimā ẓalamū*, because they did wrong. The chapter used this exact phrase once before, at verse 52, where the emptied houses of Thamūd stood as a consequence of the same thing.

The repetition ties the two scenes together. What happened to a nation in history and what happens to the contingents at the judgment are given the same explanation in the same words, which is the chapter's method throughout — the pattern visible in the ruins is the pattern that operates at the end. Al-Ṭabarī identifies the wrong specifically as their denial of the truth and rejection of what was revealed. *Ẓulm* in Qur'anic usage means putting something where it does not belong, and the chapter has already applied it to the self: Sulaymān's confession and the queen's both used it of themselves.

**They Do Not Speak**

*Fa-hum lā yanṭiqūn* — and they do not speak. Al-Ṭabarī reads it as an inability to defend themselves: nothing remains that could be offered as a case.

The silence is not imposed by force. Nothing in the verse describes them being prevented; the natural reading is that the question of verse 84 admitted no answer. A man asked whether he condemned something without examining it, when he did, has only two options, and confession is not going to help him. The verse describes the position where argument has run out. Elsewhere the Qur'an depicts the deniers at the judgment talking a great deal — blaming their leaders, asking to be returned, disputing among themselves. Here, at the point where the specific charge is put, there is nothing.

**The Verb Chosen for the Silence**

*Nuṭq* is not the general word for speech. Its sense is articulate utterance, the forming of intelligible speech, and the chapter opened one of its most famous scenes with the related noun.

When Sulaymān said he had been taught *manṭiq al-ṭayr*, the speech of birds, it was this root. The chapter that began with a king given the power to understand articulate utterance in creatures that supposedly have none ends by describing human beings who have lost it. An ant addressed her colony in intelligible speech and was understood; a bird made a report and was believed; and the deniers, at the scene where speech would finally matter, produce none. The word is rare enough in the Qur'an that the echo across a single chapter is unlikely to be coincidence.

**Silence as Part of the Penalty**

Being unable to speak to God is treated in the Qur'an as a punishment in its own right, not merely as a symptom of having no defence. Other passages state plainly that God will not speak to certain people on the Day of Resurrection, and the commentators discuss this as a form of deprivation.

What is being withheld, on that reading, is relation. The whole of an-Naml has been a chapter of addresses: God speaking from a fire, a prophet praying, a queen writing to her council, a letter read aloud, six questions put to an audience. Every one of those is an exchange, and the chapter's positive figures are the ones who answer. To be present before God and unable to produce a word is to be outside the only conversation that the chapter has treated as mattering. The next verse turns back to the living and to a sign they can still consider.

**The Decree Is Described as Deserved**

The grammar attaches the falling word to a cause, and the causal particle is doing more than transition. The sentence is constructed so that the penalty and its ground arrive in the same clause.

That construction appears throughout the chapter's accounts of destroyed nations and it is always the same shape: an outcome, then the reason, stated as a fact about what the people did rather than as a justification offered on God's behalf. Thamūd's houses were emptied because they wronged. Lūṭ's people received a rain described as evil for those who had been warned. Nothing in any of these passages argues that the punishment was fair; the fairness is presented as evident from the pairing. The chapter's confidence on this point is worth registering, since a text anxious about the charge of arbitrariness would have argued rather than asserted.

**What the Scene Leaves the Living Reader**

This is the last verse of the judgment sequence, and the chapter does not linger over the punishment itself. There is no description of what happens next — no fire, no chains, no duration. The account stops at the silence.

That is a choice, and the Qur'an is capable of the other approach; other chapters describe the outcome in considerable physical detail. Ending here keeps the emphasis where the passage has had it from verse 83: on a procedure, a question, and an answer that was not available. The reader is left holding the question rather than the penalty, and the question — did you reject without examining — is one he can still do something about. The verse immediately following turns to the night and the day, which is evidence available to anyone reading, tonight.
