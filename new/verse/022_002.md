## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:2

> The Day you see it, every nursing mother will abandon what she is nursing, and every pregnant woman will deliver her burden ˹prematurely˺. And you will see people ˹as if they were˺ drunk, though they will not be drunk; but the torment of Allah is ˹terribly˺ severe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scale of the Terror Is a Human Scale**

Verse 1 called the quaking a dreadful thing and left the dread unspecified. Verse 2 supplies the measurement, and the measurement is not geological. Nothing here describes faults opening or buildings falling. What the verse describes is a mother letting go of her child.

That is how the Qur'an calibrates catastrophe throughout: by what it does to the bonds that ordinarily hold. The nursing mother is the figure in whom instinct and duty point the same direction; if that figure drops what she is holding, no social arrangement downstream of her survives either. The verse does not need to describe the ruined cities, because the ruin of a city is less alarming than the ruin of this.

**Drunk, and Yet Not Drunk**

The comparison is made and cancelled in a single sentence: you will see people as if they were drunk, though they will not be drunk. Staggering, slurred, uncomprehending — the outward signs of intoxication without any intoxicant. The cause is named in the clause that follows: the torment of God is severe.

The self-cancelling simile does real work. A description of drunkenness alone would have been read as a moral indictment, and the scene would have become a punishment for dissipation. A bare denial would have removed the image. Holding both at once keeps the scene in the register of terror rather than guilt: these people are not being punished for drinking; they are being unmade by fright, and fright can unmake anyone.

**Which Quaking? The Commentators Genuinely Differ**

The pronoun in "the Day you see it" has been referred to the quaking of verse 1 by most commentators, but the deeper disagreement concerns which event the quaking itself is. Three positions were recorded. Some take it as the portents that precede the end — the signs in the sky and the earth during the last age. Others take it as the destruction of the world itself. Still others place it on the Day of Resurrection, after the gathering has begun.

The stronger reading, in my judgment, is the second. The details point to a scene still inside time: pregnant women miscarrying and infants being abandoned belong to a moment at which bodies are still bodies and consequences still fall. Once the Resurrection is under way, miscarriage has no purchase — the dead are being raised, not born. The verse pictures the world ending while people are still doing ordinary things in it, which is what makes it frightful. The parallel passage in Sūrah al-Muzzammil describes the same moment with the mountains in motion: *“on the Day the earth and mountains will shake ˹violently˺, and mountains will be ˹reduced to˺ dunes of shifting sand”* (73:14).

**Why These Two Bodies**

The nursing mother and the pregnant woman are not chosen at random, and they are not a pair of examples of the same thing. The pregnant woman carries a future that has not yet arrived; the nursing mother sustains a life that has. Between them they cover the whole apparatus by which a generation hands itself on.

When the quaking comes, that apparatus is the first thing to fail. The verse is thus not only about fear but about a termination: what is interrupted is not merely individual life but succession. The end of the world in the Qur'an is never described as a slow dying out. It is an interruption, and interrupting the two bodies that carry continuity is the sharpest way to say so.

**A Note on the Participle**

The Arabic word for the nursing mother is *murḍiʿah*, the active participle — a woman who is *in the act* of nursing, not *mursiʿah*, one who has nursed in the past. The distinction is not pedantry. A woman who had finished nursing might forget her child for many ordinary reasons; the woman described here is at the breast when it happens.

The grammatical form therefore sharpens the point that the self-cancelling simile already made. This is not a lapse of character or a failure of affection. It is the strongest bond in the human animal, severed by an event large enough to sever it.

**"The Day You See It": When Seeing Arrives, Belief Is Too Late**

The Hour in this verse is something *seen* — *yawma tarawnahā*, the day you see it. Everything described in the verse is visual, and the seeing is placed at the front of the sentence before anything is described. The shift from hearing to sight is the shift the Qur'an marks as irreversible.

The principle is stated plainly elsewhere: *“On the Day your Lord’s signs arrive, belief will not benefit those who did not believe earlier or those who did no good through their faith”* (6:158). Faith is a response to what has not been seen, which is why the Qur'an calls the unseen its first article. Once the object is in front of the eyes, the act that was required becomes impossible — not forbidden, but impossible, because believing what you can see is not belief.

This gives the verse its urgency. It is not describing a spectacle that will be interesting to attend. It is describing the moment after which the thing that made a human being's life meaningful can no longer be performed. The nursing mother, the pregnant woman, the reeling crowd are all people for whom the time of decision has closed.

**The Last Clause Turns the Description Into an Address**

Up to the final clause, the verse has been a scene: women, children, staggering crowds. Then the register changes. "The torment of Allah is ˹terribly˺ severe" is not a detail of the scene; it is a statement to whoever is reading it, and it explains why the scene is being shown at all.

The Qur'an regularly breaks a description with an explanatory clause of this kind, and the effect is to prevent the reader from settling into spectatorship. The horrors are not tableaux for contemplation. They are *ʿadhāb* — torment — which means they are inflicted, which means there is a reason, and the reason is not chaos. Terror without a judge behind it would be tragedy; terror with a judge behind it is justice, and the verse insists on the second.
