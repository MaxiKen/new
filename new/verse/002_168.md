## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:168

> O humanity! Eat from what is lawful and good on the earth and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. He is truly your sworn enemy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Address to Humanity**

The verse addresses all people rather than the believers alone, and the call concerns what they may eat from the earth. The Book's address to humanity appears at points where a matter concerns everyone, and the subject of food belongs to that category since the instruction is based on what God has provided for all.

The command follows the description of the hereafter in the preceding verses, and the sequence gives the reader a change of register from the judgment of the next life to the conduct of this one. What the passage establishes is that the response to revelation begins with the ordinary acts of a person's day, and food is the first of them that the sūrah names.

**Lawful and Good**

The sentence pairs two conditions: what is lawful and what is good, and the commentators explained the first as what God has permitted and the second as what is wholesome, pleasant, and free of harm. The Book's usage of the second term elsewhere includes the pure things that the messengers have been given, and the community's teachers understood the pairing to cover both the legal question and the quality of what is eaten.

The two conditions together rule out what is forbidden and what is permitted but harmful, and they also exclude the practice of treating lawful things as though they were prohibited. The verse's phrasing therefore corrects both the people who eat what has been forbidden and those who forbid themselves what God has allowed.

**The Occasion Reported for the Verse**

The commentators report that the verse was revealed in connection with tribes who had made certain animals and crops unlawful for themselves, and the reports name groups that had adopted restrictions of their own devising. The plain import of the sentence is universal, and the community's teachers read it as addressing a practice that was widespread.

The reports show the connection between the verse and the passage that follows, in which the Book describes those who say that they follow what their ancestors practised. What the sūrah establishes is that the standard for what may be eaten is the permission of God rather than the custom of a community, and the sentence states the rule before the following verses describe those who resist it.

**The Footsteps of Satan**

The sentence then forbids following the footsteps of Satan, and the image appears elsewhere in the Book in the context of conduct that leads away from what God has commanded. The commentators explained the phrase as an idiomatic description of yielding to temptation and of adopting a course of action because it has been suggested.

The Book's use of the image gives the reader a way of understanding how a person arrives at a prohibition that God has not made. The footsteps are followed one at a time, and the community's teachers read the phrase as a warning about small steps rather than about a single decisive act. What the verse asks of the reader is that he examine the source of the standards he follows, and the image identifies the source of standards that are not God's.

**A Manifest Enemy**

The closing words state that Satan is a manifest enemy to human beings, and the Book uses the description in a number of chapters. The phrase identifies enmity that is open and known, and the community's teachers understood it as a statement about a danger that has been disclosed rather than concealed.

The reports connect the subject with the verse's occasion, and one report preserved from the Prophet ﷺ describes God stating that He created His servants as people inclined to the truth and that the satans diverted them from their religion and forbade them what God had made lawful. The verse's statement about enmity is therefore supported by a report that describes precisely the practice the sentence is correcting.

**What the Passage Makes of Food**

The verse treats food as a subject that belongs to the religious life rather than to private preference, and the Book's treatment of it elsewhere covers the slaughter of animals, the giving of thanks, and the feeding of others. The command to eat what is lawful and good places the subject in the same category as the commands to worship and to give.

The passage that follows the sentence continues with a statement about what Satan commands, and then with the account of those who refuse the invitation to follow what God has revealed. The reader is shown that the refusal to accept the standard God has set is connected with the conduct the verse corrects, and the whole section deals with the question of whose instruction a person follows.

The community’s scholars drew several principles from the passage, including the rule that what the law has not forbidden remains available to the believer. The sentence in this verse states that the earth and its produce are provided for human beings, and the command to eat from them is therefore a permission as well as an instruction. The reader is shown that the default position of the Book is that the creation has been made for use, and the restrictions that the following verses describe are exceptions to a rule of permissibility.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The sentence opens a teaching about food that occupies several verses and that returns in another chapter where the subject is treated at length. The Book's instructions about what may be eaten are given in the context of its teaching about obedience, and the verse here states the principle before the following verses name the specific prohibitions. The reader is given the general rule and the particular list in the order that lets him understand what the list is for, and the passage continues with a statement about what the enemy of humanity commands.

The sentence opens a section that deals with food and with the standards a community adopts, and it follows the account of the hereafter in the preceding verses. The Book has just described the fate of those who misled others and the regret of those who followed them, and it now turns to the ordinary conduct of the people it addresses. What the passage asks of the reader is that he apply the teaching of the sūrah to the food on his table, and the sentence states the standard before the list of what is forbidden is given.
