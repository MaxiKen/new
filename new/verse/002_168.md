## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:168

> O humanity! Eat from what is lawful and good on the earth and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. He is truly your sworn enemy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Address to Everyone, About Eating**

The verse's first word sets its scope: *yā ayyuhā al-nās* — O humanity. Not O believers, as so much of this sūrah's legislation begins, but the whole species, addressed on a matter that concerns the whole species identically: eating. The command is permission shaped as imperative — *eat from what is lawful and good on the earth* — and its audience is universal because its subject is the human table. The Qur'an's choice to open its food legislation with an address to everyone is deliberate theology: before eating is a matter of Islamic law, it is a matter of human existence, and the rules that will follow are presented not as the sectarian restrictions of one community but as the sane management of an appetite all people share. The earth is named as the source — *on the earth* — which makes the verse a statement about provision as well as permission: the food is already here, spread out, lawful in its origin, and the eating commanded is a reception of what has been furnished rather than an acquisition of something scarce.

**Halāl and Tayyib: Two Conditions on the Table**

The verse qualifies the food twice, and the two qualifications are distinct. *Ḥalāl* — lawful — is the legal category: what the Shariʿah permits, from a root whose range of meaning includes to untie, to set free, to unbind, the exact opposite of *ḥarām*, the forbidden and bound. *Ṭayyib* — good — adds the qualitative dimension the exegetes specify: pleasant, agreeable, wholesome, delicious. The pairing has generated a whole ethic: the merely permitted is not automatically the good, and the tradition reads ṭayyib as covering both the clean-and-wholesome and the decently-acquired — food lawful in kind but stolen in fact is not ṭayyib. The double condition answers the two ways eating goes wrong: the wrong thing, and the right thing wrongly gotten. It also frames the occasion of the verse's revelation, preserved by al-Qurṭubī: three tribal groups — Thaqīf, Khuzāʿah, and Banū Mudlij — had declared certain animal foods off-limits to themselves, binding what God had loosed, calling ḥarām what was ḥalāl ṭayyib. The command to eat is aimed at that self-starvation in God's name: the verse's first target is not indulgence but invented prohibition.

**Footsteps, Not Foot**

The second command names the danger with an image that has no better equivalent in English: *do not follow Satan's footsteps* — *khutuwāt*, the plural of a step, the tracks in sequence. The idiom, which the Qur'an repeats (2:208, 6:142, 24:21), was explained by the commentators as the concrete picture of yielding to temptation: nobody leaps to evil in one bound; the person walks into it, one printed track at a time, each step small enough not to alarm, each landing exactly where the previous step pointed. The plural is the whole doctrine — sin as a path rather than an event, with a walker who notices each individual footfall and never the direction. Applied to the verse's occasion, the footsteps are visible: an ancestral scruple about one animal is a single step; the scruple hardened into custom is the second; the custom declared divine law is the third; and the worshipper starving at a table God spread is the destination. This is why the food command and the footsteps warning share one verse: the invented prohibitions of the tribes were not eccentricities but tracks — Satan walking them slowly toward the point where human authority usurps the divine right to forbid.

**The Enemy Declared**

The verse closes by identifying the walker whose tracks these are: *he is truly your sworn enemy* — the manifest, declared, avowed enemy. The Qur'an repeats the formula at every station of Satan's campaign: God's own reminder in the Garden — *"did I not tell you that Satan is your sworn enemy?"* (7:22); Jacob's warning to Joseph about the brothers' plot — *Satan is a sworn enemy to humankind* (12:5); the command to speak what is best, because Satan sows discord — *a sworn enemy to humankind* (17:53); the Day's reproach — *"Did I not command you... not to follow Satan, for he is truly your sworn enemy"* (36:60). The insistence serves a military purpose: footsteps can be followed by mistake, but an enemy's movements cannot be mistaken once the enmity is known. Every small step of the path — the harmless scruple, the pious-sounding addition, the cautious extra prohibition — carries the signature of a declared adversary, and the verse transfers the reader's attention from the size of the step to the identity of the walker. Nobody following a sworn enemy's tracks is taking a neutral stroll.

**"I Made It Lawful; They Made It Unlawful"**

The footsteps of this verse have a prophetic description, preserved in a sermon-report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (2865) from ʿIyāḍ ibn Ḥimār: the Messenger of God said that Allah commanded him to teach the people what they did not know, including the divine words — the property I conferred on them is lawful for them; *My servants were created by Me inclined to pure devotion (ḥunafāʾ), until the satans came and pulled them away from that religion, forbidding what I had permitted and commanding them to set up partners against Me*. The report is this verse's commentary from the unseen side. The default of creation is a lawful table and an upright heart; the satanic intervention is precisely the double corruption this verse forbids — the invented prohibition (making unlawful what God loosed) and the association (worshipping what is not God). The tribes' scruples about animals were therefore not minor legal opinions but instances of the primordial sabotage: at every point where human religion starts forbidding what God permitted, a satanic footstep has been matched, and the walker is following a track laid at humanity's first fall.

**The Morsel and the Prayer**

The tradition attached one further consequence to eating within these two conditions. A Companion asked the Prophet how a person becomes one whose supplication is answered, and the answer was: purify your food, and your calling will be heard. A forbidden morsel, he warned, bars the eater's prayer for forty days; and flesh grown on unlawful earnings deserves nothing but the Fire. The report links the table to the sky by a chain the verse's own logic supports: the person walking Satan's footsteps toward forbidden food has taken the enemy's provision into his body, and the body funded by the unlawful prays with borrowed breath. Eating ḥalāl ṭayyib is thus not a private hygiene but a condition of the community's whole worship — which is why the verse that legislates it addresses all humanity, and why the believers will be given their own version of it in the verses ahead, with gratitude added as the table's second course.
