## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:266

> Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath and as they grow very old with dependent children, a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up? This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will reflect.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Nobody Would Answer Yes**

The verse takes the form of a question addressed to each hearer individually — *would any of you wish* — and the question's construction makes the answer impossible: no one wishes the described catastrophe, which means every hearer must imagine it as loss rather than choice. The garden described is the parable's maximum: date-palms and grapevines — the two capitals of Arabian wealth — with every kind of fruit, and rivers running beneath it, the water-supply the hilltop garden of the previous verse lacked. The image is the sūrah's own paradise-vocabulary deployed on earth: the Garden promised the believers is gardens under which rivers flow (2:25), and this garden has exactly that structure — which is why its destruction lands as sacrilege and not merely as misfortune. The commentators note the deliberate escalation across the section's parables: the dust on rock (2:264), the rain-fed garden on the height (2:265), and now the fully watered, fully stocked orchard at full maturity — the best possible case, positioned to receive the worst possible weather.

**The Hour of the Whirlwind**

The catastrophe's timing is the verse's real subject, and it is tripled. The owner is old — the garden's labor behind him, its fruit his remaining provision; he has weak progeny, the commentators glossing dhurriyyah ḍuʿafāʾ as young or helpless children and grandchildren who cannot work the land or replace it; and into this configuration comes the whirlwind — iḥṣār, the simoom-like wind — carrying fire, and the garden burns. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī both fix the point: the same garden destroyed in youth would be a setback and in childlessness a private grief, but destroyed at the end of a life, with dependents who have nothing else, it is total — the past's labor, the present's provision, and the future's inheritance consumed in one hour. The verse's rhetorical question works because the hearer automatically performs the arithmetic the owner can no longer redo: at that age, with those children, there is no replanting. The whirlwind does not subtract from the man's life; it annuls it — and annulment is precisely the section's governing verb, the lā tubṭilū the two preceding verses commanded against.

**The Giver's Reading**

The first interpretation the commentators give is the section's own: the parable depicts the giver who follows his charity with reminders and injury (2:264). The mapping is element by element — the garden is his lifetime of charitable deeds, the fruits are the rewards accumulating with the years, the fiery whirlwind is his boasting and his hurtful words arriving at the end of his life, and the weak progeny are the absence of any remaining good work that could intercede or rebuild for him. On the Day, in this reading, the man arrives with the record of a garden that burned: the annulment the verse commanded against has done to his charity exactly what the whirlwind does to the orchard — not at the moment of the reminder but at the moment of the reckoning, when the loss is discovered to have been total and the season for replanting is over. The reading gives the preceding diptych its third panel: the rock shows the gift that never grew, the hill-garden the gift that doubles, and the burnt orchard the gift that grew for decades and was killed in an hour — the worst of the three fates, because it wears the longest history.

**Ibn ʿAbbās's Reading**

The second interpretation, attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and recorded by al-Qurṭubī, widens the parable beyond charity: it depicts the one who performed good works through a life and then turned to evil works at the end, the later wickedness wiping out the value of the earlier good. On this reading the garden is not one category of deed but a whole record — prayers, fasts, charities, patience — and the whirlwind is the closing apostasy or the final crimes, and the weak progeny are the deeds' inability, once annulled, to testify or to help. The two readings are not rivals but scopes: the giver-reading is the section's local application, and the Ibn-ʿAbbās reading is the general law the local case instantiates — the annulment-principle running on every accumulated good, of which the spoiling of charity is one instance. The sūrah of Hūd states the principle's positive mirror — *good deeds wipe out evil deeds* (11:114) — and the parable exists because the reverse direction operates too: the fire, like the water, does its work completely, and the verse's old man is the portrait of a record where the last hour outranked every preceding season.

**The Reflective Refrain**

The verse closes with the formula the family-code's seal used two dozen verses earlier: *This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will reflect* — the kadhālika-yubayyinu refrain with the reflection-verb, the pairing the wine-and-gambling legislation closed with (2:219) and the code's seal varied to understanding (2:242). The verb choice fits the parable: reflection — tafakkur — is the work the question-form demands, the hearer's slow imagining of the garden, the age, the children, the wind. The commentators note the refrain's function at parables throughout: at 2:266 it stands after a picture with no explicit application, leaving the mapping to the reflection commanded — the verse does not say the garden is your charity record; it asks whether you would wish it, and lets the wish expose what you already know the garden to be. The clarity named in the refrain is thus not the clarity of a labeled diagram but of a question that cannot be answered dishonestly: the signs are made clear precisely when the hearer can no longer pretend the parable is about someone else.

**Why the Section Needs the Fire**

The spending-section has now run its full rhetorical arc, and the burnt garden is its keystone. The parable of the grain promised multiplication (2:261); the conditions protected the deed from its aftermath (2:262–263); the rock exposed the deed that was never a deed (2:264); the hill-garden showed the weather of grace (2:265). What the arc lacked was the tragedy of time — the case where the deed was real, the growth was genuine, and the ruin came anyway, from inside the giver's own later conduct. The verse supplies it, and its supplied case is the one every long-term giver secretly fears: not the barren start but the late collapse, the record burnt on the eve of its presentation. The old man's helplessness — no strength to replant, no children strong enough to do it for him — is the eschatological moment's exact portrait, since the Day is when every account closes and no amendment remains possible. The reflection the refrain commands is therefore practical and immediate: the fire in the parable is lit by reminders, injuries, and late turnings — all three of which are choices still available to refuse, in the season where refusal still does something.
