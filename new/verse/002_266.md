## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:266

> Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath and as they grow very old with dependent children, a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up? This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will reflect.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put**

The sentence asks whether any of the community would wish for the situation it describes, and the community's teachers read the question as the Book's method of bringing a matter home to the hearer. The passage has presented the two harvests, and the question here presents the loss that a person may meet at the very end of his life.

The form of the address has been noticed by the commentators, since the sentence does not describe a stranger but places the hearer within the scene. The Book's use of the question in its parables appears elsewhere, and the teachers gathered the instances when they explained the passage.

The wording also names the garden as the possession of the person addressed, so that the wish is presented as the natural desire of any owner. The loss that follows is therefore measured against what the person had valued most.

**The Garden Described**

The sentence describes a garden of date palms and vines with rivers flowing beneath it and every kind of fruit within it, and the commentators identified the scene as the image of the abundance that the Book uses for the reward of the believers.

The teachers noted the elements of the description and the order in which they are named. The palms and the vines supply the produce of the settled land, the rivers supply the water on which the garden depends, and the fruits of every kind complete the account of a possession that leaves nothing to be wished for.

The community's scholars observed the connection between the figure and the earlier verses about the harvest of the giver. The garden of the parable stands for the wealth and the good deeds of a lifetime, and the sentence describes the condition of the person who has gathered them.

**The Old Age and the Children**

The sentence adds that the owner has grown old and that his children are weak, and the commentators explained the clause as the account of the moment at which the loss is least bearable. The person described has no strength to build again and no children who could take up the work that he has left.

The teachers drew attention to the detail of the children, since the Book's image of the household's continuation is present in the scene. The hope of the owner was placed in the generation that follows him, and the sentence describes it as unable to help.

The wording thus completes the picture of a person whose resources have been gathered into one place. The community's scholars read the passage as the description of the situation in which every support has been removed, and the question that opens the sentence applies to it.

**The Fiery Whirlwind**

The sentence then describes the whirlwind that contains fire and consumes the garden, and the community's teachers read the image as the statement about the event that destroys what the person had built. The figure gathers the elements of a storm and of a burning, so that nothing of the garden remains.

The commentators connected the whirlwind with the conduct that the previous verses have described, since the reminder of the gift and the injury that follows it are the wind that removes the harvest. The teachers explained the sentence as the picture of a person who stands before the account of his life with nothing left of the acts in which he had trusted.

The reading was preserved in the community's teaching of the passage, and the scholars drew from it the lesson that the value of a deed is not secure until the end of the life in which it was performed.

**The Application to the Givers**

The commentators applied the parable to those who spend their wealth and then ruin the act by their conduct, and they described the account of such a person in the life to come. The Book's earlier verses have compared the gift to a seed and to a garden, and the sentence here presents the loss of the whole harvest.

The teachers also drew a wider application from the passage, since the person described in the sentence is one whose deeds are gathered into a single store that an event removes. The community's scholars used the figure when they spoke about the need to protect good works from the faults that destroy them.

The sentence therefore supplies the counterpart of the parables that precede it. The two earlier figures have compared the grounds on which the rain falls, and the figure here describes what becomes of the garden when the last day of a life brings the storm.

**The Other Reading**

The commentators preserved a further explanation of the sentence, transmitted from one of the early authorities, which applies it to the person who performs good works and then turns to evil ones so that his earlier deeds are wasted. The teachers set out the reading beside the first and noted the subject they share.

The material shows the care with which the community's scholars handled the parables of the Book, since they gathered the explanations and did not confine the passage to one application. The verse's concern in each case is the end of a person's work, so that the readings serve the same lesson.

The wording of the sentence supports the wider application, since the garden may stand for the deeds of a lifetime rather than for a gift alone. The community's scholars used the passage when they warned the believers against the conduct that follows a good act.

**The Closing Invitation**

The sentence closes with the statement that God makes His revelations clear so that the community may reflect, and the community's teachers read the clause as the statement of the purpose that accompanies the parables. The figures have been presented in order for the hearer to consider what they require of him.

The Book's habit of attaching the invitation to reflect to its parables appears throughout the sūrah, and the community's scholars gathered the instances when they explained the passage. The sentence in this place therefore ends with the call that makes the image a matter for the hearer's own account.

The teachers drew the lesson that the community is expected to read its own life in the figure. The garden, the old age, the children, and the storm belong to the experience of the hearer, and the sentence leaves him to consider which of the two harvests he is preparing.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the parable of the garden that is consumed at the end of a life, the condition of the owner whose strength and children have failed him, the destruction that removes the whole harvest, the application of the figure to the acts that are ruined by what follows them, and the invitation to reflect that closes the passage. The community's teachers used the verse when they spoke about the security of deeds at the end of life, and the sūrah then returns to the instruction about the wealth that is spent, so that the parable and the command stand beside one another.
