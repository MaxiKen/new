## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:47

> They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting.
**Expanded Commentary**

**The Taunt**

*Wa-yastaʿjilūnaka bi-l-ʿadhābi* — and they ask you to hurry the punishment. The verb is the intensive form of "to hasten": they keep asking, and the asking is a form of mockery rather than a request for information.

The demand appears repeatedly in the Qur'an, always in the same key. The opponents do not say that the punishment is unjust; they say it is late, and they treat the lateness as evidence that it is not coming. The challenge is designed to be unanswerable: a messenger who produces the punishment looks vindictive, and a messenger who does not produce it looks mistaken.

The demand is recorded elsewhere in the same words, and in Makkah it was once phrased as a prayer: *"O Allah! If this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down stones upon us from the sky or overcome us with a painful punishment"* (8:32). Sūrah Yūnus supplies the answer the demand invites: *"Imagine if His torment were to overcome you by night or day—do the wicked realize what they are ˹really˺ asking Him to hasten? Will you believe in it only after it has overtaken you? Now? But you always wanted to hasten it!"* (10:50–51). The reply exposes the request as something other than a request for evidence. A punishment that arrives is the end of the period in which evidence could be answered at all, and the taunt is therefore a demand that the testing be brought to a close.

**The Answer's First Half**

*Wa-lan yukhlifa Allāhu waʿdahu* — and God will not fail His promise. The sentence denies the premise rather than the timing.

What the mockers have implied is that a delayed promise is a broken one. The verse says that the promise stands, which relocates the difficulty entirely: the punishment is certain, and the only variable is when. That is why the verse's second half is about time rather than about power — the objection is not that God cannot do it, and the answer is not that He can.

The grammar is emphatic. *Lan* is the strongest available negative, and it negates a verb of failing: God will not, ever, break His appointment.

The same claim about God appears on the other side of the argument, in the prayer of the believers: *"Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise"* (3:9). What the faithful rely on as their hope, the mockers cite as their proof. The verse's point is that the attribute is one and the same in both mouths, and that a promise which can be waited on is not the sort of promise that lapses.

**"A Day With Your Lord Is Like a Thousand Years"**

*Wa-inna yawman ʿinda rabbika ka-alfi sanatin mimmā taʿuddūna* — and a day with your Lord is like a thousand years of what you count.

The commentators proposed several referents. Some took the day as one of the six days of creation mentioned elsewhere in the Qur'an, comparing it with the statement that a day with God measures a thousand years by human reckoning (32:5). Others took it as one of the days of the Hereafter, meaning the Day of Resurrection or the day of reckoning itself. Al-Rāzī recorded a further reading: that a day of punishment will *feel* to the sufferer like a thousand years, so the delay is not the point — the duration, once it begins, is.

The figure has a larger neighbour that the commentators had to reconcile with it: *"˹through which˺ the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit will ascend to Him on a Day fifty thousand years in length"* (70:4). Their answers differed — that the thousand and the fifty thousand describe different days, or different experiences of one day, or the reckoning as a whole against a single stage of it — but they agreed on what the numbers are doing. Neither is being offered as a measurement. Both are the largest available figures pushed against a duration that has no human equivalent, and the argument of the verse is unaffected by which reading is taken.

**Which Reading Is Stronger**

The second reading fits the context best. The verse is answering a challenge about punishment; the most natural sense of "a day with your Lord" in that context is a day in which the divine reckoning is executed, not a day of creation. The parallel verse supports it: *“He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him on a Day whose length is a thousand years by your counting”* (32:5).

But the readings are not exclusive, and holding them together is more faithful to the verse than choosing. What all three share — and what the verse needs — is that the human and divine measures of duration are not the same. A thousand years is not a threat; it is a statement about scale. From the divine side of the comparison, the interval between the warning and the punishment is not long. From the human side, it is the whole of a life.

**The Taunt's Own Logic**

There is a further point the Qur'an makes about demands of this kind, and it is worth stating here because it completes the answer. A person demanding the punishment as a proof would not be satisfied by it if it came, because the demand is not really for evidence.

The sūrah has already said as much about the Hour: once the signs arrive, believing does nothing for a person who did not believe before (6:158). The demand to hurry the punishment is, structurally, a demand that God settle the question in a way that removes the need for faith — and the whole design of the testing period is that the question is *not* settled that way.

The delay the mockers cite against the Prophet is the same delay that keeps them alive to object. *"If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term"* (16:61). The respite is not a gap in the system; it is the system. Every day of it is a day in which the objection can still be withdrawn, and the taunt is an argument against the very arrangement that makes the taunter's existence possible.

**The Number, and the Phrase After It**

The figure is *alf sanah*, a thousand years — the largest round number in ordinary Arabic usage, and chosen for exactly that reason. It is not a measurement; it is the limit of what a listener can picture, offered as a token of something that cannot be pictured.

The words that follow matter more than the number. *Mimmā taʿuddūna* — of what you count. The measure being converted is ours: our days, our arithmetic, our sense of what counts as a long time. The verse does not say that God experiences a day as a thousand years, which would merely relocate the clock. It says that the two measures are incommensurable, and that the human one is the one being set aside.

That is the answer to the taunt. "Late" is a judgement made inside a system of counting, and the verse says the divine promise is not running on that system. A person who concludes from a decade of silence that nothing is coming has applied his own arithmetic to something that was never on his clock.

**What the Verse Does for the Prophet**

The practical effect of the verse is to remove the taunt's sting. A messenger told that a day with God is a thousand years is not being told that his opponents will wait a long time for punishment. He is being told that the delay he is mocked for is not a delay at all from the only vantage point that counts.

What he is given is not a date. It would be a misreading to hear the verse as "wait a little longer," because nothing in it promises that the wait will be short measured in his years — and the sūrah will go on to describe a community that emigrates, fights, and is tested long before it returns to the sanctuary (22:39–41, 22:58). What he receives instead of a date is a scale, and the scale is what makes the wait answerable to him: the silence is not accumulating against the promise, because the promise is not being counted in the units he is being mocked in.

That is the same consolation the catalogue of verses 42 to 44 offered from the other side: the deniers were given length, and then they were taken. Here the length is explained; there it was documented. Between them, the sūrah has answered both halves of the objection — that the punishment is late, and that being late means it is not coming.
