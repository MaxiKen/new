## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:210

> Are they waiting for Allah ˹Himself˺ to come to them in the shade of clouds, along with the angels? ˹If He did˺, then the matter would be settled ˹at once˺. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Do They Wait for Anything Less?**

The verse answers the suspended question of the verse before with its full scene: do they wait for anything less than that God should come to them in the shadows of the clouds, and the angels, and the matter be decided? The interrogative's form — do they wait for nothing else but this? — carries the rebuke: what the waiters are deferring toward is not a negotiable future but the terminal event, the one arrival after which nothing remains to wait for. The verse thus names the posture it condemns: the life lived in the assumption that the decisive moment is indefinitely postponed, that the proofs can be received and slipped against (2:209) because the reckoning waits politely at the horizon. The clouds of the scene are the scripture's fixed apparatus of theophany — the same clouds the Day's own description uses: the day the heavens are split open with the clouds and the angels are sent down in ranks (25:25) — and the verse imports that whole imagery into the present tense of the waiting: what they wait for has a described form, a published manner of arrival, and a settled consequence, and the waiting is therefore not vagueness but appointment-keeping with their own end.

**The Coming in the Clouds, and Its Reading**

The exegetes handled the verse's grammar and its theology with equal care. Al-Ṭabarī records an alternate parsing: that God should come, and the angels in the shadows of the clouds — the cloud-apparatus attaching, on this reading, to the angels rather than to the Divine coming itself. Others read the coming as the coming of God's command or judgment: what arrives in the clouds is the thing God has promised or threatened, the decree's execution rather than a bodily transport. And the tradition fixed two settings for the scene: some connect it to the angels who come at the moment of death — the arrival each soul waits for privately, its matter decided at the last breath; others, al-Rāzī among them, read it as the description of the Day of Resurrection itself, with 25:25's split heavens and descending angels as the parallel text. The readings share a structure: an arrival, a cloud-apparatus, an angelic company, and a decision that the arrival renders irreversible. The variety is the verse's own invitation — the coming concerns every waiter at the hour that is his, the deathbed's or the Day's, and in both the shadows are the same shadows the scripture has already described.

**Not Movement, Not Place**

The commentators are careful — and the care is itself part of the verse's reception — to state what the scene cannot mean: it cannot imply that God moves from place to place, or is located in a place at all, since location would limit God and introduce change into His nature. The cloud-coming is therefore read, across the tradition's schools, either as the coming of the command and the angels (the created apparatus of the decisive hour), or as an arrival befitting the Divine majesty without modality — a real coming whose how no creature's categories contain. The theological precision serves the verse's rhetorical purpose rather than deflating it: the point is not that God travels but that the decisive hour arrives with the full weight of His authority, clouded and attended and final. The waiter who imagines the postponement safe because no visible arrival has occurred is answered by the scene's own grandeur — the heavens split, the angels ranked, the shadows filling the sky — an arrival that will not be missed, not deferred, and not survived as postponement. The verse's theology and its warning are one: the Coming is certain precisely because the Coming One is beyond the creaturely limits the waiters implicitly assume.

**The Matter Decided**

The scene's third element is the decision: and the matter has been decreed. Al-Rāzī explains the finality the clause carries: it refers to the Hereafter's decisive character — at that arrival the deeds have already been done, and there is no adding to them and no subtracting from them. The probation is over; the record is closed; the portion earned (2:202) is fixed. The Qur'an states the same closure in the sūrah of the Cattle's answer to the demand for visible angels: had We sent down an angel, the matter would have been decided, and they would be given no respite (6:8) — the angelic arrival and the decision's finality bound together there exactly as here. The clause is the verse's answer to the waiting posture at its root: what the deferrer is waiting for is the end of waiting — the hour whose whole function is to settle, after which no slip can be recovered (2:209), no entry completed (2:208), no soul sold (2:207), no portion altered. The matter decided is the swift reckoning arriving in scene-form: the audit the previous verses described as instantaneous disclosed at last as event — the clouds, the angels, and the book that no longer changes.

**To God All Matters Return**

The verse's final clause widens the decision into the cosmos's constant: to God all matters are returned. The formula is among the scripture's most repeated closures — to Him belong the matters' return in Āl ʿImrān, in the Spoils, in Hūd, in the Pilgrimage, in the Angels, in Iron (3:109; 8:44; 11:123; 22:76; 35:4; 57:5) — and its sense here is the waiting's refutation: the matters do not drift, defer, or belong to the interval; every affair is already in return-motion toward its Owner. The return is understood primarily as the Hereafter's gathering — the matters of every soul delivered back for the settlement — but its grammar holds the present tense too: the matter is returned at every moment of its existence, since nothing is ever out of His hands long enough to be lost. For the waiter the clause collapses the postponement's illusion: the arrival he defers toward is not a future interruption of the present order but the disclosure of the order the present already keeps — matters returning, accounts moving, the reckoning swift. The verse thus ends where the hajj-coda ended (to Him you will be gathered, 2:203) and where the whole sūrah's legislation has been pointing: every law a matter in return, every slip a matter in return, every prayer a matter already answered in the returning.

**The Verse Between Slip and Sign**

Within the passage's movement the verse holds the middle position between two addresses. Behind it: the command to enter completely, the warning of the slip after the proofs, the knowledge of the Mighty and Wise (2:208–209). Before it: the turn to the Children of Israel and their clear signs (2:211), the world made fair to the mockers (2:212), humanity's one community and the Book sent to judge its differences (2:213). The cloud-scene is the hinge between the community's internal danger and its historical lesson: the believers who might slip are shown the terminus their slipping defers toward, and then shown the people whose story proves the terminus real — the Israelites who received sign after sign and altered the favor after it came. The waiting-question binds both panels: the slipper waits as though the decision were far; the sign-recipients lived as though the favor were theirs to change; and the clouds arrive for both at the hour that ends both illusions. The verse leaves its reader with the posture the whole passage commands — the entry complete before the shadows fill the sky, the slip recovered before the matter is decided, the knowledge of the Mighty and Wise held until the return that holds every knower.
