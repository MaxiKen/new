## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:199

> who would then recite it to the deniers ˹in fluent Arabic˺, still they would not have believed in it!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Even Fluent Recitation Would Not Satisfy Them**

The hypothetical reaches its conclusion: a non-Arab messenger could recite the Quran in fluent Arabic, and the deniers would still refuse to believe. This removes the idea that their problem is simply a foreign accent or an inaccessible language. They are imagined hearing the same clear recitation they claim to need, yet their response remains denial.

The verse does not mock the value of language. Clear communication matters, and revelation is delivered in a form the first audience can understand. It exposes the difference between a real obstacle and a condition invented after the fact. When the requested condition is supplied and belief still does not follow, the deeper resistance becomes visible.

**The Excuse Moves Because the Heart Has Settled**

A denier can say that he wants proof while secretly deciding that no proof will be accepted. He can demand a different messenger, then a different language, then a different kind of sign. The problem is not a lack of possible presentations. It is the refusal to let any presentation carry moral authority over him.

Quran 41:44 describes the same kind of objection from another angle: a non-Arabic revelation would be criticized by an Arab audience for being in the wrong language. The two passages expose a double bind. Arabic is rejected because it is familiar and foreign speech would be rejected because it is foreign. The standard is designed to protect disbelief, not to discover truth.

**Understanding and Believing Are Different Acts**

A person can understand the words of a warning and still reject the duty that follows. Language gives access to meaning; it does not force the will to accept the meaning. This distinction explains why the deniers' fluent-recitation scenario ends with disbelief. Their imagined difficulty has been removed, but their unwillingness remains.

The distinction also protects the Quran from being reduced to sound alone. Recitation has beauty and power, yet the listener must allow the message to judge conduct. A person who praises Arabic eloquence while continuing to defraud people has heard the form without submitting to the command. Belief joins comprehension to obedience.

**A Moving Standard Cannot Defeat a Clear Message**

The deniers' hypothetical shows how a community can make disbelief appear intellectual. Each new condition sounds reasonable until the conditions are placed together. Then their purpose is clear: they keep the messenger, language, and evidence in permanent disqualification so that no answer can reach the heart.

The Quran responds by exposing the pattern rather than chasing every excuse separately. It shows that the same people would not have believed even after receiving the feature they claimed to want. The decisive question is therefore not whether revelation could be presented differently. It is whether the hearer is willing to recognize the Lord's claim when the message is already clear.

This is why the verse is more than a statement about an imaginary reciter. It exposes the structure of bad faith. A person names a condition that appears to be about evidence, but when the condition is supplied he invents another. The endless movement keeps him looking outward for a defect while preventing him from asking what he would owe Allah if the message were true.

The fluent Arabic in the hypothetical also shows that eloquence alone cannot save a resistant audience. A beautiful recitation may open attention, but it does not remove pride, greed, or fear of losing social position. The deniers could have received the sound clearly and still refuse the command because obedience threatened the order from which they benefited.

The lesson applies to moral knowledge beyond language. Someone may demand a perfect teacher before accepting a principle, a complete guarantee before making a just decision, or an extraordinary sign before abandoning a known wrong. These demands can sound careful, but they become dishonest when ordinary evidence has already established the duty. No human presentation will satisfy a heart that has made refusal its goal.

The verse does not excuse careless communication. Teachers should explain, translators should be accurate, and callers should speak in a way people can understand. But once clarity is available, the hearer must not pretend that better packaging will solve a problem of will. The deniers' imagined scenario proves that the barrier lies deeper than pronunciation.

Belief therefore has an ethical dimension. It is not simply agreeing that a sound was recited correctly. It is allowing the meaning to govern conduct, even when the meaning demands repair, humility, or loss. The Quran describes the deniers so that the hearer can recognize the pattern before it becomes his own.

A moving standard is exhausting for a community because it can never produce certainty. If the messenger is local, he is dismissed as too familiar; if he is foreign, he is dismissed as unintelligible. If the recitation is plain, the denier demands a miracle; if a sign appears, he demands a different sign. The verse exposes the pattern so that a person can stop confusing endless demands with careful thought.

This does not mean that every question is bad faith. People may need clarification, and a teacher may need to explain how a message applies to a new situation. The test is whether the answer makes the person more willing to follow truth or merely supplies a new escape. Honest inquiry has a destination: understanding that leads to a responsible choice.

The fluent recitation imagined here also preserves the distinction between the Quran's form and its source. Arabic eloquence matters because it carries the revelation accurately, but the authority of the message is not created by the audience's taste. A listener may dislike a style, yet dislike cannot disprove the Lord's command. The deniers' refusal remains morally significant because the message has reached them clearly.

For a believer, the safeguard is to ask what evidence would genuinely change the mind. If the answer is nothing, then the obstacle is no longer proof. It is pride, fear, attachment, or the desire to keep a profitable wrong. The verse calls for that self-examination before a reasonable request becomes a permanent wall against guidance.
