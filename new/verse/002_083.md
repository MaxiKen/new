## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:83

> And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, “Worship none but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy; speak kindly to people; establish prayer; and pay alms-tax.” But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away—except for a few of you—and were indifferent.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charter of the Covenant**

With the eschatological interlude closed — the numbered-days claim rebutted, the two eternities filed — the recital returns to its central exhibit and quotes the covenant itself. *And remember when We took a covenant from the children of Israel*: the verse sets down the terms in the order a complete life requires them. First the vertical bond — *worship none but Allah*. Then the circle of obligation widening outward: *parents*, then *relatives*, then *orphans and the needy* — the claims of blood, of kinship, of helplessness. Then the whole of society in a single clause — *speak kindly to people*. And finally the two standing rites, *prayer* and *alms-tax*, the daily and the material disciplines that keep the charter in force. The exegetes recognize the list's genre immediately: it belongs with the Qur'an's great universal commandment-passages, the decalogue of Sūrah al-Anʿām (6:151–153) and the long ethical charge of Sūrah al-Isrāʾ (17:23–39), which the tradition counts among the *muḥkam* — the clear, foundational verses on which the rest of revelation stands. What is being quoted here is therefore not a tribal contract but the moral constitution of revealed religion itself, the same charter in every prophet's hand — which is precisely why its violation, filed in the verse's closing clause, indicts something deeper than one community's history.

**Allah's Right, the Servant's Right**

The charter's first term is its foundation, and the Prophet ﷺ once stated it as a paired doctrine of rights. Riding behind him on a donkey, Muʿādh was told: *"Allah's right on His slaves is that they should worship Him (Alone) and should not worship any besides Him. And slave's right on Allah is that He should not punish him who worships none besides Him"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2856). The covenant of verse 83 opens with exactly this right — worship none but Allah — and the ḥadīth supplies the reciprocal: the servant who keeps the first term holds a promise against punishment, the divine counterpart to the human obligation. The pairing explains why the charter begins where it does. Every other term — kindness, speech, prayer, alms — is a branch of the first; a kindness performed for social credit, a prayer offered to be seen, an alms given to purchase standing all fail at the root the root-term plants: that the act belongs to Allah alone. The sūrah's own theology of forgiveness draws the same line from the other side: association with Allah is the sin whose forgiveness He has not placed in the ordinary economy (4:48). The covenant is thus offered on a single non-negotiable condition, and its fivefold social and ritual elaboration is what that condition looks like when it is lived — a structure the recital now holds up against the record of the people who received it first.

**The Four Claims of Kindness**

The social terms of the charter are ordered by nearness, and the ordering is instruction. Parents first: the Qur'an honors them with a standing emphasis — the command runs through the Book in passage after passage (17:23, 29:8, 31:14, 46:15) — and the exegetes note that the duty binds whether the parent believes or not, pointing to Abraham's restraint with his unbelieving father (19:42–48) as its outer limit case; the prophetic tradition crowns the mother specifically, in the saying that Paradise lies at the mothers' feet. Then *relatives* — in the Arabic *dhū al-qurbā*, the possessors of nearness — the ring of blood whose claims the Qur'an files again and again beside the poor (2:177, 4:36, 17:26). Then *orphans* — the *yatīm*, classically the child whose father has died, the minor stripped of its provider and protector, whose case the Book legislates at length (4:3–10, 6:152, 107:2). Then *the needy* — the claim of want itself, owing nothing to kinship, resting entirely on the covenant. The sequence walks outward from the household to the stranger, and its moral logic is deliberate: kindness is trained on the near and tested on the far. A community that keeps the inner rings while neglecting the outer has not kept the charter, and a community that performs for strangers while devouring its own orphans has performed, not obeyed. The four claims together define the covenant's horizontal face — and the recital's audience is being shown what Allah considers the minimum furniture of a just community, in terms no one hearing it could call particular to any tribe.

**A Goodly Word to All People**

Between the circle of kindness and the rites stands a clause so general the exegetes marked its generality expressly: *speak kindly to people* — to people, without qualification. Al-Ṭabarī's reading is explicit that the command applies to all human beings, not to the community's own members only, and the interpreters gather under it the whole ethics of the tongue: the call to tawhid, the commanding of the right and the forbidding of the wrong (3:104), ordinary courtesy in ordinary exchange. The clause's placement does quiet work against the passage just behind it. The recital has spent ten verses documenting crimes of speech — the word distorted after understanding (75), the two rooms' two scripts (76), the hearsay passed off as certainty (78), the book written by hands and warranted as divine (79), the claim about Allah made without knowledge (80). Against that catalog, the covenant's speech-term is the counter-law: one tongue, kindly used, owing the same honesty to every listener. The verse thereby answers the conspirators of verse 76 in advance — a charter that commands the goodly word *to people* cannot be kept by a community that speaks one language in the meeting and another in the private room. Speech in the covenant is not decoration on the moral life but its most public organ, and its kindness is not softness: the same mouth owes the call to monotheism, which is the hardest and kindest thing it can say.

**Prayer and Alms-Tax: The Ritual Seal**

The charter closes as the sūrah's own address to the believers closed at its start — with prayer and zakāh, the pair the recital has joined since verse 43, where the community hearing this history was told: *establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and bow down with those who bow down* (2:43). The pairing is the covenant's machinery: prayer keeps the first term alive in the body's daily rhythm, alms-tax keeps the kindness-terms alive in the property's annual circulation — the vertical bond rehearsed five times a day, the horizontal claims discharged as an obligation rather than a mood. The exegetes note a grammatical feature of the verse that carries theological weight: an alternate reading puts the first term in the third person — they worship none but Allah — with the imperatives beginning mid-verse, one of the recital's characteristic shifts of address. Whether read as command or as covenant-statement, the terms are identical, and their identity with the Muslim community's own obligations is the verse's silent transfer: the charter taken from the children of Israel is the charter taken from every people given a revelation, the same five terms in the same order — God, kin, the helpless, the tongue, the rites. The recital quotes Israel's constitution to its new holders not as foreign history but as their own inheritance, with the record of the first holders attached as warning label.

**“Except for a Few of You”**

The verdict falls in the final clause: *but you turned away — except for a few of you — and were indifferent*. Three features of the sentence carry its weight. The address is second-person — the covenant is quoted to the generation that broke it as though they stood inside it, the recital's standing method of collapsing the distance between ancestral crime and present responsibility. The exception is recorded — *except for a few* — and the few matter to the verse's justice: the charter was kept by some, which proves it could be kept, and the exception separates the remnant from the mass the way Sūrah al-Māʾidah will separate them again — deceit always found on their part, *except for a few* (5:13). The few are the covenant's vindication and the many's indictment in one phrase: no generation can plead that the terms were impossible, because the impossibility is refuted inside its own ranks. And the closing word — *indifferent*, turning aside in aversion — names not the failure of the weak but the posture of the unbothered: not those who tried and fell, but those who turned away. The distinction is the verse's pastoral edge for every reader. The audit that follows this covenant through the coming verses will weigh the community's conduct, deed by deed — but the root failure it keeps returning to is this one word: a charter perfect in its terms, a few who kept it, a many who were indifferent, and a recital that files the whole scene in the second person so that no one hearing it stands outside the question of which group he belongs to.
