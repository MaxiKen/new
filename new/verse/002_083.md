## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:83

> And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, “Worship none but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy; speak kindly to people; establish prayer; and pay alms-tax.” But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away—except for a few of you—and were indifferent.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Covenant Recalled to Another Community**

The sūrah returns to the history of the children of Israel, and it tells the story in the second person, addressing the descendants of the people who made the agreement. The command to remember introduces a scene rather than a rule, and the scene is a binding undertaking made before God with a list of obligations attached.

This is not a new episode in the narrative. The sūrah has already referred to the covenant in the passage about the mountain being raised and the oath taken at Sinai, and the verses that follow will return to the same agreement twice more. The repetition is deliberate. The Book builds the case against a people out of their own recorded words rather than out of an outsider's accusation.

**Worship Directed to God Alone**

The list opens where the Book's ethical teaching always opens, with the exclusive worship of God, and the remaining commands follow as its consequences. The Qur'an does not treat the social obligations as a separate moral code that can stand without the first one. Later in the sūrah the same order appears when the Book instructs people to worship God and join no partner with Him, and immediately joins to that command kindness to parents and relatives and the feeding of the poor.

Commentators note that this opening clause is the foundation of everything the verse asks afterwards. A person who has no one above him but God has no human being whose approval he must purchase, and the kindness commanded later in the sentence rests on the independence that the first clause establishes.

**Kindness to Parents, Relatives, Orphans, and the Needy**

The Arabic verb used for the treatment of parents describes good conduct more than it describes obedience, and it covers the way a person speaks, listens, and provides. The Book develops the theme at length elsewhere, forbidding even a word of irritation to ageing parents and pairing their care with God's own right (17:23).

The list then widens from the family outward: relatives, orphans, and the poor. The choice of the orphan is not accidental in a passage addressed to a community with a long history of prophets, since the Book repeatedly reminds it that many of its own great figures were raised without fathers. The needy appear last in the list and without qualification, which the commentators noted as a sign that no distinction of tribe or religion should be applied to this obligation.

**Kind Speech as an Obligation**

The middle item in the list is the one most often overlooked: speak kindly to people. The command is addressed to the community in general rather than to its leaders, and the Arabic wording asks for a manner rather than a message. The Book states the same requirement as a test of the trust it places in its own followers, and it forbids harshness in the invitation to faith itself.

Ibn Kathīr and others noted that this clause makes courteous conduct toward everyone a covenant obligation rather than a courtesy. The community being addressed had a long record of quarrelling with its own prophets; the command to speak gently to people stands in the sentence as a correction of that habit, and it applies with equal force to the community now receiving the revelation.

**Prayer and Alms as the Spine of the Covenant**

The list closes with the two practices the Book treats as the standing marks of a life turned toward God: the prayer and the giving of alms. Both are commanded here as covenant terms rather than as personal devotions, and both appear in the Book's descriptions of every community that received a scripture.

The pairing is consistent across the Book, where the two are named together in accounts of the prophets and their peoples, and the Qur'an treats the neglect of them as evidence of a broken relation with God rather than a failure of private piety. The verse therefore ends its list with the duties that the community can neither delegate nor conceal.

**Turned Away, Except a Few**

The sentence then states the outcome, and it does so in the second person: they turned away and were indifferent. The word describing their state suggests a shrug rather than an attack, and the Book's choice of it is exact. The community did not renounce the covenant; it let the terms lapse and treated the lapse as unremarkable.

The exception in the sentence is important. The verse preserves the fact that a few of them kept their word, and the sūrah repeats this qualification at intervals throughout the passage. The Book's judgments in this section are aimed at the majority who defaulted, and the passage records from the first that faithfulness was possible and was practised.

**Why the List Is Read Before the Breach**

The sūrah presents the terms before it presents the failure, and the order is the argument. Someone hearing only of the breach would have to take the accusation on trust; hearing the covenant first allows the failure to be measured against terms the community itself accepted. That is why the verses that follow repeat the same sequence with a second set of terms and a second failure, and why the chapter's later treatment of the same community keeps returning to what they had agreed.

**The Exception as a Standing Invitation**

The closing phrase of the verse preserves the fact that a few of the community kept faith, and the sūrah repeats that qualification whenever it returns to this people. The exception does not soften the account; it keeps the account honest, and it also keeps an opening for anyone listening who had not defaulted with the majority. The Book's practice in these passages is to leave a door visible even while it records a failure, and the invitation to believe what confirms the earlier scripture runs through the whole section. What the verse offers is therefore a choice rather than a verdict. A community that turns away except for a few has shown what the majority are likely to do; it has not determined what any individual member of that community will do, and the sūrah continues to address them directly for many verses afterwards.
