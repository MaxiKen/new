## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:23

> No intercession will be of any benefit with Him, except by those granted permission by Him. ˹At last,˺ when the dread ˹of Judgment Day˺ is relieved from their hearts ˹because they are permitted to intercede˺, they will ˹excitedly˺ ask ˹the angels˺, “What has your Lord ˹just˺ said?” The angels will reply, “The truth! And He is the Most High, AllGreat.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Intercession Is Not Denied — It Is Relicensed**

The previous verse dismantled the idea that the old gods had any standing at the divine court. This verse handles the doctrine that stood behind that idea, and it does something more careful than a flat refusal. *Wa lā tanfaʿu al-shafāʿatu ʿindahu illā li-man adhina lah* — intercession brings no benefit with Him except for the one He permits. The institution survives; the qualification changes hands. In Meccan practice a deity's usefulness lay in its independent leverage: you approached the intermediary because the intermediary had standing of its own. Here standing exists only where it has been granted in advance by the very authority being petitioned, which converts intercession from a channel of influence into an act of obedience. The Qur'an makes the same point in the Throne Verse with a question nobody can answer: *"Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?"* (2:255). An intercessor who must be authorised cannot be a lever against the One authorising him.

**The Word for Terror, and Its Grammar**

The middle of the verse turns to a scene, and the scene begins with fear leaving: *ḥattā idhā fuzziʿa ʿan qulūbihim* — when terror is removed from their hearts. The verb is from *fazaʿ*, sudden fright, and the form used here is the intensive passive with the preposition *ʿan*, which in Arabic gives the sense of stripping something off, clearing it away. Something had settled on the hearts and is lifted. The reading of the Arabic here was discussed by the early authorities, since a different vowelling gives "when they are relieved of terror" as against other possibilities, and the reports preserve the discussion. What no reading changes is the shape of the moment: a silence full of dread, then release, then speech. The verse is describing the interval between a decree being issued and its content becoming known, and the interval is filled with fear.

**Whose Hearts Were Afraid**

The pronoun is not identified in the Arabic, and the translation supplies the likeliest referent: the angels. This is the reading the tradition has generally taken, and it rests on solid ground elsewhere in the Book, which describes the angels as *"wary, for fear of Him"* (21:28) despite their obedience. Their fear is not the fear of punishment, since nothing in the Qur'an charges them with disobedience. It is the awe of beings who know exactly what they are in the presence of — and awe, on this account, increases with knowledge rather than decreasing with familiarity. Fāṭir states the rule directly: *"Of all of Allah's servants, only the knowledgeable ˹of His might˺ are ˹truly˺ in awe of Him"* (35:28). If the highest servants freeze when the divine command is issued, the confidence of the Meccan petitioner who assumed his patron deity could work the room is exposed as ignorance rather than piety.

**The Question the Angels Ask Each Other**

*Mādhā qāla rabbukum* — what did your Lord say? Two features of the question carry the argument. First, they ask each other, which means the command was issued and not all of them had it; even at that altitude, information is distributed rather than universally possessed. Second, they ask what He *said*, not what He wills or intends. The object of angelic knowledge is the utterance, the decree as delivered — which is to say the angels are recipients of instruction, not consultants in its formation. This is the exact opposite of the picture the idolaters worked with, in which beings close to God participate in decisions and can be induced to shift them. Nothing in the scene shows negotiation. It shows a chain of command in which the highest ranks wait, in fear, to learn what has been decided, and then relay it.

**The Answer, and Why It Is So Short**

*Qālū al-ḥaqq* — they said: the truth. The reply is one word in Arabic, and its brevity is the substance of it. Whatever was decreed, the content is not summarised, quoted or explained; it is characterised. This is how a servant reports a master's word when the master's word is by definition beyond appeal — not "He said such and such," but "He said what is right." The two names that close the verse complete the thought: *al-ʿAliyy al-Kabīr*, the Most High and the Great. One is a name of elevation, distance above; the other of magnitude. Together they explain why the answer could be so brief. A decree from a source that high and that great needs no further ground given for it. The Prophet described this moment in terms that match the verse closely, saying that when God decrees a matter in heaven the angels beat their wings in submission to His word, like a chain dragged over rock, until, when the terror is lifted from their hearts, they ask: what did your Lord say? And they answer: the truth, and He is the Most High, the Great (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4701, from Abū Hurayrah).

**What This Does to the Petitioner Who Prays to a Patron**

The practical force of the verse lands on a particular kind of religious person — not the atheist, who is not in view here, but the devotee who has organised his security around access to a powerful friend in the unseen. That system is coherent in a royal court and incoherent in the world this verse describes. The beings with the highest access do not lobby; they wait, afraid, for a decree they did not shape and then transmit it verbatim. If they cannot influence the outcome, a carved figure in a sanctuary certainly cannot. What remains of intercession after this verse is real but transformed: a permission granted from above rather than a service purchased from the side, extended to prophets, angels and righteous servants only as and when God allows. Having removed the intermediaries, the sūrah turns next to a question the opponents must answer themselves.
