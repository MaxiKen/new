## Sūrah an-Naml 27:46

> He urged ˹the disbelieving group˺, “O my people! Why do you ˹seek to˺ hasten the torment rather than grace? If only you sought Allah’s forgiveness so you may be shown mercy!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**He Still Calls Them His People**

The nation has split into two hostile factions and Ṣāliḥ addresses the hostile one as *yā qawmi* — O my people. The possessive is not a formality. It is the same word a man uses of the tribe he belongs to, and he keeps using it after they have declared against him.

The Qur'an gives this vocative to every prophet sent to his own kin, and it survives escalation in each case. Nūḥ uses it through centuries of rejection; Shuʿayb uses it while being threatened with stoning. What the address preserves is the relationship the message is meant to save. A messenger who began saying *you people* would have conceded the separation his opponents were trying to create, and the separation is the thing he is arguing against. Within three verses a group of them will be plotting to kill him and his household in the dark, and he is at this moment still claiming them as his own.

**The Strange Charge of Being in a Hurry**

His question is not the one a reader expects. He does not ask why they disbelieve, why they mock, or why they threaten. He asks why they are rushing: *lima tastaʿjilūna bi'l-sayyi'ati qabla al-ḥasanah* — why do you seek to hasten the evil before the good?

Al-Ṭabarī reads the evil as God's punishment and the good as His mercy, which makes the question literal. They had been demanding the torment Ṣāliḥ warned of, in the manner the Qur'an records of several rejecting peoples: bring it on, if you are telling the truth. Al-Qurṭubī adds a second layer, that their deeds themselves were the evil they were hastening — they were performing the acts that earn punishment instead of those that earn reward, and so hurrying toward one outcome while a better one stood available. The two readings work together. Demanding the punishment verbally and earning it practically are the same impatience expressed twice.

**Why Anyone Would Demand Their Own Destruction**

The behaviour looks irrational until its function is understood. A man who challenges a prophet to produce the threatened disaster is not seeking death; he is seeking the discomfort's end. Warnings that hang unfulfilled are harder to live with than verdicts, because a verdict can be absorbed and a warning cannot.

There is also a rhetorical calculation. If the punishment does not arrive, the challenger has publicly exposed the warner, and every day of delay after the challenge is counted as evidence. The Qur'an records the same tactic from the Quraysh, who asked for stones from the sky if the recitation were true, and it answers such demands consistently by pointing at the delay itself as mercy rather than weakness. Ṣāliḥ's question exposes what the demand actually costs. They are using the interval — the only thing standing between them and the outcome — as ammunition against the man warning them about it.

**Forgiveness Is Offered With No Preconditions**

The second half of his speech is the alternative: *lawlā tastaghfirūna Allāh laʿallakum turḥamūn* — why not seek God's forgiveness, so that you may be shown mercy. Nothing is attached to the offer. No compensation, no penance, no probationary term.

The construction is worth noticing. *Lawlā* with a present verb functions in Arabic as an urging rather than a question — closer to *why not simply* than to a demand for an explanation. And the outcome clause uses *laʿalla*, a particle of hope rather than guarantee, which keeps the promise honest: seeking forgiveness is what opens the possibility, and the granting remains God's. Ṣāliḥ is not selling a transaction. He is telling people standing at one end of a road that the other end is still reachable, and the only thing required to start is to ask. Set against the length of the punishment they are demanding, the cost of the alternative is nothing at all.

**Two Directions Available From the Same Position**

The verse describes a fork. From exactly where Thamūd is standing, two things can be hastened, and they have chosen the one that ends them. That is the structure Ṣāliḥ is trying to make visible.

The Qur'an uses the same pairing elsewhere in a near-identical formula, describing people who would have a prophet hasten the evil before the good although examples of earlier nations had already passed. The repetition across chapters indicates a recognised pattern rather than one nation's peculiarity. What makes it durable is that hastening the evil never feels like hastening anything; it feels like standing firm, refusing to be intimidated, declining to be manipulated by a threat. Every quality the faction would have claimed for itself — courage, scepticism, loyalty to the ancestral way — is engaged in the choice. Their answer to Ṣāliḥ comes in the next verse, and it does not address his question at all.

**Seeking Forgiveness Is the Whole Requirement**

The word Ṣāliḥ uses for the remedy is *istighfār*, a verbal form that means to request pardon. Arabic builds this pattern for asking: the same shape gives the verbs for seeking help and seeking refuge. What he prescribes is an act of asking, not an act of repair.

That is significant given who he is addressing. These are people who have hamstrung a she-camel in other accounts of this story, who have divided their city, and who are shortly going to plan a murder. Nothing in his offer requires them to undo any of it first, and nothing establishes a minimum standard for eligibility. The gate is asking. The Qur'an keeps this threshold low with striking consistency, promising elsewhere that those who have exceeded all limits against themselves should not despair. Ṣāliḥ is putting the same offer to a faction currently in open dispute with him, and he puts it as a question rather than a condition, which is the least coercive form available to him.
