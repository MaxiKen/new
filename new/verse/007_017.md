## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:17

> I will approach them from their front, their back, their right, their left, and then You will find most of them ungrateful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Four Directions of the Approach**

The verse reports the plan of Iblīs in the words he used, and it describes an approach from the front, from behind, from the right, and from the left. The image is that of a force that surrounds its object on every side that a traveller might use, and the commentary tradition reads the directions as descriptions of the kinds of attack rather than of geography.

The tradition of commentary offers several readings of the four directions. The attack from the front is described as the planting of doubt about the resurrection and the judgment, together with the suggestion that the present life is all there is; the attack from behind is described as the diversion of the person's desire from the Hereafter to the goods of the world and the accumulation of what must be left behind; and the attacks from the right and the left are described as the corruption of religion through disbelief and through invented practices that have no warrant in the revelation. The four together cover the whole of a person's outlook and conduct.

**Why the Directions Are Named**

The verse names the directions for which the adversary claims responsibility, and the naming serves the sūrah's warning about the varieties of misguidance. The Book's accounts of the peoples who refused their messengers show the same varieties at work: some denied the resurrection outright, others accepted the message's language while retaining their old practices, and others resisted the messenger because his followers were drawn from the weak. The verse in front of us presents the plan in a form that covers each of them.

The approach from the left is sometimes identified with the corruption of good deeds through the introduction of practices that were never commanded, and the sūrah's audience had a large stock of such practices in its pilgrimage, its food rules, and its offerings. The chapter addresses those practices at length, and the verse in front of us states the strategy behind them.

**The Two Directions That Are Not Named**

The commentary tradition gives attention to the directions that the verse leaves out. When the adversary made his vow and the angels asked how the human being could escape an attack from every side, the answer is reported to be that two ways remain open, the direction from above and the direction from below: the human being can raise his hands in supplication and bow his head to the ground in humility, and the adversary has no claim on either.

The Book's language of supplication and prostration as the believer's refuge appears throughout, and the sūrah of the Cow ends its longest verse with the instruction to call upon God with fear and hope. The directions the adversary does not name are therefore the directions of prayer, and the reader of the sūrah is shown where the defence lies in the same verse that describes the attack.

**The Prediction About Gratitude**

The verse ends with the words of Iblīs about the descendants of Adam, that God will find most of them ungrateful. The Book's statements about the majority's response appear in several passages, and the sūrah of Sheba reports that Iblīs proved his opinion of them true and that they followed him except for a group of believers (34:20). The sūrah of Yūnus states that most of them do not give thanks, the sūrah of the Ant repeats the statement, and the sūrah of Sheba states that few of God's servants are grateful.

The prediction is part of the adversary's case, and the Book's account does not present it as a justification for his conduct. It states a fact about the human response that the sūrah addresses throughout, and the sūrah's own instruction to follow the revelation is the answer to it. The community that receives the Book is the group named in the sūrah of Sheba as the exception.

**What the Verse Establishes About the Enemy**

The verse states the reach of the adversary's ambition without stating the limits of his power. The Book supplies the limits elsewhere: the sūrah of the Rocky Tract states that he has no authority over the sincere servants of God (15:42), the sūrah of the Bee states that he has no authority over those who believe and rely upon their Lord (16:99), and the sūrah of the Night Journey states that he has no authority over the servants of God. The plan described in front of us therefore operates by suggestion rather than compulsion.

The distinction matters for the sūrah's teaching about responsibility. The human being who is misled has followed a suggestion, and the Book's repeated warnings about the enemy are addressed to people who are able to refuse him. The verse in front of us states the intention of the tempter, and the sūrah's later passages state the position of those who accept and those who reject.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands seventeenth in the chapter, and it follows the vow of the previous verse and precedes the second sentence passed on Iblīs. The account then turns to the command given to Adam and his wife to dwell in the Garden and to the temptation that follows. The verse in front of us supplies the shape of the enmity before the narrative shows it in operation.

The reader of the sūrah has been given the adversary's ambition, its directions, and its expectation. The chapter's remaining narratives and instructions are addressed to people who live inside that ambition, and the revelation that the sūrah commands them to follow is presented as the road the adversary occupies.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the sentence passed on Iblīs for the second time, that he should go forth disgraced and rejected, and that Hell will be filled with him and with those among the descendants of Adam who follow him (7:18). The vow is answered with the declaration of the outcome, and the narrative then moves to the first human pair.

The two verses together state the terms of the contest and its end. The adversary is given his time and his method, and the destination of those who follow him is named in the same breath as his own. The reader is left with the choice that the sūrah places before the children of Adam.
