## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:92

> You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish. And whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Condition Attached to Righteousness**

The verse states that righteousness will not be attained until a person gives away something he values, and it closes by saying that whatever is given, God knows it. The sentence pairs an unqualified demand with an assurance, and the two halves answer the two questions a person asks about charity: how much, and who is watching.

The Book's word here is broader than the English rendering suggests. The term covers goodness, kindness, loyalty, sincerity, and obedience, and the commentators noted that it can also designate the reward of all those things. Read in the wider sense, the verse tells the reader that the whole of the good life, and its outcome, is blocked until a particular kind of giving is done, which sets the demand apart from a general recommendation of generosity.

**"Of What You Cherish"**

The qualification is what gives the command its edge. The Book could have said that people must spend from their wealth, and it says instead that they must spend from what they love, which excludes the practice of disposing of what one is glad to be rid of.

The same qualification appears in the sūrah of the Cow, where the believers are told to spend from the good things they have earned and are warned against giving away the worthless portion. The sūrah of Man, describing the righteous, says that they feed the needy, the orphan, and the captive despite their own love for the food. The three passages belong to a single instruction, and the verse above states its condition in its most concentrated form: the test is the value of what leaves the hand.

**The Companions and the Report of a Prized Land**

The commentaries connect the verse with a Companion in Madinah who owned a tract of land he valued above all his property and who wanted to give it in the way of God.

The report is a single instance of what the verse requires, and the commentators were careful not to restrict the verse to that instance. The Book's instruction about spending is not addressed to one owner of one garden; the verse's grammar is general, and the practice it demands is one that the community of the Prophet's companions is described as carrying out so frequently that a single story cannot exhaust it.

**What the Verse Says About the Size of the Gift**

The sentence does not set a minimum, a percentage, or a category of property. It says only that the gift must be drawn from what the giver loves, which makes the measure personal rather than fixed.

The Book's treatments of obligatory and voluntary giving do contain fixed amounts, and this verse is not one of them. Its function is to state the principle that the fixed obligations apply to: a person's wealth is genuinely at his disposal in the Book's account only when part of what he values most has left it. That is why the requirement is connected with the achievement of righteousness rather than with the avoidance of a penalty, and why the verse's language about attainment describes a journey whose destination is the practice itself.

**"Whatever You Give, God Knows It"**

The second sentence is the assurance, and it does two things. It tells the giver that nothing is lost, and it removes the possibility of giving for the sake of appearances.

The Book's instructions about charity are repeatedly accompanied by this reminder, and the reason is set out at length in passages that compare the person who gives to be seen with the person who gives quietly. What is said here is shorter: God knows. The phrase covers the small gift that no one notices and the large gift whose size the giver is attached to, and it also covers the motive, which no human observer can see. For a demand as exacting as the previous sentence, the assurance is what keeps it from being a burden without a destination.

**The Ḥadīth That Echoes the Verse's Logic**

A widely transmitted report states that none of the believers' faith is complete until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself. The ḥadīth is recorded in the two most authoritative collections and graded authentic, and its logic is the same as the verse's.

Both sentences locate the measure of a person's commitment inside his own attachment. The verse speaks about the property a person loves and the ḥadīth about the good he wants for himself, and in each case the standard is what a person already values rather than a quantity imposed from outside. The verse before the reader turns that logic into a rule about wealth, and the ḥadīth states the same principle as a description of faith itself.

**The Connection With the Passages Around It**

The verse follows a passage about communities that had received a revelation and left it, and it precedes a passage about the religious practices the Israelites had taken on themselves. The two surroundings are quarrelsome and historical; the verse in the middle is neither.

Its concerns are entirely practical, and its position suggests what the sūrah means to leave with a reader after the disputes have been stated. What remains when the arguments about lineage, prohibitions, and revelations have been laid out is the conduct of the person reading, and the verse offers the one measurement the Book applies to all of it. The verses that follow return to the argument, but the reader now carries a test with him, which is what the sūrah has been building toward through its warnings about those who turned back from what they had received.
