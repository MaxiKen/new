## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:175

> They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance and forgiveness for punishment. How persistent are they in pursuit of the Fire!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Trade Restated as Double Loss**

The verse repeats the accounting of the one before and sharpens it into symmetry. There, the concealers ate fire for a fleeting gain; here the same transaction is itemized in two pairs: they purchased error at the price of guidance, and punishment at the price of forgiveness. The doubling is deliberate and complete. In the first pair the object sold is cognitive — guidance, the knowledge of the way — and the object bought is its exact negation, misguidance. In the second pair the object sold is soteriological — forgiveness, the standing the revelation existed to offer — and the object bought is its exact negation, punishment. Nothing was gained in either exchange; in each, a good was surrendered for its opposite. The verse thus refines the economics of 2:174: the fleeting gain was never really in the ledger, because what the traders actually acquired were the two negatives. The money was the excuse; the misguidance and the punishment were the merchandise delivered.

**The Verb of Purchase**

The Qur'an's word for this transaction — *ishtaraw*, they purchased — belongs to a family the Book uses with bitter consistency for bad spiritual trades. The same verb described those who sold God's covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain (3:77), those who trade the Hereafter for this life (2:86), and elsewhere those who purchase diversionary talk to mislead from God's way. The choice of commercial language is not decorative; it imports the whole apparatus of the market into the moral life — price, preference, exchange, profit — and then audits it. A market transaction is rational when the buyer values what he receives more than what he pays; the verse's claim is that these traders paid guidance for misguidance and forgiveness for punishment, meaning they paid more than they received by an infinite margin, and did it knowingly, since the concealment of 2:174 presupposed knowledge of exactly what was being sold. The image convicts not only the sin but its stupidity: the Qur'an's profiteers are never shrewd operators but men who cannot do arithmetic, and the market metaphor exists to show every reader the ledger they are keeping with their own choices.

**"How Persistent They Are in Pursuit of the Fire"**

The verse's exclamation has taxed the commentators precisely because its surface is strange: what is praised — or mocked — is the traders' *patience*, their steadfastness, in relation to the Fire. The readings recorded by al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī converge on a rhetorical question: what drove them to the deeds that brought them near the Fire? — or, what gives them such patience toward the Fire that they abandon truth for falsehood? The common sense, in al-Rāzī's summary, is simply: what made them do this? The irony is the engine of the sentence. Patience — *ṣabr* — is the sūrah's supreme virtue: the instrument the believers were commanded to take up (2:153), the quality whose bearers receive blessings and mercy (2:157), the grace promised good news (2:155). Here the same constancy appears in inverted form: a steadfastness in ruin, an endurance that spends itself hastening toward punishment. The traders have the virtue's structure — persistence through difficulty toward a goal — and the vice's direction. The exclamation thus does double duty: wonder at the magnitude of their self-destruction, and a warning that constancy itself is morally neutral until its object is named.

**Patience Toward the Fire, Patience Toward God**

Set beside the patience-verses of this very passage, the exclamation draws the sūrah's full contrast. The believers were told they would be tested with fear and hunger and loss, and commanded to endure — a patience whose object is God's will and whose outcome is blessings, mercy, and guidance (2:157). The concealers exhibit the mirror image: they endure too — the labor of editing scripture, the management of secrets, the years of profitable concealment — and their endurance is real, disciplined, and social. The difference is the direction: one patience walks toward God through difficulty; the other walks toward the Fire with the same steady step. The verse's rhetorical question — what patience do they have toward the Fire? — asks the reader to look at the misbelievers' industry and recognize it as misplaced devotion. Nobody sins lazily at this level; the great corruptions require the great virtues, perverted. This is why the Qur'an reserves its harshest astonishment not for the weak sinner but for the energetic one: the traders' persistence is the measure of what guidance would have made of them.

**Forgiveness Sold Back**

The second pair of the trade deserves its own attention, because forgiveness is the sūrah's standing offer. The passage immediately before ended with the Accepter of Repentance speaking in the first person (2:160), and the whole legislative arc about to begin — retaliation with its pardon, bequests with their equity, the fast with its ease — is forgiveness made institutional. The concealers are described as having held forgiveness and exchanged it: the revelation they hid was the vehicle of pardon for their communities, and by selling it they sold their own access to what it carried. The exegetical point is structural: guidance and forgiveness are the two goods revelation brings — knowledge of the way and absolution on the way — and the trade named here surrendered both at once, which is why the penalty of 2:174 fell on both faculties: the fire answers the belly's gain, the withheld speech answers the guidance, and the withheld purification answers the forgiveness. Each element of the sentence corresponds to an element of the sale. The verse is not adding imagery to the previous one; it is displaying its itemization.

**The Question Left With the Reader**

The closing exclamation is addressed, grammatically, to no one — it is wonder spoken aloud, the way a bystander says *how could he?* over a ruin. That unaddressed quality is its pedagogy. The verse does not answer its own question; no reason is given for the trade, because none exists that survives statement — that is the point of calling it a purchase of error at guidance's price. The reader is left holding the question and is expected to apply it: what am I persistently walking toward, and at what price did I buy the direction? The sūrah will keep posing the same audit in different currencies — wealth traded for the Hereafter, oaths traded for gain, the self traded for appetite — and every instance inherits this verse's arithmetic. The Fire here is not only the destination but the disclosure: it is what every bad trade actually delivers, named at last by its real name. The traders of 2:174 thought they were selling religion for money; the verse corrects the invoice — they were buying the Fire, and paid for it with everything that could have saved them, and kept paying, patiently, to the end.
