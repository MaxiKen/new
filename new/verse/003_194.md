## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:194

> Our Lord! Grant us what You have promised us through Your messengers and do not put us to shame on Judgment Day—for certainly You never fail in Your promise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Petition About the Promise**

The prayer asks God to give what He promised through His messengers, and the commentators record two ways of taking the phrase: that God made the promise to the messengers themselves, or that the messengers conveyed the promise to the people, or that both senses are intended. The Book's promises to the believers are stated in both forms, so the readings are not exclusive.

The petition's content is therefore a request for the fulfilment of what the community had been told to expect. The people of understanding ask that the promise reach them, and the request is expressed in the language of a claim that rests on nothing but God's own word.

**Al-Rāzī on Praying for What Is Certain**

Al-Rāzī discusses the apparent difficulty in asking God for something He has said He will do. He observes that such prayers are not attempts to change God's action; they are expressions of humility and deference on the part of the one who prays.

He cites the Book's other prayers of this kind, including the request of a prophet for his Lord to judge with truth and the prayer of those who ask God to forgive those who repent and follow His way. On his reading, the supplication is a response to what God does rather than a cause of it, and the community's prayers of this kind are exercises in the posture they express.

**The Request Not to Be Disgraced**

The second half of the verse asks God not to disgrace the community on the Day of Resurrection, and the request echoes the previous verse's statement that those who enter the fire will be disgraced. Al-Rāzī notes that the earlier verse concerned the material punishment of the fire while this prayer seeks deliverance from the shame that belongs to the Day.

The two passages together give the reader a sense of what is at stake at the end. The community is described as asking to be spared both the fire and the exposure, and the prayer places the second request in the context of the gathering on the Day of Resurrection.

**The Book's Assurance About the Meeting**

The Book states the certainty of the gathering in several passages, and one of the sūrah's earlier verses puts the declaration in the form of a prayer: that God will gather all humanity on a Day about which there is no doubt, and that He does not break His promise.

Another passage describes the righteous in the garden saying that God has fulfilled what He promised them. The verse before the reader belongs to that group and asks for the same outcome, and the community's prayers in this passage are shaped by the certainty the Book states elsewhere. The prayer takes that assurance as its ground and asks that the believer be among those who receive what has been promised.

**The Repeated Address**

The prayer in this passage addresses God as its Lord at the start of each petition, and the repetition shapes the way the whole scene reads. The community's requests are made by people who place themselves under the authority of the one they address.

The pattern appears in the Book's other recorded prayers, and the community's own supplications follow it. The address establishes the relation between the one who asks and the one who gives, and it keeps the petitions within the bounds of what a servant may ask of his Lord: nothing in the passage requests what lies outside God's promise or His will.

**What the People of Understanding Ask For**

The passage's petitions together cover the whole of a person's need: protection from the fire, forgiveness, the removal of evil deeds, the company of the righteous, the fulfilment of the promise, and deliverance from disgrace on the Day of Resurrection.

The list is arranged in the order of the prayer and the needs are stated one after another, each in a clause of its own. The community that recites the passage is given a complete set of requests, and the closing verses of the sūrah show how the requests were received. The requests are made in the plural and cover the community as a whole, so the individual prays with the body of the believers rather than for himself alone.

**The Certainty Expressed in the Final Clause**

The verse ends with the declaration that God does not fail the appointed meeting, and the sentence is a statement of confidence placed at the end of a request. The community's prayer does not end with uncertainty about the outcome but with an assertion about the one being addressed.

The Book's prayers frequently close in this way, with a statement about God that serves as the ground of the petition. The community is taught to end its requests with an acknowledgment of what God is, and the form has shaped the way the community's own supplications are constructed, ending with what God is rather than with what the supplicant wants.

**The Prayer as the Sūrah's Close**

The passage is the sūrah's final movement and its closing prayer, and the choice of this material for the end is deliberate. The sūrah has taken its readers through the community's history, its failures, its obligations, and its arguments with its opponents, and it leaves them with the community at prayer.

The address is made to God on behalf of people who have been described as remembering Him in every posture and reflecting on the creation. The next verse reports the answer that their prayer received, and the sūrah's account of what God granted them is the last major statement of the passage.
