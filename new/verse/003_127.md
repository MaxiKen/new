## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:127

> to destroy a group of the disbelievers and humble the rest, causing them to withdraw in disappointment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Purpose Stated in Verbs**

The verse sets out what the arrangement was meant to achieve: the cutting off of a faction among the disbelievers, or their humiliation, and their withdrawal in disappointment. The sentences complete the explanation that the previous verse began.

Some commentators connected the purpose with Badr, where a portion of the enemy's leadership was destroyed, and others with Uḥud, where the enemy failed to achieve what they had come for. Al-Ṭabarī recorded both positions, and the difference turns on which battle the earlier promises of aid are understood to accompany. The two readings do not affect the verse's substance, since either way the sentence names the results that the fighting produced and attributes their arrangement to God rather than to the two armies. What the community is meant to take from the clause is that the outcome served a purpose it could not see while the day was unfolding.

**"Cutting Off" a Faction**

The verb describes the severing of a part from a whole, and the Book uses the same root for cutting off something's continuation. In this context it describes the removal of a portion of the enemy's strength, and the commentators applied it to the leaders and prominent fighters who were killed.

The choice of word is precise. It does not say that the enemy was annihilated; it says that a portion of them was severed, which is what a battle does to a force that suffers heavy losses among its commanders. The Book describes the effect in the terms of the battlefield rather than in the terms of a total destruction.

**The Humiliation of the Rest**

The second outcome in the verse is humiliation, and the Book uses this vocabulary for a defeat that does not destroy. A humbled force is one that has been made to see the limit of its power, and the verse places that outcome alongside the cutting off of a part.

The pairing suggests two different ends for two different groups. Some are removed from the field altogether, and others are left to return with the knowledge of what happened. The Book's accounts of its battles regularly describe both results, and the verse names them here as the purpose of the aid that was promised.


The verse's final clause describes the enemy's withdrawal with the word for being turned back without achieving the aim. The phrase describes a retreat that is also a failure, and the commentators explained it as the community's enemies going home without the victory they had come for.

The description is exact for the battle of Uḥud, where the opposing force was able to inflict losses but was not able to take the city or destroy the community. The Book chooses to describe the day from the standpoint of what the enemy failed to obtain, and the sentence is put in the same verse as the promise of aid, so that the two are read together.

**What the Verse Says About Divine Purpose**

The Book states the purposes of events in this way so that its readers understand history as intended rather than random. The verse says that the arrangement of aid was meant to produce certain results, and it names the results.

That way of writing does not remove human action from the account. The enemy came with its own purpose, made its own decisions, and bore the consequences; the community prepared and fought and suffered. What the verse adds is the dimension the participants could not see, which is that the outcome served an intention beyond the calculations of either side.

**The Historical Shape of the Two Battles**

The two battles that the sūrah has been describing ended in opposite ways on the surface. At Badr the believers, though few, broke the opposing force and killed a number of its leaders; at Uḥud the believers suffered casualties including prominent Companions, and the enemy withdrew without pressing its advantage.

The verse's two clauses fit both events. A part of the enemy's strength was cut off at Badr, and the enemy went home disappointed after Uḥud. The Book describes the same purpose across two days that felt very different to the community that lived through them, and that is part of what the passage is teaching. A reader who compares the two accounts notices that the sūrah never presents the losses at Uḥud as a reversal of the promise recorded before them; it presents them as part of the same arrangement, in which the community's own conduct and the divine purpose both had their place.

**Why the Purpose Is Stated After the Promise**

The order of the verses is worth noticing. The promise of aid comes first, then its purpose for the believers' hearts, and only then the effect on the enemy. The community's own condition is placed before the fortunes of its opponents.

The arrangement keeps the passage's focus where the Book consistently puts it. What matters first is the state of the people being addressed, and the outcome of the conflict is described as a consequence of the divine purpose rather than as the subject of the passage. The reader is led to examine his own heart before he considers the fate of his enemies.


The verses that follow turn from the promises and their purposes to a statement about the Prophet's own position in the matter, and then to the account of the testing that the community underwent.

The movement is from the general to the particular. The reader has been told what the aid was for and what it achieved, and he is now taken into the events themselves, where the community's own conduct will be examined in detail. The purpose clause in this verse is the bridge between the two, since it states the outcome that the account is about to explain.
