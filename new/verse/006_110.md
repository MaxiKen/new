## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:110

> We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turning of Hearts and Eyes**

We turn their hearts and eyes away as they refused to believe at first. The verse states the consequence that follows a refusal, and it does so in the language of motion. The Book's word describes turning something over repeatedly, and the commentators read it as a swaying of the inner faculties, so that the hearts and eyes of the people who refused move from one state to another and never settle on the truth.

The sentence is the answer to the question the previous verse put to the believers: what will make you realise that even if a sign came the audience still would not believe? The verse in front of us supplies the reason, which is that the refusal had already occurred at the beginning, and the turning described here follows from it rather than causing it.

**The Reports About the Turning of Hearts**

The commentaries connect this vocabulary to a well-known report recorded in the Sunan of al-Tirmidhī. Umm Salama was asked which supplication the Messenger of God made most often in her presence, and she answered that it was, O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm in Your religion; when she asked him why he supplicated so often, he answered that every human heart lies between two fingers of the Most Merciful, who keeps firm whom He wills and turns aside whom He wills. The narration also appears from Anas ibn Mālik with the same phrases, and the reports describe the same divine action that this verse names.

What the verse and the reports together describe is God's complete disposal of human inward life. The Book states the same fact in its passages about the sealing of hearts, eyes, and ears, where those who persist in denial are described as unable to perceive what is addressed to them (2:7; 6:46), and the sūrah has used that description in its account of the people who turned away from the signs.

**Leaving Them in Their Defiance**

Leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance. The sentence names the condition in which the people who refused are left, and the Book's term for the wandering describes a state of confusion in which a person cannot find his way. The image belongs with the sūrah's earlier descriptions of those who walk in darkness: the man whom the satans have seduced in the open country and who has companions calling him to guidance he does not follow (6:71), and the people whose hearts and eyes were turned away in this verse.

The Book is careful to state that the wandering is the consequence of a choice, and the commentators explained the arrangement in that sense: God does not interfere with a person's decision, does not cut short his life to prevent further wandering, and does not compel the path he takes. The person who refused at the first opportunity is left to the road he has chosen, and the Book describes the road's condition rather than the man's fate.

**The Pattern of the Sealed Heart**

The sūrah has stated the same arrangement before, when it described the sealing of the hearts of those who denied the signs and the blindness that prevented them from seeing them (6:46; 6:25). The sūrah of the Cow states the pattern in its opening passage, where God is described as sealing the hearts of the disbelievers, covering their hearing, and placing a veil over their sight (2:7), and the sūrah of the Heights gives the same description with the detail that they will not believe until they see the painful punishment.

The verses in this sūrah belong to that family, and the point they make is about the relationship between a person's first decision and his subsequent capacity. The Book presents the relationship as one of consequence rather than of arbitrary imposition: the people who refused to believe when the message first reached them are left without the disposition to believe when the signs appear later.

**Why the Verse Is Addressed to the Prophet's Audience**

The verse is part of a passage in which the Prophet is being prepared for the response he will receive. The sūrah has told him to follow the revelation, to turn away from the polytheists, and that he is neither their keeper nor their maintainer, and this verse supplies the explanation for the resistance he encountered. The people who demanded signs had already decided, and the turning of their hearts was the working out of that decision.

The Book's statements about the hearts of the audience are therefore not a claim that persuasion is impossible but a description of the people who had reached the point of refusal. The same sūrah presents the message as addressed to anyone who will hear it, and it asks questions that presume the capacity for a response.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The verse that follows states the case in the strongest possible terms: even if the angels had been sent to them, and the dead had spoken to them, and everything had been assembled before their eyes, they still would not have believed unless God willed (6:111). The two verses form a single argument, and together they close the discussion of the demand for signs that began with the oaths sworn by the idolaters.

What the sūrah will do next is turn to the Prophet's own situation among those who opposed him, describing the enemies appointed to every prophet and the whisperings of the devilish among humans and jinn. The discussion of signs ends, and the discussion of the messenger's company begins.
