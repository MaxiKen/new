## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:37

> those who are stingy, promote stinginess among people, and withhold Allah’s bounties. We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Arrogant and Boastful Made Visible Through Stinginess**

This verse completes the portrait begun at the end of the previous one: *inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu man kāna mukhtālan fakhūrā* — surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful (4:36) — those who are *alladhīna yabkhalūna wa yaʾmurūna al-nāsa bi al-bukhl wa yaktumūna mā ātāhumu Allāhu min faḍlihi* — stingy, promoting stinginess among people, and withholding Allah’s bounties. The connection was read as cause and effect. *Ikhtiyāl* — arrogance, strutting — and *fakhr* — boasting — inflate the self, and the inflated self holds wealth tightly because wealth is the prop of its image. *Bukhl* — stinginess — is therefore not only a habit with coin; it is the financial form of arrogance. The sūrah has just commanded *iḥsān* — kind, beautiful doing — to parents, relatives, orphans, poor, neighbours near and distant, companions, travellers, and those in possession (4:36). Stinginess is the refusal of that entire movement outward. It keeps what was given as standing support — *qiyāman* (4:5) — for the household and the community locked in the hand, rather than letting it circulate as provision, dowry, inheritance shares, and trade by mutual consent (4:29). The verse therefore draws a straight line from worship without association (4:36) to openness of hand; to associate oneself with God in pride is to close the hand that worship should open.

**Promoting Stinginess Among People**

The addition *wa yaʾmurūna al-nāsa bi al-bukhl* — and promote stinginess among people — deepens the fault from private tightness to social teaching. The stingy person does not only withhold; he counsels others to withhold, normalizing restraint as prudence and caricaturing generosity as waste. Early commentators noted that this promotion could be open — telling others not to give — or quiet — modelling hesitation, praising hoarding, or shaming those who give to orphans, relatives, or neighbours. The sūrah has seen both forms already: guardians who consumed orphan wealth hastily before the child grew to demand it (4:6) and those who devoured wealth by falsehood in trade (4:29) each needed a justification that made taking look careful and giving look naive. To command stinginess is to provide that justification for others. The Qur’an elsewhere condemns those who *“enjoin miserliness”* and links it to disbelief in God’s bounty, because the call to withhold is also a call to distrust the One who replenishes. In a community learning to distribute estates with provision for bystanders (4:8), to give dowries graciously (4:4), and to return pledges (4:33), a voice that says keep, do not give, pulls directly against the sūrah’s ethic of circulation.

**Withholding What Allah Gave of His Bounty**

The third trait *wa yaktumūna mā ātāhumu Allāhu min faḍlihi* — and withhold Allah’s bounties — uses *katama* — to conceal, to hide — the same verb the sūrah uses for Jews who were given a portion of the Scripture and trade it for misguidance (4:44) and who conceal knowledge. Here the concealment is of bounty — *faḍl* — the surplus God gave to be spent, shared, and acknowledged. *Faḍl* in this sūrah has appeared as the apportioning among people not to be craved but to be asked for (4:32), and as the bounty of Gardens for those who keep limits (4:13). To hide bounty is both to hide wealth itself — not spending it where *iḥsān* would — and to hide gratitude — not admitting it came from God. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“if you are grateful, I will give you more”* (14:7), making gratitude the condition for increase; concealment is the opposite, a denial that the gift needs acknowledgment. That is why the verse ends with a prepared humiliating punishment for the ungrateful who cover. The humiliation matches the earlier warning for exceeding limits (4:14) and for devouring wealth deliberately (4:30); in each, the person who sought to appear larger by withholding is reduced. The sūrah has already said God is ever Merciful to you (4:29); the stingy person acts as though mercy will not return if he gives.

**Prepared Humiliating Punishment**

The recompense *wa aʿtadnā lil-kāfirīna ʿadhāban muhīnā* — We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment — gathers the three traits into the category of *kufr* — covering, ingratitude, disbelief — where the gift is covered rather than shared. *Muhīn* — humiliating — appears again for those who exceed God’s limits (4:14), showing that humiliation is the proper reversal for the arrogant and boastful. What was concealed as bounty — *faḍl* — becomes exposed as lack when the punishment arrives, and what was boasted as status becomes humbled. The verb *aʿtadnā* — We have prepared — stresses that the recompense is not improvised; like the Gardens prepared for those who keep limits (4:13) and the painful punishment prepared for those who defer repentance until death (4:18), it is readied. The sūrah elsewhere promises that *“Allah never wrongs even by an atom’s weight, and if it is good He multiplies it many times over”* (4:40), and that multiplication is the alternative economy the stingy refuse to trust. The verse therefore contrasts two economies: one where giving is loss, and one where giving is multiplication; one where withholding looks safe, and one where it is prepared humiliation. The sūrah invites the first to be exchanged for the second before the preparation becomes arrival.

**A Community Trained to Circulate**

Placed between the comprehensive ethic of kindness (4:36) and the warning against spending to show off without belief (4:38), this verse identifies the inner disease that blocks that ethic. The sūrah has taught the community to circulate wealth at every sensitive point — return the orphan’s wealth with witnesses (4:6), give women dowries graciously (4:4), divide estates after debts and bequests (4:11–12), offer provision to bystanders with kind speech (4:8), trade by mutual consent (4:29), and ask God for bounty rather than crave another’s (4:32). Each act requires a hand that opens. Stinginess closes that hand, and promoting stinginess closes others’ hands as well. The verse does not only threaten; it diagnoses. The one who does not like to give is the one who loves to be seen as high — *mukhtāl fakhūr* — and who believes bounty is his own achievement to be hidden rather than God’s gift to be shared. To heal that, the sūrah offers the opposite practice: worship without partner, which humbles the self before God, and *iḥsān* that moves outward in circles until it reaches the traveller and the servant. A community that learns those two moves will find stinginess unnatural, because the hand that has been opened in worship does not easily close when a relative, orphan, or neighbour is in need.
