## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:6

> Those gifted with knowledge ˹clearly˺ see that what has been revealed to you from your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth, and that it guides to the Path of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Knowledge Described as Something Received**

The phrase is *alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilm* — those who have been **given** knowledge. The verb is passive, and the passive is the argument. Arabic could easily have said "the learned" or "the people of knowledge," and the Qur'an does use such expressions; choosing the passive participle instead makes knowledge a thing handed over rather than a thing accumulated. This reverses the ordinary picture in which the scholar is an achiever and his learning a possession he has won. On the Qur'anic account he is a recipient, which is why the same construction appears where knowledge produces humility rather than pride: *"Allah will elevate those of you who are faithful, and ˹raise˺ those gifted with knowledge in rank"* (58:11), and *"Of all of Allah’s servants, only the knowledgeable ˹of His might˺ are ˹truly˺ in awe of Him"* (35:28). The sūrah places these people immediately after the saboteurs of the previous verse, and the contrast is precise. One group works to make the signs fail; the other has been given the capacity to see what the signs are.

**Who These Knowers Actually Were**

The identity of the group has been read three ways, and the grounds for each are worth separating. Some early authorities took it to mean the Companions of the Prophet, since they are the ones present in the sūrah's own setting and the ones whose recognition of the revelation is at issue. Others read it as the scholars among the People of the Book, because that reading has direct support in a nearby verse: al-Isrāʾ describes how the recitation affects *"those who were gifted with knowledge before it ˹was revealed˺"* (17:107), and al-Ḥajj uses the same phrase for those who recognise the revelation as truth and submit to it inwardly (22:54). A third reading declines to restrict the phrase at all: anyone with real knowledge, of whatever community, since genuine knowledge on this account leads to truth by its nature. The third reading is the strongest, and not only because it is the most generous. The verse gives no qualifier of community, and the argument it is making would be weakened by one — the point is that knowledge as such recognises the revelation, which is worth saying only if the claim is general.

**Recognition Supplied as Testimony in an Argument**

This verse functions inside a dispute, and its role is evidential. The deniers have asserted that the Hour will not come (34:3) and will shortly assert that the messenger is a liar or a madman (34:8). Against assertion, the sūrah sets a witness: there exists a class of people who, on examining the revelation, see it as true. Testimony of this kind is weak if the witnesses are partisan, which is why the identification with the People of the Book carried force in the original setting — Jewish and Christian scholars had no stake in the new preacher's success, and some of them recognised the message anyway. The Qur'an uses the same evidentiary move elsewhere, ranking knowers alongside angels as witnesses to the divine unity: *"Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge"* (3:18). Knowledge here is not being praised for its own sake. It is being called to the stand.

**What They See: Truth First, Then Direction**

The verse gives the content of their recognition in two clauses, and the order of the two is a small piece of epistemology. First, that the revelation is *al-ḥaqq*, the truth — a claim about correspondence, about the thing being so. Second, that it *guides to the Path of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy* — a claim about function, about where following it leads. These are separable judgments. A statement can be true and lead nowhere; a road can be useful and start from a lie. What the knowers see is both together, and seeing both is what distinguishes recognition from mere assent. The Arabic word for the path here is *ṣirāṭ*, borrowed into Arabic from Latin *strata* by way of Syriac, and it means a paved public road rather than a private track — the same word used in the prayer every Muslim repeats in every unit of prayer. Truth that opens onto a public road is the Qur'an's recurring image for revelation, and it is set here against a rival picture in which the message is a private invention.

**Two Names That Answer the Two Complaints**

The path leads to *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥamīd* — the Almighty, the Praiseworthy. The pairing is chosen, not automatic. *ʿAzīz* comes from a root meaning to be rare, strong, inaccessible; an *ʿizz* is a fortified height. The name carries power that cannot be overcome. *Ḥamīd* is from the same root as the *ḥamd* with which this sūrah opened (34:1), and means the One who deserves praise, not merely receives it. Naming both at the end of this verse answers the two things the opponents held against the message. They thought its followers powerless, a handful of the poor around a rejected preacher; the first name says where the real strength is. They thought the message ignoble, a break with the honour of their fathers; the second says where praise properly belongs. Al-Ibrāhīm uses the identical pair at the head of a sūrah, describing the Book as sent down to bring people out of darkness to the path *of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy*, which shows the phrase functioning as a standing description of the destination rather than a decorative close.

**A Madinan Verse Inside a Meccan Sūrah?**

Some scholars have held that this single verse was revealed at Medina while the rest of Sabaʾ is Meccan, and the suggestion is usually tied to the reading that identifies the knowers with the People of the Book, whose scholars became a live question only after the move north. The claim is reported in the commentary tradition rather than established by a report from the Prophet, and the sūrah reads perfectly well without it: a Meccan audience also contained men of learning, and the argument from recognition works in either city. What the suggestion usefully highlights is how differently this verse behaves from its neighbours. Around it the sūrah is quoting hostile speech and answering it. Here, for one verse, the voice steps back and describes a reaction rather than a rejection — a pause that makes the return to mockery in the next verse land harder.
