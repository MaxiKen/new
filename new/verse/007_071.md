## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:71

> He said, “You will certainly be subjected to your Lord’s torment and wrath. Do you dispute with me regarding the so-called gods which you and your forefathers have made up —a practice Allah has never authorized? Then wait! I too am waiting with you.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Punishment Already Decreed**

The verse reports the answer of Hūd to the demand of his people, and the answer states that the torment and the wrath of their Lord have already fallen due upon them. The messenger does not refuse the challenge; he states that the matter the people wish to hasten is not a proof to be produced but a consequence that has been established by their own conduct. The sūrah's account of the demand is thereby answered in the terms that the Book uses in its other narratives, and the sūrah of Yūnus reports the instruction to tell the audience to consider what would follow if the torment came upon them by night or by day (10:50).

The reply reverses the position that the people had taken. They had asked the messenger to produce the punishment, and the answer states that the punishment has been their portion since they made their choice. The Book's narratives of the communities present the same arrangement in each case, and the sūrah's later accounts of the destruction of the peoples state the arrival of what had been decreed.

**The Gods That Were Invented**

The second element of the answer concerns the objects of the ancestral worship, and the messenger describes them as names that the people and their forefathers had made up. The phrase states that the deities in question have no existence beyond the naming, and the Book uses the same argument in its accounts of the idols. The sūrah of Noah names five of the idols that the people of that messenger worshipped, and the sūrah of the Star states that the names the audience uses for its goddesses are nothing but names that the people and their forefathers invented.

The claim that the practice was never authorized by God appears in the sūrah's earlier passage on the forbidden things, where the audience is told that God has sent down no authority for the partners it associates with Him (7:33). The verse in front of us applies the same statement to the worship of ʿĀd, and the sūrah's narratives repeat it with the other communities. The argument is not that the practice is unfamiliar but that it has no warrant.

**The Appeal to the Ancestors Answered**

The answer in front of us closes the appeal to the forefathers that the previous verse reported, and it turns the appeal into the ground of the charge. The ancestors invented the names, and the descendants inherited the invention; the practice therefore has a human origin and no divine authorization. The sūrah's earlier rejection of the ancestral argument used the same reasoning when it stated that God never commands what is shameful (7:28), and the Book repeats the argument in the sūrah of the Ornament, where the disbelievers say that they found their forefathers on a way and are following in their footsteps (43:22).

The Book's treatment of the argument from inheritance appears in its accounts of the communities that were destroyed, and the sūrah's narratives present each of them relying on the practice of their fathers. The verse in front of us states the answer that the messengers gave, and the sūrah's reader is shown that the argument was made and answered in every case.

**The Waiting**

The verse closes with the messenger's statement that he will wait with his people for the outcome, and the phrase turns the challenge back on the challengers. The Book uses the language of waiting in its accounts of the audience that demanded the punishment, and the sūrah of the Prophets reports the question of when the threat would come to pass and the instruction to the Prophet to say that he is waiting with them (21:38). The verse in front of us places the same exchange in the narrative of ʿĀd.

The waiting in front of us is not a threat of the messenger's own making, and the Book states that the term belongs to God. The sūrah's opening verses on the reckoning have established that nothing is advanced or delayed beyond its term (7:34), and the verse in front of us applies the principle to the community that asks for the punishment to be brought forward. The answer states that the term is running and that the messenger will wait for its arrival with the people who asked.

**The Argument of the Reply**

The reply in front of us gathers the sūrah's arguments against the audience's religion and states them in the messenger's own voice. The idols are names without authority, the ancestral practice has a human origin, the punishment is the consequence of the refusal rather than an object of proof, and the outcome will arrive at its term. The Book's narratives of the communities repeat the same set of arguments, and the sūrah's account of ʿĀd supplies the first full statement of them.

The reply's function in the narrative is to close the exchange and to leave the outcome to the event. The messenger has stated the message, given the counsel, reminded the people of the favours, and answered the challenge; and the account that follows reports what the term brought. This verse supplies the last speech of the messenger before the destruction, and the sūrah's next verse states the rescue and the removal.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands seventy-first in the chapter, and it answers the challenge of the previous verse. The verse that follows closes the account of ʿĀd with the rescue of the messenger and his companions by mercy and the uprooting of those who denied the signs (7:72), and the third of the sūrah's narratives then begins. The verse here supplies the conclusion of the argument with the second people.

The reader who heard the community demand the punishment now hears the messenger state that the demand has been answered by their own conduct. The gods are names, the practice is inherited, and the term is running; and the sūrah's next verse reports the rescue of the believers and the end of the people who refused.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that God saved the messenger and those with him by mercy and uprooted those who denied the signs, and that they were not believers (7:72). The waiting of the messenger in front of us is followed by the arrival of the term, and the account of ʿĀd reaches its end in the same manner as the account of Noah.

The two verses together complete the second narrative of the series. The argument is closed, the term arrives, the believers are saved, and the people who denied the signs are removed from the earth; and the sūrah's following verses introduce the people of Thamūd and their messenger Ṣāliḥ.
