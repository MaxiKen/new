## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:175

> That ˹warning˺ was only ˹from˺ Satan, trying to prompt you to fear his followers. So do not fear them; fear Me if you are ˹true˺ believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning Traced to Its Source**

The verse identifies the fear that had been stirred up in the community as the work of Satan, who frightens his friends. The report that reached the community on the road was presented as information about the enemy, and the verse states what was behind it.

The commentators read the sentence as a statement about how the warning worked rather than only about who delivered it. Al-Qurṭūbī and al-Ṭabarī explain that Satan inspires the disbelievers to frighten the believers, so that the community's resolve would fail before its opponents had done anything. Naming the source is the Book's way of reducing a threat to its true size, and the sentence does this by identifying the influence behind the report rather than by disputing the details of the report itself.

**Satan's Friends**

The phrase describing the friends of Satan identifies those whose cause the fear serves. The Book uses the expression in its description of the people who follow their impulses and forget the remembrance of God, and it describes them as a party that is bound to lose.

The verse before the reader attaches the party to the fear it was promoting. The community is told that the people whose threats it was dreading were acting as agents of an influence that had no claim on the outcome, and the sentence reduces the threat to a single source. The phrase also shows who the community's fear would have served, since the threats came from people whose cause the Book had already described as lost.

**The Word Applied to Human Beings**

The commentators note that the Book uses the term for human beings as well as for the unseen creature, and this allows the verse to be read as applying to the disbelievers and hypocrites themselves. The sūrah's earlier passage about the community's confidants describes men whose speech carries the same suggestion.

The Book's usage of this kind is deliberate, and it teaches its readers to judge conduct by its character rather than by the nature of the actor. The verse before the reader describes an influence that works through human speech, and the sentence does not require the community to identify who was whispering in order to resist it.

**The Command Not to Fear Them**

The verse forbids the community to be afraid of the group whose threats had been reported. The command's object is the fear itself rather than the danger, and the Book's instructions about fear are usually phrased in this way.

The community's situation gave the fear a foothold. Its men were wounded, its ranks thinned, and its opponents known to be eager for a second engagement, and the Book does not tell the community that the danger was imaginary. It tells it that the fear of the enemy is not the fear it should be governed by, and the next clause supplies the alternative.

**The Command to Fear God**

The alternative is the fear of God, and the Book uses the same instruction in other passages. One states that God is more deserving of the community's fear if its members are believers. Another tells the people who were entrusted with the scripture not to fear the people and to fear God instead. A third instructs the community to be mindful of God as it is due.

The command places the two fears side by side, and the Book's teaching in all of these passages is that a person cannot be governed by both. What the community is told to fear is the one whose judgement matters and whose protection is effective, and the sentence treats the fear of anything else as a misplacement of the same instinct.

**The Condition Attached to the Command**

The verse closes with the phrase that the community's faith depends on its answer to this command, and the conditional form gives the sentence its weight. The Book attaches such conditions to its instructions when it wants to describe what a person's claim of faith amounts to in practice.

The clause does not suggest that the community's faith was in doubt. It sets out the practical content of the claim: a person who fears his enemies more than he fears God has given his fear to something other than the one he professes to worship, and the sentence states the matter in that direct form.

**Fear as a Discipline of the Community**

The Book treats the fear of God as one of the governors of conduct, and it uses the term alongside mindfulness and reverence in its instructions. The community is told in another passage that God warns it about Himself, which places the awareness of God's judgement at the centre of its self-understanding.

The instruction in this verse belongs to that body of teaching. The community had just been shown how fear had nearly governed its decision about marching, and the sentence supplies the correction. The Book's remedy is not the removal of fear but the redirection of it, and the sūrah has used the vocabulary of mindfulness since its opening verses, where the Book is described as a guidance for those who guard themselves against what they fear.

**The Affair of Uḥud Closed**

The verse brings the sūrah's account of the battle and its aftermath to an end, and the passage closes with the community's fear placed where the Book believes it belongs. The remaining verses of the sūrah turn to their opponents among the People of the Book and to the arguments of the community's own doubters.

The placement of the sentence at this point is deliberate. The community had been tested by wounds, by rumours, by the loss of its men, and by the fear of a second attack, and the sūrah leaves it with a command about what it should be afraid of. The lessons of the campaign are gathered in that sentence, and the readers of the sūrah carry it as the conclusion of the whole account.
