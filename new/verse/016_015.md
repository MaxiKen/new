## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:15

> **He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mountains As Stabilizers**

Wa-alqā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bikum — and He cast into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you. Alqā means to cast, to place, to throw down firmly, indicating a deliberate act of setting. Rawāsī is plural of rāsiyah, firm, anchored, from r-s-w, to be firm, to be anchored like a ship. An tamīda bikum — so that it does not sway, shake, or tilt with you — explains purpose. Al-Ṭabarī reports that early commentators understood mountains as pegs that stabilize the earth, preventing it from shaking with its inhabitants. The image appears elsewhere: *And We made in the earth firm mountains lest it shake with them* (21:31) and *And the mountains as stakes* (78:7). Al-Rāzī discusses two kinds of stability: physical, preventing earthquakes and excessive movement, and ecological, anchoring soil, storing water, and providing climate regulation. Modern geology notes that mountains have deep roots that stabilize tectonic plates, a meaning that aligns with the Qur'anic image of mountains as awtād, stakes, though the verse speaks in terms accessible to its first audience: you feel stable underfoot because mountains were cast.

**Rivers And The Gift Of Moving Water**

Wa-anhāran — and rivers. The word is added without verb, governed by alqā, He cast rivers as well. Anhār is plural of nahr, river, stream, flowing water. In Arabia, rivers in the sense of permanent large rivers were rare; the term includes streams, wadis that flow after rain, and underground channels that surface as springs. Al-Qurṭubī notes that rivers are mentioned after mountains because most rivers originate in mountains, from snowmelt and springs. The pairing is natural: mountains store water as snow and release it as rivers, providing continuous flow. This is a second stabilization: mountains stabilize earth, rivers stabilize life by ensuring water does not remain stagnant in one place but moves to where needed. The Qur'an often pairs mountains and rivers as in 13:3 and 27:61, showing a system: firm heights and flowing waters together make land habitable. Without mountains, earth would shake; without rivers, it would be barren.

**Pathways So You May Find Your Way**

Wa-subulan laʿallakum tahtadūn — and pathways, so that you may find your way, be guided. Subul is plural of sabīl, way, path, road. Here it means natural passes through mountains, valleys, and plains that allow travel. Al-Ṭabarī explains subul as roads and tracks that people follow to reach destinations. The purpose clause laʿallakum tahtadūn, so that you may be guided, connects physical guidance to spiritual guidance, echoing verse 9 where Allah shows the straight way. Just as Allah created mountains to stabilize and rivers to provide, He created passes so that humans can move and not be trapped. In a mountainous region, a pass is as important as the mountain itself; without it, a range is a barrier. With it, it becomes a route. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that tahtadūn here means you find your way in travel, not only spiritual guidance, though the same root is used for both. The physical paths that guide through earth are a sign of the spiritual path that guides to Allah.

**How Mountains, Rivers, And Paths Form One System**

The verse lists three creations in one sentence, showing their interdependence. Mountains without rivers would be dry weights; rivers without mountains would lack source; paths without both would be unnecessary. Together they form a habitable landscape: stable ground, flowing water, traversable routes. Al-Rāzī sees in this a proof of wisdom: the earth is not flat and featureless, nor is it impassably rugged. It has firmness and flow and openness in balance. This balance serves humans: you live without constant shaking, you drink and irrigate from rivers, you travel via paths. The phrase bikum, with you, is significant. Earth could shake without humans, but it is described as not shaking with you, emphasizing human-centered mercy. The earth is made stable for its inhabitants, not merely stable in itself. This anthropocentric framing is consistent with lakum, for you, that has run through the blessings passage.

**The Connection To Guidance And Stars**

Verse 15 mentions paths for guidance, and verse 16 will add landmarks and stars for guidance, completing the navigation theme. The sūrah is teaching that guidance is available at every level: divine names that show mercy, revelation that shows straight way, mountains that show stability, rivers that show direction of flow, paths that show passage, landmarks and stars that show location. The person who is guided physically is invited to be guided spiritually. The Qur'an often uses hadā, to guide, for both. The traveler who uses a mountain pass to find his way to a town is practicing a skill that, when turned to ultimate questions, leads to finding way to Allah. The verse's placement after sea also makes sense: after crossing sea by ship, you need firm land with mountains, rivers, and paths to continue journey. The sūrah thus traces a complete journey from sky to land to sea back to land, always with guidance.

**Lexical Note On Rawāsī And Tamīda**

Rawāsī from r-s-w means firmly anchored, used for mountains and for ships anchored. In Lisān al-ʿArab, rasā means to be firm, fixed. Tamīda from m-y-d means to sway, tilt, shake, like a ship swaying or a branch swaying. An tamīda is purpose clause with an, so that it does not sway. The construction an tamīda bikum could be read as lest it sway with you, i.e., carrying you. The verse's brevity condenses geology, hydrology, and geography into one line: mountains cast, rivers made, paths placed, all so you may be stable, watered, and guided. The simplicity invites reflection rather than technical debate, which is the Qur'an's method: it describes phenomena in terms of benefit and sign, leaving detailed mechanisms for human discovery while keeping focus on Giver.
