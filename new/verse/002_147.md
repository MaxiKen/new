## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:147

> ˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord, so do not ever be one of those who doubt.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Verdict in the Passage**

After the long movement of the qiblah section — the fools' question, the middle community, the Prophet's lifted face, the maximal evidence, the child-certain recognition and its concealment — the verse arrives as eleven words of closure. The truth is from your Lord; therefore never be among the doubters. Its brevity is functional. Everything arguable has been argued; what remains is the stance the hearer takes toward the finished argument. The verse supplies the label — *al-ḥaqq*, the truth, the real and the right — and the source of the label: not the community's consensus, not the strength of its proofs considered as human performance, but its Lord. A truth defined by its origin does not become more or less true by the reception it meets, which is why the recognition and concealment described in the previous verse can be stated without anxiety and closed with a command about the hearer's own heart.

**Addressed to the Prophet, Meant for Everyone**

The command is grammatically directed to the Prophet, and the exegetes are unanimous that it does not imply any doubt in him. The Prophet's compliance with such commands is taken as given; the address passes through him to his Companions and to believers generally. This is a standing rule of Quranic diction rather than an ad hoc rescue: seemingly superfluous commands to the Prophet — *follow what is revealed to you* (10:109), *be patient until Allah passes His judgment* (10:109) — function as public instruction delivered in the highest-register form available, because a command given to the one who least needs it binds everyone who needs it more. There is also a pedagogical economy in the form: the community learns its duties in sentences addressed to its leader, which places each duty under his example and makes the instruction impossible to receive as mere exhortation. The doubt forbidden here is therefore the community's doubt — named in the second person singular so that every reader encounters it as a personal prohibition rather than a general observation.

**The Formula's Other Appearances**

This exact closing formula — the truth has come from your Lord, so do not be among the doubters — appears twice more in the Qur'an, and the parallels calibrate its meaning. In the sūrah of Jonah: *"If you ˹O Prophet˺ are in doubt about ˹these stories˺ that We have revealed to you, then ask those who read the Scripture before you. The truth has certainly come to you from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt"* (10:94) — where the conditional is famously hypothetical, offered not because the doubt exists but because the means of dispelling it exists in the world: ask the Scripture-people, whose books corroborate. In the sūrah of the Cattle: *"Those who were given the Scripture know that it has been revealed ˹to you˺ from your Lord in truth. So do not be one of those who doubt"* (6:114) — where the prohibition again rests on the acknowledged knowledge of the very people the previous verse described as concealing. The three occurrences form a pattern: the truth is established by its divine origin, corroborated by the earlier books, and the only remaining question is the state of the hearer's heart. Each occurrence also sits at a juncture where the evidence has just been piled high — stories corroborated by Scripture in Jonah, a Book perfectly explained in the Cattle, a recognition described as parental certainty here in the Cow — and in each case the prohibition of doubt arrives not as the opening of an argument but as its closing seal.

**Why Doubt Is Named, and Forbidden Forever**

The prohibition carries an emphasis that the translation preserves: *do not ever be* renders a strengthened negative — the emphatic construction that forbids not an act but a state, and forbids it permanently. Doubt is singled out here for a precise reason: it is the only failure-mode left available to an honest hearer. Open refusal was addressed by the verses on opposition and concealed truth; ignorance was addressed by the arguments from chronology and recognition. What remains is the wavering of someone who has followed the argument and not yet settled into it — and the verse treats that wavering as a moral position rather than an epistemic accident, because after this much evidence, doubt is a thing one indulges rather than suffers. The emphatic forever closes the indulgence. In the qiblah's specific case the doubt had a social form — the community's worry about its dead, the whispers that a changing direction meant an unstable faith — and the verdict answers the social form by relocating certainty from the direction to its Source: the direction changed because the truth from your Lord includes the change.

**Truth From Your Lord**

The phrase *al-ḥaqq min rabbik* deserves its own weight, because it defines the epistemology of the whole passage. Truth is characterized by descent, not construction: it comes *from* your Lord, and the possessive — *your* Lord, in the singular addressed through the Prophet to every hearer — makes the descent personal. This is not truth as the output of a community's reasoning that the individual may audit; it is truth as a gift with a named Giver, and the hearer's relation to it is the relation of a servant to his Lord's speech. The formula also quietly answers the sectarian dispute that generated the passage: the Jews and Christians each claimed the truth as their tradition's property, while this verse assigns truth's ownership to God alone and makes every community's standing depend on reception rather than possession. What follows the verdict is not more argument but practice — the directions each community turns to, and the race in good deeds the next verse commands. Once truth is settled by its source, energy moves from disputing about it to competing within it.
