## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful,

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Root, Two Intensities**

Both names grow from *raḥmah* — mercy, or better, a mercy with tenderness inside it, the impulse that moves a stronger being to care for a weaker one. Arabic builds intensity out of roots by changing vowel patterns, and the two names use two different patterns. *Raḥmān* follows the *faʿlān* form, the same shape as words denoting a state that fills its subject completely; commentators accordingly treat it as the more emphatic and embracing of the two, mercy as a total condition. *Raḥīm* follows the *faʿīl* form, which carries a sense of constancy and repetition; its mercy is the one that keeps arriving, applied act after act, creature after creature. The pairing is not redundant but complementary — a flood and a stream from the same rain. Because the same two names appeared in the *basmalah* a verse earlier, the repetition forces the reader to slow down on them: before the sūrah moves to judgment in verse 4, mercy is stated twice, as attribute and again as its own verse, occupying the hinge of the opening.

**A Name Allah Never Shares — and One He Sometimes Does**

The two names behave differently outside the divine vocabulary, and the difference is diagnostic. *Al-Raḥmān* cannot be applied to any creature, literally or figuratively; it names the mercy by which existence itself is brought forth, and no finite being originates anything in that sense. *Al-Raḥīm*, by contrast, may describe creatures: the Qur'an itself uses the adjective of the Prophet ﷺ, *"gracious and merciful to the believers"* (9:128). Classical scholars drew the line accordingly — *Raḥmān* belongs to Allah alone, which is why the imposter Musaylimah's attempt to wear the title struck early Muslims as theft of a name. The closeness of *al-Raḥmān* to the supreme Name is stated in the Qur'an: *"Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names"* (17:110). The two are set side by side as equivalent addresses. Sūrah 55 then takes *al-Raḥmān* as its very title and opens with it, teaching the Qur'an, creating the human being, and giving him speech — mercy named before mercy's gifts.

**Universal Mercy and Special Mercy**

Commentators divide the labor between the two names. The mercy of *al-Raḥmān* is essential and universal: it is the mercy by which creation is brought forth and every existent thing receives its nourishment (*rizq*), believer and denier, animal and angel alike. None is excluded from existing, and existence is the first gift. The mercy of *al-Raḥīm* is attributive and particular: the sustained care by which each thing is kept, and, above all, the mercy Allah extends to the believers as their portion — He is *raḥīm* to them in this life through guidance and in the next through pardon. The Qur'an states the breadth of the first mercy without qualification: *"My mercy encompasses everything"* (7:156). The two aspects answer a question every reader eventually asks: if Allah is merciful, why does the world contain suffering? The names distinguish giving existence and sustenance — which is universal — from the final mercy of nearness and forgiveness, which follows the choices creatures make with what they were given.

**The Breath of the Compassionate**

Sufi cosmology built its account of creation on the first name. The immutable essences — *al-aʿyān al-thābitah*, the archetypes of all things held in divine knowledge — were brought into being when Allah breathed what the tradition calls the Breath of the Compassionate, *Nafas al-Raḥmān*, upon them. On this picture the existence of the world is not a manufacture but an exhalation of mercy; to be at all is to be breathed out by compassion. The Qur'an supports the association of *al-Raḥmān* with cosmic governance: *"the Most Compassionate, ˹Who is˺ established on the Throne"* (20:5). Names of beauty — the Kind, the Clement, the Gentle — are all manifestations of this mercy, while even the names of rigor conceal rather than cancel it, since divine power never acts apart from divine kindness. The doctrine gives verse 3 its metaphysical reach: the two names are not only descriptions of how Allah treats creatures but an account of why there are creatures. Mercy precedes the world, and the world is mercy's footprint.

**Set Between Space and Time**

The verse's placement inside the sūrah is structural. Verse 2 declares Allah Lord of all worlds — every level of being, every community of creatures, all of what might loosely be called space. Verse 4 declares Him Master of the Day of Judgment — all of time gathered to its final point. Verse 3 sits between the two, and the position states a claim without stating it: mercy fills the interval. Whatever exists, in whichever world, at whichever moment before the Day, exists inside the two names. The arrangement mirrors the Qur'an's larger habit of setting compassion between power and judgment — *"Ordain for us what is good in this life and the next"* is answered, *"My mercy encompasses everything"* (7:156). Read this way the opening of al-Fātiḥah is a small cosmology in four verses: essence and names, lordship over being, mercy pervading it, mastery over its end. The worshipper recites the map before he asks his place in it.

**Why the Prayer Repeats What the Basmalah Already Said**

A reader may ask what the second appearance of the names adds. The answer lies in the sūrah's liturgical shape. Al-Fātiḥah is a prayer, and prayers restate their ground at each turn; the *basmalah* above verse 1 is the threshold, while verse 3 makes mercy the substance of the address itself. The ḥadīth qudsī of the divided prayer marks the difference precisely: at the *basmalah*'s names in verse 3, Allah's response shifts from *My servant has praised Me* to *My servant has lauded Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a) — praise deepening into extolment. The repetition also guards against a misreading of what follows. Verse 4 will name Allah Master of the Day of Judgment, and judgment could be heard alone as menace; the two names placed immediately before it insist that the Master of that Day is the same who is Compassionate and Merciful now. Mercy is stated twice so that judgment, when it arrives, is received in its proper frame.
