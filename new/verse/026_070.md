## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:70

> when he questioned his father and his people, “What is that you worship ˹besides Allah˺?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham Begins With the Object of Worship**

Abraham's first recorded act in this story is a question. He asks his father and his people what they worship besides Allah. The question reaches both the household and the wider community. Idolatry is not treated as a private preference that no one may examine. Worship shapes a people's understanding of power, dependence, and moral duty, so Abraham addresses it openly.

The wording asks what the objects are before arguing about their claims. This is a useful beginning. A person must identify what he has placed in the position of god before he can judge whether that thing deserves worship. Abraham's question also carries a quiet challenge: the community may have inherited names and rituals, but can it explain why these objects deserve its devotion?

**The Question Includes Family Without Exempting Family**

Abraham speaks to his father as well as to the people. Kinship does not make a person beyond moral conversation. He does not begin by insulting his father or by denying the family bond. He brings the central question into the relationship where custom is often strongest. The household is not protected from the call to truth by affection or age.

This is difficult because disagreement with family can be treated as betrayal. Abraham's example distinguishes respect from surrender. A child may speak with care while refusing to call false worship true. The question is not a declaration that family has no value. It is a declaration that family loyalty cannot determine whom a person worships.

**Besides Allah Identifies the Problem as Rival Worship**

The phrase besides Allah places the idols within a larger theological claim. The issue is not that the community recognizes no reality beyond the visible world. It is that created objects have been placed beside the true Lord as recipients of worship. Abraham's question therefore exposes association: what has been added to the worship that belongs to God alone?

This wording also leaves room for a more subtle form of idolatry. A person may verbally acknowledge God while giving ultimate trust, fear, love, or obedience to something else. Abraham's immediate audience uses visible idols, but the principle reaches any created thing raised into a rival position. The question asks what is being worshipped and what place it has taken in the heart.

**A Question Can Be More Powerful Than an Accusation**

Abraham does not begin by declaring that his people are foolish. He asks them to name the object of their devotion. A question requires the hearer to participate. If the answer exposes weakness, the hearer has helped bring that weakness into view. This method can open reasoning where a blunt insult would produce only defensiveness.

The question is not neutral in its purpose. Abraham already knows that the idols cannot deserve worship, and the phrase besides Allah gives the direction of his judgment. But he invites his audience to hear the problem from their own words. Reform often begins when a person is made to articulate what he has been doing rather than hide behind a familiar label.

**The Story Connects to Abraham's Earlier Debate**

A related account in 6:74 records Abraham questioning his father about taking idols as gods. That parallel confirms that the family conversation is part of a consistent prophetic method. Abraham presses the question of what can be called a god before discussing signs in the heavens or the weakness of particular images.

The recurrence also shows that the Qur'an presents Abraham's monotheism as a deliberate break with inherited worship. He does not merely move away from idols in private. He calls the family and the community to account for the authority they have granted them. His questioning is therefore both intellectual and moral.

**Worship Is a Claim About Dependence**

To worship an object is to treat it as worthy of ultimate devotion and as connected to one's security or fate. Abraham's question asks whether the idols can bear that weight. He does not yet state their inability to hear, benefit, or harm; the next questions will develop those tests. Here he establishes the subject: what has the community made the center of its dependence?

That focus keeps worship from being reduced to ceremony. An idol is not only a statue used in a ritual. It is a claim about where help, protection, blessing, and authority come from. Abraham's challenge reaches the whole structure of life organized around the object. If the object is not truly a lord, then devotion to it rests on a false account of reality.

**The Community Must Answer Before It Can Defend Custom**

Inherited practice often begins with a command to repeat what ancestors did. Abraham's question reverses that order. Before the people explain why they follow a custom, they must say what the custom is aimed at. Naming the worship opens the way to examining its object and its consequences.

This is why the question remains relevant even where physical idols are absent. People can devote themselves to wealth, nation, status, or desire without calling those things gods. Abraham's method asks what receives the deepest obedience and hope. A community that answers honestly may discover that it has placed something besides Allah in the position reserved for Him.

The question is not satisfied by naming a ritual. It asks what the ritual assumes about dependence, protection, and authority. The answer can reveal the heart's true object. Worship is finally a claim about who has the right to command the self. That is why Abraham begins by asking what the community has placed before its eyes and heart. His method makes reflection a doorway through which inherited certainty can be examined.
