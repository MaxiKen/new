## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:40

> O children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you. Fulfil your covenant and I will fulfil Mine, and stand in awe of Me ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Named Address**

Everything before this verse in al-Baqarah spoke to humanity in general — the believers, the disbelievers, the hypocrites as types — or to Adam's children as a species. Verse 40 names a people for the first time: *Yā Banī Isrāʾīl*, O children of Israel. The vocative opens the sūrah's long central section, a covenant lawsuit running from here to roughly verse 123, in which a specific community — in its historical occasion, the Jewish tribes of Madina — is addressed as a party to a specific contract. The address will recur six times in the sūrah, at verses 40, 47, 83, 122, 211, and 246, each recurrence a bracket around a unit of argument; between the brackets runs the recital of favors, breaches, and renewals that makes al-Baqarah the Qur'an's most extended treatment of a single community's history. The naming is deliberate in its choice of title: the people are called by their ancestor's honorific — Isrāʾīl, servant of God, the name earned by Yaʿqūb — so the first word spoken to them is a word of dignity. The lawsuit opens, as lawsuits of this kind must, with the defendant's standing acknowledged.

**Remember First, Terms Second**

The first imperative of the address is not a demand but a memory: *udh-kurū niʿmatiya ʿalaykum* — remember My favors upon you. Only then come the terms: fulfil your covenant. The order is the sūrah's theology of law in miniature, and it matches the grammar of the ancient covenant documents the Arabs and Israelites alike inherited — suzerainty treaties that opened with a historical prologue reciting the lord's past benefactions before listing the obligations they grounded. Gratitude precedes command because command without remembered gift is mere coercion, and coercion produces compliance only while it is watched. *Dhikr*, remembrance, is not nostalgia here but an act of worship with a legal function: it establishes the consideration of the contract. The favors are the down payment Allah made before any promise was extracted, and the verse's structure insists that the covenant being renewed now was purchased then — in Egypt, at the sea, in the wilderness — long before Madina. A people who forget the gifts cannot keep the terms, because they will experience the terms as extraction rather than response.

**“Your Covenant and Mine”: The Bilateral Formula**

*Awfū bi-ʿahdī ūfi bi-ʿahdikum* — fulfil your covenant, and I will fulfil Mine (2:40). The two clauses mirror each other perfectly: the same verb twice, the same noun twice, the possessives exchanged. The symmetry states that this is one covenant with two sides, not a divine imposition with a human receipt. The first verb is imperative — the human side is the side that must begin, now, in performance. The second is the apocopate answer to it — *I will fulfil* — a future pledged in direct response to the command: do yours, and Mine is committed. The exegetes identify the contents along the covenant's own lines: the covenant of Allah upon them is what He obligated them to — the Torah's commands, the testimony to His prophets, and the description of the coming Messenger lodged in their own scripture (7:157); His covenant to them is what He pledged in return — forgiveness, the land, and the Garden. The fuller filing of the same contract in Sūrah al-Māʾidah states both ledgers explicitly: *"Allah made a covenant with the Children of Israel and appointed twelve leaders from among them and ˹then˺ said, 'I am truly with you. If you establish prayer, pay alms-tax, believe in My messengers, support them, and lend to Allah a good loan, I will certainly forgive your sins and admit you into Gardens under which rivers flow'"* (5:12). Conditions itemized; reward itemized; and the same verse closes the escape hatch — whoever disbelieves afterward has strayed from the right way.

**One Covenant, Many Filings**

The verse's singular *ʿahd* — my covenant, your covenant — gathers what the Qur'an elsewhere distributes across moments: the pledge taken at the mountain when it was raised over them (2:63, 2:93), the pledge of the twelve leaders (5:12), the pledge of the prophets to believe in and support the coming Messenger (3:81), the primordial pledge of every human soul to its Lord (7:172). The tradition reads these not as competing contracts but as renewals and specifications of one bond — which is why verse 40 can address seventh-century Madinans about a covenant sworn at Sinai: they are parties to it by descent, by scripture, and by the prophecy their scripture carries. The singular also protects the covenant from renegotiation. A contract with many separate filings can be argued clause against clause; one covenant with one God means every breach is a breach of the whole and every fulfillment counts toward the whole. When the coming recital lists failure after failure, the list is not an accumulation of unrelated defaults but the history of a single bond tested again and again — and each renewal offered in the recital, from the pardon after the calf onward, is the same covenant held open.

**“And Stand in Awe of Me Alone”**

The verse ends where al-Fātiḥah's theology ends: with exclusivity. *Wa-iyyāya fa-rhabūn* — and Me alone shall you dread. The pronoun is fronted before the verb, the same construction as *iyyāka naʿbudu* — You alone we worship (1:5) — and fronting in Arabic is the grammar of restriction: awe is to be directed, and directed only here. The word *rahba* names the dread that makes a person careful — not terror that paralyzes but the fear that governs conduct, the operative awe of a subject before a sovereign whose displeasure is real. The clause is aimed precisely at the obstacle the surrounding verses expose: the scholars who knew the Messenger's description and hesitated to confess it, because confession cost standing, income, and communal position (2:41). Every sale of the signs begins when something other than Allah is feared more than Allah; the fronted pronoun dissolves the veto by relocating supremacy. The covenant's final term is thus not a new duty but the condition of all the others — a single object of dread, from which obedience flows without a second loyalty to negotiate against.

**A Lawsuit That Keeps Its Door Open**

Verse 40 sets the register for everything that follows, and the register is worth stating exactly, because the coming recital will be severe. This is a prosecution that opens with favors, grounds its demands in a bilateral promise, and closes its opening line with an invitation to awe rather than a threat of force. Nothing in the verse suggests a people written off: a covenant renewed is a covenant still standing, and the God who says *I will fulfil Mine* has pledged His side in the same breath that He demands theirs. The Madinan occasion makes the address concrete — real tribes, real scholars, a real decision pending about a real Prophet ﷺ — but the six recurrences of the address across the sūrah make it permanent equipment for every reader: al-Baqarah will treat covenant-consciousness through the fullest case file revelation contains, and it begins the treatment the way all sound treatment begins — with the gift remembered before the duty is named. Remember. Fulfil. And Me alone, dread. Three imperatives, in that order, holding one contract open.
