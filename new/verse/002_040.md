## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:40

> O children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you. Fulfil your covenant and I will fulfil Mine, and stand in awe of Me ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Community Addressed by Name**

After thirty-nine verses the sūrah turns to a named people and speaks to them directly. Israel is another name for Jacob, and the Book addresses his descendants as a family with a recorded history rather than as a religious category. The community living in and around Madinah at the time of the revelation was being spoken to about its own ancestry, and the sūrah will continue in this mode, with a few interruptions, for most of its middle section.

That choice of audience explains the character of what follows. What the sūrah asks of this community is not conversion to an unfamiliar religion but fidelity to an existing relationship. The God speaking in these verses is the one who delivered their ancestors from Egypt, and the Book presents itself as a continuation of a story the audience already knows.

**"Remember My Favours"**

The favours being invoked are named a few verses later in the same recitation: deliverance from the persecution of Pharaoh, the parting of the sea, and the provisions given in the desert. The Book also summarises the same history in terms of what this people received in religious goods: *“Indeed, We gave the Children of Israel the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood; granted them good, lawful provisions; and favoured them above the others.”* (45:16). Scripture, wisdom and prophecy are named there alongside the bread and the safety.

The word for favour in this verse the Book uses to cover both kinds. Its sense is generous, and its range includes the rescue of a slave people, the sending of messengers, and the establishment of a community with a law. The command to remember is therefore a command to read history as a record of gifts rather than as a claim of ancestry, and the sūrah will shortly fill the record in for anyone who has forgotten it.

**The Covenant, and How the Book Describes It**

Two covenants are described in detail elsewhere. One is made with the Children of Israel collectively, and it lists the terms: *“If you establish prayer, pay alms-tax, believe in My messengers, support them, and lend to Allah a good loan, I will certainly forgive your sins and admit you into Gardens under which rivers flow.”* (5:12). The other covenant is made with the prophets as a body, binding each of them to believe in and support a messenger who comes after them confirming what they have (3:81).

The Book treats both as the same kind of arrangement, and the sūrah here addresses a community that inherited the first. What the verse asks is that the terms of that arrangement be honoured, and the sentence is explicit that the arrangement was mutual: they fulfil their side, and God fulfils His.

**"And I Will Fulfil Mine"**

The divine side of the promise is stated in the same sentence as the human obligation, and the Book never leaves the reader to guess at God's fidelity. The verse's grammar makes the two halves conditional on each other, but the Book elsewhere states God's trustworthiness as a settled attribute rather than a possibility: the covenant with the prophets is answered with an affirmation, they accept it, and God declares the witnesses.

Read against the human record the sūrah will go on to recount, the structure is an act of extraordinary generosity. The community being addressed has a history in which the covenant was repeatedly broken, the calf was worshipped, and the book was partly concealed. The promise in this verse is not withdrawn for that reason. It is reissued, with the terms restated, to the audience at hand.

**"And Stand in Awe of Me Alone"**

The command returns to the vocabulary the sūrah has used since its second verse: the quality rendered here as awe is the same one that describes the people for whom the Book is a guide. The word comes from a root that means to guard or shield oneself, and the Book treats it as the discipline of a person who arranges his conduct around what he knows is coming.

The addition of the word *alone* in this verse carries the weight. A community with a long history of foreign rule had every reason to fear the powers around it, and the sūrah's address to Israel includes instructions that will require courage and public conduct. The command to stand in awe of God alone is therefore not an emotional prompt but a political orientation, and it sets the terms for everything the subsequent verses require.

**Continuity Rather than Replacement**

The verse addresses a covenant rather than announcing a fresh one, and it invokes a memory rather than a discovery. That framing governs the next several pages of the sūrah, in which the revelation does not ask this community to repudiate its own scriptures but to accept what those scriptures pointed toward. The Book's claim about itself in this section is continuity, and the terms of the address in this verse prepare the reader for that claim.

What the sentence establishes is a relationship with a past, and the sūrah will make the same argument to the same community repeatedly. The favours named are their favours; the covenant is theirs; the God being addressed is the one their ancestors knew; and the request being made is that the record be honoured.

**Whose History the Sūrah Is Telling**

The address claims the audience's own ancestry and reads the record of that ancestry as a series of gifts. The Book states the terms of the covenant with this community in a later passage as an ordinary agreement with items on both sides: prayer, almsgiving, belief in the messengers and support for them, answered by forgiveness and gardens (5:12). The same chapter records the covenant with the prophets as a body, binding them to accept a messenger who confirms what they have (3:81).

Neither passage is presented as an innovation. The sūrah's argument to this audience assumes that the arrangement already exists and that the audience knows it exists, and that is why the command in this verse is to remember rather than to learn. What the Book adds is the claim that its own messenger belongs inside that existing history, which is the point the following verses develop.
