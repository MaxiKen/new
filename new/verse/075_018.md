## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:18

> So once We have recited a revelation ˹through Gabriel˺, follow its recitation ˹closely˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Follow Before You Recite**

The instruction establishes an order, and the order is the whole point. *Fa-ittabiʿ qurʾānah* — so follow its reciting — puts the Prophet in the position of follower rather than speaker. He is not to run alongside the revelation or ahead of it; he is to come after it. The sequence reverses what a person might expect from a messenger, since the natural assumption is that a prophet recites and others follow. Here the Prophet is told to follow, and only then to recite. His role at the moment of descent is to receive, and his role as reciter begins when the descent is finished. The text he will later deliver is something he first heard.

**Who Is Doing the Reciting**

The subject of the verb is God: *when We recite it*. The Qur'an attributes the recitation to its Author and treats the angel and the Prophet as the means through which it reaches human ears, and the early exegesis follows the text closely. Ibn ʿAbbās glossed this verse as an instruction to listen and be silent, and then explained that afterwards the Messenger would recite it as Gabriel had recited it to him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4928). The same narration fills in what the listening looked like in practice: whenever Gabriel came, the Prophet would listen, and when he departed he would recite as Gabriel had recited. The discipline described here became a yearly institution. Abū Hurayra reported that Gabriel used to repeat the recitation of the Qur'an with the Prophet once every year, and twice in the year in which he died, and a second narration in the same collection has Gabriel meeting him every night of Ramaḍān to review the Qur'an with him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4998; 6). The review was a listening as much as a reciting, with each party checking the other's reading. That is the discipline of this verse written into the community's calendar, and it explains why the annual revision of the text became a standing practice rather than an occasional one.

**Silence as the First Obedience**

The instruction to follow implies silence, and the implication was drawn out by the earliest commentators. To follow a recitation means not to contribute to it while it is happening — no finishing of phrases, no anticipation of endings, no simultaneous rehearsal. The prohibition in v. 16 and the instruction here are two halves of one discipline: the tongue is stopped so that the ear can work. The Qur'an states the same obligation to the believing community in a Madinan verse, where listening during recitation is commanded and silence is prescribed, with mercy attached to the practice. The principle reaches beyond the ritual setting. Before a person can respond to revelation, he has to have let it finish, and the willingness to let something finish is itself a form of obedience.

**Why the Sequence Matters Theologically**

There is a reason the order is fixed rather than incidental. If the Prophet had been permitted to recite along with the revelation, the distinction between what was revealed and what he produced would have been harder to maintain in practice, and the community's confidence in the text would have rested on his care rather than on the promise in v. 17. The sequence protects the text's origin. It also protects the Prophet from a burden he could not discharge, since a man cannot simultaneously attend to what is being said to him and secure it against loss. The divine recitation comes first, the human recitation second, and the second is described in the following verse as having its explanation supplied by the same Speaker.

**The Pace of the Descent**

The same principle governs how the revelation reached the community. It was not sent down in a single delivery but in stages, and the reason given for that arrangement is the reason given in this verse: *“˹We have sent it˺ as such ˹in stages˺ so We may reassure your heart with it. And We have revealed it at a deliberate pace.”* (25:32). The objectors wanted it all at once, on the ground that a scripture should arrive complete; the reply is that the pace was chosen for the recipient's sake. *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace.”* (17:106). The objectors who wanted the whole scripture at once were asking, in effect, for a text they could control — a document complete on delivery, available for consultation, usable on their own timetable. What they were given instead was a recitation that arrived in instalments and had to be followed. Listening is therefore built into the structure of the revelation rather than demanded only of its first hearer.

**A Rule for Reading**

The verse has always been read as a rule for anyone who opens the text, and the rule is simple to state and hard to keep. Follow the recitation: let the passage say what it says before deciding what it means, and let the whole of it be heard before any part is put to work. The opposite practice is the one the Qur'an complains about — a people who received the recitation with neglect (25:30) — and its most respectable version is the hurry of the student who reaches conclusions on partial evidence. The next verse completes the arrangement by assigning the explanation to God, which leaves the reader with the one task he can actually perform: attending until the Speaker has finished. Nothing in that instruction diminishes the reader's effort. It places the effort where it can do some good, which is after the text has been heard rather than during it.
