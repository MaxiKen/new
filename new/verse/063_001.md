## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:1

> When the hypocrites come to you ˹O Prophet˺, they say, “We bear witness that you are certainly the Messenger of Allah”—and surely Allah knows that you are His Messenger—but Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are truly liars.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A True Sentence That Is Still a Lie**

The sentence they pronounce is correct. Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah, and they say so. The verse offers no correction of the wording, because none is needed; what collapses is the speaker, not the statement. Faith in the Qur'an is an affirmation of the heart, and the tongue is its witness. When the two separate, the testimony becomes perjury. So the verse does not say that they misspoke. It says that Allah bears witness that they are liars, and it delivers that verdict in the same breath as its confession of the Messenger's rank.

The ninth sūrah makes the same move against the same men: *“They swear by Allah that they never said anything ˹blasphemous˺, while they did in fact utter a blasphemy, lost faith after accepting Islam, and plotted what they could not carry out.”* (9:74). An oath repeated is not evidence. Knowledge belongs to God, and His knowledge of what a man holds inside is what this verse appeals to. Speech is judged here by the state that produces it.

**Two Witnesses at One Threshold**

The sentence is built on a deliberate contrast of verbs. Of the Messenger it says that Allah *knows* — a statement of fact, undisputed. Of the hypocrites it says that Allah *bears witness* — the same word the hypocrites had just used of themselves. They arrived with a shahāda, a formal attestation, and they receive one back. The difference is that the second attestation cannot be impeached.

There is also an asymmetry in the naming. The Messenger is identified personally and emphatically: *you are certainly the Messenger of Allah*. The accusers remain a category: *the hypocrites*, a class, not a roster. The verse declines to convict named individuals on its own authority, because the record of their words is enough. What the sūrah will do afterwards is describe them so precisely that the description itself becomes the identification.

The outcome of the whole chapter is thus announced at its opening. This is not an inquiry into who is sincere. It is a disclosure of what God already knows, set down for a community that could not see it.

**The Report Behind the Verse**

The occasion that Muslims have transmitted for this passage is concrete. During an expedition, a quarrel broke out between a man of the Emigrants and a man of the Helpers, each calling on his own group for support. ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl, the leading man of Madinah before Islam arrived there, said of the Emigrants that they should not be supported and that on the return to Madinah the mightier would drive out the weaker. Zayd ibn Arqam, then a young man, heard it and reported it. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2772 collects the narration of Zayd himself, who said that the Prophet sent for Ibn Ubayy and his companions; they swore that they had said nothing, and the sūrah then came down. The Prophet recited it to Zayd and told him, *Allah has confirmed your statement*.

Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4907 preserves the same episode from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh, including ʿUmar's demand that the man's head be struck off and the Prophet's refusal: *Leave him, lest the people say that Muhammad kills his companions*. The revelation of the verse, in other words, did not end an argument; it settled one, publicly and in writing.

**"When They Come to You" — the Habit, Not the Incident**

The opening uses the plural noun with the present-tense verb: whenever the hypocrites come to you, they say. The construction describes a practice rather than an accident. These men return, repeat, and repeat again, and the verse marks the repetition.

Their movement is also directional. They come *to you* — to the Prophet in person, not to a doctrine in the abstract. What they are seeking is proximity, and proximity in Madinah was worth having: it carried standing, protection, and a share in decisions. The declaration of faith is the price of admission, and the verse reports how cheaply they pay it.

That is the difference between this passage and a general denunciation of unbelief. An open denier stays away; the hypocrite arrives, sits in the gathering, and speaks the community's own language. His attendance is the disguise. The rest of the sūrah will strip it away piece by piece, beginning here with the words he uses to enter.

**The Burrow With Two Doors**

The Arabic noun for hypocrisy, nifāq, is built on nafaq, a tunnel or burrow, and the lexicographers connect it to the desert jerboa, which digs a passage with more than one opening. One mouth is plain to see; the other, the nāfiqāʾ, is hidden, and through it the animal escapes when the first is threatened. Al-Qurṭubī records this derivation and draws the moral from it. A hypocrite is a creature of two exits, always able to leave by the door nobody is watching.

The image explains the behaviour in the two clauses that follow: an oath becomes a shield, and the covenant is kept only while it protects. It also explains why this verse can call sincere-looking men liars without alleging that every sentence they utter is false. The lie is structural. What they present in the open and what they keep in reserve are not the same thing, and they know it.

The form of the word is durative, a pattern that suggests habitual practice rather than a single lapse. Hypocrisy, in this reading, is not a moment of weakness but a settled method of moving through a community.

**Faith Is Held in the Heart, Not Sold by the Tongue**

The Prophet ﷺ described the behavioural marks of the disease with precision: Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 33 has three — lying when speaking, breaking promises, betraying trusts — and Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 34 adds a fourth, foul and unrestrained speech in disputes. He called the man who carries these traits a hypocrite *until he gives it up*, which means the condition can be shed by repentance and repair. The scholars of the discipline distinguished this lesser, practical hypocrisy from the greater hypocrisy of belief that this verse confronts.

The Qur'an states the greater case plainly. Those who say one thing in public and another in private are described as mocking even while they testify: *“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.””* (2:14). And their self-deception is measured in another place: *“They seek to deceive Allah and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it.”* (2:9).

Both verses hold the tongue and the heart to a single standard. What 63:1 records is the failure at the point where the two are supposed to meet.
