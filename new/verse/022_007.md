## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:7

> And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Is Grammatically the Tail of the One Before It**

The Arabic of verse 7 is a continuation: *wa-anna al-sāʿata ātiyatun lā rayba fīhā* — and *because* the Hour is coming, no doubt in it. The same causal particle *anna* that opened all three clauses of verse 6 opens this one. In the grammar, verses 6 and 7 are a single sentence: that is because God is the Truth, because He gives life to the dead, because He has power over everything, and because the Hour is coming.

So the Resurrection is not added to the argument after the argument is finished. It is the fourth premise, listed in the same breath as the divine attributes. The effect is to make the Hour a matter of what God *is* rather than a matter of what will happen on a schedule. A God who is *al-Ḥaqq*, who gives life, who is capable of everything, is a God before whom graves are not a difficulty. Denying the Hour on this reading is not an error about the future; it is a mismatch in one's account of God.

**Where the Argument Stops and the Assertion Begins**

Verses 5 and 6 argued. Verse 7 stops arguing and asserts: the Hour is coming, there is no doubt in it.

The move is characteristic and worth naming, because it looks like a gap and is not. Reasoning brings a person as far as the conclusion that the Resurrection is coherent and more than coherent — grounded in what he already accepts about embryos, fields and the divine power. Reasoning cannot supply certainty about a future event, and the Qur'an does not pretend otherwise. It supplies the assertion instead, and it supplies it after the reasoning so that the assertion does not have to do the reasoning's work.

This ordering protects both halves. Without verse 5, verse 7 would be an unargued claim. Without verse 7, verse 5 would be an interesting observation about biology.

**"No Doubt in It" Removes the Doubt, Not the Difficulty**

*Lā rayba fīhi* — no doubt in it. The phrase is used in the Qur'an's opening for the Book itself, and its transfer to the Hour is a deliberate equivalence. The Book and the Hour are the two things the Qur'an describes this way, and the shared formula ties them together: the text that cannot be doubted announces the event that cannot be doubted.

The negation also tells us where the doubt actually sits. It is not said that the Hour is easy to conceive, or that the mind finds it natural. What is denied is that the doubt has an object. A person who doubts the Hour is not responding to a genuine uncertainty in the evidence; he is responding to something else entirely, which the sūrah named two verses earlier as disputing without knowledge (22:3).

**"Those in the Graves"**

The second assertion is *wa-anna Allāha yabʿathu man fī al-qubūri* — God will raise those who are in the graves. The word is *qubūr*, the ordinary plural for graves, not a poetic term for the underworld.

That plainness is the point. The Qur'an's claim is not about a general immortality of the soul or a survival of influence; it is about the contents of specific holes in the ground. Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt puts the same vision in a single line: *“Do they not know that when the contents of the graves will be spilled out”* (100:9). Sūrah Fāṭir uses the same phrase to mark the limit of the Prophet's own mission: *“Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ makes whoever He wills hear, but you ˹O Prophet˺ can never make those in the graves hear ˹your call˺”* (35:22).

The pairing of the two texts is instructive. Human calling stops at the grave; the divine raising begins there. The same bodies that cannot hear a prophet will hear the summons that ends the world, and the verse in front of us is the promise that it will happen.

**The Hour Is Coming in the Present Tense**

*Ātiyatun* is an active participle: coming, arriving, on its way. Not *will come* in the flat future sense, but *is coming* — already in motion toward its appointment.

The grammatical form does the work that a whole paragraph of exhortation would otherwise have to do. A thing on its way has a direction and a clock; the interval between this sentence and that arrival is not an open-ended postponement but a distance being covered. The sūrah that opened with the world shaking apart now says that the shaking is in transit.

**Doubt Is Traced as a Condition Throughout This Sūrah**

The word *rayb* does not appear here by accident. It is the same noun the sūrah used in verse 5, when it addressed people *in doubt* about the Resurrection, and it will return at verse 55, where the disbelievers remain in doubt *“until the Hour takes them by surprise, or the torment of a terminating Day comes to them”* (22:55).

Read together, the three occurrences describe a progression rather than a repeated word. In verse 5 the doubt is a starting point that can be argued with and is argued with, at length. In verse 7 the doubt is denied any object: there is nothing in it. By verse 55 the doubt has become permanent, and the thing that ends it is not an argument but the arrival of the very event that was doubted.

That is the sūrah's diagnosis, and it is more precise than a simple condemnation of scepticism. Doubt, in this account, is not the absence of evidence but a settled posture that evidence does not move. The difference between the audience of verse 5 and the people of verse 55 is not that one group had better arguments. It is that one group was still listening.

**Resurrection Precedes Judgment in the Sūrah's Order**

Notice where this verse falls in the sūrah's architecture. The court scenes — the garments of fire, the boiling water, the maces of iron — do not appear until verses 19–22, and the great judgment passage is reserved for verses 56–57. The Resurrection is established first, on its own, with no description of what follows it.

The order is a teaching decision. Judgment is only dreadful to someone who expects to stand in it, which requires first believing that the dead are raised. A sūrah that opened its judgment scenes before it established the Resurrection would be describing a courtroom to people who do not believe they will attend it.
