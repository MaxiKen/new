## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:88

> This is Allah’s guidance with which He guides whoever He wills of His servants. Had they associated others with Him ˹in worship˺, their ˹good˺ deeds would have been wasted.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guidance Identified as God's Gift**

This is Allah's guidance with which He guides whoever He wills of His servants. The sentence states what the catalogue of names has been demonstrating, and it names the guidance as God's rather than as the possession of the men who received it. The Book uses the same construction when it describes the guidance given to the prophets of the line of Abraham, and the possessive form keeps the gift attached to its giver.

The statement also answers the question the sūrah's audience had been raising about the Prophet. If guidance is given to whoever God wills, then the identity of the man carrying the message is not the decisive question, and the Book had already said as much in the verse about the poor believers when it stated that God knows best who is grateful (6:53). The catalogue in the previous verses shows the same principle at work across centuries.

**The Conditional Sentence About the Prophets**

Had they associated others with Him in worship, their good deeds would have been wasted. The sentence is the sharpest in the passage, and it makes a claim about the men whose names the sūrah has just honoured. Even the prophets, if they had committed the one offence the sūrah has been describing, would have lost the value of everything they did.

The Book states the same rule elsewhere in the second person. The sūrah of the Troops records the revelation given to the Prophet and to the prophets before him, that if anyone associates others with God his deeds will certainly be void and he will be among the losers (39:65). The verse in front of us takes that rule and applies it hypothetically to the greatest figures in the catalogue, which is the Book's way of making the point impossible to evade by reference to ancestral standing.

**What the Sentence Is Not Saying**

The verse is not reporting a fault in the prophets, since the Book declares them protected in their mission and honours them throughout. What the hypothetical does is remove the audience's last refuge in genealogy. The Makkans were arguing that the religion of their fathers was sufficient because their fathers were the men of the tradition, and this sentence states that the tradition's own heroes would have been ruined by the practice the audience was defending.

The point lands with particular force because the sūrah has just included Abraham in the catalogue, and Abraham's declaration ended with the words that he was not one of the polytheists (6:79). The audience's claim to his legacy is thus met with a statement about what would have happened to him had he done what they were doing, and the sūrah supplies no room for negotiation on that point.

**The Doctrine of Wasted Deeds**

The expression about deeds being wasted appears repeatedly in the Book for those who mix association with belief, and it describes a loss that cannot be made up. The sūrah of the Family of Imran says of those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers that their deeds are wasted in this world and the next, and the sūrah of the Cow uses the same language for the works of those who deny the signs of God and the meeting with Him.

The Book's teaching on this subject explains what the sūrah's audience stood to lose and what it did not. Their generosity, their hospitality, their keeping of promises, and their care for their families were all real, and the Book does not say that God ignores the good that people do. What it says is that the value of a deed depends on the axis around which a life is built, and the deeds of a person who sets up partners with God are described as scattered.

**The Warning at the End of the Abraham Section**

The verse closes the pericope that began with Abraham's address to his father, and it closes it with a warning rather than with a conclusion about Abraham's greatness. The sūrah has taken the audience through the story of their ancestor, his reasoning, his declaration, his argument with his people, and the line of prophets that followed him, and it now states the one thing that would have destroyed all of it.

That is a rhetorical move the Book uses at the ends of its stories, and it leaves the reader with the practical question the passage was designed to raise. The line of men the audience honoured had all been guided by the same God on the same path, and the practice the audience had inherited was the one thing the sūrah says could have taken that guidance away from any of them.

**What the Sūrah Says Next**

The verse that follows this one describes the men of the catalogue as those to whom God gave the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood, and it warns that if the audience disbelieves in the message, God has already entrusted it to a people who will not disbelieve in it (6:89). The two sentences belong to one movement: the gift is named, the danger to its recipients is stated, and the message is passed to those who will carry it.

The sūrah is thus completing its treatment of the audience's reliance on lineage. Having shown the line, honoured it, and stated the condition attached to it, the sūrah now announces that the trust has been transferred, and it does so without naming the people in question and without softening the sentence. The next verses continue with the same theme, describing the men who were given the Book and the guidance that came with it.
