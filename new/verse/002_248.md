## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:248

> Their prophet further told them, "The sign of Saul's kingship is that the Ark will come to you—containing reassurance from your Lord and relics of the family of Moses and the family of Aaron, which will be carried by the angels. Surely in this is a sign for you, if you ˹truly˺ believe."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sign Announced**

The prophet's answer to the objection moves from argument to evidence: the appointment will be confirmed by a sign, and the sign is the Ark — the tabūt, the sacred chest of Israel's sanctuary-history, missing from the community's life, now promised back. The verse's structure is precise about what the sign does: it authenticates the kingship (*the sign of Saul's kingship*), and it authenticates the prophet who announced it — al-Ṭabarī reads the prophet's gesture as self-validating: the Ark returns while I am your judge, so believe my prophecy about your king. The narrations describe the Ark's history among the Israelites as one of loss — captured in battle, or hidden, or neglected into disappearance — and its return as an event that could only be divine: a lost national sanctuary restored, carried by angels, arriving as the credential of an unlikely king. The chiefs had asked for deservings and resumes; the answer gives them a miracle with a luggage-list.

**Sakīnah: The Reassurance Inside**

The Ark's first content is *reassurance from your Lord* — sakīnah, the tranquility-word the Qur'an reserves for divine calm sent into human hearts. The commentators divide on its mode here: an object inside the Ark whose presence arouses tranquility, or the sakīnah itself — the quality, contained. Al-Rāzī notes the grammar permits a third reading: the Ark comes *on account of which* there will be tranquility — the chest as the sign's vehicle rather than the calm's container. Whichever mode is meant, the word's Qur'anic network is fixed and martial: the sakīnah sent down upon the Messenger and the believers after the battle's shaking (9:26), sent down again in the cave and at the treaty-year's crisis; the serenity sent into the believers' hearts *so that they may increase even more in their faith* (48:4). In every instance the word names God's answer to a community's fear — and its placement here, ahead of a campaign, tells the objecting chiefs what their king's real equipment is: not wealth, but the divinely-sent steadiness the Ark carries.

**The Relics of the Two Houses**

The second content is *relics of the family of Moses and the family of Aaron* — baqiyyah, a remnant, a remainder. The exegetes enumerate the candidates the tradition preserves: the staff of Moses — the rod that struck the sea and the rock (2:60); the fragments of the stone tablets brought down from Sinai, the broken pieces preserved after the first tablets' shattering (7:150); the manna of the wilderness years (2:57); and the vestments of the two brothers, including the sandals Moses was told to remove in the sacred valley (20:12). The list's significance is cumulative rather than archaeological: everything inside the Ark is a residue of revelation — objects that touched the founding events of Israel's faith, kept by God's people and lost by them, now returned. The two families named are the two offices of that founding — prophecy in Moses, priesthood in Aaron — and the remnant of both houses, riding in one chest, tells the objecting chiefs that the kingdom being authenticated stands in the line of both.

**Carried by the Angels**

The mode of the Ark's arrival is its own claim: *which will be carried by the angels*. The narrations describe the manner variously — the chest borne between heaven and earth in plain sight, or delivered by angelic carriage to the appointed place — and the variation does not touch the point: the return is visibly supernatural, performed in a mode no human caravan could imitate and no thief could stage. The angelic carriage answers the sign's evidentiary requirement exactly. A recovered artifact could be explained — found, bought, forged; an arrival carried by angels cannot. The commentators note that this is the verse's response to the chiefs' implicit demand for proof commensurate with the appointment's strangeness: an unlikely king is authenticated by an impossible event, and the impossibility is public, witnessed, and attributed — the angels are named so that no one can credit the prophet's politics with the chest's return.

**The Biblical Background and Its Divergence**

The exegetes are candid that much of the surrounding material is biblical, and the divergences are as instructive as the parallels. In the books of Samuel the Ark was captured by the Philistines, afflicted its captors with plagues until they returned it, and then sat for decades in a private house at Kiriath-jearim — a period the biblical record itself marks as religious decline, Israel indifferent to its own sanctuary; David would later say, *we did not turn to it in the days of Saul* (1 Chronicles 13:3). The Bible's chronology thus places the Ark's return long before Saul's reign and records its neglect during it; the Qur'an gives no chronology at all, and al-Ṭabarī records the reading that resolves the tension: the Ark's restoration to Israel's life — functionally, as a sign — occurs around this appointment, whether or not the chest had sat physically nearby. One Muslim narration adds that from its Philistine return until Saul's time the people were prevented from approaching the Ark, a single family guarding it: present but inaccessible, its blessing withheld. On every reading the Qur'an's interest is identical — not where the chest was, but what its coming means: a neglected sacred history returning as the credential of God's chosen king.

**A Sign for You, If You Believe**

The verse's closing conditional ties the sign to its receivers: *Surely in this is a sign for you, if you truly believe*. The conditional has a double edge the commentators draw out. For the believing, the sign is a sign — the returned Ark confirms the appointment and the community rallies behind its king. For the objecting, the same event confirms nothing, because a sign is not a proof that compels but an address that requires a hearer: the sakīnah it carries is for hearts disposed to be steadied, and the relics it carries speak only to those who remember the houses they came from. The verse thus ends the authentication where the objection began — in the chiefs' own interior — and hands them the decision the whole story has been building toward: the evidence has arrived, carried by angels, containing the reassurance their fear of the coming war will need; whether it functions as evidence is a property of their faith, not of the Ark. The next scene opens with the king on the march, and the sign's conditional will be answered not in argument but at a river.
