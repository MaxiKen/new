## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:45

> Recite what has been revealed to you of the Book and establish prayer. Indeed, ˹genuine˺ prayer should deter ˹one˺ from indecency and wickedness. The remembrance of Allah is ˹an˺ even greater ˹deterrent˺. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows what you ˹all˺ do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Commands Joined in One Sentence**

The instruction pairs recitation of the Book with the establishment of prayer, and the two are connected rather than merely listed. The Arabic verb used for prayer means to set it up, to make it stand, which describes an institution maintained deliberately rather than a feeling that comes and goes. Recitation is the traffic between the believer and revelation; prayer is the traffic between the believer and his Lord, and the verse gives both to a Prophet standing in a hostile city. The command arrives immediately after a passage about how things that are relied on can fail, and its placement suggests the answer the sūrah is developing: what holds a person up is not a structure he builds but a relationship he maintains.

The pairing also shows that the two activities belong to one programme. Recitation without prayer leaves a hearer informed but undisciplined, and prayer without recitation leaves him performing a form whose content he does not carry, so the Book asks for both in a single sentence.

**Prayer as a Deterrent, Stated as a Fact**

The verse does not merely recommend prayer; it states an effect. Genuine prayer should deter a person from indecency and from wrongdoing, and the Arabic construction carries the sense of a designed purpose. The criterion is unusually practical, since it gives the one praying a way to test what he is doing. A prayer that leaves the person exactly as he was, with the same conduct and the same appetites, is still obligatory and still valid in form, but the Book describes its intended function as unachieved. Commentators discuss at length whether the deterrent operates automatically through the act itself or through the understanding produced by what is recited in it, and the two explanations are complementary in practice: the rite is the container, and the meaning is the load.

**The River at the Door**

The Prophet described the effect of the five daily prayers in a comparison that has been preserved with its chain and its collection: he asked his companions whether a man with a river at his door who bathed in it five times a day would retain any dirt, and when they answered that no trace would remain, he told them that this is the likeness of the five prayers, with which God erases sins (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 528, narrated by Abū Hurayrah). The image is chosen for its ordinariness. Nobody would call a bath a heroic act, and the Prophet's point is that the benefit lies in the repetition of something unremarkable at a fixed address. What wears away a person's failings in this account is not intensity but return.

The image also makes the standard of purity ordinary rather than heroic. A person is asked to bathe in water he can already see, which means the instruction assumes a resource at hand rather than an exceptional effort, and the same assumption runs through the worship the sūrah asks of its audience.

**The Sanctity of the One Who Prays**

The Book states in another place that God accepts only from the reverent (5:27), and the earliest readers of this verse drew the same line between the act and its fruit. Al-Ṭabarī, commenting on this verse, records from Ibn ʿAbbās that the one whose prayer does not restrain him from indecency and wrongdoing gains nothing from God through it but distance, and he records the same verdict from al-Ḥasan and Qatāda. Al-Baghawī carries the saying from Ibn Masʿūd and Ibn ʿAbbās in a fuller form — prayer contains a stopping point and a restraint against disobedience, and the one unrestrained by it gains only distance — and adds a harsher formulation from al-Ḥasan and Qatāda, that such a prayer turns against the person offering it. He also records from Jābir, with its chain of narration, a report that a man told the Prophet of someone who recited the Qur'an all night and then stole in the morning, and received the answer that his recitation would restrain him.

Ibn Kathīr supplies the fact-check rather than another chain. He lists the versions that raise this wording to the Prophet and concludes that the soundest of them all are the ones stopping at the Companions and the Successors, naming Ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan, and Qatāda; and through al-Aʿmash from Mālik ibn al-Ḥārith he reports that Ibn Masʿūd, told of a man who prayed at great length, answered that prayer benefits only the one who obeys it, then recited this verse. Al-Qurṭubī preserves the same exchange in a shorter form. The verse itself remains the canonical statement of the principle and is enough for the reader's purposes. What all of these formulations share is a refusal to separate the act from its fruit, and the Book never treats worship as a transaction that can be completed in isolation from the life that contains it.

The protection is also stated in a way that does not depend on the worshipper's reputation. What restrains him is the act itself rather than the standing he has built up, and generations of readers have taken from the sentence the reassurance that a person's worship counts for something even when the rest of his record is uneven.

**The Remembrance That Is Greater**

The clause that follows has been read in two main ways, and both are preserved in the commentaries. One reading takes remembrance of God as the greatest of all acts of worship, greater than the rest of what a person does. The other takes the comparison differently: God's remembrance of the believer is greater than the believer's remembrance of Him, since the second is bound to time and place while the first is not. Both readings fit the Book's usage, and a third possibility is that the sentence deliberately holds them together. On any reading the verse refuses to leave prayer as an isolated duty and connects it to a state that continues outside it, which is why the Book elsewhere gives the command to establish prayer for the sake of remembrance (20:14).

**What Recitation Contributes**

The command to recite placed at the head of the verse gives the believer a supply of content for the inner life. The Book describes itself as a healing and a mercy for the believers (17:82) and as guidance for the conscious (2:2), and the meaning of what is recited is what makes the deterrence of the previous clause intelligible. A person who recites a passage about the fates of towns that answered their messengers with mockery carries the argument with him into the rest of his day. The sūrah's historical review, which ends in this verse, was not assembled for the reader's information; it was assembled so that it could be recited, and this sentence tells him to keep reciting it.

The recitation also supplies the content that prayer then expresses. A community that recited these verses knew what it was asking for and what it was committing itself to, and putting reading beside prayer treats the text as the working material of the devotion rather than as a preliminary to it.

**Prayer That Is Work Rather Than Relief**

The Book never presents the prayer as a pause from the obligations the sūrah has been describing; it presents it as the resource that makes them possible. Believers are told to seek help through patience and prayer, with the added note that this is a burden except for the humble (2:45), and the Book's list of the qualities of successful believers opens with those who humble themselves in prayer (23:1–2). The connection to this sūrah is direct. A community being told to hold a claim under pressure, to refuse a parent's wrong instruction, and to leave a city that will not allow worship is not being handed a ritual for its spare hours. It is being handed the discipline that keeps those other duties standing, and the verse places recitation and prayer at the head of the instructions for exactly that reason.

**The Closing Knowledge Clause**

The verse ends by stating that God fully knows what people do, and the clause reprises a theme the sūrah has used before when it said that God knows best what is in the breasts of all beings. The placement here rules out the reading of prayer as a performance mounted for an audience, which was exactly the failure described in the chapter's earlier portrait of the wavering believer. Since the One addressed knows both the deed and the intention, the quality of a prayer cannot be improved by display and cannot be concealed by formality. The command, the promise of deterrence, the greater remembrance, and the knowledge that closes the sentence together define worship as something that happens between a person and the One who already knows him.
