## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:16

> But they turned away. So We sent against them a devastating flood, and replaced their orchards with two others producing bitter fruit, fruitless bushes, and a few ˹sparse˺ thorny trees.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words for a Civilisation's Decision**

*Fa-aʿraḍū* — but they turned away. The whole failure of Sheba is recorded in a single verb, and it is not a verb of attack. *Aʿraḍa* means to turn one's side to something, to present a shoulder rather than a face; it describes averting attention, not opposing. They did not argue against the instruction they had been given, did not denounce it, did not drive out anyone who repeated it. They simply stopped attending. This is the most common form of religious failure in the Qur'an's account and the hardest to notice from the inside, because nothing dramatic marks its onset. A people busy with harvests and trade routes drifts away from the two-word command attached to their prosperity, and no day arrives on which the drift is announced. The brevity of the clause matches the nature of what it describes. There was no confrontation to narrate.

**The Flood of the ʿArim**

*Sayl al-ʿarim* is the phrase, and the second word has been read three ways. It can be a proper name for the valley or the structure; it can be an adjective meaning violent or overwhelming, so that the sense is simply a devastating torrent; or it can mean the dam itself, giving "the flood of the dam" — the deluge released when a barrier fails. The third reading has the strongest external support. The root *ʿ-r-m* appears in South Arabian inscriptions in exactly this technical sense, used of dam works in the region. Inscriptions from the mid-sixth century record major repair campaigns at the Maʾrib dam, one of them commissioned under the ruler Abraha, and they use the word for the barrier. So the Qur'an's phrase is not a poetic name for a disaster. It is the local technical vocabulary for what happened when a specific piece of infrastructure gave way, and the reader of this translation can hear the specificity in the rendering *a devastating flood*.

**Water as Blessing and Water as Ruin**

The mechanism of the punishment is worth stating plainly because of its irony. The same water that made the two orchards possible destroyed them. For centuries the dam caught the seasonal flood and spread it through sluices across the plains on either bank; when the structure failed, the identical flood arrived unregulated and took the fields, the channels and the soil with it. Nothing new was introduced into the valley. A blessing was released from its restraint. This is a recurring shape in the Qur'an's descriptions of collective punishment — the thing a people relies on is the thing that turns — and it fits the sūrah's larger argument about provision. Al-Naḥl describes a town secure and provisioned from every side whose people met God's favours with ingratitude, *"so Allah made them taste the clutches of hunger and fear"* (16:112). The sequence is not that a gift is confiscated and a punishment substituted. The gift stops being held in place.

**Two Gardens Replaced by Two Gardens**

The verse's sharpest stroke is that the exchange keeps the form and empties it. They had two orchards; they are given two orchards. The Arabic uses the same word, *jannatayn*, for both the before and the after, so the sentence reports a swap rather than a removal, and the bitterness lies in the matching count. What grows in the replacement is then itemised in a descending list. *Ukul khamṭ* is bitter or brackish fruit — produce that exists and cannot be eaten with pleasure. *Athl* is the tamarisk, a hardy desert tree that yields nothing edible at all, rendered here as fruitless bushes. And *shayʾun min sidrin qalīl* is a few lote trees, the thorny jujube that survives on almost no water and bears a small fruit. The list is an accurate botanical description of what colonises an abandoned irrigation zone once the water regime collapses: salt-tolerant scrub, then thorn. The land did not become a desert in the sense of bare sand. It became a place where things grow that no one planted and nobody wants.

**The Contrast Between Left Standing and Taken Away**

Reading this verse against the one before it shows how carefully the pair is built. There the produce was called *rizq*, provision, and the instruction was *eat*; here the produce is bitter and nothing is said about eating. There the land was *ṭayyibah*, wholesome; here it grows tamarisk. There a forgiving Lord was named in the same breath as the good soil; here the sentence contains no divine name at all, only the verb *baddalnāhum*, We exchanged for them. The removal of the name from the closing position is itself part of the description of what was lost. What the two verses together establish is that the punishment was proportionate in a specific way: not a plague, not an invading army, not fire from the sky, but the withdrawal of the exact favour that had been named and the exact favour that had been unacknowledged. A people that stopped noticing where the water came from were shown where it came from.

**A Real City, and Why That Matters Here**

Archaeology and inscriptions place the Sabaean state at Maʾrib from roughly the early first millennium BC, sustained by dam-fed agriculture and by control of the incense route north. The dam suffered breaches in the fifth and sixth centuries AD and was repaired at least twice at great expense before the final failure, after which the oasis contracted and the population dispersed. The Qur'an's audience in Mecca sat on the northern end of that same caravan road and knew the ruins by reputation; the Yemen leg of their winter journey ran through territory whose greatness was visibly over. This is why the story functions as an argument rather than a legend. The sūrah is speaking to traders who could see, at the far end of their own trade route, what became of the last people in Arabia who had everything and stopped saying thank you.
