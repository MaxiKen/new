## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:26

> Moses added, “˹He is˺ your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses Brings Lordship From the Cosmos Into the Family**

After speaking of the heavens and earth, Moses makes the claim personal. The Lord is not only the ruler of distant realms; He is your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers. The words address Pharaoh and his court directly. They cannot place God far away and treat the message as a theory about a remote universe. The One who sustains creation also sustained the people standing in that court and the generations from which they came.

The appeal to forefathers also answers a common defense of power. A ruler may inherit a throne and imagine that inherited status gives his claim a sacred origin. Moses reverses the argument. Pharaoh's ancestors were themselves creatures under God's lordship. Their antiquity does not make them divine, and their relation to the royal line does not elevate Pharaoh above the Lord who gave them life.

**Ancestry Cannot Manufacture Divinity**

The court's respect for lineage may have made the reference to forefathers especially sharp. Pharaoh's rule depends partly on continuity: the present ruler represents a chain of rulers and traditions. Moses does not need to deny the historical chain. He places it in its proper order. Ancestors can transmit customs, property, or political titles, but they cannot transmit ultimate lordship because they never possessed it.

This argument also protects families from becoming idols. Honoring parents and ancestors can be a duty, but inherited practice cannot override the Creator's command. If forefathers were themselves under God's care, then following them is right only when their path agrees with truth. Moses' wording does not attack memory or kinship. It attacks the use of kinship as a shield against judgment.

**The Pronoun Your Removes the Court's Distance**

The word your carries a directness that the universal description alone might not. Moses is not speaking about a lord relevant only to the Israelites. The Lord addresses Pharaoh's life, Pharaoh's ancestors, and Pharaoh's own position. The ruler is included in the created order he pretends to command. He cannot dismiss the message as a foreign theology brought by an outsider.

This is also why the answer can be confronting without being sectarian. Moses does not say that the Lord is merely the patron of one tribe. He says the same Lord is related to the ruler and his forefathers as Creator and Sustainer. The claim creates a shared ground that Pharaoh may reject but cannot honestly call irrelevant. The Lord's authority does not depend on Moses' ethnicity or on Pharaoh's permission to enter the conversation.

**Forefathers Reveal the Mortality of the Throne**

The earlier generations are no longer ruling. Their passing exposes the temporary nature of royal power. A dynasty may speak as though it will last forever, yet every ancestor named in its line has already become part of the past. Moses invokes them not to shame the dead but to remind the living ruler that succession is evidence of mortality, not proof of permanence.

The same reasoning appears in many Qur'anic critiques of inherited pride. A person who boasts of ancestry forgets that the ancestors themselves depended on God and eventually returned to Him. Pharaoh's court can celebrate the past, but it cannot turn the past into a guarantee for the future. Lordship belongs to the One who remains while generations pass.

**The Answer Rejects a Private Definition of God**

Pharaoh's question about the Lord of all worlds could have been answered in a private or philosophical register. Moses continues to describe the Lord through relations visible in life: the Lord of people, parents, and generations. The answer teaches that knowledge of God is not detached from how human beings understand their own origin and dependence.

A ruler may dislike the idea that his own ancestors were dependent creatures, because that idea removes the sacred aura from his lineage. Yet the claim is not an insult to the ancestors. It gives them their true honor as creatures of God rather than turning them into links in a chain of self-worship. Moses' speech makes the royal past answerable to the same Lord who hears the present.

**Personal Address Makes Evasion More Difficult**

When a claim is universal, a person may hide behind abstraction. When it is addressed as your Lord, evasion becomes harder. Pharaoh must decide whether he accepts that his life is received, his authority limited, and his ancestors judged under the Lord named by Moses. The question is no longer only what the title means. It is what the title means for the man who has placed himself above other people.

That is the force of this short verse. It neither adds a new deity nor changes the subject. It presses the universal claim into the ruler's family history. Pharaoh's ancestors did not make him a god. They were evidence that he, like every ruler before him, is a dependent servant before the Lord.

**The Family Reference Also Limits Moses' Own Pride**

Moses does not say that his family possesses a special god unavailable to Pharaoh. He names the Lord as the Lord of Pharaoh's forefathers as well as Pharaoh himself. That universality keeps the messenger from turning revelation into a tribal possession. The Israelites' history matters, but the Lord who sends Moses is not contained by Israel's history or by Egypt's court.

The wording also prevents a messenger from speaking as though he stands outside the judgment he announces. Moses is a descendant of human beings who depended on God. He speaks with authority because he has been sent, not because his ancestry makes him divine. The court can reject his message, but it cannot honestly accuse him of claiming a private privilege for his own family.

A ruler who hears this should recognize that the messenger is not offering a rival dynasty. He is placing every dynasty under one Lord. That is why Pharaoh's inherited status cannot answer Moses. The lineage Pharaoh trusts becomes evidence of dependence, and the family history he uses to support authority becomes a reminder that authority ends with the generations God creates.
