## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:38

> So the magicians were assembled at the set time on the appointed day.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Court Fixes Truth Into a Public Appointment**

The magicians are gathered at a set time on an appointed day. Pharaoh's response to Moses has become a scheduled event, with a place in the kingdom's calendar and a public expectation attached to it. The wording gives the confrontation order and visibility. It will not be a hidden exchange where the court can later alter the story. The chosen time makes the encounter a test before witnesses.

The Qur'an elsewhere identifies the day as a festival day when the people would gather after the sun had risen high. That detail in 20:59 explains why the appointment could draw a large audience. The court selects a time that is socially available and symbolically useful. A religious or civic gathering becomes the stage on which Pharaoh hopes to defend his authority.

**An Appointment Can Serve Justice or Propaganda**

Setting a time is not wrong in itself. A public claim may need a public hearing, and an agreed appointment can protect both sides from secret manipulation. The problem is the purpose attached to this appointment. Pharaoh's court has not arranged a balanced inquiry. It has organized a contest designed to make the ruler's explanation appear victorious.

The same structure can serve opposite ends. A fair hearing lets evidence be seen and arguments be answered. A propaganda event uses the appearance of openness to direct the audience toward a chosen conclusion. The verse does not need to state which purpose Pharaoh has in mind; the surrounding accusations and threats make it clear. The appointment will give the court a stage, but it will also give God a public witness.

**A Set Time Removes the Excuse of Confusion**

The gathering at an appointed time allows the people to prepare and attend. No one need claim that the signs were shown in a hidden corner or that the magicians were brought without opportunity to see the event. Public timing increases the evidence available to the audience. It also increases responsibility. People who attend cannot later say that the confrontation was merely rumor.

Pharaoh may intend the crowd to validate his interpretation, but God can use the same crowd to establish a record. The more open the scene, the more visible the difference between what happens and what the ruler says it means. A scheduled spectacle can therefore turn against the power that organized it. Witnesses may remember the event more clearly than the official account permits.

**The Day Carries the Weight of Decision**

An appointed day is more than a date. It gives the confrontation a moment toward which the court, the magicians, and the people move. The waiting period can increase suspense, but it also concentrates moral choice. Pharaoh has time to reconsider, the magicians have time to prepare, and the public has time to decide whether to attend.

This forward movement distinguishes the scene from a casual display. A decision is being staged about who has the right to define power. The court thinks the day will confirm Pharaoh's rule. Moses' message places the day under God's judgment instead. The appointment is fixed by officials, but its meaning will be revealed through what the witnesses see and how they respond.

**Gathering Specialists Does Not Create a Second Source of Power**

The assembled magicians may represent the highest human skill available to Pharaoh, but their gathering does not make them equal to the Lord who sent Moses. Their number and reputation belong to the created order. The day will compare human performance with a divine sign, not two independent gods competing for territory.

This distinction prevents the event from becoming a simple contest of competing magic systems. Pharaoh's court wants the public to ask which practitioner is stronger. Revelation asks a deeper question: which act is a sign from God, and which acts are human attempts to imitate or deceive? The appointment brings both before the people so that the difference can be judged.

**The Festival Setting Makes Power Sociable**

A festival can turn political authority into shared celebration. People gather, watch, repeat stories, and experience themselves as one crowd. Pharaoh's event seeks to use that social energy. If the magicians prevail before a large audience, the ruler can present the result as the people's verdict as well as his own.

Yet crowds can also witness what rulers would rather keep private. Collective attention does not guarantee collective honesty. It gives each person a larger responsibility because the event is being experienced together. The appointed day will test whether the people follow spectacle, follow the court, or allow the sign to challenge the order that brought them there.

**The Time Is Fixed, but the Outcome Is Not**

The verse carefully says when the magicians gathered, not that they had already won. Appointment is not victory. A ruler may control the date, the mobilization, and the publicity while remaining unable to control the truth that appears. The court's planning can prepare a stage, but it cannot write the final meaning of what the stage reveals.

This gives the scene its tension. Pharaoh's system has done everything it knows to do: assemble experts, choose a public day, and gather people. The next question is whether organization can overcome divine evidence. The appointed day has arrived, but the court has not yet secured the allegiance it hopes to display.

The fixed appointment also protects the memory of the event from being dissolved into rumor. A specific day and a gathered audience make the confrontation part of communal history. Pharaoh intends that history to celebrate his victory; God can make it record the moment when the court's interpretation was exposed as fear.
