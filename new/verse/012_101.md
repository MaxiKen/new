## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:101

> “My Lord! You have surely granted me authority and taught me the interpretation of dreams. ˹O˺ Originator of the heavens and the earth! You are my Guardian in this world and the Hereafter. Allow me to die as one who submits and join me with the righteous.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter's Only Extended Prayer From Joseph**

Joseph prays several times in this sūrah, and the chapter quotes him briefly each time: a plea for the women's scheme to be turned away (12:33), a preference for prison over what he is being called to (12:33), a statement about the soul that commands evil (12:53). Verse 101 is different in kind. It is a sustained address with a structure — gifts acknowledged, God named, a request for the manner of his death, a request for his company afterwards. The chapter places it at the end of the narrative, after the prostration, after the interpretation of the dream, after the explanation of the brothers' conduct. Everything Joseph has been given is now in his hands, and the first thing he does with it is pray.

**"You Have Granted Me Authority"**

The word denotes sovereignty, dominion, rule — and the prayer qualifies it. Joseph has been given something of it, not all of it. The commentators explain the qualification by reference to his actual position: the highest standing in Egypt next to the king (12:54, 12:56). He controls the granaries, settles wherever he pleases, and holds a personal attachment to the ruler, and he describes all of it as a portion. The verse is the chapter's own assessment of worldly power, spoken by the person holding most of it. Nothing in the prayer claims the authority as earned, deserved or permanent; it is stated as a gift received, in the same clause as the second gift. The grammar of the clause carries that meaning. Authority is described as something granted, in the same sentence and the same construction as the second gift, so both are received on identical terms. Joseph does not present his office as the outcome of his competence, though the chapter has documented his competence at length; he presents it as one of two things given to him.

**"And Taught Me the Interpretation of Dreams"**

The second gift is named with the same verb the chapter has used from the beginning. Joseph's father told him that God would teach him the interpretation of dreams (12:6); the narrator reported that God established him in Egypt in order to teach him that interpretation (12:21); two prisoners asked him for it (12:36); a court declared itself unable to supply it (12:44). Verse 101 is the only place where Joseph himself states that he was taught. The prayer therefore confirms the chapter's central claim about his knowledge from inside his own mouth, at the end of the story, after every prediction he made has been verified by events. The verification is what makes the claim modest rather than proud. Every interpretation Joseph offered has been fulfilled: two prison dreams, a king's vision of fifteen years, a blind father's sight, and a reunion he predicted to his brother in confidence. He names the skill at the end of the record, after the reader has watched it work, and attributes it to the One who taught it.

**"Originator of the Heavens and the Earth"**

The address shifts from gifts to the Giver, and the name chosen is one of origination — the bringing into being of something that did not exist. The Qur'an opens another sūrah with the same title: *“All praise is for Allah, the Originator of the heavens and the earth”* (35:1). The choice is significant in context. Joseph has just watched a family bow to him in fulfilment of a vision given to him as a child, and he has just attributed the hostility that produced the delay to Satan. The name he addresses is the one that describes the beginning of everything rather than the governance of a part of it, and it puts the thirty years of the story into its proper scale. The scale qualifies everything else in the prayer. A man holding the granaries of Egypt addresses the One who originated the heavens and the earth, and the address reorders his achievement before he mentions it. The chapter sets the cosmic title first and the two gifts second, so that authority and knowledge are received inside a frame far larger than either.

**"My Guardian in This World and the Hereafter"**

The word denotes a protector, an ally, one who is close to a person and responsible for him. The Qur'an states the general form of the claim elsewhere: *Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light* (2:257), and it describes those under that protection as people for whom there will be no fear and no grief (10:62). Joseph applies the term to himself across both abodes, which is a larger claim than protection in this world alone. He has been guarded in a well, in a market, in a household, in a prison and in a palace, and the prayer states that the same guardianship extends past death. The extension makes the prayer more than thanksgiving. Joseph has been guarded through a well, a slave market, a locked room and a prison, and the verse asks that the same relationship continue where no event can confirm it. He describes a guardianship tested across thirty years and requests it beyond the period in which it is capable of being tested.

**"Allow Me to Die as One Who Submits"**

The request concerns the manner of his death rather than its timing. The Qur'an makes the same request a general instruction to believers: *“Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves, and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺”* (3:102). Joseph's formulation is personal and it is placed at the moment of his greatest success, which gives it a particular force. A man who has just been bowed to by his family, who holds authority over a country and who has seen a childhood vision fulfilled asks not for more of any of it but that he should die in a state of submission. The chapter's ranking of the two worlds, stated in verse 57, is here enacted.

**"And Join Me With the Righteous"**

The final clause asks for company. Joseph does not ask for a rank, a reward, a station in Paradise or a reckoning made easy. He asks to be joined with the righteous, and the chapter has already told the reader who some of them are: his father, his forefathers Abraham and Isaac (12:6), and the categories described in verse 57 as those who believe and are mindful. The request is the prayer of a man who has been separated from his family for most of his life — sold by brothers, imprisoned among strangers, elevated among officials — and whose last stated wish is not to be alone. The verse ends where the story began, with the family, and moves past it. Moving past the family is the prayer's last movement. Everything Joseph has sought in this chapter has concerned his household — a brother, a father, a reunion, a settlement in Egypt — and the final request concerns a company that includes them but is not defined by them. The verse ends with the family present and the speaker looking beyond it in a single clause.

**The Prayer Placed at the Summit of the Story**

The chapter could have ended with verse 100: a dream interpreted, a family reunited, a famine survived, an explanation given. Verse 101 continues past the reunion into a prayer, and the placement is the sūrah's final judgement on everything that preceded it. Authority, knowledge, reconciliation, honour and a fulfilled vision are all in hand, and the response to all of them is a request to die submitting and to be joined with the righteous. Verse 57 stated that the reward of the Hereafter is better for those who believe and are mindful. Verse 101 shows a man who has received the reward of this world in full asking for the other one, which is the chapter's way of confirming its own ranking without arguing for it.
