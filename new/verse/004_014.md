## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:14

> But whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and exceeds their limits will be cast into Hell, to stay there forever. And they will suffer a humiliating punishment.

### Expanded Commentary

**The opposite boundary**

The warning follows immediately so that no reader can reduce the preceding rules to recommendations. To disobey Allah and His Messenger and to cross the limits is presented as a grave spiritual act. Inheritance is one of the clearest places where greed attempts to borrow the language of family affection: “we have always done it this way,” “she does not need it,” or “the estate should stay with the men.” The verse identifies such rationalizations as transgression when they contradict the revealed shares.

**Humiliation is part of the judgment**

The punishment is described as humiliating, not only painful. The wrongdoer sought honour through control of property, status within the clan, or public appearance. The final judgment exposes that apparent honour as disgrace. The orphan, widow, or daughter whom he treated as insignificant is not forgotten, and the inheritance he seized cannot purchase dignity before God.

The language of permanence is read by theologians in light of the Qur’an’s broader treatment of disbelief and grave sin. It certainly establishes a terrifying warning and the seriousness of knowingly violating divine limits. It should not be used to close the door of repentance that God has opened elsewhere, but repentance must include restoration of rights. A person who returns to Allah while still retaining another heir’s property has not completed the repair.

**Law guarded by conscience**

The verse also protects against the opposite error: treating legal compliance as a licence for cruelty. Giving a lawful portion while humiliating a grieving relative, concealing assets, or forcing a renunciation still contradicts the chapter’s justice. The limits include the manner in which rights are delivered. The family that fears this warning will use transparent accounts, impartial advice, and respectful speech, because it understands that the estate is being divided under the sight of the One who revealed it.

**The danger of calling injustice tradition**

Many violations of inheritance begin as apparently respectable customs. “This is how our family has always done it” can conceal a history of excluding daughters or pressuring widows. The warning against transgressing God’s limits gives believers a criterion stronger than ancestral prestige. A custom deserves preservation only when it can stand before justice.
