## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:12

> On that Day all will end up before your Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Mustaqarr: Where a Thing Finally Settles**

The word rendered as destination is *al-mustaqarr*, from a root meaning to settle, to become still, to come to rest. It is the noun of arrival after movement, and it carries the sense of a thing that has stopped travelling because it has reached the place it was travelling toward. The word appears in the Qur'an for the resting-place of the embryo in the womb and for the term appointed to the world itself, and in every case the idea is the same: movement with an end built into it. Applied to a human being on that Day, it means the wandering is over. Not the body only, and not the soul only, but the whole person, at rest because there is nowhere further to go.

**The Destination Is a Person**

What makes the sentence severe is its preposition. The settling is not *in* a place but *to* the Lord: *ilā rabbika*. The Qur'an could have said that everyone comes to rest in the grave, or in the Fire, or in the Garden, and each of those would have been true. It says instead that the terminus is God, which relocates the whole matter from geography to audience. A person's end is not a location he reaches but a Person he meets, and every description of that Day in the Qur'an is arranged around this: the record is opened, the witnesses arrive, and the judgment is pronounced in the presence of the One who was present all along. The horror and the comfort of the verse are the same fact seen from two sides. The same idea is put the other way round in the last verse of the sūrah of the Morning Brightness, where the terminus is named in the same preposition: *“and that to your Lord ˹alone˺ is the ultimate return ˹of all things˺.”* (53:42). A person's life is movement with an end built into it, and the end is not a place on a map but an audience. That is why the Qur'an's descriptions of the Hereafter are saturated with the language of meeting, presence, and being seen. The Garden is not described as a location so much as a nearness, and the Fire is not described as a place so much as a separation from the One before whom everyone has arrived.

**To Your Lord: Why the Prophet Is Addressed**

The possessive is singular and it is addressed to the Messenger. That choice does two things at once. It universalises the statement, since the Lord of the Prophet is the Lord of everyone, and it personalises it, since the one hearing it first is the man who has been told to warn his people. Every human being will come to rest before the Lord whom Muhammad ﷺ proclaimed, which is the sūrah's answer to the mockery in the opening movement. The people who asked *when* are now told *before whom*. The question of the date, which the Qur'an consistently declines to answer, is replaced by the question of the identity of the Judge, which it answers constantly.

**Sovereignty Without Intermediaries**

The scene the Qur'an builds for that Day strips away every other claim to authority. *“the Day all will appear ˹before Allah˺. Nothing about them will be hidden from Him. ˹He will ask,˺ ‘Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!’”* (40:16). *“All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them.”* (22:56). Everything that mediated power in this life — tribe, wealth, office, the protection of a patron, the opinion of a crowd — is absent from these descriptions, and the absence is the argument. A creature who has arranged his standing through intermediaries is brought into a court where no intermediary is recognised, and the verse's plainness is deliberate: no advocate is mentioned, no negotiation is described, and no one speaks except by permission.

**One Verb, Two Outcomes**

The noun is neutral, and that neutrality is theologically important. *Al-mustaqarr* names the arrival, not the accommodation, and the arrival is common to everyone; what differs is where a person is sent from the audience. The sūrah itself will divide the arrivals two verses later into faces that are bright and faces that are gloomy, and the Qur'an's descriptions of the two destinations are equally concrete. Both are called a settlement, both are permanent, and both are described as the fitting outcome of a life. The verse thus refuses to let its hearer decide in advance which side of the division he occupies. The neutrality also guards against a lazy assumption. A person may tell himself that arriving before the Lord is automatically good news, since God is merciful; the Qur'an refuses to let mercy be used as a solvent for the reckoning. The same Lord whose mercy is invoked is the Lord who judges, and the two are not in tension in these texts. Mercy is what makes the warning possible in the first place, since a God who did not warn would not be merciful at all.

**The Close of the Sūrah's First Movement**

Verses 1 through 12 form a single arc, and this line is its closing statement. The sūrah opened with an oath by the Day of Resurrection and by the soul that accuses itself; it named the denial, answered it from the body's own extremities, described the collapse of the sky, and recorded the cry of a person looking for somewhere to go. Each of those steps moved toward this one sentence, which supplies what the whole passage was missing: a destination. What follows will not be more scenery. Having established that everyone arrives before the Lord, the sūrah turns to what happens at the arrival, beginning with the account a person is given of his own life.
