## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:21

> Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His signs? Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Ranks Wrongdoing**

The sentence is put as a question with no answer available, and the form appears across the Book whenever a particular offence is being placed at the top of the list. Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies against Allah (11:18; 18:15; 61:7)? Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His revelations (7:37; 10:17)? The same interrogative is used of those who bar God's name from being mentioned in His places of worship (2:114) and of those who hide testimony they received from God (2:140).

Ranking wrongs is not the Book's usual habit, and the device is a reminder that the two crimes named here are not moral slips. They are attacks on the source of the whole moral order, and they are committed by people who know the stakes, since the audience hearing this verse had already heard the message of the First Sūrah and the one who was named there as the "Lord of all worlds" (1:2).

**Fabricating a Lie Means Claiming Divine Guidance**

The Arabic phrase is specific. To fabricate against God is to invent a religious practice or belief and present it as God's command, which is why the Book attaches the charge to the ritual customs of the Makkans. When they commit a shameful deed they say, "We found our forefathers doing it and Allah has commanded us to do it," and the reply cuts them off: God never commands what is shameful (7:28). The same charge is laid against the invented cattle categories, the baḥīrah, sā'ibah, waṣīlah, and ḥām, which the sūrah itself treats at length: God never ordained them, but the disbelievers fabricate lies about Him (5:103).

An invention is not a harmless opinion, because a person who adds a rule to the religion has appointed himself a legislator beside God. He is not merely mistaken about the law; he is claiming the authority to make one.

**The Other Crime: Denial of the Signs**

The second half of the verse names the opposite operation. Denial does not add to God; it removes what He has already shown, refusing the evidence that the sky, the harvest, and the record of ruined nations were said to supply. Both are named together in 7:37 and 10:17, and the pairing is deliberate, because the two offences describe the same act of rearranging authority: one party edits the message, the other deletes it.

Between them, the two clauses close the exits. A person cannot escape into tradition, since tradition is where the inventions were lodged, and he cannot escape into scepticism, since scepticism has already been named as a crime rather than a method. The verse leaves the reader standing between the two doors with nothing on either side.

Denial also has a working form in the audience's own city. Signs were not met with silence; they were reassigned. The recitation was called magic, the messenger a poet or a soothsayer, and the warnings of the earlier scriptures were treated as material that had been borrowed and polished. Each reassignment let the sign be counted as something else, so that the person doing the counting never had to answer the question the sign raised. That is why the Book treats denial as an activity rather than an opinion, and why the crime is put on the same level as inventing a religion.

**What "Success" Means in the Book's Vocabulary**

The verb at the end of the verse is the one the Book uses for salvation. Believers are described as successful because they believe in the unseen, keep the prayer, and spend from what they are given (2:5); the people of the Gardens are described with the same word. When the verse says the wrongdoers will never succeed, it is not promising a business failure or a lost battle. It is denying them the only success the Book recognises.

That denial is total in both directions. Commentators explain that the wrongdoers will not find what they seek in this world or the next, which is why the same verb is used where the wrongdoers are seen to have failed after their apparent prosperity was stripped away (28:82). The Makkans would have read the word differently, since their own success was measured in caravans and shrines.

**The Audience That Recognised Its Own Customs**

Nothing in the verse is abstract for the people first addressed by it. They practised the invented restrictions, they petitioned the stones, they repeated the ancestral justifications, and they dismissed the recitation as old stories. The verse asks them which wrong could be greater than what they were doing while they were doing it.

This is the sūrah's method throughout, and it explains why the charge is stated in the present tense of a question rather than the past tense of a report. The verse has no interest in convicting a dead generation. It puts a live audience in front of a live accusation and waits.

**Why the Accusation Is Followed by the Gathering**

The verse refuses to leave the crime without a hearing. The sentence that follows opens with the Day when all of them will be gathered and asked where the gods they claimed have gone. The order is the sūrah's own logic: an offence against God's authority cannot be judged inside a life, because a life is too short to contain its consequences.

What the verse establishes is jurisdiction. If the crime is to take God's authority, the trial must belong to God, and the Book therefore moves from the question about wrongdoing to the scene of the gathering, where the answer will be given by the accused themselves.
