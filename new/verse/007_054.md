## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:54

> Indeed your Lord is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne. He makes the day and night overlap in rapid succession. He created the sun, the moon, and the stars—all subjected by His command. The creation and the command belong to Him ˹alone˺. Blessed is Allah—Lord of all worlds!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Six Days and the Throne**

The verse names God as the One who created the heavens and the earth in six days and then established Himself on the Throne, and the pairing appears in several passages of the Book. The sūrah of Iron states that God created the heavens and the earth in six days and then established Himself on the Throne (57:4), the sūrah of the Prostration states that He established Himself on the Throne after creating in six days and conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth (32:4–5), and the sūrah of Jonah and the sūrah of Hūd repeat the arrangement. The verse in front of us states the creation and the rule in a single sentence.

The image of the establishment on the Throne is understood by the tradition as the statement of divine sovereignty rather than of place, since the Book presents God as beyond the limits of bodies. The Cow's account describes the Throne as encompassing the heavens and the earth and states that neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes its Owner (2:255). The verse in front of us supplies the sovereignty that the following verses apply to the ordering of the world.

**Night and Day in Rapid Succession**

The verse states that the day and the night overlap in rapid succession, and the Book uses the same image of one wrapping around the other. The account of the Troops states that God wraps the night around the day and the day around the night (39:5), and the sūrah of the Family of Imran and the sūrah of the Night Journey cite the alternation of the two as a sign for people of understanding. The verse in front of us places the alternation among the works of the Creator whose command governs the world.

The succession is presented as a search that never pauses, and the commentary tradition notes that the image describes the pursuit of one by the other without interval. The Book's use of the cycle of night and day as evidence for the resurrection appears in several passages, and the sūrah's following verses draw the same argument from the rain and the dead land. This verse states the mechanism that the later verses use as a sign.

**The Sun, the Moon, and the Stars Subjected**

The verse names the sun, the moon, and the stars as created beings subjected by God's command, and the Book mentions the subjection of the heavenly bodies in several places. The Troops' account states that God has subjected the sun and the moon, each running for an appointed term (39:5), and the sūrah of the Cow states that the sun and the moon are made to follow a reckoning. The word used in front of us for the stars describes them as created and directed, and the verse places them under the same command as the rest.

The subjection in front of us is the answer to the audience's veneration of the heavenly bodies. The Cattle's account records Abraham's argument from the rising and setting of the star, the moon, and the sun to the conclusion that he would not worship things that pass away (6:76–78), and the sūrah's audience had its own attachment to the worship of the sky. The verse here names the created lights and states that they are directed rather than divine.

**"His Are the Creation and the Command"**

The verse states that creation and command belong to God alone, and the sentence gathers the two halves of the argument that the passage has been making. The Book's account of God as the Creator of all things appears in the sūrah of the Cattle, where the declaration is followed by the command to worship Him (6:102), and the sūrah of Iron's account of the six days closes with the statement that He knows all things (57:4). The present verse states the claim in the form of a pronouncement.

The two terms cover the making of the world and its governance, and the verse's audience is told that both belong to the One it is being called to worship. The sūrah's opening command forbade the taking of protectors besides God, and the sentence in front of us supplies the reason: nothing else made the world and nothing else directs it. The claim is stated before the passage turns to supplication.

**Blessed Be God, Lord of All Worlds**

The verse closes with the declaration that God is blessed and the Lord of all worlds, and the phrase echoes the opening of the Book. The first sūrah praises God under the same title (1:2), and the sūrah of the Forgiver uses it in its account of the blessings of creation (40:64). The verse under discussion places the title at the end of the description of the Creator, and the declaration gathers the whole list of His acts under one name.

The title states the scope of the lordship, and the sūrah's argument depends on it. The audience is told that the Lord of the worlds is the One who created, ordered, and governs, and the command to call upon Him in the following verse addresses the same Lord. Here the verse supplies the name before it states the command.

**The Days and Their Measure**

The commentary tradition discusses the six days, and the discussion turns on the Book's own statements about the length of a divine day. The account of the Pilgrimage states that a day with the Lord is like a thousand years of what people count (22:47), and the sūrah of the Prostration states that the affairs ascend to Him on a day whose measure is a thousand years (32:5). Some commentators therefore treat the six days as a measure of stages rather than of twenty-four-hour periods, and others hold to the plain reading.

The tradition notes a difference between the Book's account and the account of the earlier scriptures, since the Book attributes no rest to God after the work of creation. The sūrah of the Cow states that neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him (2:255), and the point matters for the practices of the religion, since no day of rest is prescribed. This verse states the completion of the creation without the interval of repose.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands fifty-fourth in the chapter, and it opens the passage on the signs of the created order that runs to the description of the rain and the good land (7:57–58). It follows the account of the Book and its warning, and it prepares the command to call upon God that comes next. The verse here supplies the ground on which the supplication rests.

The reader who has been shown the two companies and the fulfilled warning is now directed to the world's construction. The heavens, the earth, the night, the day, and the lights are named as the work of One Lord, and the sūrah's next verses instruct the audience on how to address Him.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse commands the audience to call upon their Lord humbly and in secret and states that He does not love the transgressors (7:55). The description of the Creator in front of us is followed by the command to address Him, and the passage then adds the conditions under which the address is made.

The two verses together move from the works of God to the worship of God. The One who made the heavens and the earth and directs the night, the day, and the lights is the One to whom the next verse directs the supplication; and the verses that follow add the other requirements of supplication and the warning against spreading corruption in the land.
