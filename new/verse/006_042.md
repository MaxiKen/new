## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:42

> Indeed, We have sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ to other people who We put through suffering and adversity ˹for their denial˺, so perhaps they would be humbled.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A History of the Same Mission**

The verse places the Prophet's work inside a long sequence, and it does so with the messengers rather than with the prophets' biographies as the main subject. Communities before this one received messengers, and the pattern of sending has not changed; what changed in each case was the treatment of the warning. The Book states the same claim in other sūrahs, where messengers were sent to communities before and Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them (16:63).

The point of the sequence is not repetition for its own sake. It tells the Prophet that his situation is a case within a rule, and it tells the Makkans that their response is being compared to the responses of peoples whose endings they already knew.

**Suffering and Adversity as Instruments**

The verse names two forms of hardship, and commentators distinguish between them: the first involving loss of wealth and straitened circumstances, and the second illness and physical suffering. Others read the pair as covering the whole range of affliction that befalls a community, from famine and drought to defeat and disease. The sūrah of the Heights describes the same programme in almost identical words: whenever a prophet was sent to a society, its people were afflicted with suffering and adversity so that they might be humbled (7:94).

Both forms are described in the Book as tests rather than as punishments, and the same two experiences are named among the marks of the righteous, who are said to be patient in times of suffering, adversity, and battle (2:177). That dual use of the vocabulary matters. The same event can be a purification for one person and a summons for another, and the verse is concerned with how a people responds to being shown its own helplessness.

**Why Hardship Was Thought to Produce Humility**

A community that has grown comfortable tends to treat its prosperity as a verdict on its conduct. When the rain fails, the caravan is lost, and the children fall ill, the arrangement that supported that confidence has been interrupted, and the interruption is meant to be noticed. The Book says elsewhere that God tested the dispersed tribes with prosperity and adversity so that they might return to the right path (7:168), and that testing with good and evil is the standing condition of human life (21:35).

The affliction is therefore not evidence of God's anger in every case. It is a message delivered in a language that a comfortable person can still read, and the whole point of the verse is that the message was delivered to these earlier peoples in exactly that form.

**The Hope Attached to the Sending**

The verse ends with a purpose clause: so that they might be humbled. Commentators read this as the objective God intended for the communities, and the Book's many accounts show that the objective was not always achieved. Some responded and were saved with their messengers, and others hardened and were destroyed, which is the subject the next verse takes up.

Read carefully, the clause tells the Prophet something about his own work as well. The test of a community is not only the warning it receives in words; it is also the pressure applied to its comfort, and God applies both so that a people cannot say it was never given an opportunity to reconsider.

**What Hardship Does and Does Not Do**

The Book is careful not to claim that suffering automatically produces humility, because it does not. Some people are driven deeper into resentment, and others emerge from a crisis more arrogant than before, convinced that their recovery was deserved. The next verse names that outcome for the communities in question, and the sūrah of Jonah describes a people who responded in time and were spared for a while.

The verse therefore sets up a contrast it does not yet resolve. Hardship was sent with the hope that the people would be humbled, and the question that follows is why the hope was not realised. The answer the sūrah gives is about what the people did with the opportunity rather than about what was missing from it.

**The Consolation for the Messenger**

For the Prophet, the verse carries the same message as the passage about rejected messengers a few lines earlier. The difficulties of the mission are not unique events; they are the ordinary shape of a summons that a society does not want to hear. The Book consoles its messenger by showing him the pattern and telling him that the outcome of every previous case is known.

That pattern also removes a burden he might have carried. If earlier communities had suffered as his own people were suffering, then the suffering was not evidence that he had failed or that God had withdrawn from him. The verse adds the Makkans to a list that already included communities whose stories the Prophet had been reciting, and the next sentence asks why the list ends the way it does.

The universality of the pattern is stated elsewhere in the Book in a form that leaves no community outside it. Every community has had a warner (35:24), a messenger was sent to every community with the call to worship God and shun false gods (16:36), and once a community's messenger has come, judgment is passed on it in fairness and it is not wronged (10:47). The Makkans were therefore not being singled out for hardship. They were being treated at the point in the sequence where their own messenger had arrived.
