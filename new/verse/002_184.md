## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:184

> ˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days. But whoever of you is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺. But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them. And to fast is better for you, if only you knew.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Days Specified**

The verse states that the fast is for a fixed number of days, and it then provides for those who are ill or travelling and for those who can only fast with difficulty. The sentence follows the command of the previous verse and begins to set out the terms on which it is observed.

The Book's method of moving from a general obligation to its particulars appears throughout its legislation, and the community's scholars read the two verses as a single instruction. What the reader receives is the statement that the fast is limited in duration, and the sentence then names the conditions under which the obligation is met in another way.

**The Concessions for Illness and Travel**

The sentence permits a person who is ill or on a journey to fast an equal number of days after the month, and the community's jurists discussed what kind of illness and what kind of journey qualify. The reports and the later rulings treat the concession as an instance of the principle that the Book states when it says that God intends ease rather than hardship.

The wording gives the reader the alternative rather than the exemption, since the obligation remains and its time changes. What the sentence establishes is that the practice is not suspended for those who cannot observe it in the month, and the community's practice has preserved the making up of the missed days as an obligation alongside the concession.

**The Compensation for Those Who Cannot Fast**

The sentence then provides that those who can only fast with extreme difficulty may compensate by feeding a needy person for each day not fasted, and the commentators discussed how widely that provision applies. Some connected it with the permanently unable, such as the elderly and the chronically ill, and others with a stage in the practice before the obligation was fixed.

The reading the community's jurists preferred applies the compensation to those whose condition prevents the fast indefinitely. What the reader receives is a provision that preserves the obligation's purpose for a person who cannot perform the act, and the sentence shows that the practice is not a burden beyond what its observance requires.

**Voluntary Generosity**

The sentence states that a person who volunteers more does better, and the commentators explained the addition as giving beyond the required compensation. The Book's habit of commending the voluntary along with the obligatory appears in its treatment of alms and of prayer, and the sentence here applies the pattern to the feeding of the needy.

The clause gives the reader an opportunity to exceed the minimum in a matter where the minimum is already an alternative to the fast. What the sentence establishes is that the practice of the month includes provision for the poor even in the cases where the fast is not observed, and the community's scholars drew from it the recommendation of extra generosity in the month.

**"To Fast Is Better for You"**

The sentence closes with the statement that the fast is better for the community, if only they knew, and the clause confirms that the original obligation remains the preferred course for those who can observe it. The community's teachers read the ending as a response to any inclination to treat the compensation as an equivalent.

The Book's wording places the preference with the practice that was commanded at the outset, and the reader is given a hierarchy rather than a choice between equals. What the sentence establishes is that the concessions are provided for the people they fit, and that the fast remains the better course for the person who is able to keep it.

**The Interpretation of the Numbered Days**

The commentators differed on whether the days specified in the verse refer to the month of Ramadan or to the fasts that the community observed before the obligation of the month. The reports describe the community fasting three days each month and the day of ʿĀshūrāʾ in Madinah, and they show the Muslims finding the Jewish community fasting on a day they adopted.

The community's teachers gathered the reports and discussed whether the earlier fasts were obligatory, and al-Ṭabarī among others held that no firm report establishes that a fast before the month was made binding. What the reader receives from the passage is the account of an obligation whose final form is presented in the following verse, and the discussion of the earlier practice illustrates how the community's scholars approached the history of its own legislation.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the duration of the fast, the concessions for illness and travel, the compensation for those who cannot observe it, and the preference for the fast itself. The reader is given the terms on which the obligation is met, and the verse that follows names the month in which it is observed and the purpose that the whole arrangement serves.
