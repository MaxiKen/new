## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:184

> ˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days. But whoever of you is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺. But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them. And to fast is better for you, if only you knew.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Days, Numbered**

The verse opens by describing the fast as *numbered days* — ayyām maʿdūdāt — and the commentators divided over what those days first named. One reading, recorded by al-Ṭabarī, identifies them with the days of Ramadan itself, the verse being a compact statement of the month-fast with its exemptions. The other reading identifies an earlier, shorter obligation: before Ramadan was legislated, the community is reported to have kept three days of fasting each month along with the fast of ʿĀshūrāʾ, the tenth of Muḥarram — and this verse, on that account, prescribed those numbered days, with the Ramadan-verses that follow superseding them. The ʿĀshūrāʾ practice is well attested: the reports describe the Muslims fasting the tenth of Muḥarram, with some connecting it to the Jews' fast on that day, others to the day Noah's ark came to rest on the mountain. Al-Bukhārī preserves the decisive exchange (2004): the Prophet came to Medina and found the Jews fasting ʿĀshūrāʾ because it was the day God saved the Children of Israel from Pharaoh, for which Moses fasted in gratitude — and he answered, *we have more right to Moses than you*, fasting the day himself and commanding the fast (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2004). The numbering matters for another reason: a fast of counted days is a fast with an end, and the Qur'an's first word about the discipline is that its duration is finite, bounded, and known in advance — the ease-theme of the whole legislation stated in its opening noun.

**Was the Earlier Fast Obligatory?**

The three-days reading generated a secondary question the exegetes pursued honestly: if those numbered days preceded Ramadan, was their observance an obligation? Opinions differed. Some held the three days a genuine duty, later lifted by the month-fast. Others maintained no fast was ever obligatory before Ramadan. Al-Ṭabarī weighed the reports and gave the decisive methodological verdict: there is no reliable report that any specific fast before Ramadan was ever an obligation — the evidence simply does not establish it, and the question is left where the evidence leaves it. What the tradition unanimously affirms is the continuation of the practice as merit: the three days each month remained a beloved observance of the Prophet and the righteous after Ramadan was legislated, never abrogated as a voluntary act because it was never established as a compulsory one. The scholarly episode is a small exhibit in the Qur'an's own evidentiary ethic — the exegetes distinguishing what the reports compel from what they merely permit, and refusing to legislate certainty where transmission is silent.

**The Sick and the Traveler**

The exemption clause is the verse's operative law: whoever among you is sick, or on a journey, fasts a number of other days instead. The structure is substitution, not cancellation — the missed fast is not forgiven but *deferred*, kept as a debt to be discharged in healthier or settled days. The jurists read the clause as requiring the concession to be used where the harm is real: the sick person for whom fasting impedes recovery, the traveler whose journey meets the law's threshold, are not merely permitted to break the fast but in many readings expected to, since the verse grants them the exemption as a gift from the same hand that gave the obligation. The details the schools developed — what degree of illness, what distance of journey, whether the traveler may fast if he prefers — belong to the verse's afterlife, and the next verse will carry them forward; here the principle stands: the law of the fast is written for bodies, and bodies differ, so the law differs with them. A numbered fast with numbered makeup days is an obligation that has done arithmetic on human weakness and legislated for it.

**Those Who Can Bear It Only With Difficulty**

The third clause — *and upon those who can bear it only with difficulty is a redemption: the feeding of a poor person* — was read in two directions, and both readings are classical. The first, reported from the early community and recorded by al-Qurṭubī, treats the clause as an original option: in the fast's earliest legislation, whoever wished could feed a poor person for each day and not fast at all — a concession later withdrawn by the Ramadan-command that whoever is present in the month should fast it (2:185), which abrogated the choice and left the feeding only for those incapable. The second reading, which al-Qurṭubī preferred on the grammar, finds no abrogation: the root ṭ-w-q here denotes the ones who bear the fast with hardship rather than the ones who merely could but need not — the chronically ill whose condition will not lift, the elderly whose strength is spent — and for them the feeding of a poor person per day is not a purchased exemption but the substitute duty their incapacity creates. On this reading the makeup-days clause covers the temporarily unable, and this clause covers the permanently unable, and the two together leave no body without a law. The grammar that supports it is the reading's own evidence: the difficult-bearing are those for whom the fast remains possible but punishing, and the verse prices that punishment at a meal.

**Whoever Volunteers More**

The clause that follows — whoever volunteers good, it is better for him — was explained by al-Qurṭubī in two concrete directions. The volunteer may feed more than the minimum: a poor person for each day becomes two, and the extra is his own merit. Or he may keep feeding beyond the redemption, giving the poor even when the law's requirement is already discharged. The voluntariness is the verse's hinge between duty and virtue: the feeding is fixed at one poor person per day, but the good above the fixed amount is welcomed without limit, and the verdict on it is unambiguous — it is better. The same verse thereby answers a question the exemption system always raises: whether concessions cheapen devotion. They do not; they relocate it. The one who cannot fast feeds; the one who feeds beyond the requirement has taken the fast's inner logic — hunger transferred into generosity — further than the fasting itself would have. The law's floor is exact, and above the floor the verse opens the voluntary ascent that the whole sūrah's charity-legislation has been describing since the spending-verses of its opening.

**Fasting Is Better for You, If You Knew**

The verse closes by returning to the fast itself with a comparative verdict: to fast is better for you, if you only knew. Al-Qurṭubī's glosses gather the comparison's terms. Better than the redemption — for the one capable of both, the fast outvalues the feeding, because the discipline does to the soul what the substituted meal cannot. And better even within the exemptions: for the traveler and the sick person for whom fasting is not severely harmful, keeping the fast outranks taking the concession — a ruling the exegete attributes in its general form to the practice of the Companions, noting that the details differ and that knowledge of them rests with God. The clause *if you only knew* is the verse's tenderness toward the exempted: the concession is real, the permission is God's own, and still the fast is named the better path for those it does not harm, because its goods — the practiced taqwā, the tasted hunger, the secret kept between the servant and the Lord — are known fully only to those who have kept it. The verse ends where the prescription began: numbered days, numbered exemptions, numbered redemptions, and above all the arithmetic a comparative the reader is invited to verify by fasting.
