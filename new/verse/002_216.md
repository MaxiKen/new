## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:216

> Fighting has been made obligatory upon you ˹believers˺, though you dislike it. Perhaps you dislike something which is good for you and like something which is bad for you. Allah knows and you do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Obligation Stated**

The verse states that fighting has been prescribed for the community and that it is hateful to them, and the sentence then states the principle that a person may dislike what is good for him. The Book acknowledges the aversion in the same sentence that imposes the duty.

The acknowledgement is significant, since the Book does not present the command as something the community would find attractive. The community's teachers read the sentence as a statement about the relation between a person's preferences and his welfare, and the reader is prepared for the command by being told that his own feelings are not the measure of what benefits him.

**The Reports About the Obligation**

The commentators discussed whether the command applied to the companions individually or to the community as a whole, and several explained that the duty became a collective obligation once the religion was established. The reports describe the community's situation in Madinah, where survival required the defence of the community.

The discussion reflects the difference between the situation in which the community was founded and the situation that followed, and the community's scholars used the sentence when they set out the rules about who is obliged to fight. Readers are shown that the Book's command was understood in the light of the community's circumstances, and the reports about the early teachers record the debate.

**The Report About Ibn ʿUmar**

The commentaries preserve the answer of a companion who was asked why he did not take part in the fighting during a period of civil conflict. He replied that during the Prophet's ﷺ time, when the religion was small, a person would be tried in his faith and killed or tortured for it, but that the religion had since become strong and the trial had ended.

The report shows how one of the community's most careful scholars applied the command, and the community's teachers cited it when they discussed the conditions under which the obligation applies. The reader is given the account of a companion's reasoning rather than a simple rule, and the material shows that the community's understanding of the verse developed with its circumstances.

**The Application in the Community's Law**

The community's jurists concluded that the obligation is collective in ordinary circumstances and that it becomes the duty of each person when the community's lands are attacked. The distinction was drawn from the combination of the verse and the reports about the Prophet's ﷺ campaigns.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere includes the provisions for those who are excused, and the community's scholars arranged the material into a body of law. Readers are shown that the command was not left as a general statement, and the sentence in this place supplies the ground from which the rulings were drawn. The community's teachers also connected the verse with the passages that permit the conclusion of peace.

**"God Knows and You Do Not Know"**

The sentence closes with the statement that God knows and the community does not, and the clause supplies the reason for accepting an instruction whose benefit is not apparent. The Book uses the same pair of clauses at points where a command runs against the community's inclination.

The community's teachers read the ending as the answer to the aversion the sentence has acknowledged, and they noted that the matter is placed with the knowledge of God rather than with the community's estimate. The text supplies the assurance that the command's wisdom is not measured by what they can see, and the sentence leaves them with the statement about the two kinds of knowledge.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The sentence follows the account of the community's trials and the instruction about giving and precedes the question about fighting in the sacred months. The Book has described what the community will endure and has now commanded the defence of the community, so that the reader meets the obligation after being told what holding to the religion costs.

The passage's arrangement gives the reader the instruction and the discussion that followed it, and the community's teachers used the sequence when they taught the subject. What the reader receives from the sentence is the command, the acknowledgement of the difficulty, and the statement of where the knowledge of the matter belongs.

**The Command and the Reluctance**

The sentence reports the reluctance of the community alongside the command that was given to it, and the exegetes noted that the Book names the difficulty rather than concealing it. The permission to fight came after the emigration, and the obligation followed in Madinah when the community had a place of its own and an enemy at its borders.

The verses that follow describe the expeditions of the early years, and the reports record the anxiety of those who were called to leave their families and their trade for the field. The answer that the sentence gives is not a rebuke of the reluctance, since the wording treats it as a fact of human nature. The clause places the judgment with God, who knows what the community does not.

The teachers drew the lesson that the harm the community fears may be the door of what benefits it, and the benefit it loves may be the road to what harms it. The Book's own account of Badr shows the small company that went out expecting an easy prize and found the day that established the community.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the obligation, the community's aversion to it, the principle that a person's preferences are not the measure of his good, and the knowledge of God that governs the instruction. The reader is given the command together with the reason for accepting it, and the community's teachers used the verse when they explained that the Book addresses its commands to people whose feelings may not agree with what they are told to do.

The verse supplies the ground of the obligations that the community accepted for its defence, and the jurists read it with the passages that describe the conditions of the duty. The sentence's acknowledgment of the reluctance has been understood as a mercy, since it removes the notion that the believer must feel no fear before the command.
