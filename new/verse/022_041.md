## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:41

> ˹They are˺ those who, if established in the land by Us, would perform prayer, pay alms-tax, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil. And with Allah rests the outcome of all affairs.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Power Is Described as Something Granted**

*Alladhīna in makkannahum fī al-arḍi* — those who, if We establish them in the land. The verb is *makkannā*, from *makkana*: to give someone a firm place, to put him in a position where he can act.

The subject of that verb is God. These people do not seize power; they are established in it. That is the first thing the sūrah says about the community that has just been authorized to fight, and it is the frame for everything that follows. A community that wins is not a community that has taken something; it is a community that has been placed, and the placing is conditional on what it does there.

**The Sentence Is a Conditional, Not a Prediction**

*In makkannahum* — if We establish them. The particle is a condition, and a condition can go unmet.

The sūrah is being realistic here, and the realism is part of the instruction. Power is described as a possibility that God may grant, not as a promise already made; the community reading this verse in Makkah had no sovereignty and no certainty of obtaining any. What they are told is what would be expected of them if it came.

The conditional also removes any suggestion that the fighting permission of verses 39 and 40 was a grant of territory. It was leave to resist an expulsion. Whether those resisting would ever be established in the land is left open in the grammar, and the conditionality is what keeps the four duties from reading as a programme for conquest.

**Four Acts, in Ascending Difficulty**

The four things they do once established are: they establish the prayer, they give the alms, they command what is right, and they forbid what is wrong.

The order is not random. The first two are what a person does about God and about his wealth — acts that can be performed privately, at least in part. The second two are necessarily public. Commanding right and forbidding wrong means intervening in other people's lives, including the lives of people who do not want it, and it is the act that a community with power can perform and a community without it mostly cannot.

That is why the list is attached to *tamkīn* in the first place. The question the verse answers is what power is for, and the answer is not security or prosperity but the public maintenance of a moral order.

**The Fourth Item Is the Dangerous One**

Forbidding wrong is the item that gets communities into trouble, and the sūrah names it without qualification.

The reason is that the alternative is worse. A community that prays, pays and keeps to itself has abandoned the public character of the religion it was expelled for saying out loud — the very sentence for which these people lost their homes was a public claim about God. The verse is describing a consistency: the people who were driven out for saying something publicly are the people who, once established, will say it publicly again.

The tradition's discussions of *al-amr bi-l-maʿrūf* focus almost entirely on its limits and etiquette — who may do it, when, and by what means — which is the recognition that the fourth item is the one that requires judgement.

**Why the Private Acts Come First**

The list opens with the two acts that can be performed alone and closes with the two that cannot, and the ordering is a safeguard rather than a formality.

A community that commands right and forbids wrong without praying and giving has become a political movement with a moral vocabulary. Its interventions will be about power, because power is all it has. Putting the prayer first is the Qur'an's way of ensuring that the public acts are performed by people who have first been before God about them.

The same ordering appears wherever the Qur'an pairs worship with social obligation. The pattern is always: what you owe God, then what you owe other people, and never the second without the first. The sūrah has been insisting on this since verse 32, when it located the value of the rites in the heart, and it will insist on it again in verse 77, when it commands bowing, prostration, worship and good deeds in a single breath.

**Who Is Being Described**

The commentators proposed several referents. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī took the verse as describing the conditions laid on whoever God establishes in the land, meaning those given sovereignty. Others took it as a description of the Emigrants specifically, or of the Muslims generally.

The first reading has the most to recommend it, because the verse's grammar is a conditional: *if* We establish them. A conditional describes what follows from a condition rather than what a named group happens to do. Whoever holds power is being told what the power was given for.

Read that way, the verse is a standing criterion rather than a historical description, and it applies to the community in every generation in which it has been established — including the generations in which it was an empire.

**"With God Is the End of Affairs"**

*Wa-lillāhi ʿāqibatu al-umūr* — and to God belongs the outcome of all matters.

The closing clause does two things. It states that the results of the four acts are not the measure of them — a community can pray, pay, command and forbid, and still not be given the outcome it hoped for. And it states that the results are not lost either; they go somewhere and are gathered to someone.

Placed immediately after a verse about fighting and immediately before a passage about the nations that denied their messengers, the clause is also a warning against reading history backwards. Victory is not a verdict, and defeat is not one either. The sūrah's next verses will make the point at length: the nations that destroyed their messengers were given power first and were destroyed anyway.
