## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:197

> Was it not sufficient proof for the deniers that it has been recognized by the knowledgeable among the Children of Israel?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Knowledgeable Witnesses Are Evidence Against Denial**

The verse asks whether recognition by knowledgeable people among the Children of Israel should not have been enough proof for the deniers. It does not say that every member of that community agreed, nor does it make ancestry a substitute for truth. It points to people who possessed the relevant scriptural knowledge and could compare the Quran's claim with what they already knew.

A witness with knowledge can notice features that an outsider misses: the continuity of the prophetic call, the themes of revelation, and the signs connected with a coming messenger or scripture. Such recognition does not compel every listener, but it removes the excuse that no informed person could see a relation between the Quran and earlier guidance.

**Knowledge Is Valuable Because It Can Recognize Truth**

The verse honors knowledge that leads to recognition rather than knowledge used for status. A scholar may know languages, texts, and histories, yet still resist what that knowledge demands. The people named here are valuable as witnesses because their learning gives their testimony weight, not because their group identity makes every opinion correct.

Quran 46:10 presents a witness from the Children of Israel as recognizing the truth of the Quran and believing while others act arrogantly. The two passages together show that inherited access to scripture can become either a path to recognition or a reason for pride. Knowledge is a responsibility: it should make a person more honest before evidence, not more skilled at avoiding it.

**A Particular Identification Has a Chronological Difficulty**

Some exegetical reports connect the knowledgeable witness with a particular Jewish scholar who accepted Islam in Medina. That identification has a difficulty when applied directly to this verse, because al-Shuʿarāʾ is generally treated as a Makkan sūrah and the verse addresses earlier deniers in a different setting. The report may illuminate a later example of recognition, but it should not narrow the verse to one individual.

The wording itself speaks broadly of knowledgeable people among the Children of Israel. A plural, general reading fits the argument better: informed members of the earlier scriptural community could recognize the Quran's relation to revelation, while the Makkan deniers remained responsible for their refusal. The verse does not depend on naming one witness to make its point.

**Recognition by Others Does Not Replace Personal Faith**

The question asks whether this was not sufficient proof for the deniers, not whether another community's testimony could save them without their own response. Evidence may come through a knowledgeable witness, but each hearer must still decide whether to accept the message. A person cannot outsource belief to a scholar, just as he cannot dismiss truth because a scholar is not present.

The verse therefore combines intellectual humility with moral independence. Listen to those who know, weigh their recognition fairly, and avoid the arrogance that treats unfamiliar guidance as impossible. At the same time, seek understanding for yourself. The testimony of the knowledgeable removes a barrier; it does not remove the duty to believe and obey.

The verse also teaches the proper use of expert testimony. A specialist can help a community recognize a pattern, explain a difficult text, or correct a false claim. The hearer should neither dismiss that knowledge out of pride nor surrender judgment so completely that every statement is accepted without examination. Respect for learning includes asking whether the witness is speaking accurately and honestly.

The knowledgeable among the Children of Israel had a particular position because they knew the language of scripture and the history of prophetic promise. Their recognition could expose the deniers' claim that the Quran had no relation to revelation. The evidence was especially relevant to people who already appealed to the People of the Book when it suited them, then ignored their testimony when it challenged their desires.

That inconsistency is one of the verse's sharp questions. If the deniers trusted learned people enough to ask them about prophecy, why did they refuse to consider their recognition of the Quran? Selective consultation is not a search for truth. It is the use of expertise as decoration for a conclusion already chosen.

The verse does not say that every learned person believed, and it does not turn disagreement among scholars into a reason for paralysis. It identifies a real body of knowledge that could recognize the message. The deniers remained responsible because evidence does not need to receive unanimous applause before it becomes evidence.

For later readers, the passage encourages careful learning across communities. A person should welcome a truthful witness even when that witness comes from outside his group. The source of truth is Allah, not the pride of one's own community. Knowledge becomes honorable when it leads to recognition, fairness, and a willingness to follow what has been shown to be true.

The verse's question is also a rebuke to the deniers' narrow circle of trust. They were willing to consult learned people when they hoped for an answer that protected their position, but recognition from knowledgeable witnesses became inconvenient when it supported the Quran. This is not a lack of evidence; it is an unfair rule for using evidence.

A witness from another community can be especially valuable because he is not repeating the deniers' inherited assumptions. His familiarity with earlier scripture lets him notice continuity that a local opponent might prefer to hide. Yet the witness still needs integrity. Knowledge without honesty can be used to confuse, just as ignorance can be used to mock.

The passage therefore encourages a culture in which claims are heard across boundaries. A Muslim should not fear truthful testimony simply because it comes from a person outside the community, and a scholar should not expect obedience merely because of a title. The source of guidance is Allah, while people are responsible for speaking and listening honestly.

Recognition by the knowledgeable among the Children of Israel was sufficient to remove one excuse, not to cancel the deniers' freedom. They could still refuse, but their refusal would no longer be sheltered by the claim that no informed person saw what the Quran was. The verse asks whether such evidence was not enough; the question exposes the cost of choosing pride over understanding.
