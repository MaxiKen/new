## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:147

> But if they deny you ˹O Prophet˺, say, “Your Lord is infinite in mercy, yet His punishment will not be averted from the wicked people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instruction for the Case of Denial**

But if they deny you, say, Your Lord is infinite in mercy, yet His punishment will not be averted from the wicked people. The verse gives the Prophet the words for the situation in which the audience rejects the message, and it is placed immediately after the account of the earlier communities' dietary laws and the testimony that closed it. The instruction covers both sides of the matter in a single sentence: the mercy that gives the deniers their time and the punishment that remains in place for those who persist.

The Book's habit of pairing the two attributes is consistent throughout. The sūrah of the Rocky Tract tells the Prophet to inform the servants that God is the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful, and that His torment is the most painful (15:49–50); the sūrah of the Forgiver describes God as the Forgiver of sin, the Accepter of repentance, the Severe in punishment, and the Infinite in bounty (40:3); and the sūrah of the Heights records God's answer to Moses that His mercy encompasses all things while His punishment falls on those who persist (7:156). The verse in front of us states the pair in the context of the Prophet's own situation.

**The Mercy That Is Infinite**

The Book's word for the mercy here describes a quality without limit, and the phrase appears elsewhere in the Book's statements about God. In the sūrah of the Heights, God tells Moses that His mercy encompasses all things, and the sūrah of the Troops asks the audience whether they despair of the mercy of God. The verse in front of us states that the mercy is the reason the punishment has not yet arrived.

The Book's teaching about the delay that mercy produces appears in its accounts of the communities that were given time. Āl ʿImrān warns the disbelievers not to think that the respite granted to them is good for them, since they are given time only to increase in sin, and Sūrat al-Kahf states that God has overlooked much of what people have done. The sentence in this verse states the arrangement in the form of an answer the Prophet is to give.

**The Punishment That Is Not Averted**

Yet His punishment will not be averted from the wicked people. The second half of the sentence states the limit of the delay, and the Book's word for the wicked describes those who persist in wrongdoing. The sūrah has described the same outcome for the communities that rejected their messengers, and it has stated that the destruction of a society is not attributed to God before the warning has been delivered (6:131).

The Book's statements about the punishment attached to persistent denial appear throughout the chapter. The sūrah has described the wrongdoers in the throes of death and the torment that follows (6:93), the humiliation and severe punishment awaiting those who demanded the messenger's office (6:124), and the fire described as the home of the people who misled and were misled (6:128). The sentence in front of us states the same outcome in the form of an answer to denial.

**Why the Verse Is Addressed to the Prophet**

The instruction is given to a man whose people had rejected him, and its purpose is to place the matter where the sūrah has consistently placed it. The Prophet is not told to defend himself or to secure a verdict; he is told what to say, and what he says is a statement of the two attributes by which God deals with those who persist and those who turn.

The Book's instructions to the Prophet in similar situations follow the same pattern. The sūrah of the Prophets tells him that he is a warner and that the outcome belongs to God, and the sūrah of the Troops tells him that his duty is only to remind. The verse in front of us supplies the content of the reminder in this particular case, and it does so by stating the mercy and the punishment together.

**What the Verse Says About the Audience's Opportunity**

The sentence states that the audience's position was not yet settled. The mercy that gives time is named first, and the punishment is attached to the wicked rather than to the people who have merely heard and not yet responded. The verse therefore leaves the possibility of turning open, and the sūrah has made the same point throughout its treatment of guidance and refusal.

The Book's addresses to the community that had not yet accepted the message consistently leave the door open in this way. The sūrah of the Cave states that the message is the truth from the Lord and that whoever wills may believe, and the sūrah of the Troops tells the Prophet to inform the servants of the mercy before it states the severity of the punishment. The verse in front of us follows the same order, and the order is the point.

**The Place of the Verse in the Section**

The verse stands between the account of the earlier communities' dietary restrictions and the testimony that the revelation's account of them is true (6:146), on one side, and the polytheists' argument that God's will lay behind their practices (6:148), on the other. It functions as the answer the Prophet is to give when the account is denied, and it prepares the reader for the fuller argument about divine will and human responsibility that follows.

The sūrah's treatment of the audience's appeal to God's will is one of the most important passages in the chapter, and the verse in front of us states the framework within which that appeal is answered. The mercy and the punishment are the two terms of the arrangement, and the audience's plea does not remove them.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse records the polytheists' argument that if God had willed, neither they nor their forefathers would have associated others with Him or made anything unlawful, and it states that earlier communities made the same argument before they tasted the punishment (6:148). The passage that follows asks whether the audience has any knowledge to produce and states that they follow nothing but assumptions.

The two verses belong together, since the appeal to divine will is the denial the previous verse anticipated, and the answer to it occupies the verses that follow. The sūrah's treatment of the subject ends with the declaration that God has the most conclusive argument and that He could have imposed guidance on everyone had He willed (6:149), and the verse in front of us opens that final exchange.
