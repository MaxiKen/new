## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:65

> If you question them, they will certainly say, “We were only talking idly and joking around.” Say, “Was it Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger that you ridiculed?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Joke Cannot Erase Its Target**

The hypocrites defend themselves by calling their words idle talk and jokes. The answer does not reject humor as such. It asks what was made the object of the humor: Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger. A joke is not morally empty simply because the speaker laughs or claims not to mean it. Its target, meaning, and effect still matter.

People often use humor to test how far they can cross a boundary while retaining an escape route. If challenged, they say the audience was too serious. This verse removes that route when the joke is directed at sacred truth. Laughter can make contempt socially acceptable, but it cannot make contempt innocent. The servant must judge the act before the audience decides whether it was entertaining.

**Intentions Do Not Make Contempt Harmless**

A speaker may claim that he intended only to pass time, yet the content can reveal an intention to diminish. The verse does not say that every clumsy word has the same judgment as deliberate blasphemy. It does insist that people do not hide behind a vague claim of play when their speech attacks the foundations of faith.

This distinction requires knowledge and fairness. A person who misspeaks, recognizes the error, and repents is not the same as one who persists in mockery and invents excuses. Listeners should not rush to declare someone outside faith based on an unclear phrase. They should also not laugh along with a clear insult merely because the speaker calls it a joke. Caution and seriousness belong together.

**Sacred Things Must Not Become Props**

Allah, revelation, and the Messenger are not props for social performance. The hypocrites use them as material for a private bond built on shared contempt. Such joking creates an inner circle: those who understand the insult feel clever, while the believers are treated as naive. The result is not harmless amusement but a community of disdain.

A believer can enjoy humor about ordinary human weakness while protecting what Allah has made sacred. He can laugh at his own mistakes without making revelation ridiculous. He can use lightness to comfort people without using it to erase moral boundaries. The test is whether humor makes truth easier to receive or makes contempt easier to express.

**Questioning Is Not the Same as Ridiculing**

The verse should not be used to silence sincere questions. A person may ask why a command exists, seek evidence for a report, or struggle to understand a difficult passage. Questions can be signs of honesty when they are asked with respect. Ridicule is different because its goal is not understanding. It turns the sacred object into a target and the audience into accomplices.

This distinction protects learning. Teachers should answer questions patiently and correct mockery firmly. Students should feel free to say that they do not understand, but they should not make ignorance into a performance of superiority. The believer approaches revelation with humility, even when he is seeking clarity about something difficult.

**An Excuse After Exposure Reveals Character**

The response “we were only joking” comes after they have been questioned. It is not offered as a sincere explanation before harm occurs; it is used as a shield after the words have been exposed. This teaches the community to examine how a person responds to correction. Repentance accepts the wrong. Evasion argues about the audience's sensitivity.

A person who has caused harm should be able to say, “That was wrong, and I will not repeat it.” Such an admission may feel humiliating, but it preserves the soul. A person who keeps refining the excuse protects the image while deepening the defect. Allah values truthfulness more than a clever defense.

**Humor Needs a Moral Boundary**

The boundary is not that believers must be gloomy. The boundary is that nothing becomes funny merely because it is sacred and someone has found a way to mock it. The Messenger's mission deserves respect, and revelation deserves careful speech. When a society normalizes contempt, the cost reaches beyond one joke: reverence weakens, cruelty becomes stylish, and the vulnerable learn that no truth will be protected.

The believer keeps joy without surrendering reverence. He chooses words that bring relief rather than humiliation, and he corrects a joke that crosses into contempt. If he has crossed that line, he repents without hiding behind the audience's laughter. The question of the verse remains decisive: what exactly was being ridiculed?

A believer should also learn to apologize without demanding that the injured person immediately laugh again. Repentance is not complete when the offender becomes uncomfortable with the consequences. It includes giving the other person room to recover and proving through later speech that the contempt has ended. Teachers and leaders should correct ridicule without turning correction into a new humiliation. The aim is reverence and repair. Humor remains a gift when it relieves fear, strengthens friendship, and exposes the ego without attacking revelation. Once sacred truth becomes the object of contempt, the speaker has crossed into a different moral field, and a casual excuse cannot carry him back.

A community that protects this boundary will still have joy. It will laugh at human weakness without making worship ridiculous, tell stories without inventing contempt, and use wit to expose arrogance rather than revelation. The listener who hears a harmful joke can respond calmly: the issue is not whether laughter occurred, but what was placed beneath it. If the speaker repents, the door remains open. If he persists, the community does not join his audience. Reverence is preserved through speech, and speech is one of the clearest places where the heart reveals what it honors.
