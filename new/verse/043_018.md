## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:18

> **˹Do they attribute to Him˺ those who are brought up in fineries and are not commanding in disputes?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Without a Verb**

The verse is a fragment, and the fragment is deliberate. It begins with the particle a-wa, "and is it that", followed by man, "one who", and then a description, with no main verb. The listener has to supply the missing clause from the context, and the exegetes supply it in one of two ways: is it one who is reared among ornaments that they attribute to God? Or: do they make one who is reared among ornaments into a child of the Compassionate? Either way the verse is an exclamation of disbelief. The broken grammar imitates the speaker's astonishment, as if the sentence cannot finish because the idea is too absurd to complete. Arabic rhetoric calls this ellipsis, and the Qurʾān uses it where emotion outruns syntax. The description that fills the fragment is a portrait of a female child as the Makkans saw her: raised in ḥilyah, ornament, and unable to hold her own in khiṣām, argument. The verse takes their portrait and asks whether this is what they have made into a deity.

**Reared in Ornament: The Makkan Picture of Women**

Yunashshaʾu fī l-ḥilyah, brought up in adornment, describes how the Makkans raised their daughters. Girls were dressed in jewellery from infancy, and the jewellery was understood as compensation. Ibn Kathīr explains the idolaters' view: women lacked something that had to be made up with gold and ornament. The verse reports this picture without endorsing it. It uses the passive verb, "is reared", which places the responsibility for the girl's condition on those who reared her. If she is decorated rather than educated, that is a fact about her upbringing and not about her nature. The word ḥilyah will return in the sūrah's most famous passage, verses 33 to 35, where God says He would have given the disbelievers houses with silver roofs and gold ornament, zukhruf, if it were not that people would then all become disbelievers, since all that is only the enjoyment of this world. The girl reared in ḥilyah and the disbeliever coveting zukhruf are measured by the same standard: ornament is what people who cannot see worth in a thing pile on top of it.

**Not Clear in Argument: The Second Charge**

The second descriptor is fī l-khiṣāmi ghayru mubīn, not clear or not commanding in dispute. Khiṣām is contention, litigation, the public argument in which a man of the tribe defended his rights or the rights of his kin. Women were not admitted to it, and the Makkans took this exclusion as evidence that women could not argue. Mubīn is once again the word from verse 2, where the Book is clear, and from verse 15, where the ingrate is clear in his ingratitude; here the female child is not clear, not able to make her case plain. Ibn Kathīr states the idolaters' logic: they do not allow women to be heard in a dispute, so how could they attribute femaleness to beings they call divine? The verse is again arguing from the opponent's premise. It does not say women cannot argue. It says the Quraysh believed they could not, and asks them how a being they regarded as incapable of self-defence could be a partner of the Almighty. The whole force of the passage is that the idolaters are being convicted by their own estimates.

**A Second Reading: The Ornamented Idols**

Al-Ṭabarī records a different way of taking the verse that shifts its subject from daughters to idols. On this reading the one reared in ornament is the idol itself, carved and then decked with gold and silver by its worshippers, and the one not clear in dispute is the same idol, which cannot speak at all, let alone argue. The reading has a certain fit with the sūrah's title and its later verses, and it makes the verse a satire on image-worship in the manner of Ibrāhīm's mockery of his people's statues in 21:63–66. But the majority reading, which takes the verse as continuing the argument about daughters from verses 16 and 17, is stronger. The verb yunashshaʾu, is reared or brought up, describes the growth of a living being, not the manufacture of an object; the previous verse has just described a man's reaction to the birth of a daughter; and the next verse names the angels explicitly as the beings called female. The idol reading is a possible secondary sense, but the passage is about the Makkan doctrine of God's daughters, and this verse describes the daughter.

**Turning the Insult on Its Speaker**

The verse's rhetoric deserves close attention because it is easy to misread. It repeats a demeaning description of women, and a careless reader might take the Qurʾān to be endorsing it. It is not. The description is quoted, in effect, from the idolaters' own mouths and turned against them. Their theology said the angels were female and divine. Their culture said females were ornamental and inarticulate. The verse places the two claims together and asks the idolaters which one they actually believe, because they cannot hold both. If females are as they say, then attributing them to God is an insult to God. If females are fit to be attributed to God, then their contempt for their own daughters is indefensible. Either way the idolater loses. The Qurʾān's own view of women is stated elsewhere, in verses that give them the same origin, the same accountability, and the same reward as men (4:1, 33:35, 16:97). What this verse gives is not a view of women but a mirror held up to men who despised women and worshipped them at the same time. The next verse states plainly what they had done to the angels and asks what evidence they had.
