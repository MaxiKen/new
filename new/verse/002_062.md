## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:62

> Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Names on One Promise**

The verse lists four communities and hangs one promise on all four: the believers — the Muslim confessors — the Jews, the Christians, and the Ṣābiʾūn. The third name is the verse's historical puzzle, and the exegetes' candidates map the puzzle's shape. *Ṣābiʾ* derives from a root meaning to leave one religion for another — or, in a second etymology, to rise, as stars rise — and pagan Arabs used it as a slur for the Prophet ﷺ himself, who had left the religion of his forefathers. The commentators propose identifications across a spectrum: monotheists with no book, rites, or prophet, saying only *there is no god but God*; angel-venerating worshippers who faced a qiblah and recited the Psalms; defectors from the People of the Book; followers of the religion of Noah; or star-influenced monotheists whom some classify as unbelievers. History supplies two concrete groups: the people of Ḥarrān, who adopted the name Ṣābiʾ in the early Islamic centuries to claim the Qur'an's protected status for their Hellenized Near-Eastern religion, and the Mandaeans of southern Iraq and Iran — monotheists who revere John the Baptist, practice frequent baptism, and are still called ṣābiʾa today. The list's fourth slot thus names either a specific community or a category of the in-between, and the verse's ruling is framed to work either way.

**The Condition Is Creed and Conduct, Not the Label**

The list is immediately overridden by a relative clause, and the clause is the verse's actual ruling: *whoever truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good*. The four names enumerate; the condition defines. Three elements constitute it — belief in Allah, belief in the Last Day, and righteous action — and the verse attaches the promise not to any name in the list but to whoever meets the three. The structure does deliberate theological work. A reader expecting communal soteriology — Jews saved as Jews, Christians as Christians — finds instead individual criteria stated in the terms the Qur'an uses everywhere for the substance of faith: the two beliefs that orient a life (its Lord and its end) plus the conduct that proves them. The adverb the translation supplies — *truly* — renders the Arabic's own emphasis device, and it guards the clause against nominal membership: the Jew, Christian, or Ṣābiʾun in view is the one whose belonging has become belief. The verse is therefore not a list of saved communities but a statement that salvation runs through persons and their creed-and-conduct, with the communities named as the addresses among whom such persons are found.

**Their Reward With Their Lord**

The promise is stated in the Qur'an's fixed eschatological idiom: *their reward is with their Lord*, and *no fear upon them, no grief*. The two halves are the charter formula of this sūrah — the same pair that closed the descent's guarantee to Adam's children: whoever follows the guidance, *"there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve"* (2:38). The reuse is the verse's strongest structural signal: the people of verse 62 are being admitted to the charter's own promise, in the charter's own words, which places the verse inside the sūrah's first architecture rather than beside it as an exception. The formula's two negatives are precise about what salvation removes — fear of what is coming, grief over what was left — covering the future and the past in one stroke, the exact double anxiety the Last Day generates. And the reward's location — *with their Lord*, stored in His custody rather than distributed by any community's gatekeepers — removes the promise from every institutional claim: no priesthood, no lineage, no confession holds the keys to this verse's treasury. The reward is with the Lord of the four named groups, which is the verse's quiet way of saying He is one and His custody is single.

**The Parallel Filing in al-Māʾidah**

The Qur'an states this ruling twice, and the second filing confirms the first's permanence: *"Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians and Christians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve"* (5:69). The parallel is near-verbatim, with one variation — the Ṣābiʾūn move ahead of the Christians in the order — and variation of order without variation of content is the Qur'an's way of filing a principle rather than an occasion. A concession to a moment is stated once; a standing rule is restated in a later sūrah, in a different context, with the same clause carrying the same weight. Al-Māʾidah's filing sits among verses on communal difference and judgment — God will decide between the communities on the Day of Judgment — which frames the rule there as eschatological business belonging to Allah alone. The double filing also answers the historicist reduction from within the text: whatever historical group the Ṣābiʾūn were, the ruling's grammar — whoever believes and does good — is built to outlive every candidate identification, and the second sūrah's repetition publishes that intent.

**Before Islam or After? The Abrogation Debate and Its Better Answer**

The exegetes ask whether the verse's promise runs past the Prophet's ﷺ mission, and three answers have their grounds. The first limits it: the verse concerns Jews, Christians, and Ṣābiʾūn who kept their original faith before Islam and, when the Prophet ﷺ came, acknowledged him — people like Salmān al-Fārsī, Abū Dharr, and the monks Waraqa ibn Nawfal and Baḥīra; on this reading the labels name communities, and the condition names their faithful members' completion in Islam. The second claims abrogation: a view cited from Ibn ʿAbbās holds that 3:85 — whoever seeks a religion other than submission, it will not be accepted — cancels this verse. The counter-principle defeats it on the tradition's own terms: abrogation applies to legal rulings and commands, not to descriptive statements, and least of all to statements about persons' standing with Allah in the hereafter; verse 62 is description, not legislation, and survives. The third, al-Zamakhsharī's, reads *those who believe* ironically as the hypocrites — outward confessors lumped with the named groups, against whom the verse's second half then defines true believers. The reading collapses without it: nowhere else in the Qur'an does *those who believe* mean hypocrites, and a Jew who followed Muhammad's law would simply no longer be called a Jew. The stronger construction keeps the plain sense and reads the condition as the verse does — creed, eschatology, and good works as the substance the labels stand for, with the commentators' common gloss that true belief after the mission's publication includes acceptance of the Messenger, since rejecting a prophet one has truly known is not belief in Allah but belief on one's own terms.

**Al-Ghazālī's Latitude and the Verse's Placement**

The verse's hardest question — who counts as having truly believed and done good, judged across barriers of information — received its most careful Islamic answer from al-Ghazālī. In his *Fayṣal al-tafriqah* he argues that whole populations fall inside divine mercy: the Byzantine Christian or the Turk who never met the Prophet's ﷺ message except as caricature — who heard only that a man called Muhammad claimed prophethood and was, as presented to them, a liar — cannot be convicted of rejecting him, because what they rejected was not him. Birthplace, upbringing, and the quality of one's informants mediate every person's knowledge of religion, and al-Ghazālī gives those mediators full weight: denial deserving of condemnation requires access to the thing denied. The argument is the verse's condition applied with epistemic honesty — *whoever truly believes* is a statement about hearts and their actual information, which belongs to Allah's audit and not to human label-checking. The verse's placement makes the same point structurally. It stands in the middle of the Israel recital — between the wilderness verdicts and the covenant of the mountain — inside the sūrah's most particular prosecution, and it interrupts the prosecution with a universal principle. The recital audits a community by name; the verse declares that reward runs by creed and conduct in four communities at once. Particular judgment and universal criteria are thus filed adjacently, each guarding the other: the audit prevents the principle from becoming indifference, and the principle prevents the audit from becoming exclusion.
