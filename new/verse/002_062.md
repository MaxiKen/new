## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:62

> Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Communities Named Together**

The verse lists four groups in one sentence: those who believe, those who follow Judaism, the Christians, and the Sabians. The last of these is the least familiar, and the commentators supplied several identifications for it, from a community of monotheists without a recognised scripture to groups outside Arabia whose practices mingled older traditions. The Book does not explain the term, and the uncertainty about its reference has never affected the meaning of the sentence, since the condition that follows applies to whoever satisfies it.

What the list does is widen the frame of the passage at a point where the reader might expect a narrowing. The sūrah has spent twenty verses describing a particular people's history, and this verse steps back and states a criterion that no community owns. The named groups are the religious bodies known to the audience of the revelation, and they are all placed on the same footing before the same requirement.

**The Condition Stated Twice Over**

The requirement is given in three parts: belief in God, belief in the Last Day, and good conduct. The two objects of belief are the ones the sūrah has emphasised throughout: the Creator whose signs fill the world, and the reckoning that the earlier verses described as a day on which no soul helps another. The third part is action, and the Book never separates it from the other two.

The Arabic construction makes the requirement individual rather than communal. The promise is attached to *whoever* satisfies it, not to a people, a lineage or a congregation, and each of the four groups named is therefore being addressed as a collection of persons who will each be measured by the same standard. The Book's usual practice with such formulas is to place the responsibility where it can be exercised, which is in the individual life.

**The Reward Named: With Their Lord**

The phrase that describes the reward locates it rather than specifying it. The Book does not promise the community a garden, a land or a victory in this sentence; it states that their reward is held by their Lord. The wording is the same as the one used for the faithful elsewhere in the sūrah, and it makes the promise a matter of a relationship rather than of a payment.

That phrasing also keeps the sentence consistent with the Book's treatment of generosity. A reward kept with God is not measured by the terms of an exchange, which is why the sūrah's parables can describe a man trading guidance for misguidance as having made a bad bargain. The people described in this verse have not purchased anything; they have been promised something that belongs to someone else to give.

**Fear and Grief, Removed Again**

The closing words repeat a formula the sūrah used when it described the guidance promised to Adam's descendants: no fear for them, and no grief. The Book attaches the same pair to several categories of people across its pages, including the close servants of God (10:62), and it consistently uses it to describe the removal of both anxiety about the future and regret about the past.

The repetition of the formula in this verse is significant in its position. The passage has just described a community that was fed and then complained, and it now states the reward of anyone who believes and acts rightly. The Book places the universal promise immediately after the particular history, so that the reader who has followed Israel's record understands that the criterion has been available to everyone throughout.

**What the Commentators Disputed**

The discussion of this verse among the classical commentators concerned whether the communities named are saved only in the period before the coming of the Prophet ﷺ or also afterwards, and whether a believer who does not accept the final revelation can be included. Al-Ghazzālī argued at length that the obstacles to reaching a truthful account of a prophet's message are real and varied, and that a person raised amid hostile reports of Islam should not be treated as having rejected what he never properly encountered.

The verse itself states a criterion and does not resolve those disputes. Its language is about belief in God and the Last Day and about doing good, and the Book applies those terms consistently to people who accept what has been sent to them. What the sentence rules out is the assumption that a person's standing before God is settled by the community he was born into, and that is the point on which all the readings agree.

**A Universal Statement Placed Inside a Particular Address**

The sūrah has been speaking to the Children of Israel, and it here announces a condition that includes them without privileging them. That movement from the particular to the general is characteristic of the Book's method, and it appears again at the head of the sūrah, where the Book is called a guidance for the mindful before any community is named.

The verse therefore functions as a hinge between the narrative that precedes it and the covenant passage that follows. The reader has watched one people's gifts and failures; he is now told what the requirement has always been, and the address that resumes in the next verse returns to that people's history with the standard now clearly stated.

**Why the Verse Names Communities Rather Than Persons**

The list of four groups gives the sentence a reach the surrounding passage would otherwise lack. The sūrah has been addressing one people and reciting its history, and a reader could easily conclude that the standing it describes is a property of a lineage. The naming of Jews, Christians and Sabians alongside the believers removes that conclusion, and it does so without disparaging any of the communities named.

The terms the verse uses for the requirement are the ones the Book uses everywhere: belief in God, belief in the final reckoning, and conduct. Nothing in the sentence mentions a book, a messenger or a community, and the promise attached to it is held by God rather than issued by an institution. What the verse does is state the criterion in the middle of a long account of one people's privileges, and it places that criterion above them.
