## Sūrah an-Naml 27:24

> I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of Allah. For Satan has made their deeds appealing to them—hindering them from the ˹Right˺ Way and leaving them unguided—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Actual Charge, Delivered Last**

Now the report reaches its point. Everything before this — the woman ruling, the full provision, the great throne — was context. What brought the hoopoe back across two thousand kilometres was that an entire kingdom bows to the sun.

Note what he does not object to. Not her rule, not her wealth, not her throne, not the foreignness of the place. The single fault named is the direction of their worship, and the phrase that carries it is *min dūni Allāh*, instead of God, or short of God. The objection is not that they worship but that they stop at the sun. This is the Qur'an's consistent framing of idolatry: the problem is rarely that people worship nothing, and almost always that they attach worship to something real but created. The sun is not an imaginary deity. It is the most visible, most obviously powerful object in the sky, and that is exactly what makes it dangerous.

**Why the Sun Is the Hardest Case**

Sun worship is the most defensible form of idolatry available, and it is worth saying why rather than treating it as obvious folly. The sun genuinely does sustain life. It is visible to everyone, reliable in its movements, indifferent to human politics, and the source of heat, light and every crop. A community that oriented its rituals toward it would not be worshipping a fiction; it would be worshipping the most powerful thing it could see.

Sun cults existed across the ancient world for exactly these reasons, from Egypt to Mesopotamia, and they were the religions of advanced states rather than of primitive ones. Sheba was a wealthy trading kingdom with engineering works that outlasted it by centuries. Its people were not simple.

That is the Qur'an's actual complaint. The error is not a mistake about the sun's importance but a failure to ask what the sun depends on. The Book makes the argument explicitly elsewhere through Ibrāhīm, who watches a star, then the moon, then the sun, and rejects each one as they set — the reasoning being that whatever sets is governed, and what is governed is not the governor. Sheba had noticed the greatest visible power and had stopped there. Their worship was not irrational. It was insufficiently curious, which is a different and more common failing.

**Satan Adorns; the Chapter Has Said This Before**

The second half of the verse names the mechanism, and it uses the same verb the chapter used in verse 4: *zayyana lahum al-shayṭān aʿmālahum* — Satan made their deeds appealing to them. There the agent was God; here it is Satan; elsewhere in the Qur'an the verb stands in the passive with no agent named at all.

The three formulations describe one process from three vantage points, and this verse supplies the immediate level. Satan is the proximate cause — the one who presents the wrong act wearing the appearance of the right one. God is named as the agent in verse 4 because the law under which persistent refusal hardens into blindness is His law. The passive is used where the point is only that it happened. Reading the three as contradictory requires ignoring that the Qur'an places two of them in the same chapter twenty verses apart, which no author does by accident. What all three agree on is the effect: the people concerned are not miserable about their condition. Their deeds look good to them.

**Two Verbs for What the Adornment Did**

The verse describes two consequences with two distinct verbs, and they are not the same thing. *Ṣaddahum ʿan al-sabīl* — he barred them from the way. *Fahum lā yahtadūn* — so they are not guided.

The first is obstruction: something has been placed between them and the road. The second is a state: they are not finding it. Obstruction explains why a person does not advance; the absence of guidance explains why he does not even search. Put together they describe a closed condition. If only the road were blocked, a traveller would look for another; if only guidance were absent, he might stumble onto the path by accident. Both at once is the situation the hoopoe found. And the phrasing recalls verse 4's ending — *so they wander blindly* — which described the deniers of the Hereafter in almost the same terms. Sheba, a sophisticated and prosperous kingdom half a world from Pharaoh's court, has arrived at the same diagnosis by a completely different route.

**A Report on Others That Contains No Contempt**

There is a tone in this verse worth marking, because it is easy to miss. The hoopoe describes idolatry in the strongest terms available and still reports the queen and her people as victims of an adornment rather than as villains. Satan is the one who did the barring; they are the ones who were barred.

That framing has a practical consequence in the story. The response to Sheba is not an army. Sulaymān commands a force of jinn, humans and birds, and he sends a letter. The whole subsequent narrative is an argument conducted by correspondence, gift and demonstration, ending with a queen who changes her mind and says so. If the hoopoe had reported a nation of wilful enemies, a different response would have followed. He reported a nation that had been misled, and the intervention was designed accordingly. The bird's own reaction to what he found is stated in the next verse, and it is framed as a question rather than a verdict.
