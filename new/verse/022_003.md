## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:3

> ˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, and follow every rebellious devil.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Argument Appears Next to the Earthquake**

The sūrah has just described the world coming apart, mothers dropping children and crowds reeling. Verse 3 turns, without transition, to a man holding an opinion: *wa-mina al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bighayri ʿilmin* — and among the people is he who disputes about God without knowledge.

The abruptness is the connection. The Hour was announced in verses 1–2 as a fact; the fact has to be received or rejected; and the rejection is what verse 3 presents. Disputation about God is not a separate topic that happens to follow the eschatology. It is the form that rejecting the eschatology takes in a person who will not say plainly that he rejects it. The sūrah shows, in three verses, the sequence that runs through the whole Qur'an: the warning is given, the warning is argued with, and the arguing is what decides the man.

**The Indictment Names Ignorance, Not Error**

The charge is not that these people reach wrong conclusions. It is that their conclusions rest on nothing: *bighayri ʿilmin*, without knowledge. The Qur'an's complaint against its opponents is remarkably consistent on this point, and it is almost never that their reasoning fails. It is that the reasoning has no starting point — no revelation, no observation, no transmitted report — and therefore no way of being wrong, since a claim with no ground cannot be corrected.

This is why the criticism is sharpened in verse 8, where the same disputant is described as lacking three things at once: knowledge, guidance, and an illuminating scripture. The three are not synonyms. Knowledge is what a person can arrive at; guidance is what he is given; scripture is the external, checkable record. To have none of the three is to be arguing from taste.

**Who Is Meant: Two Candidates, and the Text Covers Both**

The commentators divide over the reference. One reading takes the verse as aimed at those who denied the Resurrection specifically — the people whose objection is preserved elsewhere in the Qur'an: *“When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?”* (37:16). On this reading the disputant here is the man of verses 1–2, the one who has just been told that the world ends and answers that bones do not come back.

The other reading, preferred by several later commentators, takes it as aimed at those who dismissed the revelation wholesale, of the sort reported saying: *“We have already heard ˹the recitation˺. If we wanted, we could have easily produced something similar. This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!”* (8:31).

The two readings need not be fought over. The phrasing is *wa-mina al-nāsi man* — and *among people* is one who — a partitive construction that describes a type rather than naming an individual. Some commentaries name particular men of Makkah as the occasion, though the wording is general and the sūrah treats the type as a recurring one. **"Every Rebellious Devil": Shayṭān Is a Category, Not an Individual**

The disputant follows *kulla shayṭānin marīdin* — every rebellious devil. The indefinite, distributive "every" matters as much as the word itself. The Qur'an does not present one adversary with a plan; it presents a class of tempters to which a person can hand himself over wholesale.

*Shayṭān* in the Qur'an's usage covers the jinn who rebel, and it extends to human beings who play the same role. The word is built on a root conveying distance — the shayṭān is the one set at a distance from good, who works to set others at the same distance. A man who argues about God with no ground does not, on this verse, hold a private opinion. He has enrolled himself under whichever rebel happens to be calling, which is why the verse says *every* and not *a*.

**The Second Verb: Following**

The verse pairs ignorance with an activity: he disputes without knowledge *and follows* every rebellious devil. The two halves are cause and effect, and the order is fixed. A man without a ground for his belief does not become neutral; he becomes available.

*Yattabiʿu* is the verb of following, of walking behind. It is the same word used for following guidance, and that is the point. Every human being follows something. The question the verse poses is not whether a person is a follower — he is, by construction — but what he is walking behind. Deprived of knowledge, he does not stand still; he attaches himself to whichever rebel is nearest, and because the verse says *every* rebellious devil, the attachment is promiscuous rather than loyal.

The psychology here is exact. The person who has no way of testing a claim does not therefore withhold assent. He grants it more easily, and he grants it to the loudest voice. Ignorance in the Qur'an is not a vacuum; it is a liability that someone else will occupy.

**The Hidden Positive: Disputation With Knowledge Is Something Else**

The critics of this verse, from al-Qurṭubī to al-Rāzī, drew the inference the wording invites. If what is condemned is disputing *without knowledge*, then disputing with knowledge is a different act, and the Qur'an itself commands its opposite number — calling to the way of God on the basis of what has actually been revealed.

The distinction protects theology as a discipline. The verse is not a prohibition on argument about God; it is a prohibition on argument about God from nothing. What separates the two is not tone or politeness but sources: the man who has revelation, reasoning and a checkable text has somewhere to be corrected from, and the man in this verse has nowhere.
