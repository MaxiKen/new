## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:41

> No! He is the only One you would call. And if He willed, He could remove the affliction that made you invoke Him. Only then will you forget whatever you associate with Him ˹in worship˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Supplied Before the Audience Speaks**

The verse opens with a refusal that is also the answer to the previous question. No, they would not call on anyone else; He is the only One you would call. The Book frequently supplies the answer on behalf of the people it questions, and it does so because the answer is not in dispute. Everyone in the audience knew what happens to a man in a storm, and the verse simply records it as a fact about them.

What makes the sentence forceful is its second-person address. The Prophet is told to say "you," not "they." Each person listening is described to himself in the moment of his own greatest need, which is a more difficult position to argue from than a general statement about idolaters in the abstract.

**The Removal That Depends on His Will**

The next clause preserves God's freedom in the answer: if He willed, He could remove the affliction that made you invoke Him. The Book states elsewhere that God responds to those who call on Him (2:186), and that He has proclaimed that His servants should call on Him and that He will respond (40:60), and it also refuses to make the response a mechanical consequence of the request.

Commentators drew different conclusions from this. For the Ashʿarites the clause supported their position that God's response depends entirely on His will, while the Muʿtazilites read it as meaning that God answers in whatever way serves the greater good. The text itself keeps the request and the answer distinct, which is the posture the Book recommends throughout: ask with urgency, and leave the outcome with the One being asked.

**The Forgetting That Follows Rescue**

The verse closes on a diagnosis of the rescued: only then will you forget whatever you associate with Him. The Book documents this pattern in the same detail in several places. When hardship touches people they cry out to their Lord, turning to Him alone, and as soon as He gives them a taste of His mercy, a group of them associate others with their Lord again (30:33). The gratitude promised at sea is not delivered on land, as the sūrah itself has stated a few verses earlier (6:64).

The word "forget" is exact. The idols were not refuted in the storm; they simply were not there, and once the danger passes the memory of their absence fades with it. The Book treats that forgetting as the real problem, because a person who has seen the truth and lost it is in a different position from a person who never saw it.

**What the Confession Proves About the Idols**

The verse functions as a demonstration of the idols' standing, and the demonstration uses the audience's own conduct rather than an argument about the stones. The Book presses the point elsewhere in a challenge addressed to the same people: those you invoke besides Allah are created beings like yourselves, so call upon them and see if they will answer you, if your claims are true (7:194). It then asks whether those objects have feet to walk with, hands to hold with, eyes to see with, or ears to hear with (7:195).

A deity with no capacity to answer is not a lesser god; it is not a participant in the transaction at all. Once the audience conceded, in the storm, that only Allah is called upon, the whole system of association had been stripped of its function by the people who maintained it.

**The Warning in the Closing Clause**

Read as a warning, the last clause is the most serious part of the verse. A person who calls on God in danger and returns to his old practice once delivered has not merely relapsed; he has made the mercy the reason to continue. The same reversal is described in the sūrah of the Spider, where those rescued from the waves associate others with Him again (29:65), and where the pattern is called out as a mark of the ungrateful.

The sūrah will turn next to a different aspect of the same problem: communities that were tested with hardship and did not humble themselves, and what was done to them in the end. The verse in front of us supplies the moral mechanism, and the verses that follow supply the historical record.

**What the Audience Could Do With This Sentence**

The question had been put to the Makkans about a moment of crisis. The answer told them what they already knew about themselves, and the last clause told them what they usually did with the knowledge once the crisis ended. There was no room in the verse for the comfortable position of a man who has been saved and has not drawn any conclusion from being saved.

The sentence therefore works as a test rather than as an accusation, and the sūrah uses the same test again when it addresses the sailors, the travellers, and the people of the towns that were punished after long prosperity. The pattern is fixed, and the verse leaves the hearer to place himself inside it.

The Book also records what happens when the call from the depths is sincere. The Man of the Whale cried out in the veils of darkness that there is no god worthy of worship except God, and that he had certainly done wrong, and the answer came: So We answered his prayer and rescued him from anguish, and so do We save the true believers (21:87–88). The rescue in that account arrived while the man was still inside the problem, which is the mercy this verse's audience had never seriously asked for, since they called on God only when the water was already at their throats and returned to the shrines as soon as the shore appeared.
