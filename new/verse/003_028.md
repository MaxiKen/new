## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:28

> Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers—and whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah— unless it is a precaution against their tyranny. And Allah warns you about Himself. And to Allah is the final return.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Walī: One Word Behind Several Relationships**

The term rendered "guardians" is *awliyāʾ*, the plural of *walī*, and the dictionaries give it a wide band of meanings: protector, ally, patron, client, close friend, the person to whom one's affairs are entrusted. In the tribal society the Qur'an addresses, the word described the relationship that determined a person's safety, obligations, and standing.

That is why the verse is not a rule about private friendship. It concerns allegiance, the arrangement by which a person places his security and loyalty in someone else's hands. In a city where the Muslim community was a minority with powerful neighbours, the question of which protector a believer attached himself to was the central political question of his life, and the sūrah addresses it directly.

**"Instead of the Believers" — the Clause That Defines the Crime**

The prohibition is qualified by a phrase that is easy to read past: *instead of the believers*. The offence described is not contact, trade, or courtesy toward people of another faith; it is the replacement of the community of believers with another community as the source of protection and belonging.

The Book draws the line itself in a passage that explains this verse at length. Toward those who are not at war with the Muslims, the instruction is generous: *“Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly and fairly with those who have neither fought nor driven you out of your homes. Surely Allah loves those who are fair.”* (60:8). The prohibition is attached to the hostile case: *“Allah only forbids you from befriending those who have fought you for ˹your˺ faith, driven you out of your homes, or supported ˹others˺ in doing so.”* (60:9). The criterion throughout is conduct in the conflict, not the label a neighbour carries.

**The Consequence: Hope Withdrawn**

The middle of the verse states the result of such an alliance with unsparing clarity: the person who does it *will have nothing to hope for from Allah*. The Book is careful about hope; it is a currency it treats as the property of the believer, and here it is removed.

The same argument appears twice more in the Qur'an with a questioning tone: those who choose disbelievers as allies are asked whether they are seeking honour through that company, when all honour belongs to God (4:139); and the believers are warned against handing God a clear case against themselves (4:144). The point is not that God punishes a person for making friends. It is that a man who has placed his protection outside God's community has already decided where he expects help to come from, and the expectation is answered in the terms it was made.

**Prudence, Not Dissimulation: the Exception Stated Here**

The verse permits one escape: *unless it is a precaution against their tyranny*. The Arabic noun *tuqāh* is related to *taqwā*, the guardedness that protects a person from harm, and the commentators read the exception as covering a believer who fears for his life or his family under a power that would persecute him.

The Book's parallel ruling is stronger and clearer: a person who is forced to deny his faith verbally while his heart remains firm is not condemned (16:106). The commentators drew a careful boundary around this permission. Words spoken under compulsion may be excused; the major prohibitions of the religion may not be violated under their cover, and a coerced denial does not license theft, murder, or adultery. What the exception protects is a life, not a licence.

**"Allah Warns You About Himself"**

The verse repeats a formula that appears here and again two verses later, and its wording is unusual enough to have occupied the commentators: the warning is not about punishment in the abstract but about God Himself. Al-Rāzī discussed whether an elided phrase is implied — a warning of His punishment, or a warning of the thing that has been forbidden — and settled on the reading that God is warning people about a matter whose seriousness lies in the one who is warning.

The effect is to concentrate the fear. A person might fear a penalty, a court, or a loss of standing; the sentence tells him that what he is dealing with is the authority behind all of them. The Book uses the formula to end its most serious instructions, and its repetition here marks the severity of the instruction just given.

**"To Allah Is the Final Return"**

The verse closes with the destination, and the closing clause explains the whole prohibition. Allegiance is a wager on where a person's affairs will finally be settled. A believer who attaches himself to a powerful patron is betting that protection in this world is the protection that matters; the final return says otherwise.

That is why the instruction can be obeyed without bitterness toward the people it concerns. The verse does not tell the believers to despise their neighbours or to treat them unjustly; it tells them where their security lies. A community that has settled that question is free to be fair, hospitable, and honest to everyone around it, because it is no longer asking anyone around it to be its refuge.

**Loyalty and the Long View**

Read beside the prayer that opened this passage — the one that put authority, honour, and disgrace in God's hands — this verse is a practical deduction. If God moves kingdoms, then the kingdom a believer leans on may be moved tomorrow, and the community he abandons for it will still be there, answering to the same Lord.

The following verse turns from public allegiance to private thought, since the two are connected: a person may conceal an alliance, and the Book goes on to say what concealment is worth.
