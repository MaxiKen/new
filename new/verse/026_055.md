## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55

> who have really enraged us,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pharaoh Describes the Oppressed as the Offenders**

Pharaoh says that the departing people have enraged him. The sentence shifts attention from what he has done to how he feels. The Israelites' movement away from enslavement is presented as an insult to the ruler, while the oppression that caused the movement disappears from his account. Anger becomes evidence of the victims' guilt rather than a reaction that the ruler must examine.

The phrase exposes a common pattern in tyranny. A powerful person treats resistance to harm as a personal offense. The victim is then accused of provoking the conflict by refusing to remain in the position assigned to him. Pharaoh's anger is real, but its reality does not make its object just. Emotions can reveal wounded pride as easily as they reveal moral danger.

**Enragement Is Not Proof of Wrongdoing**

A ruler may feel anger when his authority is challenged, but anger cannot serve as a legal judgment. The question should be what the Israelites have done and whether Pharaoh has a right to pursue them. Instead, the ruler offers his emotional state as a reason for mobilization. His people are expected to accept that because he is enraged, the departing community must be guilty.

This substitution is dangerous because it removes evidence from the discussion. A person can be sincerely furious and still be wrong. Injustice often becomes more violent when the wrongdoer treats his anger as a moral certificate. Pharaoh's statement asks the cities to join his emotional interpretation before they ask whether the Israelites have committed a crime.

**The Plagues Have Become a Story of Insult**

The anger can refer to the plagues and signs that struck Egypt, as well as to Moses and the Israelites' rejection of Pharaoh's religion. The ruler experiences the consequences of his own refusal as an attack from below. Instead of reading the plagues as warnings, he reads them as provocations. That change of interpretation allows him to pursue the people with resentment rather than repentance.

A warning becomes useful only when it leads to correction. If a person treats every consequence as humiliation imposed by an enemy, he learns nothing from it. Pharaoh's anger shows that the signs have touched his power but not softened his pride. He is enraged that the oppressed have become the occasion for his exposure.

**Tyranny Personalizes a Challenge to Justice**

Moses' demand concerned the rights of the Children of Israel and the lordship of God. Pharaoh reduces that public matter to his personal feeling. The people have enraged us, he says, making the ruler and his circle the main subjects. The plural us expands the ruler's pride into a collective grievance. Officials who benefit from the order are invited to treat the loss of domination as their shared injury.

This language hides the difference between the ruler's interests and the community's interests. Pharaoh speaks as though whatever weakens his control harms everyone. But a state is not identical with its ruler, and a court's anger is not the same as the people's welfare. The verse teaches caution whenever a leader claims that criticism of him is an attack on the nation.

**Anger Can Make Pursuit Feel Like Self-Defense**

A person who is enraged often experiences his own aggression as justified response. Pharaoh's anger prepares the moral cover for the chase. He can tell the mobilizers that they are recovering what has insulted them rather than attacking people who have been commanded to leave. Emotion changes the appearance of the action without changing its nature.

The proper discipline is to separate feeling from fact. What happened before the anger? Who had power? What demand was made? What response would justice require if the ruler were not personally offended? These questions recover the context Pharaoh has removed. His feeling may explain his behavior, but it cannot justify it.

**The Ruler's Anger Is a Sign of Lost Ownership**

Pharaoh does not say that the Israelites have harmed Egypt's people in this verse. He says they have enraged him. The object is the ruler's ego and the court's sense of possession. Their departure proves that Pharaoh's claim over them is no longer accepted. The anger is therefore the emotion of an owner who sees what he calls his property leaving.

God's command has already changed the moral status of the people. They are servants of God, not Pharaoh's possessions. The ruler's anger cannot reverse that truth. It can only reveal how deeply he has confused power with ownership. The more he insists that their freedom is an insult, the more clearly he shows why freedom is necessary.

**A Shared Grievance Can Recruit Ordinary People Into Wrongdoing**

Pharaoh's statement is addressed through mobilizers to the cities. The language of shared anger invites ordinary Egyptians to participate in a ruler's personal crisis. People may join the pursuit because they are told that the Israelites have offended the whole society. Once anger is made communal, violence can feel like solidarity.

The verse warns against that emotional contagion. A community should not inherit a ruler's anger without examining its cause. Solidarity with the powerful is not the same as justice. The Israelites' departure may frustrate Pharaoh, but frustration is not a wrong that authorizes pursuit. The ruler's feelings have been placed before the people's conscience, and the next words will add a claim about the strength of the host prepared to act on them.

The warning is especially sharp because Pharaoh's anger is being converted into a public command. Once officials and soldiers are told that the fugitives have enraged everyone, they may treat cruelty as the expression of a shared emotion. The verse separates the feeling from the duty: anger can be reported, but it cannot be allowed to decide whether a people may go free.
