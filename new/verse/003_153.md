## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:153

> ˹Remember˺ when you were running far away ˹in panic˺—not looking at anyone—while the Messenger was calling to you from behind! So Allah rewarded your disobedience with distress upon distress. Now, do not grieve over the victory you were denied or the injury you suffered. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scene Reconstructed**

The verse describes the battlefield in the hour of the collapse: the believers moving away, refusing to look at anyone, while the Messenger of God called to them from behind. The picture is assembled from three details, and each of them adds to the account.

The community's flight is presented without softening, and the sūrah reports it as a fact of its own history. The Book's willingness to describe the conduct of the believers at their weakest moment is part of what gives the passage its authority, and the verse is addressed to the people who had lived through the day.

**The Verb and the Terrain**

The verb describing the movement has the basic sense of going up, and al-Ṭabarī records a difference of opinion about what it denotes in this form. Some held that the form used here means to travel along level ground, which would fit the account of the men coming down from the high ground to the field. Others took it as describing flight up the slopes of the mountain.

Both readings describe the same disorder. On the first, the community is depicted streaming across open ground with the enemy behind it; on the second, it is scattered up the hillside. The disagreement is a reminder that the commentators were working with detailed reports of the terrain and the positions, and that they cared about getting the picture right before drawing lessons from it.

**Casting a Glance to No One**

The phrase about not looking at anyone describes a person so absorbed in his own flight that he has no attention left for those around him. The commentators explain it as not caring about anyone else.

The image is the opposite of the solidarity the sūrah has been urging on the community since its commands about holding fast together. The men on the field were not looking to one another, and the verse lets the picture carry the point without adding a reproach. What the community is shown is what its division looks like from the outside, at the moment when it mattered most.

**The Call From Behind**

The reports describe the Prophet calling after the retreating men, and the words attributed to him in the accounts are an address to the servants of God, repeated, with the call to come back to him. The verse records the call as coming from the rear, which is where the Messenger had remained with those who stood firm.

The detail is among the most affecting in the sūrah. The person the community had come out to defend was calling it back while it fled, and the reports place him among a small group holding the position. The verse mentions the call in a subordinate clause and leaves the image to do its work, and the sorrow the next sentence announces is placed in relation to it.

**Sorrow Upon Sorrow and Its Explanations**

The verse states that God recompensed the community with one sorrow upon another, and the commentators offer several accounts of what the two sorrows were. On one reading, the first was the grief of having caused the Prophet grief, and the second was the loss they suffered afterward. On another, the sorrows were the loss of the battle and the loss of the spoils, followed by the enemy's rejoicing. A further reading holds that the first was the defeat and the second the fear that the enemy would return to finish what it had begun.

Al-Rāzī adds that the retreat itself was a form of sorrow, since a person who runs away feels his own weakness. The variety of explanations reflects the density of the experience, and the verse's phrase holds all of them: the day delivered one grief after another, and the community was made to feel the weight of the whole.

**The Purpose Stated in the Sentence**

The verse attaches a purpose to the sorrow: so that the community would not grieve over what escaped it or what befell it. The teaching is that the loss of advantage and the loss of life are both to be held in a scale that does not make them the measure of everything.

Al-Rāzī explained the clause as a means of loosening the community's attachment to the goods of this world, so that it would neither be saddened by losing them nor gladdened by gaining them. Read this way, the sorrow of the day was itself an instruction, and the community's education in detachment came through the experience of losing what it had hoped to keep.

**The Discipline of Fear**

The verse's account of the collapse also treats the fear that produced it as a condition to be governed. The Book's commands about courage in this sūrah rest on the same ground as its commands about patience: the person who knows that the outcome is in the hands of God is not ruled by the prospect of loss.

The community at Uḥud had been given a demonstration of what fear does to a body of people, and the sūrah preserves the record so that later generations could learn from it. The lesson is not that the believers were cowards but that a failure of steadiness in one place spreads through everything that depends on it, and the verse supplies the sequence in which that happened.

**The Closing Statement About Awareness**

The verse ends by stating that God is aware of everything the community does. The sentence is not a threat appended to a narrative; it is the ground of the account that has just been given.

The Book knows what happened on the field, and it knows what was in the people who ran and in the Messenger who called. The community that recites this verse is told that its conduct is seen by the one who pardoned it, and the next verse continues the account by describing what came down upon the believers after the sorrow had done its work.
