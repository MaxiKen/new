## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:60

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, “Strike the rock with your staff.” Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place. ˹We then said,˺ “Eat and drink of Allah’s provisions, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer Answered Through an Ordinary Object**

The request in this verse comes from Moses on behalf of the people, and the answer comes in the form of an instruction about a stick. The Book states the sequence in a single sentence: a prayer, a command, and water. Nothing is said about the manner in which the prayer was made, and the reader is left with the chain of events and the fact that the first link is a human request.

The same event appears in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf with the springs and the tribes in more detail, and the account there closes with the words *“They ˹certainly˺ did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves.”* (7:160). The repetition of the story in two sūrahs with the same elements — the staff, the rock, twelve springs — tells the reader that the incident is part of the fixed record of this community's time in the wilderness.

**Twelve Springs and Twelve Tribes**

The number of the springs matches the number of the tribes, and the verse makes the correspondence explicit by saying that each group knew its own drinking place. The Book is describing an arrangement that prevented dispute as much as it relieved thirst: a single source would have required a queue, a distribution and an authority, and the twelve-part provision removed the need for all three.

The Book's care with the number shows how it treats the community's structure. It had been organised into tribes since the descent from Egypt, and the provision given in the desert preserved that structure rather than dissolving it. What the community received was not only water but water that arrived in the shape of its own composition, which is the Book's way of describing a gift tailored to the people receiving it.

**Moses' Role in the Miracle**

The Book attributes the act to God and the instrument to Moses, and it keeps the two roles distinct throughout its account of the exodus. Moses prays; God answers; Moses strikes; the rock produces. Nothing in the sequence gives the prophet a power independent of the command he received, and nothing in it reduces him to a spectator. He is the man through whom the provision arrives, and the provision is not his.

The Book applies the same pattern to every messenger it describes, and it states the principle in the sūrah of the Cave when it has Moses himself explain that his acts are performed only by the permission of God. What the reader takes from the arrangement is a definition of prophethood as service rather than as authority over the created order.

**The Instruction That Accompanies the Gift**

The provision is followed by two commands, and they form a pair. The people are told to eat and drink from what God has provided, and they are told not to spread corruption in the land. The first names the gift, the second names the conduct that would spoil it, and the Book places them in one sentence because it treats the two as related. A community living on a daily provision has no economic reason to despoil its surroundings.

The command not to spread corruption recurs throughout the Book's account of this people, and the sūrah has already used the same term for the activity of the hypocrites earlier in the chapter. The instruction here is therefore addressed to a community in possession of everything it needs, and it warns against the one kind of conduct that would turn a gift into a source of damage.

**A Wilderness Supplied for Forty Years**

The Book's account of this period is a list of provisions: shade, manna, quails, water from a rock. None of it is produced by the community, and all of it arrives on a schedule the community does not control. What the people are asked to do in return is to keep within limits and to avoid wrecking what has been given, which is the same requirement that was placed on Adam in the garden.

Read with the sūrah's other narratives, the sequence describes a people being taught the arrangement of a dependent life. The generation that received these gifts was also the generation that failed at the gate and demanded to see God, and the Book records the provisions and the failures in the same passage without giving either of them priority. Both are evidence, and the address to their descendants rests on the whole of it.

**Water as Evidence in the Book's Argument**

The Book returns to water as a sign in a great many places, and it consistently presents it as a gift whose source is beyond human control. The verse in this passage is unusual in the specificity of its arrangements, but its argument is the one the sūrah made earlier when it described the sky sending down rain to produce fruit. What is at stake is not the miraculous character of the events but the identity of the one who arranged them.

That is why the command that accompanies the provision begins with eating and drinking rather than with believing. The Book's invitations to gratitude are usually attached to ordinary actions, and it treats the consumption of a provided meal as an acknowledgement of the provider. The community in the desert was given that acknowledgement as a daily practice, and the instruction not to corrupt the land was given in the same breath.
