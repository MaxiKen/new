## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:60

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, “Strike the rock with your staff.” Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place. ˹We then said,˺ “Eat and drink of Allah’s provisions, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prophet's Prayer for Water**

The verse opens with need rather than command: Moses sought water for his people — *istisqā*, the seeking of drink, a prophet reduced to the desert's one question. The Qur'an states the prayer and moves immediately to the answer, and Sūrah al-Aʿrāf files the same sequence with the same economy: *"We revealed to Moses, when his people asked for water, 'Strike the rock with your staff.'"* (7:160). The form of the scene became Islamic practice: the prayer for rain, ṣalāt al-istisqāʾ, established in the Prophet's ﷺ sunnah and performed to this day when the rains fail — a community asking its Lord for water through its imam, in the grammar this verse first wrote down. The detail worth holding is the direction of the asking. The people's thirst is real; the prophet does not lecture them about it but carries it upward. The recital elsewhere shows this community's needs expressed as complaint (the food fatigue that follows in the file); here need is expressed as intercession, and the answer comes through the intercessor's channel. The verse thus distinguishes, without naming the distinction, two ways of being needy before Allah — the way that asks Him through His appointed, and the way that demands of Him as a customer — and grants the first without a word of rebuke.

**The Staff Again**

The answer is an instruction addressed to a tool: strike the rock with your staff. The staff is by now the recital's standing instrument. At the sea the same command produced the same grammar — *"Strike the sea with your staff," and the sea was split, each part was like a huge mountain* (26:63) — and the two filings together establish the pattern of prophetic miracle in the Qur'an: the extraordinary act is routed through an ordinary object wielded by an obedient hand. Nothing in the staff parts seas or opens stone; the verse's theology is that divine power does not bypass means but honors them, so that the means stay visible and the power stays unmistakable. The staff's own history deepens the choice: it was Moses's shepherd's stick — the one he leaned on and beat down leaves with for his flock, as he told the One who spoke to him at Ṭuwā (20:18) — and it became the sign-object of his mission. A herdsman's tool, retained through prophethood, is now the instrument of a nation's water. The Qur'an keeps selecting for its signs the objects people already carry, which is its standing answer to demands for spectacle: the miracle is not the absence of ordinary things but what obedience does with them.

**Twelve Springs for Twelve Tribes**

The struck rock yields structure, not just water: twelve springs gush out, and each tribe knows its drinking place. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf supplies the correspondence explicitly — *"We divided them into twelve tribes— each as a community"* (7:160) — so the water-system mirrors the camp-system, spring for tribe, and the miracle includes its own administration. The commentators elaborate with reports of a portable stone — carried through the wilderness, struck on each of four faces, three streams from each face, twelve in total — and with the reading that each tribe's spring was assigned by lot or by marking; the reports are tradition's furniture rather than the verse's content, and the verse's own point stands without them: grace arrived organized. A camp of twelve tribes at one water source is a riot; a camp of twelve tribes with twelve springs is a settlement. The detail answers the desert's second scarcity after food — not water alone but water without conflict — and it prefigures the verse's closing prohibition, since competition at wells is where corruption in the land typically begins. Some exegetes add that each tribe knew its place because the springs were marked to them or because each stream followed its tribe's banner; whatever the mechanism, the stated outcome is a provision that arrives with peace built into its plumbing.

**The Rock in Two Scriptures**

The scene has a biblical twin, and the comparison is instructive in what each scripture emphasizes. Exodus 17 places the striking at Horeb, with the place named Massah and Meribah — testing and quarreling — because the people quarreled and tested the Lord there, asking whether He was among them or not. The Qur'anic filing removes the quarrel-frame entirely: the people ask, Moses intercedes, the water comes. What the Qur'an adds is the twelve-springs correspondence and — decisively — the speech attached to the water: eat and drink, and do not corrupt. The two testaments thus remember the same rock with different centers of gravity: one preserves the scene as a story about Israel's testing of Allah, the other as a story about Allah's provisioning and its ethics. Neither emphasis is absent from either text's wider corpus, but the Qur'an's version is the one this recital needs, because the recital is building a case about how provision is handled. The naming difference does real work: a scene remembered as Meribah teaches the danger of testing God; the same scene remembered as twelve springs teaches the responsibility that rides on organized grace. The Qur'an files the second and lets the wilderness's later verses — the complaint, the exchange, the refusal — supply the testing.

**Eat and Drink, and Do Not Corrupt**

The speech the water arrives with is the verse's law in miniature: *eat and drink of Allah's provisions, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land*. The first half is permission with a possessive attached — the provisions are Allah's, and the eating is of them, a formulation that keeps the Provider inside every meal the way verse 57's ṭayyibāt-command did. The second half converts the meal into a covenant term. *Corruption in the land* — fasād — is the Qur'an's name for the whole family of public wrongs: injustice, violence, fraud, the breaking of the social order through which provision flows. Joined to eat-and-drink in a single sentence, the prohibition states the ethics of receiving: grace licenses enjoyment and forbids predation, and the two halves bind each other — the people who eat Allah's provision are the people who may not corrupt Allah's land. The wilderness setting makes the join concrete. A camp fed by manna and watered by twelve springs has no economy of its own to exploit; its only possible corruption is against each other and against the terms of the gift. The verse thereby universalizes while it particularizes: every community living on provision it did not produce — which is every community — receives here its two-clause constitution. Take what is given, thankfully; do not turn the given land into a corrupted one. The spring-fed camp is the model polity of the recital, legislated in a sentence spoken over water.

**The Rock and the Heart**

The verse carries a second reading the sūrah itself authorizes. Fourteen verses later the same sūrah compares this community's hearts to stone — and to stone that waters: *"Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah"* (2:74). The comparison presumes exactly what verse 60 staged: rock is not barrenness in the Qur'an's mineralogy but potential — the hard thing that, struck in obedience, pours. The Sufi commentators drew the inference explicitly: Ibn ʿAjībah reads the stone as the heart, the staff as spiritual resolve (*himmah*), and the striking as the discipline that breaks the ego's hardness so wisdom and unveiling flow — twelve streams for the twelve faculties of a restored soul. The esoteric reading is legitimate precisely because verse 74 installs the rock-heart equation in the text; the historical event remains the verse's primary sense, and the symbolic reading rides on it rather than replacing it. Both registers converge on one claim: the water came from the hardest thing present, at a stroke performed in obedience, through a stick that was only a stick. The camp drank from a rock that day, and the recital keeps the scene on file for every later reader whose own hardness is waiting for the same command — strike, and what was stone will water twelve directions.
