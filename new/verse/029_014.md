## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14

> Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Thousand Years, Less Fifty**

Nine hundred and fifty years separate the sending of Noah from the coming of the flood, and the Book states the figure where a general phrase would have carried the same warning. Numbers of this kind are meant to be weighed rather than passed over. No other preacher in the Qur'an is given a duration, and none is given one of this size. The reader is being told that the interval between the call and the judgment can outlast every human expectation of what an interval should be, and that people who live inside such a stretch of time will be tempted to conclude that no judgment is coming at all. Noah's patience is measured by the same arithmetic: he spent close to ten centuries repeating a message to audiences that had heard it before. The Book reports the expenditure without a word of complaint, and that silence is part of the lesson about what faithfulness looks like when the road never ends.

**The Mission That Outlasted Generations**

The Qur'an supplies the content of those centuries in Sūrah Nūḥ, where the prophet reports his own methods to his Lord: he called his people day and night (71:5), he preached to them publicly and privately (71:9), and each time he invited them to seek forgiveness they pressed their fingers into their ears, wrapped themselves in their garments, persisted in denial, and behaved with arrogance (71:7). Three modes of address, three rejections. Elsewhere the Book gives the results in a single line: none of your people will believe except those who have already believed (11:36). The record is important because it removes a comfortable assumption from the mind of anyone who measures a mission by its returns. By that standard Noah failed for nine and a half centuries. The Book treats him as one of the greatest of the messengers, which tells the reader what the actual standard is: delivery, endurance, and refusal to abandon the task.

**The Verdict Given Before the Water Came**

Before the flood, Noah receives an instruction that few preachers ever receive: stop grieving over what they are doing, because no one else among them is going to believe (11:36). The sentence would be crushing to a man who had been working toward a harvest, and liberating to a man who had been asked to keep planting. It also marks a boundary in the story. Until that moment the mission was open and the warning was live; after it, the matter was settled, and the remaining task was the construction of the vessel. The Book tells the reader what it had told Abraham, and what it will tell Muḥammad in the last sūrah of this chapter's world: the messenger's duty is to deliver the message clearly, and the outcome belongs to God. Responsibility and results are separated in the Qur'an, and Noah is the man who proves the separation holds over centuries.

**Overtaken While Still Wrongdoing**

The sentence ends with a description of the state in which the water found them. The Book does not say the flood came after they changed their minds or that it interrupted a period of reflection. It came on them while they were doing the thing they had been warned about. This is the pattern the chapter has already laid down: the wrongdoer's confidence is built on the assumption that he will have time to arrange matters, and the Book answers him with a term that arrives without negotiation. Sūrah al-Qamar adds the mechanism, describing the gates of the sky opened with pouring rain (54:11) while the earth's springs gushed, so that the two waters met by a decree already issued. There is no weather in the account and no accident. What drowned a civilization was a decision that had been announced for centuries to the people it drowned.

**Why the Longest Trial Stands in This Sūrah**

The chapter is built on the claim that faith is tested and that the test can be expensive, and it now supplies the most extreme case in the Book. A community that endured under persecution for years could measure itself against Noah and find its own patience small. That is the point of placing him here, between the account of the wavering believer and the account of Abraham. The sūrah's argument runs from the daily test to the exceptional one, and it shows that the same rule governs both: the mission is to hold the line and to keep delivering the message, and the schedule is not disclosed. What Noah's case proves is that duration is not evidence of failure, since the longest held position in the Book ends in a rescue rather than in a conclusion anyone would have chosen for themselves.

**A Time-Scale That Belongs to God**

The figure of nine hundred and fifty years sits oddly against the human units of the surrounding verses, and the Book elsewhere warns its readers not to measure divine patience by the clock they carry: a day with your Lord is like a thousand years by your counting (22:47). The sentence about Noah's tenure is therefore as much a lesson about time as about endurance. A community that finds a decade of difficulty unbearable is shown one that lasted for nearly a hundred of them, and the comparison is not offered to shame the reader but to correct his arithmetic. The Book repeatedly places human frames of reference beside a schedule that is not human, and the story of the flood is where the difference is measured in generations.

**The Prayer That Ended the Reprieve**

Sūrah Nūḥ closes with a petition that marks the end of the opening. Noah asks his Lord not to leave a single disbeliever on the face of the earth, then gives the reason: if they are spared, they will mislead His servants and produce only wicked offspring and hardened deniers (71:26–27). The prayer is severe, and it comes from a man who had watched nine centuries of persuasion fail. What it shows is that the end of a period of respite is not an arbitrary act of power; the record was complete, and the request came from the person best placed to read it. The Book allows its prophets to reach that point but never before it, and it lets the reader see the full ledger first. The flood is presented as an answer to a question that had been asked for a very long time.
