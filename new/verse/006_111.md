## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:111

> Even if We had sent them the angels, made the dead speak to them, and assembled before their own eyes every sign ˹they demanded˺, they still would not have believed— unless Allah so willed. But most of them are ignorant ˹of this˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Three Signs Named in the Verse**

Even if We had sent them the angels, made the dead speak to them, and assembled before their own eyes every sign they demanded. The sentence names the strongest evidence a person could imagine and treats it as insufficient. The three items are the angels coming in visible form, the dead rising to speak, and every remaining sign gathered together and set before the audience, and the verse states that even this would not produce belief.

The Book has described the audience's demand for exactly these kinds of evidence elsewhere. The sūrah of the Night Journey records their asking the Prophet to bring God and the angels before them or to send down a book they could read, and the sūrah of the Discernment records the demand that a treasure be sent down with him or that angels accompany him. The verse in front of us takes the demands at their full strength and states their result.

**The Refusal That Would Remain**

They still would not have believed unless Allah so willed. The exception clause keeps the statement from becoming an absolute claim about human nature, and it aligns the verse with the rest of the sūrah's teaching about God's will. Belief is possible when God wills it, and the people described here were not among those for whom that will had been exercised.

The Book states the same exception in other places where belief is attributed to the will of God. The sūrah of Yūnus says that no soul can believe except by God's permission (10:100), and the sūrah of the Bee says that God guides whom He wills and that the Prophet's task is to convey the message. The verse here applies the principle to an audience that had seen enough evidence to believe and had refused it.

**The Ignorance of Most of Them**

But most of them are ignorant of this. The closing sentence explains why the demand for signs continued despite the answer the audience had received. The verse's claim is that the crowd did not understand what the signs were for: they treated the sign as a lever that would settle the matter, while the Book describes it as a gift that a willing heart receives and a reluctant heart resists.

The sūrah has used the charge of ignorance throughout its treatment of the audience. It said that they followed nothing but conjecture and that they spoke about God what they did not know; it described the attribution of children to God as a claim made out of ignorance (6:100); and it described the deniers as unable to perceive what was put before them (6:110). The sentence that closes this verse belongs to that pattern.

**The Reports About the Request and the Refusal**

The reports about the demand for a sign include the audience's request that a hill near the sanctuary be turned to gold, and they describe the Prophet beginning to supplicate for it before being told that such signs are followed by punishment when they are rejected. The verse's three items belong to the same family of demands, and the Book's answer is that they would have made no difference.

The Book's explanation for that judgement lies in its account of the audience's inner condition. The sūrah has stated that their hearts were turned away after their first refusal (6:110), and this verse supplies the consequence in terms of evidence. A person whose heart has been left to wander will interpret any sign he is shown in the terms of his existing position, and the Book describes that condition plainly.

**The Pattern in the Earlier Communities**

The Book states the same judgement in the sūrah of the Night Journey, where God says that nothing keeps Him from sending the demanded signs except that the earlier peoples had already denied them, and where the she-camel given to Thamūd is named as an example (17:59). The verse establishes both halves of the principle the sūrah is stating here: signs are withheld in mercy, and the communities that received them and refused were destroyed.

The Book applies that precedent to the audience's demands at several points, and it consistently treats the request for a sign as a claim on God that He answers as He chooses. The sūrah of Yūnus says that no soul believes except by God's permission and that His wrath falls on those who are unmindful (10:100), which is the same arrangement the verse in front of us describes when it makes belief contingent on God's will.

**What the Verse Says About Evidence and Belief**

The verse does not belittle evidence; it states its limits when it is offered to people who have already refused. The sūrah has spent its first hundred verses presenting evidence of every kind, and the created order itself has been laid out as a demonstration. What this verse adds is the recognition that evidence can be resisted, and that the resistance belongs to the will rather than to the intellect.

That recognition is the foundation of the Book's treatment of the earlier communities as well. The sūrah of the Cattle records the fate of the communities who saw the signs and did not believe, and it presents their destruction as the consequence of a decision they had made rather than of a deficiency in the evidence. The verse in front of us states the principle before the sūrah turns to the enemies that every prophet has had to face.

**The Transition to the Prophets' Enemies**

The next verse announces that God has appointed for every prophet enemies among the devilish of humans and jinn, who whisper to one another with elegant words of deception (6:112). The transition is natural: having described an audience that would not be moved by any sign, the sūrah turns to the mechanism by which such refusal is sustained and spread.

The two passages belong to the sūrah's larger purpose of preparing the messenger for the opposition he faced. The signs have been presented, the refusal has been explained, and the sūrah now describes the company of opponents and the instructions the Prophet receives about them. What follows is one of the sūrah's most striking accounts of how the message's enemies work against it.
