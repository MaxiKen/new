## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:79

> I have turned my face towards the One Who has originated the heavens and the earth—being upright—and I am not one of the polytheists.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Act of Turning**

I have turned my face towards the One Who has originated the heavens and the earth. The sentence is built on the image of a person who changes direction, and the Book uses the same verb for the act of turning to God in repentance and for the turning of a face away from what is false. Abraham's declaration begins with a movement rather than with a proposition, and the movement is the whole of his religious position in a single image.

The word for the One he turns to describes the originator, the one who brings a thing into being for the first time, and the sūrah has used it of God twice already in similar declarations. The Prophet is commanded to say that he will not take any guardian other than God, the Originator of the heavens and the earth, who provides for all and needs no provision (6:14), and the sūrah later uses the same title in the passage about God's having no offspring (6:101). The term anchors Abraham's declaration in the sūrah's account of creation.

**The Meaning of Upright**

The word the translation renders as being upright is the Book's technical term for the faith of Abraham, and the commentators explained it as a person who turns away from every deviation toward the straight path. Its root carries the sense of inclining and of deviation, and the term as the Book uses it describes a person whose whole orientation is set on one point rather than distributed among several.

The Book applies the description to Abraham in a series of passages that read like a refrain. Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he submitted in all uprightness and was not a polytheist (3:67); the believers are told that the faith of Abraham the upright, who was not a polytheist, is the answer to the claims of the People of the Book (2:135); and the Book describes him as a model of excellence, devoted to God, perfectly upright, and not a polytheist (16:120). The recurrence of the same sentence across sūrahs shows how much work the Book assigns to it.

**Affirmation and Denial Together**

And I am not one of the polytheists. The Book never allows the affirmation of God's oneness to stand alone, and Abraham's declaration is the model of that practice: he states what he has turned toward and what he has left behind in the same breath. The denial is not an afterthought, since the audience's error lay precisely in combining the acknowledgment of a creator with the worship of other beings.

That combination is the practice the sūrah has been dismantling all along, and it is the form of religion the Makkans actually held. They admitted that God made the heavens and the earth, and they still directed their devotion to the objects of the shrine. Abraham's sentence is the correction of that arrangement, stated by the man whose descendants they claimed to be.

**The Prophet and the Faith of Abraham**

The Book commands the Prophet to follow the same way, and it does so in the language of this verse. He is told that his Lord has guided him to a straight path, a perfect way, the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists (6:161); the Book commands him to follow the Way of Abraham the upright (3:95; 16:123); and it says that God revealed this instruction to him. The sūrah's Abraham section is therefore not only an argument against the Makkans but a statement about the identity of the community being formed.

The connection runs through the House as well. The Book records Abraham raising the foundations of the House with Ishmael and praying that it be accepted (2:127), and it recalls God assigning to him the site of the House with the instruction not to associate anything with God in worship and to purify it for those who circle it and bow and prostrate (22:26). The religion being restored was the one Abraham had practised at the same place, which is why the declaration in this verse matters for the city the sūrah is addressing.

**What the Declaration Cost**

The Book records what Abraham's people did with his religion in the sūrah of the Prophets, where he smashed their idols and left the greatest of them standing so that the people might turn to it for answers (21:58), and where his community concluded that they should burn him to avenge their gods (21:68). The sūrah of the Ranged records the same verdict in the words of the crowd that called for a furnace for him (37:97). The declaration of belief is followed in the Book's narrative by a trial of fire.

That sequence gives the sūrah's audience a frame for the verses they had just heard about the Prophet. He had been told to declare that he did not worship what they invoked (6:56), and the section that follows shows what such a declaration had meant for the man whose tradition they claimed. The Book is not presenting Abraham's story as a distant legend; it is presenting a precedent.

**The Sentence That Ends the Search**

The declaration ends the search that began with the star. Abraham had moved through the visible world and found nothing to rest on, and the verse states what he found instead: the One who originated the heavens and the earth, whom he faces with a settled direction, and whom he refuses to associate with any created thing.

The sūrah's next verses record his people arguing with him, and they record his answer, which is not a defence of himself but a statement of what his Lord has given him. The demonstration concludes with the Book's own verdict: this was the argument given to Abraham against his people (6:83).
