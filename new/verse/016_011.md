## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:11

> **With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Water To Crops To Fruits**

Yunbitu lakum bihi al-zarʿa wa-l-zaytūna wa-l-nakhīla wa-l-aʿnāba wa-min kulli al-thamarāt — with it He produces for you crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and from every fruit. The pronoun bihi refers back to the water in verse 10. One water, many outputs. Zarʿ is general crops, grains like wheat and barley that are staple food. Zaytūn, olives, provide oil, food, and light. Nakhīl, palm trees, provide dates, shade, building material, and fiber. Aʿnāb, grapevines, provide fruit, drink, and raisins. Then the summary wa-min kulli al-thamarāt — and from every type of fruit — widens beyond the four named to include all fruits known and unknown. Al-Ṭabarī notes that these four are named because they were the most valuable and familiar in the Hijaz and Syria, representing both staple and luxury, both food and trade goods. The order moves from general necessity (crops) to specific valuable trees that require years to grow and thus show long-term planning. Olives and palms live decades, even centuries; their inclusion shows that the provision is not only for today but for generations.

**One Water, Many Tastes And Colors**

The same water that you drink and that grows pasture now grows crops that differ in taste, color, smell, and use. Al-Rāzī makes this a central proof of divine wisdom: one water, one earth, yet outputs are vastly diverse. The soil is one, the water is one, the sun is one, yet from them come sweet dates and sour grapes, bitter olives and bland grains, all from the same source. If nature alone acted without a Wise Maker, why would one cause produce many effects that are perfectly suited to human need? The Qur'an asks this elsewhere: *And in the earth are neighboring tracts, and gardens of vines, and crops, and palm trees sharing one root and others with single roots, watered with one water, yet We make some excel others in taste* (13:4). The diversity is not chaos; it is organized diversity that serves human life. Olives give oil for cooking and lighting, dates give concentrated energy that preserves without refrigeration, grapes give quick sugar and can be dried. Each fruit has a role. The phrase min kulli al-thamarāt emphasizes completeness: not just some fruits, but from every kind, indicating abundance that cannot be exhausted.

**A Sign For Those Who Reflect**

Inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yatafakkarūn — surely in this is a sign for those who reflect. This seal appears several times in al-Naḥl, each time with a slightly different audience: those who reflect, those who understand, those who are mindful. Here it is yatafakkarūn, those who think deeply, from f-k-r, to reflect, to consider. Al-Zamakhsharī explains that tafakkur is thinking that turns over a matter, examining it from different sides, not passing thought. The sign in this verse is not only that crops grow, but that they grow from one water into many forms that meet human needs, and that this happens regularly, dependably, yet remains beyond human power to replicate. A person who reflects will ask: who designed this system? Who made water fall from sky, earth absorb it, seeds sprout, trees bear fruit year after year? The answer, given the previous huwa al-ladhī formula, is Allah alone. The seal therefore turns a description of agriculture into a call to reflection. The farmer who plants and harvests is invited to see his field as a book, not only as a source of income.

**Why Olives, Palms, And Grapevines Are Singled Out**

These three trees have special status in Qur'anic imagery. Zaytūn is mentioned in Sūrat al-Nūr as the blessed olive tree whose oil gives light: *lit from a blessed olive tree* (24:35). It was central to Mediterranean economy, providing oil for food, lamp, soap, and medicine. Nakhīl, palms, are called *like clustered columns* and are praised for their resilience in desert, providing shade where no other tree grows. Dates were staple food that could be stored for months, crucial for travel. Aʿnāb, grapevines, were the fruit of highlands and gardens, requiring trellising and care, symbolizing cultivation and settlement. By naming these three, the Qur'an covers different ecologies: olives for hills, palms for desert oases, grapes for valleys and terraces. It shows that Allah's provision adapts to different lands and climates, not only one. Al-Qurṭubī adds that these three are also mentioned together in 6:99 and 13:4 as examples of diverse fruits from one water, indicating they were recognized as a set representing agricultural diversity.

**The Connection To Gratitude And Ingratitude**

In the context of Sūrat al-Niʿam, this verse deepens the argument against ingratitude. If Allah produces for you crops, olives, palms, grapes, and every fruit, then to attribute this to idols or to one's own effort alone is to cover the favor, which is the literal meaning of kufr. The verse says lakum — for you — twice in verses 10-11, stressing that the production is for human benefit. The appropriate response is shukr, gratitude, which in the Qur'an includes acknowledging the Giver, using the gift in obedience, and not giving the gift's credit to others. The seal li-qawmin yatafakkarūn suggests that ingratitude is a failure of reflection. Those who do not reflect see only crops, not the One who grew them. Those who reflect see through crops to the Creator. The sūrah will later say *If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never number them* (16:18), and this verse gives a taste of why counting fails: even from one water, the types of fruit are too many to list.

**Translation And Linguistic Note**

The translators add ˹various˺ in brackets before crops to reflect that al-zarʿ is generic and includes many kinds, not a single crop. The Arabic lacks a word for various but the indefinite form and context imply variety. Yunbitu is from n-b-t, to cause to grow, to sprout, in form IV indicating causation: He causes to grow, not merely grows. This causative form appears throughout the agricultural passages to stress that growth is not automatic; it is caused by Allah. Thamarāt is plural of thamarah, fruit in general, including fruits of trees and fruits of effort. The phrase min kulli al-thamarāt does not mean every single fruit exists in every land, but that from every type of fruit there are examples produced by this water somewhere, showing comprehensive provision. The verse's rhythm — short list of four items then broad summary — mirrors the experience of walking through a garden: you notice specific trees, then you realize the variety is endless.
