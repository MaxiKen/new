## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:95

> Indeed, Allah is the One Who causes seeds and fruit stones to sprout. He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. That is Allah! How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Splitting of the Seed**

Indeed, Allah is the One Who causes seeds and fruit stones to sprout. The verse opens a series of signs drawn from the created order, and it begins with the smallest and most ordinary of them. A seed is buried, splits, and sends out a living shoot; a fruit stone does the same, and the process repeats itself in every field and orchard the audience walked past. The Book treats that daily event as one of the plainest arguments available for the power of God.

The choice of the image connects directly to the sentence that follows it. What happens when a seed splits is life emerging from what appears to be dead matter, and the Book uses that observation to make its case about resurrection. The sūrah of Yā-Sīn puts the two together when it says that there is a sign in the dead earth: God gives it life and produces grain from it for people to eat (36:33).

**The Name That Comes From Splitting**

Commentators noticed that the Arabic term for the One who splits the seed shares its root with the divine title the Originator, which the Book uses for God in the phrase Originator of the heavens and the earth. The title appears in the sūrahs of Ibrāhīm, Fāṭir, the Troops, Consultation, and elsewhere, and the sūrah used it in the declaration Abraham makes when he turns his face toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth (6:79).

That connection gives the image its theological weight. Creation in the Book's language is not assembly from existing parts but an originating, and the splitting of a seed is the visible form of the same power at work. The sūrah's audience, who watched the fields around their city come to life every season, was being shown the operation of the creating command in the ordinary course of nature.

**Bringing the Living From the Dead**

He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. The sentence states the cycle in both directions, and the Book repeats it in several sūrahs. The sūrah of the Family of Imran uses it in a prayer that addresses God as the one who causes the night to pass into the day and brings the living from the dead (3:27); the sūrah of Yūnus includes it in a list of questions the idolaters answer by naming God (10:31); and the sūrah of the Romans states it and then says that this is how people will be brought forth from the grave (30:19).

The commentators explained the two directions in several ways. The living coming from the dead covers the creation of a human being and the raising of the dead; the dead coming from the living covers death itself and the natural cycle by which a living plant produces a seed that must be buried before it grows. The two divine names that the theologians attach to these acts, the Giver of Life and the Bringer of Death, name the same pair of operations.

**The Argument From Ordinary Observation**

The strength of the verse's argument lies in its ordinariness. The audience did not need to travel or to be shown anything they had not seen. They lived from agriculture and from the produce of the soil, and every harvest repeated the demonstration that God brings life out of death. The Book makes the same point about rain when it says that God sends the winds and drives the clouds to a dead land so that the earth revives, and then states that this is how He will bring the dead to life (7:57).

The Book uses this form of argument repeatedly because it does not depend on accepting the messenger's authority. Long before the question of revelation is raised, the audience had watched the process they were being asked to accept as a sign, and the sūrah expects them to draw the conclusion from what they already knew.

**The Conclusion Drawn**

That is Allah! How can you then be deluded? The sentence states the conclusion and then asks how the audience could have missed it, and the Book's word for delusion describes being turned away from a clear path. The question is not an expression of surprise at the audience's ignorance; it is a statement about the evidence available to them.

The sūrah has been asking this form of question throughout. It asked how those who had been rescued at sea could then associate others with God (6:64), and it asked how the people who argued with Abraham could fail to be mindful of the One who encompasses everything in knowledge (6:80). The question here closes the first of a series of signs, and the same structure returns in the verses that follow.

**The Sequence the Sūrah Is Building**

The verses that follow continue with the dawn, the night, the sun, the moon, and the stars, and they form a catalogue of the created order similar to the arguments the sūrah has used against the Makkans throughout. What distinguishes this passage is its position in the sūrah: it comes directly after the description of the wrongdoers arriving before God with nothing, and it functions as the answer to the isolation described there.

The people who were stripped of their intercessors and their ties are the people who lived in a world full of signs of the power they had ignored. The sūrah does not leave that contrast implicit. It names the signs one by one, and it ends the series with a statement about how clearly they have been made for those who know.
