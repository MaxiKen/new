## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:14

> Also, they have a charge against me, so I fear they may kill me.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses Names a Real Political Danger**

The final concern is not imagined embarrassment. Moses knows that a charge lies against him and fears that Pharaoh's people may kill him. The charge refers to the Egyptian who died when Moses intervened in a fight involving an Israelite, an event recounted in 28:15–21. The memory has a legal and political life beyond the original moment. A ruler who wants to silence a messenger can turn an old incident into a reason for execution.

Moses therefore understands the mission at two levels. He may be rejected as a messenger, and he may be attacked as a man with a vulnerable past. Tyranny often combines those tactics. It questions the speaker's character so that the message need not be answered, then uses the accusation to justify force. By mentioning the charge before he goes, Moses shows that he is not confusing faith with a denial of danger.

**A Wrong Act and a Prophetic Mission Must Be Distinguished**

Moses does not present the Egyptian's death as irrelevant. His fear acknowledges that the event has moral and legal weight. Yet a past act does not automatically make every later word false. The question is how the act is understood, repented of, and judged, not whether a servant must remain silent forever after having been in need of forgiveness.

This distinction matters whenever a ruler uses a person's history to avoid the truth of a present claim. Accountability should not be suspended, but neither should accusation become a substitute for examination. Moses will have to answer for what happened while also carrying God's command. The verse allows both facts to stand. A messenger can have a past that requires mercy and still be chosen for a task that confronts injustice.

**Fear of Death Is Not Lack of Faith**

Moses says he fears being killed. The Qur'an records the fear without treating it as cowardice. Human life has value, and a servant may recognize the danger of losing it. Faith does not demand that a person feel no attachment to life or no concern about violent power. It asks him to place that concern before God and to obey when the duty is clear.

The distinction between fear and surrender is important. Fear describes an emotion and an assessment of what may happen. Surrender would be abandoning the command because the danger is unpleasant. Moses has not yet refused to go. He is explaining why he needs support and protection. His prayer shows that courage can consist in moving forward while danger remains visible rather than pretending that danger has ceased to exist.

**An Accusation Can Be Used to Protect Injustice**

A charge against a messenger may be examined fairly, or it may be weaponized by people who benefit from the existing order. Pharaoh's court has an interest in keeping the focus on Moses' past instead of on the enslavement and arrogance that his message will expose. The verse prepares the reader for that shift in argument. A powerful audience may defend itself by putting the speaker on trial before it considers the charge against itself.

The just response is neither blind loyalty to Moses nor automatic trust in Pharaoh. It is moral clarity. The death must be understood in its circumstances, Moses' own acknowledgment must be heard, and Pharaoh's use of the accusation must be judged by what it seeks to conceal. Revelation does not ask the audience to ignore evidence. It asks the audience not to let a real accusation erase a larger truth.

**The Past Follows the Servant Into the Call**

Moses left Egypt because he feared the consequence of the killing. Years later, the mission sends him back toward the same power. The return is not a denial of history; it is the place where history is brought under a new purpose. The man who once fled now approaches with a command from God, still conscious of what happened and still dependent on protection.

This movement teaches that repentance does not always remove every worldly consequence. A forgiven person may still have to face people harmed by his actions or systems that remember them. What changes is the meaning of the encounter and the source of strength with which he meets it. Moses cannot rewrite his past, but he can refuse to let Pharaoh define the rest of his life by it.

**God's Protection Answers the Fear Without Denying It**

The next divine response will answer Moses' concern with reassurance. That answer will not imply that the charge never existed. It will mean that Pharaoh's threat cannot prevent the mission God has ordained. Divine protection often works by placing a servant inside a danger without allowing the danger to determine the final outcome.

Moses' prayer thus models a sound way to speak before God. Name the risk accurately. Do not hide a moral fault or inflate it into a reason for permanent silence. Ask for the protection and assistance that obedience requires. The servant who speaks this way is neither self-justifying nor paralyzed. He is ready to meet a powerful accuser under the judgment of the Lord who knows the whole event.

**A Servant May Need Protection While Telling the Truth**

The existence of a past charge does not make physical safety irrelevant. Moses asks for help because a righteous task can still expose a servant to an unjust response. Some people treat concern for safety as proof that conviction is weak; this verse rejects that measure. Care for life can coexist with readiness to obey, especially when the danger comes from a ruler who can turn law into an instrument of fear.

Protection, however, is not the same as arranging a life with no risk. Moses will have to return to the place from which he fled. The difference is that he will not return on his own terms or in his own strength. He goes under a divine commission, with a companion, and with signs. Courage receives its shape from these means rather than from a denial that death is possible.
