## Sūrah an-Naml 27:6

> And indeed, you ˹O Prophet˺ are receiving the Quran from the One ˹Who is˺ All-Wise, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Turns to One Man**

After five verses about groups — believers, deniers, losers — the sentence swings round to a single person. The Arabic verb is second-person singular, and the emphatic particle *inna* with the pronoun attached makes it insistent: you, specifically, are the one receiving this. Everything said so far about the Book has been in the third person. Now the Book's recipient is addressed directly and told where his material comes from.

The verb *tulaqqā* is worth pausing on, because it is passive. Muḥammad is not described as composing, gathering or even seeking the Qur'an. He is described as being met with it, having it handed to him. Arabic could have said he recites it or he brings it; the form chosen removes him from the position of author entirely. This matters because the accusation in circulation was exactly the opposite — that a man in Mecca was producing these verses himself, or having them produced for him. The grammar of the verse denies the charge before any argument is offered, simply by putting the Prophet in the receiving position and someone else in the giving one.

**Wise and Knowing: Two Names Chosen for This Sentence**

God is not named here by His usual titles. He is not called the Merciful, the Mighty or the Creator. He is identified by two attributes that bear directly on the question of where a text comes from: *ḥakīm*, wise, and *ʿalīm*, knowing. Knowledge concerns content — what is true, what happened, what will happen. Wisdom concerns arrangement — what to say, to whom, in what order, and when to stay silent.

Those are precisely the two capacities an accusation of forgery must deny. A forged book would fail on knowledge, since its author could only report what he had access to, and it would fail on wisdom, since its author could only arrange for effects he could foresee. Naming the source as both All-Wise and All-Knowing answers both failures at once. The pairing also anticipates the chapter's contents. What follows is a set of narratives about hidden knowledge — a fire on a mountainside that turns out to be a summons, a bird carrying news from a kingdom nobody in the story had heard of, a throne moved across a desert in less time than a glance. A chapter about knowledge that exceeds the visible is introduced by naming its author as the One who knows.

**A Hinge Between Two Halves of the Chapter**

Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī both read this verse as a joint: it closes the opening section and opens the narrative one. Everything before it describes the Book; everything after it, from verse 7 to the end, mostly tells stories. Placed exactly at the seam, this verse explains why the stories are being told and on what authority.

The placement solves a real problem for the audience. Mūsā at the fire, Solomon and the ant, the destruction of Thamūd — these are events no Meccan witnessed and most had no reliable record of. A listener could reasonably ask how the man reciting them knows. Verse 6 supplies the answer in advance: he is receiving them from the One who was present and who knows how to tell them. This is not an incidental piece of framing. The Qur'an uses the same device in Sūrah Hūd and Sūrah Yūsuf, attaching a statement about the source of the report to the beginning of a long narrative sequence. The stories are presented as testimony, and testimony requires a witness to be named.

**Receiving Is Continuous, Not Finished**

The verb form here describes an ongoing action rather than a completed one. At the moment this verse was recited, the Qur'an was not a closed book; revelation was still arriving and would continue for years. The Prophet is told that he *is receiving*, present tense, which places him in the middle of a process whose end he cannot see.

That detail carries practical weight for the situation the verse addressed. A man in the middle of receiving something cannot answer every question put to him immediately, cannot produce a sign on demand, and cannot promise what the next installment will contain. The Qur'an elsewhere records the pressure this created and refuses to relieve it. The present tense here quietly defends a prophet who could not control his own supply. It also draws a line under the claim of authorship from a different angle: an author knows what his book will say next. This one did not, and the verse describes him accordingly.

**Hearing the Book Through Its Source**

A strand of later Islamic spirituality reads this verse past its immediate context and applies it to every reciter. The argument runs that if the Qur'an is genuinely *from the presence* of the Wise and Knowing, then reciting it while thinking only of the words on the page misses what the sentence claims. Aḥmad al-ʿAlawī and others in that tradition held that nobody is counted among those firmly grounded in knowledge — the phrase comes from *“As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.””* (3:7) — until he recites the Book as though hearing it from its source rather than reading it from a text.

This is an extension of the verse, not its plain sense, and it should be labelled as such. The verse addresses the Prophet about his own situation. But the extension is a defensible inference rather than an imposition, because it takes the verse's own logic — that the Book's value derives from where it comes from — and asks what follows for the person holding it. What follows is a standard of attention. A message that arrives from the Wise and Knowing deserves to be received with the alertness one brings to words from someone who knows more than we do. The chapter's first narrative, beginning in the next verse, opens with a man who notices something on a dark horizon and goes to investigate.
