## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:26

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please; You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please—all good is in Your Hands. Surely You ˹alone˺ are Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer Commanded, Not Improvised**

The verse opens with the imperative *say*, which places this supplication in the same category as the rest of the revelation: it is taught rather than discovered. The Prophet is not reported here as a man overwhelmed by circumstances who poured out his feelings; he is told what words to use, and those words are addressed to God about power.

This is the Qur'an's way of disciplining political emotion. A community that had been defeated, exiled, and outnumbered could easily produce prayers for revenge or despair. Instead it is given a prayer about God's ownership of authority, which puts the current arrangement of power under a larger fact and leaves the community asking for nothing narrower than what God's own will decides.

**Mālik al-Mulk: the Title and Its Weight**

The address *Lord over all authorities* renders *mālik al-mulk*, and the two words of the phrase are related to each other and to a family of terms: *malik*, a king; *mulk*, dominion or rule; *malakūt*, the inner sovereignty. The title appears at 67:1, where the Book states that all authority rests in His hands, and 25:2 describes Him as the one to whom the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs, who has never had offspring and no partner in governing.

The sūrah states the same claim at its end: *“To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.”* (3:189). Setting that sentence beside this prayer shows how the Book approaches politics. Earthly rule is not ignored or dismissed; it is located inside a larger ownership, so that no throne is described as independent and no dynasty is described as permanent.

**"You Give Authority to Whoever You Please"**

The second clause turns the doctrine into history. Authority is granted and withdrawn by a will that is not accountable to the holders, and the Book illustrates the point with rulers of very different kinds. Saul is appointed over a people who complained that he lacked wealth and lineage, and the appointment is defended on grounds they had not considered: knowledge and stature (2:247). Nimrod argues with Abraham on the strength of his kingship and is silenced by a question about sunrise (2:258). Korah, who trusted in his own power, is swallowed by the earth with no helper left (28:81).

None of these narratives treats the ruler as the author of his own standing. The sūrah's prayer and the Book's histories agree: a crown is a loan with a term attached, and the term belongs to the lender.

**Honour and Disgrace, the Two Companions of Rank**

The prayer pairs honour and disgrace as it paired giving and removing, and the pairing is not a rhetorical flourish. In the culture the Qur'an was addressing, honour was the social currency on which safety, marriage, and livelihood depended, and disgrace was close to civic death. The Book's assertion is that both are conferred.

Earlier in this sūrah the theme of honour is turned inward: those who take disbelievers as allies are asked whether they seek honour in that company, and the answer is that honour belongs to God (4:139). The verse above gives the same truth the form of an address, so that a believer states it to God rather than merely holding it as an opinion. Rank, then, is a moment in a larger administration, and it can be transferred in a night.

**"All Good Is in Your Hands"**

The sentence adds a restriction that is easy to pass over: all *good* is in His hands. No parallel clause says that evil is in them, and the commentators drew conclusions from the omission. Whatever God decrees is good in itself, even when it arrives as loss, and what appears evil in the world is attributable to the acts of creatures or to the limited view of the observer.

The Book supports the restriction with a blunt statement about jurisdiction: *“He cannot be questioned about what He does, but they will ˹all˺ be questioned.”* (21:23). That sentence is the reason the prayer can put the whole of political fortune into God's hands without embarrassment about the human cost. The Book does not explain every event; it locates the authority that governs events beyond the reach of cross-examination.

**"Most Capable of Everything" — the Ground of the Prayer**

The address closes with the attribute *qadīr*, capable of everything, and the sūrah repeats it at 3:29 and 3:189. Its function in this verse is to make the earlier clauses more than a description. It is one thing to say that God owns authority; it is another to say that He can move it, and the closing phrase states the second.

Without that attribute, the prayer would be a statement of resignation. With it, the prayer becomes a request that expects an answer, and the community praying it is told where to direct a petition about its own survival. Nothing in the sentence asks God to be generous to a particular side. It asks Him to do what He already does.

**What This Prayer Teaches a Minority**

The verses that follow will describe the community's real problems: the temptation to take powerful allies who do not share its faith, the danger of hidden disloyalty, and the fear of an accounting that reaches into the heart. The prayer above is placed before those verses for a reason.

A believer who has said out loud that authority, honour, and disgrace are all dispensed by God has already answered the central political question of his own life. He may still be prudent, but he cannot be fundamentally impressed by the people who hold power, and he cannot expect to hold it himself in any way that outlasts the giver's will. That is the frame inside which the sūrah's instructions about loyalty will be given.
