## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:111

> They argued, “How can we believe in you, when you are followed ˹only˺ by the lowest of the low?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chiefs Judge Truth by the Followers**

Noah's opponents ask how they can believe in him when the people following him are, in their view, the lowest of the low. Their objection does not examine the message first. It examines the social position of the believers and treats that position as evidence against Noah. A claim becomes unacceptable to them because humble people have accepted it.

The argument exposes a hierarchy that has become a spiritual idol. The chiefs assume that wealth, rank, and public influence reveal who is worthy of truth. Noah's followers overturn that assumption simply by believing. Their presence shows that faith is not reserved for those who already possess social honor.

**“The Lowest” Is a Social Insult, Not a Moral Verdict**

The phrase lowest of the low expresses the chiefs' judgment. It does not establish that the believers were morally inferior. People can be low in wealth, status, or political influence while being high in sincerity and obedience. Noah's opponents collapse those categories because they are accustomed to measuring people by worldly rank.

This is why the insult matters. It is not a neutral description of poverty. It is an attempt to make the believers' social condition disqualify the message they accepted. The chiefs want Noah to see his followers through their contempt, but faith refuses to let wealth become the measure of a person's worth before God.

**The Question Hides Fear of Losing Privilege**

The chiefs' words sound like a request for evidence, but their standard reveals a fear of social reversal. If the lowly can recognize truth before the powerful, then rank no longer guarantees wisdom. If Noah's community treats the poor as equal worshippers, the chiefs lose the exclusive control that their status has given them.

A demand that the messenger remove humble believers is therefore not merely a request about public order. It is a demand to protect privilege from revelation. The chiefs will believe only if the message is rearranged to flatter their hierarchy. Noah must choose whether to grow the movement through status or preserve its truth through justice.

**Faith Is Not Validated by Elite Approval**

The chiefs ask how they can believe when the poor follow Noah. Their question reverses the proper order of judgment. A message is not true because elites accept it, and it is not false because people without status accept it. Truth must be tested by revelation, reason, and the character of the call rather than by the income or ancestry of its first followers.

This principle does not romanticize poverty or condemn wealth. A wealthy person can believe sincerely, and a poor person can be arrogant. The error lies in turning social position into a gatekeeper for faith. Noah's opponents do that because status is the one form of evidence that protects their own place.

Quran 11:27 records the chiefs of Noah's people saying that only the lowliest follow him and that they see no advantage in the believers. The parallel confirms that the insult is a repeated feature of their rejection, not an accidental phrase. They treat social difference as proof that Noah's message has no divine value.

The parallel also gives Noah's response a wider setting. The issue is not simply whether a few individuals are poor. It is whether a messenger may build a believing community without submitting to the chiefs' class conditions. Revelation refuses that bargain. The door of faith cannot be reserved for the socially powerful.

**A Messenger Must Not Trade Justice for Expansion**

A movement can grow faster if it removes anyone whom influential people dislike. Noah's opponents are testing whether he will make that trade. If he expels the humble, the chiefs may become more willing to listen, but the message will have accepted their standard of human worth.

Noah's integrity requires the opposite. The believers must be judged by their faith and conduct, not by the contempt of people who want them excluded. A messenger cannot ask the poor to leave so that the rich may feel comfortable approaching God. Such a condition would turn revelation into a club for privilege.

The presence of low-status believers is itself a sign. It shows that the call can reach hearts not protected by worldly prestige. The chiefs are being tested by what they cannot tolerate: equality before God. Their discomfort reveals the distance between their public honor and their inner submission.

A person who refuses truth because a disliked group accepts it is allowing resentment to govern belief. He may not be examining the message at all. Noah's opponents must decide whether their concern is truth or the social identity of those who have recognized it. Their question exposes the answer.

**The Objection Confuses Social Visibility With Spiritual Worth**

The chiefs see who follows Noah, but they do not see the full state of those people's hearts. They assume that visible poverty means invisible inferiority. That assumption is precisely what a prophet's call corrects. God knows the servant's sincerity, patience, and repentance; human beings see only the outward conditions by which they rank one another.

This does not mean that outward conduct is irrelevant. A community must protect justice and hold people accountable for actions. It means that worldly status cannot decide who is eligible for guidance. The chiefs' argument fails because it asks the messenger to judge what they themselves have not understood.

**The Question Demands a Clear Moral Answer**

How can we believe in you is answered by the message, not by the social profile of the believers. If Noah is trustworthy and his call is from God, the presence of humble followers cannot make it false. The chiefs are invited to abandon their pride and listen as servants rather than as owners of truth.

Their objection remains a warning wherever people reject a teaching because of the people who accept it. The wise listener separates truth from group rivalry, honors sincere believers regardless of rank, and refuses to make privilege a condition of faith. Noah's community shows that the road to God is open to those whom the world overlooks.
