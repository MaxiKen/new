## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:52

> From this, Joseph should know that I did not speak dishonestly about him in his absence, for Allah certainly does not guide the scheming of the dishonest.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Speaker Is Genuinely Uncertain**

No name and no verb of saying introduce this verse. It follows the wife's confession in verse 51 and precedes a statement the majority assign to Joseph in verse 53, and the commentators divide. One group holds that the speaker is Joseph, continuing the speech he began in verse 50 and explaining why he refused to leave the prison: so that his master would know he had not betrayed him in secret. Another holds that the speaker is still the ʿAzīz's wife, completing her confession by adding that Joseph will now know she has told the truth about him behind his back. Both readings are grammatically sound and both are supported by the sequence. The Qur'an's silence about the speaker is the reason the dispute exists, and the dispute itself is worth preserving, because the two readings produce two different lessons from the same words.

**If the Woman Speaks, the Confession Is Completed**

On that reading the sentence is the last of her three statements and the most disinterested. She has admitted the attempt and named Joseph as truthful; she now adds that the purpose of the admission is that he should know she did not betray him in his absence. The phrase *bi-l-ghayb*, in the unseen or in absence, carries the force of behind his back. Years earlier she had done precisely the opposite: she described him to her husband as an aggressor while the truth was the reverse, and she did it to his face and behind his back alike. Her final recorded act is to reverse that account voluntarily, in a forum where the reversal cannot be rewarded. The moral of the verse on this reading is that a lie can be undone by a truthful statement made at cost.

**If Joseph Speaks, the Verse Explains the Refusal in Verse 50**

On the majority reading the sentence answers an objection the reader might have raised. Joseph declined release until an enquiry was held, and the enquiry could look like an act of vengeance against a household that had once sheltered him. His explanation removes that appearance: the demand was made so that the ʿAzīz — the man who gave him honourable lodging in verse 21 — would know that his guest had not betrayed him in secret. The object of the investigation is therefore not the woman's punishment but the husband's information. Nothing in the chapter suggests Joseph sought to damage the household that owned him; what he sought was that the head of it should not go on believing a falsehood about a person he had protected. That reading gives the refusal a moral shape beyond self-defence. Joseph is concerned with a third party's belief, and the concern survives his imprisonment; he wants the man who sheltered him delivered from an error, which is a different object from being acquitted, and a considerably harder one to hold on to from inside a cell.

**"God Does Not Guide the Cunning of the Treacherous"**

The closing maxim is the verse's general contribution and it is stated impersonally, so that it applies regardless of who is speaking. *Inna Llāha lā yahdī kayd al-khāʾinīn* — God does not give success to the stratagem of those who betray trust. The word *khāʾin* denotes a person who violates a confidence placed in him, and the chapter has produced several instances: the brothers, entrusted with a child by their father; a wife, entrusted with a guest by her husband; a household, entrusted with a cleared man and imprisoning him anyway. The maxim does not say their schemes failed at once. It says they do not reach their destination, and the sūrah's plot is the demonstration — every concealed arrangement in it ends somewhere its authors did not intend. The generalisation is what makes the verse a verdict rather than a complaint. It does not say that this particular plot failed; it says that plots of this kind do not succeed, and it offers the eighty verses behind it as the evidence. The claim is doctrinal and the narrative is its demonstration, which is the structure the whole sūrah has been building.

**Kayd Is Judged Here for the Last Time**

The chapter's vocabulary of stratagem reaches its verdict in this verse. *Kayd* named the brothers' plot (12:5), the women's device (12:28), the pressure Joseph asked God to remove (12:33), and the removal granted (12:34). Joseph referred to it once more when demanding the enquiry (12:50). Now the word is attached to treachery and denied success. The sequence is a complete argument: concealed planning is real, it is effective in the short term, it costs innocent people years of their lives, and it does not ultimately arrive at what it aimed for. The verse states the last of those four propositions, and the eighty preceding verses have supplied the evidence for the first three. The verdict is delivered by the person with least to gain from it, which is the chapter's final argument for its own reliability. A defendant asserting his innocence proves very little; the accuser confirming it, before a court, in the absence of the accused, ends the matter. Nothing further is needed and nothing further is said about the charge anywhere in the sūrah.

**The Verse Separates the Record From the Remedy**

Note what is absent. No restitution is proposed for the years in prison, no penalty is named for the false accusation, and no procedure is described for correcting a judicial record. What the verse supplies is knowledge: let him know. The chapter consistently treats the correction of a false account as the primary remedy available to an injured person, and treats punishment as secondary or absent. That priority will hold at the end of the story as well, when Joseph holds the power to destroy the brothers who sold him and instead tells them there is no blame on them today (12:92). The maxim about treachery is the theological ground for both: God does not guide the scheming of the treacherous, so the schemer's defeat does not require the victim's revenge.

**Two Readings, One Claim**

Whichever speaker is intended, the sentence asserts the same fact about the same person: Joseph did not betray a trust placed in him, and the truth of that is now available to the one who needed it. The uncertainty about the speaker therefore does not weaken the verse. It enlarges it, because the claim is made both by the woman who wronged him and, on the majority reading, by Joseph himself without boasting — and in the second case with an explicit refusal to take credit, which the next verse states outright. A fact testified to by its beneficiary and by its victim is about as well established as a fact can be inside a narrative. The two readings also remove every motive for fabrication. If Joseph is the speaker, he has already been vindicated and gains nothing; if the woman is the speaker, she loses her reputation and her standing. Whichever the reader prefers, the statement costs its author something, and the chapter places it at the point where cost and credibility exactly coincide.
