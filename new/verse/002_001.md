## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Letters, Not Words**

The sūrah opens with three letters and no sentence. Twenty-nine of the Quran's 114 sūrahs begin this way — a count that can be taken directly from the text — and in every case what is recited is the *name* of the letter, not the sound it makes: *alif, lām, mīm*, not "a, l, m." A reader reciting this verse is therefore pronouncing something that carries no stated meaning, which happens nowhere else in the Book.

The oddity is the point of departure for everything said about these openings. Elsewhere in the Quran, a difficult word is explained by its context, by a parallel passage, or by the Prophet's own explanation. Here the community has recited the letters from the first generation onward without an agreed account of what they denote. That combination — universal in practice, unsettled in meaning — is what makes the opening of this sūrah the most discussed verse in the Quran after 3:7, the verse that divides the Book into clear and elusive.

**The Reading That Makes Them a Challenge**

One observation is available to anyone who opens the text, and it has carried more weight than any of the reconstructions. In all twenty-nine sūrahs, the letters are immediately followed by a statement about the Book itself: *Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the clear Book* (12:1); *Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom* (10:1); *˹This is˺ a Book whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained* (11:1); *These are the verses of the Book* (13:1); and in the shorter sets, *These are the verses of the clear Book* (26:2) and *These are the verses of the Quran; the clear Book* (27:1). Three sūrahs place an oath directly after the letter: *Qãf. By the glorious Quran!* (50:1), *Ṣãd. By the Quran, full of reminders!* (38:1), and *Nũn. By the pen and what everyone writes!* (68:1).

The argument built on this is that the letters draw attention to the raw material. The Book the Arabs were being challenged to match was composed of the very letters they used every day, and the challenge is stated in those terms: *And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true* (2:23), and later, *If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other* (17:88). The openings announce that the tools were available to everyone and the result was not.

**The Oldest Guess: Abbreviations**

The alternative with the oldest pedigree holds that the letters are ciphers. Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd are both reported to have favoured the view that each letter stands for a word or phrase connected with God and His attributes, and the most common filling of this sūrah's three letters makes the *alif* Allāh, the *lām* Jibrīl, and the *mīm* Muḥammad — the descent of revelation from God, through the angel, to the Prophet.

The objection to the whole approach is arithmetical rather than pious. Arabic has twenty-eight letters and the openings use fourteen of them; the number of words or phrases that can be built from a given letter is very large, so a reconstruction can always be made to fit, and two reconstructions that both fit cannot both be right. Muhammad Asad's conclusion is representative: the possible combinations are practically unlimited, so such interpretations are arbitrary and "devoid of any real usefulness." The cipher reading survives mainly because it was said early, not because it can be tested.

**Counting the Letters**

A related family of readings treats the letters as numbers, since each Arabic letter carries a numerical value — *alif* is 1, *lām* is 30, *mīm* is 40. The discipline called *al-jafr*, comparable to gematria, builds chronologies and predictions on these values. Other readings note that exactly half the alphabet occurs in these positions, or that some of the groups double as the names of their sūrahs — *Yā Sīn*, *Ṣād*, *Nūn*, *Qāf* are all sūrah titles — while others, including this one, are not.

None of these systems can fail a test, and that is their weakness as well as their appeal. A calculation that yields a date cannot be checked against anything independent, and the observation that half the alphabet is used is true without explaining why these fourteen appear in these combinations. What the counting does establish is that the groups are not random noise: they cluster (seven sūrahs open with *Ḥā Mīm*, six with *Alif-Lām-Mīm*), which is why the scribal-monogram theories of Nöldeke and Hirschfeld were proposed and why they remain minority positions.

**Hidden Meaning, and the Argument Against It**

Some of the earliest authorities treated the letters as knowledge withheld. A saying attributed to Abū Bakr has been widely reported: *In every divine writ there is an element of mystery, and the mystery of the Quran is in the openings of the sūrahs.* A parallel saying attributed to ʿAlī makes the letters the *ṣafwah* — the quintessence — of the Book. [UNVERIFIED: both sayings are reported in the commentaries, cited to al-Zarkashī's *al-Burhān* and al-Baghawī; I could not verify a chain for either.] Al-Rāzī went further, proposing that the reciter's ignorance here functions like the rituals of the pilgrimage: acts performed in trust whose wisdom is not disclosed, and whose very opacity keeps the heart oriented toward what it does not already possess.

Theologians objected, and their objection is the stronger argument. The Quran repeatedly describes itself as clear: *a light and a clear Book* (5:15), verses *well perfected and then fully explained* (11:1), a revelation *˹in˺ eloquent Arabic* (16:103). To hold that a passage of the Book has no knowable meaning at all is to make the claim of clarity hollow, and to say that a text was sent to people in a language they could not understand is to say it was not sent to them. The compromise most commentaries settle on treats the letters as meaningful but unspecified: the reciter affirms that they are from God and does not claim to know more than has been given.

**An Oath Sworn by the Alphabet**

The reading with the most textual support inside the Quran itself is that the letters are an oath. Three sūrahs remove the speculation by following the letter immediately with a sworn formula — *Qãf. By the glorious Quran!* (50:1), *Ṣãd. By the Quran, full of reminders!* (38:1), *Nũn. By the pen and what everyone writes!* (68:1) — and the Quran swears by created things constantly, in sūrahs that open with a series of oaths: *By the sun and its brightness, and the moon as it follows it* (91:1–2). On this reading, God swears by the alphabet that carries His speech.

The image behind the oath is the one some commentators drew from the letters' own articulation: the *alif* opens from the deepest point of the throat, the *lām* is formed in the middle of the mouth, and the *mīm* closes on the lips — the beginning, middle, and completion of articulate sound. Speech itself is the thing sworn by. From letters come words, from words the Book, and from the Book the world it addresses; the sūrah that is about to speak for 286 verses opens by naming the material it is made of.
