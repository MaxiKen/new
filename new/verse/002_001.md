## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Letter Names That Refuse Translation**

The longest chapter of the Qur'an — 286 verses — opens with a line that is not a sentence. *Alif. Lām. Mīm.* are the names of three Arabic letters, recited by name and not by sound: the reader says *alif*, not *a*. Twenty-nine of the Qur'an's 114 chapters begin with such letter sequences, and this particular trio heads six of them — al-Baqarah, Āl ʿImrān, al-ʿAnkabūt, al-Rūm, Luqmān, and al-Sajdah. Nothing in the line parses as ordinary Arabic: no verb, no subject, no predication. It arrests the listener at the threshold of the Book with material rather than message, and fourteen centuries of readers have taken that arrest seriously, treating the letters as one of the Qur'an's most deliberate enigmas. Any account of them must admit two facts at once. They were recited from the first day and preserved with the text — nothing so conspicuous survived by accident. And no explanation of them has ever commanded consensus, which is itself information about their function.

**A Mystery the Caliphs Acknowledged**

The earliest Muslims did not solve the letters; they located them. Abū Bakr is reported to have said that every book has a mystery, and the mystery of the Qur'an is in the openings of its chapters. ʿAlī's formulation runs in parallel: every book has a quintessence, and the quintessence of this Book is in the spelled-out letters. Ibn ʿAjībah went further and confined the knowledge to the elite among Allah's friends. These sayings are not evasions but claims about the architecture of revelation — that the Book possesses a layer held back from casual grasp, positioned where every reader must pass it. The tradition that stacks the secrets of the learned, the successors, the prophets, the angels, and finally Allah Himself makes the same point as a hierarchy: each rank is surpassed by a knowledge it cannot carry, and the letters stand at the border where reading turns into reverence. A book entirely transparent would demand nothing but comprehension; these openings demand something else first.

**Al-Rāzī's Riverbeds and the Measure of Capacity**

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī pictured the problem as water. The sea pours into rivers, rivers into streams, streams into rivulets; force a rivulet to carry the river's whole volume and it bursts its banks and is destroyed. He grounded the image in the Qur'an itself: *"He sends down rain from the sky, causing the valleys to flow, each according to its capacity"* (13:17). Revelation on this picture is apportioned — the same downpour fills each bed only as far as its walls allow. The letters then function as a marker of the surplus: the part of the sea no rivulet carries. The doctrine protects both sides of the relationship. Allah's speech is not diminished by human limits, and human beings are not crushed by contact with what exceeds them. Capacity becomes a spiritual category rather than an insult: the valleys differ in width, and the rain does not complain of any of them.

**The Objection: A Book That Calls Itself Clear**

Theologians pressed the other way. The Qur'an describes itself as clear, as guidance, as revealed in a plain Arabic tongue; a Book part of which no one can read would address Arabs in what amounts to a foreign language, and al-Rāzī himself records the objection. The Qur'an anticipates the tension and legislates a posture toward it: *"He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive… but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah. As for those well grounded in knowledge, they say, 'We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord'"* (3:7). The letters belong to the elusive class by design. The mature response in that verse is not solution but inclusion — belief extended to what remains sealed. Clarity and mystery are therefore not rivals in this Book's self-description; the clear carries the weight of practice, and the sealed carries the weight of awe.

**Trust Without Transparency: Al-Rāzī's Compromise**

Al-Rāzī resolved the standoff with an analogy from practice. Some of the Law's wisdom is legible — the harms behind the prohibitions of wine and gambling can be argued. Other requirements, like particular rites of the pilgrimage, are performed without any accessible reason at all. The unreadable portion of the Qur'an belongs to this second kind: obeyed, recited, carried, but not cashed out into understanding. Far from being a defect, the arrangement trains a faculty that transparent religion never exercises — trust. A person who acts on wisdom he can see is partly relying on himself; a person who recites *Alif. Lām. Mīm.* morning and evening without grasping it relies on the Speaker alone. Al-Rāzī adds that the practice keeps the heart oriented beyond the world and beyond what it already knows. The letters are thus pedagogy through opacity: they teach the reader where his knowledge ends while his recitation continues.

**Building Blocks, Oaths, and a Standing Challenge**

Concrete interpretations multiply. Some read the letters as abbreviations — *alif* for Allah, *lām* for Jibrīl, *mīm* for Muḥammad, the whole descent of revelation compressed into three signs. Some mapped them onto speech itself: *alif* the origin of sound, *lām* its middle in the mouth, *mīm* its consummation on the lips. The numerologists counted values — *alif* one, *lām* thirty, *mīm* forty. Others hear an oath, Allah swearing by the letters as He elsewhere swears by created things. Strongest of all is the reading woven into this very sūrah: the Book is built from the same letters its hearers command, so the challenge stands open — *"produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true"* (2:23). The raw material lay in every Arab mouth; the composition defeated them all. What the letters precede, the next verse declares: a Book beyond doubt.
