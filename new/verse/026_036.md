## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36

> They replied, “Let him and his brother wait and dispatch mobilizers to all cities

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chiefs Choose Delay Instead of Judgment**

The chiefs do not answer whether Moses' signs are true. They advise Pharaoh to let him and Aaron wait while the state gathers specialists from the cities. The proposal creates time, but not for reflection. It delays the demand for Israel's release and moves the court toward a public contest that can be managed by royal power. The issue of revelation is turned into an event the government can schedule.

The verb behind let him wait can carry the sense of postponing, holding back, or delaying. Some commentators understood it as a request to keep Moses and Aaron confined for a while, while others took it as a proposal to postpone the confrontation. The latter fits the immediate wording better: the chiefs are arranging the collection of magicians, and the next verses describe the gathering. In either case, the purpose is containment while Pharaoh prepares a response.

**Delay Can Be a Form of Resistance**

Not every refusal is spoken as a clear no. A ruler may ask for another committee, another inquiry, or another performance until the original demand has lost its urgency. Pharaoh's court uses delay in this way. The Israelites remain under control while officials organize a spectacle. Time is presented as prudence, but the delay protects the existing order.

This does not mean that every pause is dishonest. Complex decisions sometimes require consultation and preparation. The moral question is what the pause serves. Here the chiefs do not propose investigating the slavery or hearing Moses' evidence more carefully. They propose gathering people who can counter the sign. The pause is therefore strategic, not open-minded. It gives power room to build an alternative story.

**Mobilizers Turn the Kingdom Into a Stage**

The order to send mobilizers to all cities reveals the scale of Pharaoh's administrative reach. The state can move through its towns, summon specialists, and bring them toward a central event. This machinery is impressive, but it also shows that Pharaoh's power is dependent on networks of command. The ruler who claimed absolute authority needs officials, messengers, and public organization to maintain the appearance of control.

The mobilizers are not sent to protect the people from oppression or to release the Israelites. They are sent to collect magicians. Government capacity is being spent on defending a ruler's interpretation of a divine sign. The verse therefore raises a question about public resources: what does a state mobilize for, and whose interests determine its urgency?

**A Court Answers the Messenger by Recruiting Experts**

The chiefs assume that Moses' claim can be defeated by finding people with comparable technical skill. Their response reveals the category in which they have placed the signs. Instead of asking whether the event came from God, they search for performers who can compete with it. Expertise is treated as a weapon in a contest rather than as a means of discovering truth.

This is a common response when an institution feels threatened by evidence. It looks for specialists who can reproduce an appearance, create doubt, or give the public a rival explanation. Such a strategy may confuse the audience even if it does not answer the original claim. The court hopes that a more impressive performance will make the moral demand disappear.

**The Brothers Are Kept Within the Ruler's Reach**

The advice concerns Moses and his brother before it concerns the cities. The chiefs do not allow the messengers to leave and address the people freely. Their movement is to be managed while the state prepares. Even if the phrase means only postpone, the intended effect is containment. Pharaoh wants control over where the brothers stand, who hears them, and what happens next.

This is how a public authority can make a hearing appear open while controlling every condition of the hearing. The messenger is allowed to remain visible, but only inside a stage designed by the ruler. The court hopes that visibility without freedom will work in its favor. Moses' signs will have to stand not only against magic but against the political management of attention.

**The Advice Shows Confidence Mixed With Fear**

The chiefs speak as though they can solve the problem by gathering enough magicians. Yet the scale of the response suggests anxiety. A harmless speaker would not require mobilizers from every city. Pharaoh's court recognizes that Moses has reached a point where ordinary ridicule may not be enough. It answers fear with organization and calls the organization wisdom.

That combination appears often in unjust power. Confidence is displayed in public while extraordinary measures are taken in private. The measures reveal what the rhetoric conceals. Pharaoh's court is not simply arranging entertainment. It is building a collective defense against a claim that could loosen the ruler's hold over the people.

**Consultation Is Not Wise When the Premise Is False**

The chiefs are asked for counsel, but good counsel must begin by describing the problem honestly. They have accepted Pharaoh's accusation that Moses is a magician who threatens the land. Without examining that premise, their advice can only strengthen the error. A council may be full of experienced people and still produce foolish policy if it serves fear rather than truth.

The verse therefore separates consultation from wisdom. Consultation gathers voices; wisdom judges the frame within which those voices speak. Pharaoh's chiefs know how to summon magicians. They do not show that they know how to answer a prophet. Their plan will create a public test, but it will also create witnesses to the court's attempt to replace moral judgment with spectacle.

A ruler can delay a just demand, but delay cannot make the demand disappear. The Israelites remain enslaved while the court organizes its defense, and that continued captivity gives the planned contest its moral background. The more elaborate the spectacle becomes, the clearer it is that Pharaoh is avoiding the simple command to release the people.
