## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:67

> Every ˹destined˺ matter has a ˹set˺ time to transpire. And you will soon come to know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence About Appointments**

The verse states that everything destined has a time at which it arrives, and the word used for the appointment comes from the same root as the terms and spans the Book applies to human lives and communities. The sūrah used it a few verses earlier when it said that people are revived each day so that an appointed term may be completed (6:60), and it used it for the communities whose fixed spans cannot be delayed or advanced (7:34; 10:49). Here the same structure is applied to the events the audience was impatient about.

The Book states the principle in almost this exact wording at the end of a passage about the demands for signs, where it says that no messenger could bring a sign except by God's permission and that every destined matter has a set time (13:38). Read together, the two verses answer the Makkan demand for a miracle and the Makkan demand for the punishment in the same currency: the timing of both rests with God.

**The Threat and the Promise in the Same Clause**

And you will soon come to know is a formula the Book uses in several tones. It appears where it warns people who reject the revelation that they will soon know (37:170), where the Prophet is told to bear with the mockers and answer them with peace because they will soon come to know (43:89), and where the Book tells the audience that they will certainly know the truth of the warning before long (38:88). The same words serve as a warning and as an assurance depending on who is hearing them.

That double edge is the point of placing the formula here, immediately after a verse that has acknowledged the Prophet's rejection and denied him the role of keeper. The audience is told that the delay in their case does not mean the matter will not be settled, and the Prophet is told that the settlement is coming and that he is not required to produce it.

**The Impatience of the Audience**

The sūrah has recorded the demand to hasten the punishment several times, and the Book treats that impatience as a feature of the human being rather than a considered position. It says in the sūrah of the Prophets that humanity is made of haste, and follows the observation with a warning that the signs will be shown and should not be demanded ahead of their time (21:37). The sūrah of Yūnus ends a discussion of the delay with the instruction to keep waiting, since the Prophet is also waiting (10:102).

The impatience has a function in the Book's argument. It shows that the audience knew the message was making a claim about the future and that the claim mattered to them, since nobody demands the hastening of a matter he regards as fiction. Their demand was a form of acknowledgment, and the verse's answer treats it as such while refusing to move the appointment.

**Waiting as a Form of Conduct**

The Book's instructions about waiting are never passive. The sūrah of Ṭā-Hā ends with the Prophet being told to say that each party is waiting and that the outcome will soon be known (20:135), and the context of that verse is a community being asked to continue in the conduct the message requires. Waiting in the Book's vocabulary means going on with the work while the appointment approaches.

The same structure appears in the sūrah of the Cave, where the audience is told that the message is the truth from the Lord and that whoever wills may believe and whoever wills may disbelieve (18:29). What looks like a release from obligation is a statement about where the obligation lies. The reader is told that the outcome is fixed for a time of its own choosing, and that the interval belongs to him.

**The Coming of Knowledge**

The clause about coming to know deserves attention because knowledge is the thing the audience had been demanding on its own terms. They wanted to know when the punishment would arrive, and they were told that they would know at the time appointed. The Book's answer is not that the information is being withheld arbitrarily but that the schedule of knowledge is itself part of the appointment.

That principle runs through the Book's treatment of the Hour, whose timing is stated to belong to God alone, and whose arrival is described as something that will take people by surprise. The verse in front of us applies the same arrangement to the smaller events of judgment that the Makkans were demanding to see, and it places the whole subject inside the doctrine of a fixed term that the sūrah has been teaching since its account of the keys of the unseen.

**The Transition It Creates**

The two verses just completed move the sūrah from public argument to practical instruction. The Prophet has been told that he is not a keeper over his people (6:66), and the appointment of every matter has been stated (6:67). What follows is a set of directions about how the believers are to conduct themselves when the revelation is mocked, and how far their responsibility extends toward the mockers.

That transition is worth noting, because it shows what the Book does with the anxiety of a messenger who cannot compel belief. Having removed the responsibility for the outcome, the Book replaces it with a defined responsibility for conduct. The next verses state exactly where the limits of the believer's obligation lie.

The Book keeps a copy of the same appointment in its account of the Hour, where the Prophet is told to say that knowledge of the time belongs to his Lord alone and that it will take people by surprise (7:187). The two statements belong to one doctrine: what the audience was demanding to see was already scheduled, and the One who scheduled it had described the effect of its arrival as a surprise rather than as an announcement. The verse therefore leaves the makkans with certainty about the fact and no information about the date.
