## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:2

> **He sends down the angels with revelation by His command to whoever He wills of His servants, ˹stating:˺ “Warn ˹humanity˺ that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Me, so be mindful of Me ˹alone˺.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From The Command That Comes To The Spirit That Descends**

Verse 1 spoke of Allah's amr arriving. Verse 2 explains how that amr reaches human beings: yunazzilu al-malā'ikata bi-l-rūḥ min amrihi. He sends down the angels with the rūḥ, the spirit, from His command. The verb yunazzilu is in the present tense, indicating a repeated, ongoing act. Revelation is not a single event locked in the past; it is a sending down that Allah does whenever He wills, to whomever He wills among His servants. The pairing of amr in both verses is deliberate. The same decisive authority that brings judgment also brings guidance, and guidance comes first. Before the command that ends history, there is the command that gives history its meaning. Al-Ṭabarī notes that rūḥ here is understood by most early commentators as revelation itself, called spirit because it gives life to hearts as spirit gives life to bodies. Al-Rāzī adds that just as a body without spirit is dead, a community without revelation is dead though it walks and trades.

**Why Revelation Is Called Rūḥ**

The Qur'an uses rūḥ for several related realities, and the commentators distinguish them carefully. Sometimes rūḥ is the angel Jibrīl himself, called al-Rūḥ al-Amīn, the Trustworthy Spirit. Sometimes it is revelation, because revelation animates. Sometimes it is the secret of life that humans know little about, as in 17:85: *They ask you about the spirit. Say, the spirit is from the command of my Lord, and you have not been given knowledge except a little* (17:85). The verse here brings two of those senses together: angels descend, and they carry rūḥ. The angel is the carrier, the rūḥ is what is carried. Al-Qurṭubī points out that calling revelation a spirit teaches something about how it works. A spirit does not merely inform; it revives. A person may know many facts and still be spiritually lifeless. When revelation enters, intentions change, fear and hope find their proper objects, and the heart begins to move toward its Creator. This is why the same root appears in 42:52: *And thus We have revealed to you a spirit from Our command. You did not know what the Book was nor what faith was, but We made it a light by which We guide whom We will of Our servants* (42:52). The verse in al-Naḥl therefore defines the purpose of prophethood in one image: dead hearts brought to life by a descent from above.

**Angels As Bearers, Not Authors**

Yunazzilu al-malā'ikata — He sends down the angels. The plural suggests the host that accompanies revelation, though the direct bearer is Jibrīl. The Qur'an elsewhere names him explicitly: *He brings it down upon your heart by Allah's permission* (2:97). The point of mentioning angels in the plural is to show the honor and protection around the message. Revelation does not drift down like rumor; it is carried by pure, obedient beings whose only task is to execute Allah's command. Ibn Kathīr notes that this guards against the Makkan claim that the Prophet ﷺ invented the message or learned it from a human teacher. Angels are not of this earth. Their descent marks the message as coming from beyond human craft. At the same time, the phrase *to whoever He wills of His servants* removes any human claim to control who receives it. Prophethood is not earned by lineage, wealth, or eloquence. It is a selection, an iṣṭifā', and Allah knows best where to place His message, as 6:124 says. The Makkans had asked, *Why was this Qur'an not sent down upon a great man from the two towns* (43:31), meaning a rich leader of Makkah or Ṭā'if. The answer is in this verse: choice belongs to the One who sends, not to those who receive.

**The Core Message In One Sentence**

What do the angels say when they descend? An anḏirū annahu lā ilāha illā anā fa-ittaqūn — warn that there is no god worthy of worship except Me, so be mindful of Me alone. This is the summary of every messenger's call. Al-Ṭabarī cites 21:25: *We did not send any messenger before you except that We revealed to him, there is no god but Me, so worship Me* (21:25). The command anḏirū, warn, shows that revelation begins with warning before promise, because a heedless heart does not feel need for promise until it feels danger. The warning is not about tribal defeat or poverty; it is about the foundation of worship. Lā ilāha illā anā — there is no deity except Me. The pronoun anā, I, is direct, without intermediary, to stress that Allah speaks about Himself. Then comes fa-ittaqūn — so be mindful of Me, fear Me, guard yourselves by reverence for Me. Taqwā comes from w-q-y, to shield or protect. It is not mere terror; it is the carefulness of one who knows a danger is real and takes protection. Here protection is found in turning to Allah alone. The two halves of the message mirror the two halves of the shahādah: negation of all false gods, affirmation of Allah alone, then the practical consequence in conduct.

**A Verse That Defines Prophethood For The Whole Sūrah**

Placed at the beginning of Sūrat al-Niʿam, this verse tells the reader how to understand every blessing that follows. Cattle, rain, crops, sea, mountains, honey — all will be listed as gifts. But the greatest gift is the one that tells you who gave the gifts and what He asks in return. Without revelation, a person may enjoy blessings while forgetting the Bestower, which is the very definition of ingratitude that this sūrah condemns. With revelation, the same blessings become signs. Al-Rāzī makes this connection explicit: the physical blessings sustain the body, the blessing of revelation sustains the spirit, and the second is greater because the body perishes while the spirit returns to its Lord. The verse also establishes a criterion for evaluating any claim to guidance. Any teaching that does not center on lā ilāha illā Allah is not from the rūḥ, even if it carries spiritual language. The angels descend only with tawḥīd. That is the test.

**Lexical Note On Nazzala And Taqwā**

The verb nazzala is in the form II, which often indicates repeated or gradual descent, as opposed to anzala which can indicate a single sending down. Many commentators note that the Qur'an uses nazzala for the gradual revelation over twenty-three years, which matches the historical reality of the Prophet's experience. The command ittaqūn is plural, addressed to humanity through the messengers. The translators add ˹alone˺ in brackets to make clear that mindfulness here means mindfulness of Allah exclusively, not a general carefulness. In classical Arabic, ittaqā with direct object means to fear or be wary of that thing itself, so ittaqūn here means fear Me, not merely fear My punishment. This nuance matters because it places the motive in the person of Allah, not only in consequences. The heart that knows who Allah is will naturally guard against what displeases Him.
