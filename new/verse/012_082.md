## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:82

> Ask ˹the people of˺ the land where we were and the caravan we travelled with. We are certainly telling the truth.’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Witnesses Offered Rather Than Proof Produced**

The speech composed in verse 81 ends with a direction to seek verification. The brothers do not ask Jacob to accept their word, and they do not produce an object to support it. They name two groups he can question. The move is unusual for this family and the contrast with their earlier report is exact. When they came home about Joseph they carried evidence — a shirt stained with false blood — and offered no witnesses whatever (12:18). Here they carry nothing and offer witnesses instead. The chapter's comparison of the two reports is completed by this verse: fabricated proof replaces inquiry in the first case, and available inquiry replaces proof in the second. The difference is not merely rhetorical. One report was designed to end questioning, and the other is designed to invite it.

**"Ask the Town Where We Were"**

The commentators understand the town as the people of Egypt — the inhabitants of the place the brothers have just left. That is a wide and largely inaccessible category for an ageing father in Canaan during a famine. He cannot travel to a rationed capital to question granary officials about a search conducted at a loading yard, and the brothers know it. The offer may therefore be less a practical proposal than a statement of confidence: our account would survive scrutiny by anyone who was present, including the people who conducted the search. The chapter does not test the offer and Jacob does not take it up in verse 83. What the offer establishes is that the report was made in a jurisdiction full of witnesses, which is the strongest thing a party of foreign travellers can say about its own conduct.

**"And the Caravan We Travelled With"**

The second group is more usable, and the commentators identify it as the people of Canaan — travellers who made the journey with the brothers and would return to the same region and the same families. One account adds that when these travellers came back to Jacob they testified to the brothers' truthfulness, which means the offer was taken up and produced the confirmation the brothers expected. The Qur'an does not record that testimony, and the chapter's silence leaves the matter where the brothers left it: verification was available, it was named, and the report was made in the knowledge that it could be checked by people who would soon be standing in Jacob's own household. That knowledge shapes the wording of verse 81, which claims only what was seen.

**Two Categories of Witness Covering Two Different Claims**

The pairing is deliberate and the two categories are not interchangeable. The town represents the officials and residents of the jurisdiction where the events occurred — the people who announced the loss, offered a reward, conducted the search and applied a penalty. They can confirm what happened. The caravan represents fellow travellers from the brothers' own region, who observed the party on the road, at the gates of the city and during the loading of provisions. They can confirm who the brothers are and how they behaved. Together the two groups cover both halves of the account: the fact of the detention and the character of the men reporting it. A single category would leave one half unverified, and the verse names both.

**The Claim Is Truthfulness, Not Innocence**

*We are certainly telling the truth.* The assertion is confined to the accuracy of the report and extends no further. The brothers do not claim that Benjamin is innocent, that the detention is unjust, that the penalty was misapplied, or that their father's grief is misplaced. They do not ask for anything. The narrowness is consistent with the limit on testimony stated in the previous verse: a person who says only what he saw can defend the saying, whereas a person who vouches for someone else's innocence cannot. The chapter shows these men learning to make the smaller claim, and the smaller claim is the one that survives — not Jacob's acceptance in verse 83, which is withheld, but the reader's, who has watched the whole search and knows exactly what the brothers did and did not see.

**The Verse Ends a Speech and Begins a Journey**

The closing quotation marks the end of the eldest's address, and the narrative immediately changes location. Verses 80 to 82 have been a single scene in Egypt, conducted privately among nine brothers and one who has vowed to stay. Verse 83 moves to Canaan and to Jacob's answer. The chapter does not describe the journey home, the arrival, or the moment the report is delivered; it passes from the composition of the message to its reception. That economy is typical of the sūrah's transitions. What matters is the message and the answer, and the weeks of travel between them are omitted because nothing in them affects either. The reader is carried from a granary in a famine to a household in mourning in the space of a single verse break.

**A Family That Now Prefers Verification**

The chapter's interest in evidence runs from beginning to end. A torn shirt established Joseph's innocence inside the ʿAzīz's household (12:26–28). A public enquiry before the king established it again, at Joseph's own insistence and at the cost of further years in prison (12:50–51). An oath with an exception clause governed Benjamin's journey (12:66). A stated penalty governed his detention (12:75). And here, at the moment of a second loss, the brothers offer inquiry instead of an object. The progression is never announced, but it is visible across the verses and it runs in one direction. A family that once lied to its father with a bloodied shirt is now composing a report that invites checking, and the chapter records the change without praising it or pretending that it will be believed.
