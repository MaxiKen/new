## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:191

> Kill them wherever you come upon them and drive them out of the places from which they have driven you out. For persecution is far worse than killing. And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they attack you there. If they do so, then fight them—that is the reward of the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Permission's Scope and Its Symmetry**

The verse extends the previous verse's permission into the field, and its two commands are drawn with deliberate symmetry. *Kill them wherever you find them* — the fighting is not restricted to set-piece encounters; the combatant met anywhere within the war's theater is within the permission. And *expel them from where they expelled you* — the geography of the response is the geography of the injury: the expulsion of the believers from their homes and their sanctuary licenses the reciprocal expulsion of the expellers, and licenses exactly that, no further. The symmetry is the verse's legal structure: each command answers a specific act of the persecution — the killing answers the war waged, the expulsion answers the expulsion inflicted. The tradition read both commands inside the previous verse's two limits: the fighters, not the population (the reports prohibiting the killing of women, children, monks, the aged, and the non-combatant peasants govern *wherever you find them*); and the response, not the excess (*expel from where they expelled* is reciprocity legislated as a maximum). The verse thus does not widen the war; it specifies the permission already bounded, giving the community the field-rules of a conflict whose entire legitimacy rests on the injuries it names.

**Fitnah Is Greater Than Killing**

The verse's central declaration — *fitnah is greater than killing* — supplies the war's moral calculus, and the word's meaning here is fixed by the context and the exegetes together: fitnah as persecution, as the trial inflicted on believers to make them abandon their religion — torture, expulsion, the pressure that produced the martyrs of Mecca and the Hijrah itself. Some commentators read the word here in its sharpest sense as persecution culminating in shirk — the forcing of idolatry, the greater killing, which is the killing of the soul's allegiance. The declaration's logic is comparative and consequentialist in the precise sense: killing is terrible, and the verse does not soften it; but the campaign to destroy faith itself — the fitnah the Quraysh had conducted for thirteen years — is worse than the killing that resistance to it entails. The sūrah restates the same ranking in its sacred-month legislation: fighting in the sacred month is great, but barring people from God's path, disbelieving in Him, and expelling the Mosque's people is greater with God, and fitnah is greater than killing (2:217). The two statements together form the war-law's foundational weighing: the injury that justifies war is not death inflicted but faith attacked — and the permission to kill exists to end the greater killing.

**The Sacred Mosque's Regime**

The second half of the verse legislates the sanctuary: do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they fight you there; if they fight you there, fight them. The clause is the war-law's sharpest particular limitation, and its background is the sūrah's own: the Mosque's inviolability had been established in the Abraham-legislation (2:125–126), its security made a divine endowment, and the Hudaybiyyah context of these verses meant the community was currently barred from it by treaty — a people with every grievance and a standing permission to fight, legislated now to keep their swords sheathed at the very site of their exclusion. The regime the clause builds is conditional to the point of passivity: at the Sacred Mosque the believers may not initiate combat at all; the permission activates only inside an attack already begun there. The commentators noted the clause's answer to a real dilemma — what a persecuted community does when the persecutor uses the sanctuary as a shield — and the answer keeps the sanctuary's holiness intact while refusing to make it a refuge for aggression: the Mosque is not fought in, and the one who fights in it forfeits the protection his own violation destroyed. Such is the recompense of the disbelievers, the verse adds: the counter-fighting at the sanctuary is not the believers' transgression but the attacker's reward.

**Where the Permission Stops**

The verse's limits become fully visible against the Qur'an's asylum legislation. The sūrah of Repentance, dealing with the same post-war population, commands: if anyone of the idolaters seeks your protection, protect him until he hears the word of God, then deliver him to his place of safety (9:6) — the enemy not merely spared but escorted, given the hearing he fought against, and returned safe to his home. The same population the battlefield verse addresses is thus covered, outside battle, by a law of refuge; and the reports' prohibitions on killing non-combatants fence the battlefield itself. Read as one system, the permission of these verses reaches only the fighting enemy, in the fighting, without transgression (2:190), with the sanctuary held out of the war unless the war enters it, and with asylum, safety, and peace legislated at every margin — the moment the enemy inclines to peace, the whole apparatus stands down (8:61). The verse that sounds, in isolation, like the war-law's hardest sentence is in fact its tightest channel: killing permitted where persecution made killing the lesser evil, expulsion permitted where expulsion was the injury answered, the Mosque protected even against the justified army, and every exit from the fighting left open and commanded.

**The Greater Killing as the War's Object**

The declaration that fitnah is greater than killing does more than justify; it defines the war's end. If the greater evil is the persecution of faith, then the war exists to end the persecution — not to punish, not to convert by the sword, not to take the expellers' city as prize. The next verse will state this object explicitly: fight until there is no fitnah and religion is God's alone — and if they desist, the enmity ends with the aggression. The verse's own expulsion-clause confirms the reading: the expulsion commanded is *from where they expelled you* — restoration, not conquest; the return of the exiles to their homes, not the seizure of new ones. And the Sacred Mosque clause confirms it again: the sanctuary is where the fighting must *not* spread, because the war's object is the freedom of worship there, not its capture — the same community already believed the Mosque was God's house, and the verse's whole concern is that worship at it be free of fitnah. The moral arithmetic of *greater than killing* thus runs through the legislation as its purpose-clause: killing is permitted against the greater killing of souls from their faith, and the permission expires the moment the greater killing ends.

**The Recompense Named**

The verse's last phrase — such is the recompense of the disbelievers — attaches the counter-fighting at the sanctuary to a category the sūrah has used consistently: the punishment that is the crime's own shape returned. The disbelievers who made the Sacred Mosque an instrument of persecution — barring its worshippers from it (2:217), using its sanctity to shield their aggression — receive, when they fight there, the fighting they chose: the protection they violated withdrawn, the retaliation they invited arriving at the exact site and in the exact form of their violation. The phrase is judicial rather than vengeful: the recompense is described as theirs by right of their own act, the same structure the retaliation-law gave the murderer (a life for the life taken) and the transgressor after settlement (2:178). And it is bounded by that structure — the recompense of the fighters at the Mosque is the fighting at the Mosque, and no further: not their families, not the sanctuary's desecration, not the excess the previous verse named unloved by God. The verse ends, as it began, inside its limits: the permission specific, the sanctuary honored even in its violation, the fitnah named as the evil that outranks killing, and the recompense measured to the crime — the whole passage a war-law written, line by line, against the war it legislates for.
