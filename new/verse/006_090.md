## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:90

> These ˹prophets˺ were ˹rightly˺ guided by Allah, so follow their guidance. Say, “I ask no reward of you for this ˹Quran˺—it is a reminder to the whole world.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Follow the Guidance of the Prophets**

These prophets were rightly guided by Allah, so follow their guidance. The command is addressed to the Prophet and through him to the community, and it treats the catalogue of the previous verses as a practical model rather than as a historical record. The line of men who received the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood are to be followed in the guidance they carried, and the book of their message is the same book the Prophet was reciting.

The Book issues the same command in connection with Abraham, telling the Prophet that God revealed to him to follow the faith of Abraham the upright (16:123), and telling the believers that they follow the way of their forefather Abraham (22:78). The sūrah of the Family of Imran states the same instruction twice, first as an answer to the claims of the People of the Book (3:95) and then in the declaration that God has guided the Prophet to the faith of Abraham (6:161, in this sūrah). The command in this verse extends the model to the whole prophetic line.

**What Following the Guidance Involves**

The Book describes the content of the guidance in the verses that precede this one: the worship of God alone, the declaration that He has no partners, the refusal to invoke what cannot benefit or harm, the argument against the worship of created things, and the conduct of patience and prayer. The Prophet is told to follow that guidance, which means that his message is not a departure from the tradition of the prophets but the restoration of it in the language of his own people.

The Book states the continuity explicitly when it says that God has ordained the way He decreed for Noah, for Abraham, for Moses, and for Jesus, and that the instruction given to all of them was to uphold the faith and make no divisions in it (42:13). The sūrah is therefore presenting the Prophet's mission as the final link in a chain whose earlier links the audience claimed to honour, and the command to follow the guidance of the earlier men is the practical form of that presentation.

**The Refusal of Payment**

Say, I ask no reward of you for this. The sentence detaches the messenger from any material interest in the response, and the Book repeats it in several sūrahs. The Prophet is told to say that he does not ask for any reward but that whoever wishes may take a path to his Lord (25:57); the sūrah of Sheba gives the answer that if he had ever asked for a reward they could keep it, since his reward comes from God alone (34:47); and the sūrah of Yūnus records the same declaration in the words of Noah to his people (10:72).

The Book's insistence on this point is a statement about the nature of the message. A prophet who profited from his mission would give his audience a reason to suspect his motives, and the Book's messengers are consistently described as men who worked with their hands and asked nothing of their peoples. The declaration also protects the message from the suspicion that it was tailored to produce a following.

**A Reminder to the Whole World**

It is a reminder to the whole world. The sentence states the scope of the message in the widest terms available, and the Book repeats it in the same words in other places, saying that the Qur'an is nothing but a reminder to the whole world (12:104; 38:87). The sūrah of the Prophets states the same universality in a different form, telling the Prophet that God has sent him only as a mercy for the whole world (21:107).

The claim has consequences for the audience's assumption about their own standing. A reminder addressed to the whole world cannot belong exclusively to one city, one clan, or one line of descent, and the sūrah has just said that the message would be entrusted to a people who would not disbelieve in it (6:89). The two sentences together define the message's reach and remove the audience's ability to treat it as a family possession.

**The Prophet's Position Among the Guided**

The verse places the Prophet in the line of the catalogue without diminishing his mission. He is told to follow the guidance the earlier men received, and the Book elsewhere says that God has elevated him in rank above the others and given him what the earlier messengers were given. The Book's teaching on the prophets is that they form a single community with a single message and that God has favoured some of them over others in particular respects (2:253).

The audience was being asked to consider what it means that the man addressing them stood in that line. The sūrah has named the men they honoured, shown what they received, warned of what could have ruined them, and announced the transfer of the trust. Now it commands the Prophet to follow them, which means the message he was bringing was their message, and the argument about who owned it was over.

**The Transition to the Next Subject**

The next verse turns sharply from the prophets to their opponents with a statement about those who have not shown God His proper reverence, since they said that God had revealed nothing to any human being (6:91). The contrast is deliberate: the line of men who received the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood is set against a claim that revelation does not happen at all.

The sūrah has been building toward that confrontation for a long stretch of verses, and the two declarations in this verse supply the terms of it. The message is the continuation of a chain and the reminder is for everyone, and the next verses take up the argument with those who denied that any of it had been sent down.
