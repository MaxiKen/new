## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:48

> Many are the societies whose end We delayed while they did wrong, then seized them. And to Me is the final return.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Restates Verse 45 With One Word Changed**

*Wa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin amlaytu lahā wa-hiya ẓālimatun thumma akhadhtuhā* — and how many a town did I grant respite while it was doing wrong, then I seized it.

The sentence is almost identical to verse 45, and the difference is one verb. Verse 45 said *ahlaknāhā*, We destroyed it. Verse 48 says *amlaytu lahā*, I gave it rein. The destruction is still there, at the end, but the emphasis has moved from the end to the interval.

This is a deliberate repetition with a purpose, and recognising the purpose is the key to the passage. Verse 45 established that destruction happens. Verse 48 establishes what happens in between: a length of time, granted by God, during which the wrongdoing continued and nothing appeared to be done about it.

The pronoun shifts with the verb. Verse 45 said *ahlaknāhā* in the plural of majesty; verse 48 says *amlaytu lahā*, I gave it rein, in the first person singular. The act of granting time is attributed personally to God, and the sūrah will do the same two clauses later, where the destination is likewise *to Me*.

**Delay as a Deliberate Act**

The verb *amlā* is significant. It is the word for letting out a rope, for giving an animal more rein, for allowing someone to continue without interruption. It describes a decision rather than an oversight.

That has a consequence for how the interval is read. The centuries during which a wrongdoing community flourished were not a period in which God was unaware or unable. They were granted. The Qur'an has a technical term for this condition and a doctrine built on it — the drawing-on of a person by stages, while he interprets the silence as approval.

Sūrah al-Anʿām states the mechanism: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!”* (6:44). The gifts keep arriving, the warnings stop feeling urgent, and the recipient concludes that the silence of heaven is a verdict.

**"Then I Seized It"**

*Thumma akhadhtuhā* — then I took it. As in verse 44, the verb is plain and the *thumma* marks an interval whose length is not stated.

The pairing of the two clauses is the whole doctrine: a long silence, then a single action. The sūrah is describing the same pattern it has described three times already — in the respite granted to the deniers of verse 44, in the Hour that is coming rather than arrived (22:7), and now here.

For the audience in Makkah, the application was direct and uncomfortable. They were, at that moment, inside the interval. The town they lived in was prosperous, the trade routes were working, and nothing had happened to them. The verse's point is that this is exactly what the interval before the seizure looks like, in every recorded case.

The interval is not described as a period of improvement. The wrongdoing continued through it — *wa-hiya ẓālimatun* is circumstantial here as it was in verse 45 — which means the respite was granted while the thing that provoked the seizure was still being done.

**"To Me Is the Destination"**

*Wa-ilayya al-maṣīr* — and to Me is the place of arrival. The noun is from the root meaning to become, to arrive at a state, to end up somewhere.

The clause is short and it closes the passage, and it does two things at once. It states where the delayed communities went — not merely that they were destroyed but that they arrived somewhere, and that the somewhere is God. And it states the same for the people hearing the verse: the destination is the same for everyone, and the only question is the condition in which a person arrives.

The word is *maṣīr*, the place at which a journey ends, and it is the same noun the sūrah will use in its closing movement for the Fire (22:72). Here it is neutral: a destination rather than a sentence. The communities arrived somewhere, and the audience will too — which is why the clause can serve as a threat and a comfort at once, depending on the condition in which a person arrives.

**Why the Repetition Is Not Redundancy**

A reader in a hurry will see verses 45 and 48 as the same verse twice, and will be wrong. Verse 45 was addressed to the evidence: look at what is left of them. Verse 48 is addressed to the objection: they were given time, and you are being given time.

The two are answering different questions. The first answers "does God destroy communities?" The second answers "then why is nothing happening?" — which is precisely the question the previous verse recorded being thrown at the Prophet.

The two questions are asked by two different people. The first is the question of a reader looking at evidence and wondering whether the pattern is real; the second is the question of a person living inside the interval and wondering whether the pattern applies to him. The sūrah answers both, and the order matters — a community that has accepted that God destroys towns has still to accept that the silence it is currently enjoying is the same silence that preceded every destruction on the list.

**The Sūrah Returns to Where It Began**

The respite theme is what ties this passage back to the sūrah's opening, and the connection is worth drawing out because the chapter is nearly two-thirds over by now.

Verse 1 announced a quaking that had not happened. Verse 5 addressed people *in doubt* about a resurrection that had not happened. Verse 7 said the Hour is coming. Verses 8 to 13 described people who argued without ground. Verses 42 to 48 have now supplied the historical pattern behind all of it: communities are given length, and then they are taken.

The sūrah will return to the same point one more time, in verse 55, where the disbelievers are said to remain in doubt until the Hour comes upon them suddenly. That verse is the closing bracket on an argument this sūrah has been making since its first line.

Read in that light, the delay is not a subplot of the sūrah; it is the sūrah's central subject, and the pilgrimage passage at its heart is the rite a community performs while it waits.

**The Passage's Conclusion**

Verses 42 to 48 form one movement, and its shape is worth seeing whole. Nine communities, all denied, all given length, all seized. Ruins visible on the trade routes. A demand for the punishment to be hurried. An answer about the measure of time. And then this verse, which restates the entire pattern in a single sentence with the delay put at the centre.

What the sūrah has established by the end of it is that the apparent inactivity of God is a feature of how history runs, and that it has never, in any recorded case, meant what the mockers take it to mean.

What the passage leaves its reader with is a position rather than a prediction. Nine communities were given length and taken; the audience is being given length; and the only variable left open is what the length is used for. That is why the next movement turns immediately to the faculty that would have to change — hearts that travel and do not see (22:46) — rather than to the production of more evidence.
