## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:36

> And two other servants went to jail with Joseph. One of them said, “I dreamt I was pressing wine.” The other said, “I dreamt I was carrying ˹some˺ bread on my head, from which birds were eating.” ˹Then both said,˺ “Tell us their interpretation, for we surely see you as one of the gooddoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Servants of the King, Imprisoned for a Different Reason**

The word for the two men is *fatayān*, young men or servants, and the commentaries identify them as members of the king's household — one his cupbearer, the other his baker. The reported occasion is a plot to poison the king: the baker was caught and imprisoned, and the cupbearer was imprisoned because the king suspected him of complicity. The biblical account preserves the same two offices and the same setting, with the pair committed to custody in the place where Joseph was confined (Genesis 40:1–3). The Qur'an gives no reason for their arrest at all, which is characteristic of its handling of everything except Joseph. What matters for the story is not why they are there but that they belong to the royal household, and that fact is the hinge on which the entire second half of the chapter turns.

**They Enter With Him, Which the Verse States Plainly**

*Dakhala maʿahu al-sijna* — two young men entered the prison with him. The preposition does the work. Joseph is not discovered in a cell already populated; the verse synchronises the three arrivals so that the reader understands the meeting as arranged rather than accidental. Nothing else in the chapter's Egyptian section happens by coincidence that is not also marked as providence, and this is the clearest instance. A man imprisoned to stop a rumour is placed in the same building as two servants of the crown, at the same time, and the narrative records the conjunction without comment. The following verses will show what the conjunction was for.

**Two Dreams Reported in the Plainest Possible Terms**

The content of the visions is domestic and occupational. One man sees himself pressing wine; the other sees himself carrying bread on his head while birds eat from it. There is nothing symbolic in the ordinary sense — no animals devouring one another, no celestial bodies, no numbers requiring arithmetic. Each dream is drawn from the dreamer's own work, which is why neither of them can read it. The imagery also carries the answer inside it, though the dreamers cannot see that: pressing wine implies a return to the cupbearer's post, and birds eating bread from a man's head implies a body left exposed. Joseph will state both outcomes in verse 41 in the same plain register, and the symmetry between the two dreams is what makes the double interpretation credible rather than arbitrary.

**The Request Is Addressed to a Fellow Prisoner**

*Inform us of their interpretation.* The men are asking a slave purchased for twenty dirhams, imprisoned on a charge the household had already discredited, with no standing and no prospects. The request makes sense only because of the clause that follows it, and the clause is the verse's most important sentence. Nothing in Joseph's appearance, his clothing or his legal position recommends him as an interpreter of dreams. What recommends him is his behaviour in the prison, which the two men have been watching long enough to form a judgement about.

**"We See You as One of the Good-Doers" Is a Reputation Earned in a Cell**

The word is *muḥsinīn*, the doers of good — the same term the chapter applied to Joseph in verse 22 as the reason for his reward, and the term that will appear again in verses 56, 78 and 90. Here it is spoken by two strangers in a prison. Al-Rāzī explains the basis of their judgement: they had observed his character, his kindness toward the other inmates and the regularity of his worship, and concluded that a man of that quality could be trusted with an interpretation. The inference is not that piety produces technical skill. It is that piety produces credibility, and credibility is what makes an interpretation receivable. A dream interpreter whom nobody trusts is functionally not an interpreter at all, and Joseph acquired the necessary trust in the least promising institution in Egypt.

**The Verse Opens the Second Movement of the Story**

Structurally, verse 36 closes the Egyptian household and opens the prison. The seduction arc has ended with an innocent man in a cell and his accusers unpunished; the elevation arc begins with two royal servants asking him a question. The gift promised by his father in verse 6 and stated as the purpose of his establishment in verse 21 is now about to be exercised for the first time in public. The chapter has been careful to establish that this gift is a divine teaching rather than an acquired technique, and equally careful to establish that Joseph's character survived the test that preceded it. Both qualifications are needed for what comes next, because the interpretation he is about to give will decide two men's fates and, through one of them, the fate of a kingdom.

The Qur'an's account differs from the biblical one in ways that are worth noting. Genesis 40 supplies the king's anger, the custody of the two officials under the captain of the guard, Joseph's own dejection at their appearance, and his complaint that nobody has remembered him. This verse supplies two men, two dreams and one request. The emotional interior of the prisoners is not given, the reason for their arrest is not given, and Joseph's state of mind is not given either. What is given instead is their assessment of him, in the final clause, and that assessment does the work all the omitted detail would have done. The chapter's method of characterisation is consistently external: it tells the reader what a person is by reporting what others say about him after long acquaintance, which is the only evidence that carries weight in a story about false accounts.
