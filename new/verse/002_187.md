## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:187

> It has been made permissible for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast. Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them. Allah knows that you were deceiving yourselves. So He has accepted your repentance and pardoned you. So now you may be intimate with them and seek what Allah has prescribed for you. ˹You may˺ eat and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night, then complete the fast until nightfall. Do not be intimate with your spouses while you are meditating in the mosques. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not exceed them. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to people, so they may become mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rule and Its Early Hardship**

The verse legislates by easing, and the reports behind it describe what was eased. In the fast's first years the night was not free: food and marital relations were permitted only until the night prayer, after which the fast effectively began — one school of reports even describing abstinence through the entire month's nights, with the concession coming only in response to the community's failure to keep it. The case the tradition names most often is ʿUmar's: he confessed to the Prophet that he had approached his wife after the night prayer, and the answer came that he had not been firm in holding to the original restriction — a rebuke accompanied by relief, since the verse that followed made the whole night lawful. Al-Qurṭubī preserves the companion case of Qays ibn Ṣirmah, who worked his land all day during Ramadan, came home exhausted, and — his wife having gone to fetch food while he slept, which under the old rule ended his eating for the day — passed out; the next day's collapse in the Prophet's presence brought the question of the rule's severity into the open. Against this background the verse's opening verb is significant: it has been *made permissible* — a permission granted, an earlier narrowness lifted by the same authority that imposed it.

**They Are a Garment for You**

The easing arrives with the Qur'an's most intimate marriage image: your wives are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them. The exegetes read the metaphor in both directions — the covering, the protection, the mutual envelope spouses provide one another, each concealing what the other would not wish exposed and each warming what the other cannot warm alone — and the reciprocity is the image's heart: the garment clause runs both ways in a single sentence, making the marriage a mutual indwelling rather than a one-sided shelter. The choice of metaphor serves the law beside it: the night before the fast belongs to the marriage, and the marriage belongs to both parties equally. The verse had legislated the fast as a full day's abstinence from food, drink, and sexual relations; this clause restores the third to its night, and in restoring it describes what is restored — not an indulgence tolerated between obligations but the spouses' mutual covering, itself a good the fast's daytime suspension now protects by bounding rather than destroys. The tradition drew the practical consequence the commentators state plainly: the concession is a relaxation within the worship, so that the month of the body's discipline is also the month of the marriage's lawful warmth.

**He Knew You Were Deceiving Yourselves**

Between the permission and its image stands a diagnosis: *God knew that you were deceiving yourselves* — betraying yourselves, as the word's root suggests — and He turned to you and pardoned you. The self-deception named is the community's under the old rule: the reports of ʿUmar and others describe believers failing the night-restriction and then bearing the failure as guilt, and the verse identifies the double harm — the rule exceeded the people's constancy, and the exceeding produced not merely infractions but self-betrayal, the worshiper at war with his own nature inside his own devotion. God's knowledge of this is the verse's tender clause: the Lawgiver watched the community deceiving itself, and the response was not enforcement but *turning* — the same word the repentance-passages use, God turning to the sinner — followed by pardon. The legislative easing is thus framed as an act of mercy rather than retreat: the law changed because the Lawgiver knew, turned, and forgave, and the new permission arrives inside that sequence. The verse teaches a principle of revelation's dealing with human weakness: the command is not adjusted to appetite, but the community's honest failure is met by a mercy that re-legislates — the fast remains, the night is freed, and the self-deception ends.

**Seek What God Has Ordained**

The permission is then enlarged: be with them now, and seek what God has ordained for you. The exegetes read the seeking in two directions, both rooted in the word's usage. It is the seeking of children — the marital night open to the generation the fast's daytime rule suspends, the ordinance of progeny continuing its course through the holy month. And it is the seeking of the Night of Power within those same nights — the night better than a thousand months, on which the Qur'an was sent down (97:1), sought by the worshiper in the month's latter part even as the marriage keeps its rights in the month's every night. The double reading composes the verse's vision of Ramadan's darkness: it is not a void between fasted days but a field of ordained goods — the children conceived, the night-prayer kept, the decree-night watched for — and the concession that freed the night for the marriage freed it for all of these at once. The wording *what God has ordained for you* keeps the emphasis where the easing placed it: the night's goods are not tolerations of the flesh but ordinances of the Lawgiver, licensed from the same hand that licensed the day's abstinence, so that the believer moves between the two without contradiction — the fast's discipline by daylight, the ordainment's warmth by dark, both commanded, both worship.

**The White Thread and the Black Thread**

The verse's most famous detail fixes the fast's beginning: eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct from the black thread of night. The idiom — the two threads as the first light against the last dark — was initially taken literally by some Companions, and al-Bukhārī preserves the case of ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim (1917): he placed a black cord and a white cord under his pillow and watched them through the night, unable to tell one from the other, until the Prophet explained that the verse means the darkness of night and the whiteness of dawn. The report does double duty. It documents the community's scrupulosity — a man watching cords rather than guess at his fast's boundary — and it models the Qur'an's idiom being read as idiom: the threads are the horizon's two colors, not two objects, and the distinction is the true dawn's lateral spreading light against the night. The other reports of the verse's descent add the phenomenon's detail: the light of dawn appearing in the sky, the tops of mountains and houses catching illumination, the white thread emerging from the black. The resulting law is the fast's daily architecture — from true dawn to sunset, the boundary marked by light itself, so that every fasting community reads its obligation off its own sky, and ʿAdī's cords remain the tradition's emblem of literalism corrected by the Prophet's own exegesis.

**Retreat, Limits, and the Signs Made Clear**

The verse closes with three clauses that frame the whole legislation. First, the boundary on the concession: do not approach your wives while you are in retreat in the mosques — the *iʿtikāf*, the practice of staying in the mosque for days of prayer and remembrance, which the Prophet observed in Ramadan's last ten days, and twenty days in the year of his death. The retreat suspends even the night's permission: the mosque-stayer has left the marriage's hours entirely for worship, and the law marks that leaving as inviolable. Then the formula: *those are the limits of God — do not approach them*. The wording is deliberately stronger than transgression-language: the limits are not merely not to be crossed but not to be approached, the believer stationed away from the boundary rather than balanced on it — the same precaution the sūrah applied to the property of orphans and the sacred months. And finally the verse's own purpose: thus God makes His signs clear to people, that they may be mindful. The clarity is the gift — the fast's hours defined, its nights freed, its exemptions itemized, its retreat bounded, nothing left to guesswork — and the clarity serves the same end every law in this passage has served: taqwā. The verse that opened by easing the marriage-night closes by fencing it, and between the easing and the fence the whole Ramadan stands legislated: the day of discipline, the night of ordinance, the mosque of retreat, and over all of them the limits made clear by the God who knew, turned, and pardoned.
