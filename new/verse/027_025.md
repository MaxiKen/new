## Sūrah an-Naml 27:25

> so they do not prostrate to Allah, Who brings forth what is hidden in the heavens and the earth, and knows what you ˹all˺ conceal and what you reveal.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Bird Makes a Theological Argument**

The hoopoe has finished reporting and now reasons. Having described a people prostrating to the sun, he states what they are failing to do and describes the one they are failing to do it to — not by name alone but by two activities. God brings out what is hidden; God knows what is concealed and what is shown.

The argument is compact and it is aimed. Sun worship is worship of the visible. A creature answering it points to the invisible, twice: to what lies hidden in the heavens and earth, and to what lies hidden inside people. The sun illuminates surfaces and nothing more. Whatever is buried in the ground, folded in a cloud, or held in a chest is outside its reach entirely. The bird has selected the exact attribute that the object of Sheba's worship conspicuously lacks, and he has done it in one sentence.

**Two Ways to Read the Opening Word**

The verse begins with a word the reciters read in two ways, and both are canonical. Read as *allā*, it continues the previous verse: Satan turned them from the way, so that they do not prostrate. Read as *alā*, it becomes an exclamation and a rhetorical question: will they not prostrate to God?

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both record the two readings and treat them as accepted. The difference is one of grammar, not of doctrine. Under the first, the failure to prostrate is the consequence of the adornment described in verse 24, and the sentence is a diagnosis. Under the second, the hoopoe breaks off reporting and addresses an appeal to the court — a call, not a description. Each reading has support in the flow of the passage. The first keeps the syntax continuous with what precedes; the second explains the shift to direct address in the verse's final clause, where the pronoun turns to *you*. Neither reading can be eliminated, and Arabic manuscripts of the period, written without short vowels, would have carried both.

**"What Is Hidden" — Rain, Growth, or Everything?**

The phrase *al-khab'* means the concealed thing, and the commentators offered a range. Al-Ṭabarī reports rain among the interpretations — *ghayth*, the water held in the sky until it is released — and also the produce of the earth, the seed that lies under soil until it comes up. A broader reading takes it as everything in the heavens and earth known only to God, and al-Qurṭubī allows mysteries in general.

The concrete readings are the more powerful ones in context. Rain and crops are the two things a sun-worshipping agricultural kingdom most depends on, and they are precisely the two things the sun does not produce. Sheba was famous for irrigation; its later ruin came when a great dam failed and the water went. A people whose survival rested on water from the sky and growth from the soil were bowing to the one visible power that supplies neither. The hoopoe's argument lands on their own economy. Whatever they thought they were thanking the sun for, the two inputs that kept them alive came out of hiding, and something else was bringing them out.

**The Argument Turns on the Listener**

The verse's last clause changes its target. Up to this point the discussion has been about Sheba in the third person: they prostrate, they were barred, they are not guided. The final words are *mā tukhfūna wa mā tuʿlinūn* — what you conceal and what you reveal — in the second person plural.

The shift is abrupt and it is the sharpest thing in the speech. A bird defending himself before a king ends his report on a foreign nation's idolatry by reminding his audience that God knows what they are hiding. Whether the *you* addresses Sulaymān's court, the reader, or humanity at large, the effect is the same: the case study stops being about somebody else. This is a standard Qur'anic move — a narrative about a distant people closing on a pronoun that includes the listener — and its function is to prevent the comfortable reading in which idolatry is always what other people do. Concealment and disclosure are things everyone does daily, and the verse claims that the difference between them exists only for us.

**Prostration Is the Word the Whole Story Turns On**

*Sajada* has now appeared twice in two verses, and it will govern the rest of Sheba's narrative. She prostrates to the sun here. Later she will step onto a floor she mistakes for water, understand her error, and declare her submission along with Sulaymān to the Lord of all worlds. The story is, structurally, about redirecting one action.

The verb means to put the forehead to the ground, and Arabic uses it for the lowest physical position a body can take. Nothing about the gesture is ambiguous; whoever performs it has placed himself beneath whatever he performs it toward.

This is worth stating because it clarifies what the episode is not. Sheba is not conquered, not deposed, and not converted by force; the Qur'an never reports an army being sent. What changes is the direction of a bow. The hoopoe's speech here identifies that as the sole problem, the letter that follows will call for exactly that correction, and the demonstration in the palace will produce it. A chapter that contains jinn, transported thrones and armies of birds locates its decisive event in a single act of worship turning ninety degrees. The bird finishes his argument in the following verse by naming the one he has been describing.
