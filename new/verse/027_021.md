## Sūrah an-Naml 27:21

> I will surely subject him to a severe punishment, or ˹even˺ slaughter him, unless he brings me a compelling excuse.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Threat With an Exit Built Into It**

Sulaymān's sentence has three parts, and the third changes the meaning of the first two. Severe punishment, or execution, *unless* he produces a compelling excuse. The escape clause is not added afterwards under questioning; it is part of the original statement, spoken while the bird is still missing and before any defence has been heard.

That structure is what separates a judicial threat from a fit of temper. An angry king announces a punishment. This one announces a punishment and, in the same breath, the condition that would cancel it. The hoopoe is condemned in advance and acquitted in advance, and which of the two applies depends entirely on evidence he has not yet been asked for. Al-Ṭabarī reads the whole statement as conditional on the bird's failure to justify himself, which is the plain sense of the Arabic. Whatever else this is, it is not arbitrary. A subject of this kingdom knows the standard he will be judged by before he is judged.

**Why Absence Was a Serious Offence**

Modern readers usually find the severity disproportionate, and the reaction deserves an answer rather than an apology. The hoopoe was not a pet; verse 17 placed him in *junūd*, the military forces, marshalled in ranks. What is being described is desertion from an army on campaign.

Under that description the penalty stops being strange. Every organised military force in history has treated unexplained absence from the line as a capital matter, for reasons that have nothing to do with cruelty: a formation in which members leave at will is not a formation. The previous verses went out of their way to emphasise the discipline of this host, and discipline that carries no consequence is a description rather than a fact. There is also the specific danger the ant identified. An army that moves in tight order is dangerous precisely because it is reliable, and reliability requires every member to be where he is expected. The hoopoe's absence is a crack in the thing that makes the whole host safe to command.

**"A Compelling Excuse" — the Word Is About Proof**

The Arabic for the escape clause is *sulṭān mubīn*. *Sulṭān* is usually the word for authority or dominion, and it is the same root behind a ruler's title. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it here as clear evidence or a plain justification for the absence, and the shift of sense is natural in Arabic: an argument that has authority over the hearer is one he cannot resist.

The choice of word sets a high bar. Sulaymān does not ask for an apology, an explanation, or a reason. He asks for something with the force of proof — a justification that compels rather than requests. Note also the adjective, *mubīn*, clear, which by now has run through this chapter three times: the clear Book in verse 1, the manifest sorcery of Pharaoh's court in verse 13, the clear favour Sulaymān named in verse 16. The standard being applied to the bird is the same standard the chapter applies to everything else. Bring something unambiguous, or the claim fails.

**The King Does Not Judge Before He Hears**

Read this verse against the one before it and a procedure emerges. Verse 20 had Sulaymān questioning his own perception first — perhaps I am not seeing him — before entertaining the possibility of absence. Verse 21 states the penalty, and attaches to it the condition that the accused may speak.

Announcing a penalty is not the same as imposing one, and the distinction is the difference between law and violence.

No sentence is carried out in this verse. Nothing is done at all; a rule is announced. The hoopoe, when he returns, is not seized, and the verses that follow show him speaking first and at length, without interruption, before any response from the king. Whatever the harshness of the language here, the practice that follows it is a hearing. Al-Qurṭubī, discussing Sulaymān's conduct in this episode, treats the whole sequence as an illustration of governance rather than of severity, and the text supports that reading: the threat exists to make the inquiry serious, and the inquiry actually happens.

**Anger Is Recorded, Not Edited Out**

The Qur'an could have reported this scene without the threat. It would have lost nothing narratively — the bird returns, delivers his news, and the story proceeds to Sheba. Instead the text preserves a prophet-king saying he will punish or kill a subordinate, in words strong enough to make later readers uncomfortable.

The pattern holds across the Book. Yūnus left his post in frustration and the Qur'an says so; Yaʿqūb wept until his sight failed; Ayyūb complained of what had touched him. None of these moments is softened, and none of them is held against the man.

This candour is characteristic and worth naming. The same chapter recorded Mūsā running away from a staff. Both details make their subjects less impressive and both are kept. The Qur'an's prophets are shown reacting — with fear, with anger, with impatience — and the reactions are neither praised nor censured but simply reported as part of what happened. What is consistently marked is what they do next. Mūsā was reassured and went to Pharaoh. Sulaymān, having threatened, listened. There is a further point in the specific offence that provoked him: the king is angry about a breach of order in the ranks, which is the one thing he has responsibility for. The bird's answer arrives immediately in the following verse. The threat is stated in full and then immediately qualified, which is the pattern a legal proceeding follows rather than the pattern of an outburst.
