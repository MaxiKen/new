## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:183

> Those ˹are the same people˺ who say, “Allah has commanded us not to believe in any messenger unless he brings us an offering to be consumed by fire ˹from the sky˺.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Other prophets did in fact come to you before me with clear proofs and ˹even˺ what you demanded—why then did you kill them, if what you say is true?”

**Expanded Commentary**


The verse reports a claim made by a group: that God had bound them not to believe in any messenger until he brought them an offering consumed by fire from heaven. The sentence presents the claim as the group's own account of its obligation.

The Book records several such claims made by the group in question, and it treats them as conditions that the claimants had constructed for themselves. The verse reports this one in the form the claimants used, and the reply that follows takes up the terms of their own condition.

**The Historical Challenge**

The commentators describe the occasion as a challenge that prominent members of the Jewish community in Madinah put to the Prophet. They asked him to produce a sacrificial offering that fire from heaven would consume, presenting it as the criterion they had always required.

The commentaries observe that fire descending on an offering is described in their own scriptures in connection with a number of prophets, and they list the occasions. The challenge therefore drew on a tradition the group knew, and the verse's reply treats it as a criterion that had been applied selectively.

**Why the Challenge Was Empty**

Al-Rāzī and others note that the sign demanded had not been granted to all the prophets of Israel, and that the practice of animal sacrifice at the temple had ceased long before. By the time the challenge was made, the group was setting a condition that its own history had already made impossible.

The observation explains the reply's tone, since the condition the group had set was one its own tradition had already answered. The Prophet is told to point out that messengers had brought the proofs they asked for, and that the ancestors of the group had killed some of those messengers. The condition the group proposed had been met before and had not produced the belief it was supposed to require.

**The Reply and Its Logic**

The reply is stated as a question: messengers came before me with clear proofs and with that of which you speak, so why did you kill them, if you are truthful? The sentence turns the challenge back on the challengers by using their own criterion as evidence against them.

The Book's manner of arguing in such passages is to show that the objection raised by its opponents condemns them by their own record. The reply does not dispute the value of a spectacular sign; it observes that the sign had been given in the past without producing the result the group claimed it would produce.

**The Killing of the Prophets Revisited**

The verse's closing question refers to a subject that the sūrah has just raised in its account of those who spoke against God. The Book states in another sūrah that each time a messenger came to the people of Israel with what they did not want, they rejected some and killed others.

A further passage describes a community that made a covenant and then broke it, rejecting the signs and killing the prophets unjustly. The verse before the reader takes the same history and uses it as the answer to a demand for further proof.

**The Standard the Group Proposed**

The passage's argument concerns the standards people use to decide what to accept. The group proposed a test that its own history had already answered, and the reply shows what the test had produced when it was applied.

The Book treats such standards with respect when they are honest, and it answers them by referring to the evidence that had already been given. The verse's reply does not dismiss the value of a sign; it observes that the sign had been supplied before, in the history the group itself claimed to honour. One of the sūrah's earlier verses states that a group of the People of the Book recited the signs of God through the night while prostrating, and the Book's argument throughout this section is with those who had evidence and did not act on it.

**The Verse's Connection With the Earlier Statements**

The verse follows the taunt about God's poverty and the general statement about what deeds are recorded, and it continues the sūrah's account of the community's opponents. The two passages together describe the same quarter in the same posture.

The connection between them is the group's treatment of the messengers. The earlier verse mentioned the killing of the prophets as part of the record, and this verse makes the same history the basis of an argument. The sūrah is teaching its readers how the refusal of the message had actually operated in the past, and it does so in a form that its own community could apply to the arguments it was meeting in Madinah.


One of the readings recorded in the commentaries applies the verse to the inner life, describing the soul's refusal to follow the way and its production of excuses designed to satisfy itself. On this reading the demand for a spectacular sign is the mind's way of postponing a decision it has already made.

The reading is offered as an application rather than as the verse's plain sense, and it has been useful in the community's teaching about self-deception. The verse's own reply is a matter of history, and the application draws the same lesson from it for anyone who sets conditions on what he will accept, since a delay in accepting what is evident is usually a matter of the will rather than of the evidence.
