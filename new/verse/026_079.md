## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:79

> ˹He is˺ the One Who provides me with food and drink.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham Sees Provision as a Divine Act**

Abraham continues his description of the Lord by naming food and drink. These are ordinary necessities, but their ordinariness is precisely the point. Divine care is not limited to extraordinary signs. The Lord who created and guides also provides what sustains the body day after day. Abraham's worship grows from noticing the familiar gifts that habit can make invisible.

Food and drink come through many visible means: cultivation, labor, animals, water, trade, and the hands of other people. Abraham's statement does not deny those means. It identifies the ultimate Provider behind them. Created causes have a role, but they do not become independent lords. The idol cannot produce the life it claims to protect, while the Lord gives the conditions under which every meal and drink become possible.

**Provision Shows Continuing Dependence**

Creation describes the beginning of Abraham's existence; provision describes its continuation. He is not created and then left to maintain himself. Every day exposes his dependence again. Hunger and thirst remind the body that it does not contain the source of its own preservation. The Lord's provision is therefore a repeated lesson in servanthood.

This dependence is not humiliating in the sense of degrading the human being. It is the truth that protects him from false pride. A person may plan, work, store, and share, but he cannot make himself independent of the created order or guarantee the result of his labor. Recognizing provision as God's gift turns need into gratitude rather than into worship of the means.

**Food and Drink Make Theology Practical**

A theology of provision must change daily conduct. If food and drink are gifts from God, waste and arrogance become difficult to defend. The person who receives provision should remember those who lack it and treat the means of provision as trusts rather than possessions without limit. Gratitude is expressed not only in words but in restraint, sharing, and lawful use.

The verse does not offer a detailed economic program, yet it establishes a moral orientation. Provision comes from the Lord, so no human being should claim absolute ownership over what God has placed within creation. Work remains meaningful, and exchange remains possible, but the receiver is never entitled to forget the Giver or despise those whose provision has been restricted.

**The Lord Provides Through a World of Causes**

Some may think that saying God provides ignores farmers, cooks, parents, merchants, or the natural processes that make food available. Abraham's wording teaches the opposite. Those means can be appreciated without being elevated into gods. The Lord's provision is often mediated through ordinary effort, which makes gratitude responsible rather than passive.

A person should therefore thank God and acknowledge human service. The two responses are not rivals. Gratitude to the Provider gives dignity to the people who serve as means, while gratitude to the people remains proportionate and does not turn them into ultimate sources. The idol is not needed to complete this chain. God governs the chain from beginning to end.

**Provision Reaches Body and Spirit**

Food and drink nourish the body, but the verse also invites a wider awareness of dependence. The human being needs physical sustenance and also needs guidance, meaning, companionship, and mercy. The same Lord who provides the meal provides the direction by which the meal should be received and used. Material gifts become fully good when they are joined to right purpose.

This keeps spirituality from despising bodily need. Abraham does not speak as though worship requires pretending that food and drink do not matter. The body is part of creation and is cared for by the Creator. At the same time, bodily provision is not the whole of success. It should lead the recipient to gratitude, not to the conclusion that material abundance itself is a god.

**Hunger Exposes the Limits of Human Control**

People can make remarkable arrangements for obtaining food and water, but no arrangement makes them absolute providers. Drought, illness, conflict, loss, and changes beyond individual control can interrupt the plans on which a household depends. Abraham's sentence does not invite fear. It locates security in the Lord rather than in a fragile system of human control.

This reliance should not become an excuse for neglecting preparation or justice. To trust the Provider is to use the means responsibly while knowing that means are not sovereign. A person plants, earns, stores, and shares, then leaves the outcome to God. Such action combines effort with humility and resists both fatalism and self-worship.

**Provision Creates a Community of Gratitude**

No one eats entirely alone in the moral sense. Food carries the labor of many people and the gifts of soil, water, animals, and time. Remembering God as Provider widens the heart's field of gratitude. It becomes harder to treat another person's hunger as irrelevant when one's own nourishment is understood as mercy rather than entitlement.

Abraham's singular “me” does not make provision individualistic. He speaks from personal dependence so that the listener can recognize his own. Each person is invited to see the same relation. The Lord provides for servants through different portions and circumstances, but every portion remains a reason to thank Him and to act mercifully toward others.

**The Verse Keeps Worship Close to Ordinary Life**

The idols may be associated with festivals and dramatic rituals, but Abraham points to food and drink. He grounds worship in the daily fact that life is sustained. A person need not wait for a spectacular miracle to recognize God's care. The next meal, the water that relieves thirst, and the means by which they arrive can all become reminders of the Provider.

That remembrance changes the meaning of routine. Eating is no longer merely consumption; it can become gratitude. Receiving is no longer a reason for pride; it becomes an occasion for humility. The Lord who feeds Abraham is worthy of worship not only in moments of danger but in the quiet continuance of life.
