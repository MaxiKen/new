## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:58

> ˹As for˺ those who believe and do good, We will certainly house them in ˹elevated˺ mansions in Paradise, under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness!˺—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise Framed as a Residence**

The reward is announced in the vocabulary of housing, and the choice is deliberate in a chapter that has just instructed believers to leave their homes for the sake of their worship. The Book does not say the emigrants will be compensated with gardens; it says they will be settled in elevated chambers, and the verb carries the sense of giving a person a place to stay after a period of movement. Sūrah al-Naḥl uses the same language for the reward of this life when it promises those who emigrated after being persecuted a good home, while noting that the reward of the hereafter is greater (16:41). The sūrah under discussion moves directly to the greater one, and it describes a dwelling that no hostile authority can take from its occupants.

The order of the two sentences is part of the instruction. A promise offered before a warning reads as an inducement, while the same promise placed after one reads as an assurance to people who have already been told what their position costs. The Book follows that sequence whenever it addresses a community under pressure, and it does so here because the audience had just been commanded to leave the ground they were standing on. What they received first was the difficulty of the command; what followed was the address they were moving toward.

**Chambers Raised One Above the Other**

Commentators note that the mansions promised here are described in another chapter as elevated chambers built one above the other with rivers flowing beneath them, and the Book attaches a guarantee to that description: it is the promise of God, and God never fails in His promise (39:20). The architecture is worth pausing over. Elevation in the Book is consistently associated with rank and with security from what lies below, and the chambers of this verse are described as elevated within a garden rather than as a tower in a landscape. The image answers the sūrah's earlier picture of the spider's house directly. Where a person's shelter was once a structure he spun for himself, it is now a residence prepared for him, and the two are as different in origin as they are in permanence.

**The Reward of Those Who Work**

The exclamation that closes the verse names the recipients as workers of righteousness, and the title is not incidental. The Book's promises in this sūrah have been attached to belief joined with action from the beginning, and the phrase here keeps the requirement in view at the moment of reward. Commentators explain the pairing by noting that belief provides the direction and work provides the substance of a life, and that a reward described as a wage implies the labour it pays for. The Book never presents the gardens as a gift given without reference to what a person did, and it never presents the deeds as effective without the belief that directed them. The exclamation is a verdict on the whole arrangement rather than on the builders' strength.

**Forever, in a Chapter About Temporary Things**

The phrase that describes the residence as permanent stands out against the sūrah's long catalogue of things that end. Idols cannot provide, patrons disown their clients, wealth sinks with its owner, cities are overturned, and every soul tastes death. Against all of that, the verse places a dwelling described with the word the Book reserves for what does not pass. The contrast is the sūrah's final answer to the question it raised in its opening verses about what a person can rely on. Everything the chapter has examined for durability has failed except one thing, and the sentence that describes it uses rivers, chambers, and permanence to say what cannot be said directly.

The word stands at the close of a chapter that has described present arrangements as passing, and it is placed there deliberately. Every dwelling the sūrah has named so far belongs to a category with an ending: the house of the spider, the towns that were overturned, the sanctuary the Quraysh enjoyed in safety. The dwellings promised here are the first in the sūrah that are not described with a limit, and that contrast is the point of the sentence.

**The Rooms That Are Seen From Within and Without**

The Prophet described these dwellings in a report transmitted on the authority of ʿAlī and graded ḥasan by al-Albānī: in Paradise there are rooms whose outside can be seen from within them and whose inside can be seen from without, and when a man from the desert asked who they were for, he answered that they are for those who speak good words, feed others, fast regularly, and stand in prayer at night while people sleep (Sunan al-Tirmidhī, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī). The description is unusual in its emphasis on visibility. The rooms are not hidden behind walls but transparent, which fits a dwelling whose occupants have nothing to conceal, and the qualifications attached in the report are the practices of people who had spent their lives on exactly that.

The verb the verse uses for the reward's condition is the ordinary word for labour, and it covers effort that produces nothing visible for a long time. A community scattered by pressure, working at trades in towns where it had no standing, is exactly the audience the sentence fits, and the promise attaches to the effort rather than to the result. Nothing in the wording requires the worker to see the outcome of his work, which is the assurance a migrant household most needed.

**Why the Promise Follows the Warning**

The placing of this verse immediately after the description of the fire is part of the sūrah's method of closing. A reader who has just been told that the punishment will come from above and below is now told what the alternative looks like, and the alternative is described with the same concreteness. The Book's habit of ending a passage of threat with a passage of promise serves the reader who is still alive and still deciding, which is the only reader it ever addresses. The following verse names the two qualities that belong to the people being promised this residence, and it does so in a way that ties the whole chapter's teaching about trial to the reward it now describes.

**A Home That Cannot Be Emigrated Away From**

The audience receiving this promise was made up of people who had left homes, or were considering it, or had watched others leave. The Book's answer to the loss is not a sentimental assurance that the old house will be restored; it is the announcement of a dwelling whose tenancy is described as permanent, with rivers running beneath it and no clause that could end the arrangement. Commentators link the promise to the difficulties of migration directly, noting that whatever a believer gives up for the sake of God is answered by these chambers if not in this life, then in the next. The reward is therefore presented as a settlement rather than a recompense in cash, and the difference matters to people who had spent their lives in a city where a person's standing depended on the house his family owned. The verse replaces that standing with a lease that no authority on earth can revise.
