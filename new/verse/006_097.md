## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:97

> And He is the One Who has made the stars as your guide through the darkness of land and sea. We have already made the signs clear for people who know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Stars as Guides**

And He is the One Who has made the stars as your guide through the darkness of land and sea. The sūrah has just described the sun and the moon marking time; this verse names the stars and gives them a different function, which is navigation. The Book uses the same image in the sūrah of the Bee, where it says that by landmarks and stars people find their way (16:16), and it repeats the phrase about the darkness of land and sea in the passage on the keys of the unseen (6:59).

The pairing of land and sea covers the two forms of travel the Makkans knew, and both depended on the night sky. A caravan crossing the desert steered by the stars, and a ship on the water did the same. The Book takes the most ordinary use of the heavens in the audience's daily economic life and presents it as a gift with a purpose, which is the same method it used with the seed, the dawn, and the night.

**The Stars as Subjected Creatures**

The Book's account of the stars in other sūrahs makes their status plain. The sūrah of the Heights describes the sun, the moon, and the stars as subjected by God's command and says that creation and command belong to Him alone (7:54); the sūrah of the Bee lists the stars among the things made subject for human benefit and says that in this there are signs for people who understand (16:12); and the sūrah of the Discernment describes the constellations God placed in the sky along with a radiant lamp and a luminous moon (25:61).

There is a pointed contrast between the Book's treatment of the stars and the practices of the audience. The Makkans venerated certain heavenly bodies and consulted astrologers about their influence, and the sūrah of the Cattle earlier showed Abraham testing the star and rejecting it when it set (6:76). The same objects that were folded into the region's astrology are presented here as servants of the travellers who used them.

**The Darkness That Requires a Guide**

The phrase about the darkness of land and sea deserves attention because the Book regularly uses darkness as an image of confusion. The sūrah described the man seduced by satans in the open country as one who has lost his way (6:71), and it uses darkness for the state of a person without guidance in several passages. The gift of the stars is therefore an image of guidance in the plain sense as well as a service to travellers.

The Book states that God guides the believers out of darkness into light in several sūrahs, and it describes the disbelievers as those in layers of darkness, one above another, so that a man cannot see his own hand. Against that background, the sentence about stars guiding through the dark is more than physical: it names the visible form of the guidance that the sūrah has been offering since its opening verse.

**The Signs Made Clear for People Who Know**

We have already made the signs clear for people who know. The sentence closes the verse with a statement about the audience's capacity rather than about the quality of the signs. The clarity has been supplied, and what the verse names is the kind of reader who benefits from it, which is the same qualification the sūrah attached to its earlier account of the varying of the signs (6:46).

The Book attaches the same qualification to other passages about its signs, saying that they are made clear for people who know, for people who understand, and for people who believe. The phrasing is the Book's way of stating that the evidence is not the variable. What differs between two people looking at the same sky is the knowledge they bring to it, and the sūrah has spent many verses describing what happens when that knowledge is absent.

**What the Audience Did With the Sky**

The sūrah has already recorded the audience's use of the heavens in the story of Abraham, where the star, the moon, and the sun were examined and abandoned as candidates for divinity (6:76–78). The same city that steered its caravans by the stars had also built its religion around them, and the two uses of the same objects are set side by side in the sūrah's argument. The verse in front of us names the gift of guidance through the night and leaves the reader to notice what the audience did with it.

That is the practical form of the sūrah's claim about knowledge. The stars were available to everyone in the region, and the difference between a traveller who reached his destination and one who lost the road lay partly in his ability to read them. The sūrah uses the image because it wants the audience to see the same arrangement in matters of revelation: the sign is there, and the reading of it is the work the Book asks for.

**The Series and Its Conclusion**

The passage that began with the seed and the fruit stone has now run through the daybreak, the night, the sun, the moon, and the stars, and each item has been an object the audience used or watched. The series is not a catalogue of wonders but a list of services: food from the soil, rest in the dark, a calendar, and a compass. The Book's argument in this sūrah typically proceeds in that way, since its aim is to show the audience that the powers they credited to other beings were exercised by the One who made them.

The verse ends with the audience's knowledge rather than with the signs themselves, and the sūrah's next verse continues the series with a different kind of evidence. Having described the order of the created world, the sūrah turns to the origination of human beings themselves, and it will make the same argument from the formation of a single person that it has just made from the sky.
