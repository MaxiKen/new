## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:36

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely ˹it is˺ my Lord ˹Who˺ gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. But most people do not know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply Attacks the Premise, Not the Boast**

The elite had claimed more wealth and more sons, and concluded that they were safe (34:35). The answer does not dispute the inventory and does not argue that they will in fact be punished. It goes underneath both and addresses the assumption connecting them: *inna rabbī yabsuṭu al-rizqa li-man yashāʾu wa yaqdir* — my Lord expands provision for whom He wills, and restricts it. If the distribution is decided by a will that is not explained, then the amount in a man's hands carries no information about his standing. The boast collapses not because its second half is false but because its first half was never evidence. This is the more economical refutation, and the harder one to answer. A man told that he will be punished can dispute the prediction; a man told that his wealth was never a signal has nothing left to point at.

**Two Verbs From the Vocabulary of Measuring**

The Arabic pairs *yabsuṭu* and *yaqdiru*, and both are physical words before they are economic ones. *Basaṭa* means to spread out, to unroll — the same root gives *bisāṭ*, a carpet laid flat, and *basṭ*, extension. *Qadara* means to measure out, to apportion, to set a limit; it is the same verb used when David was told to get the gauge of his armour links right (34:11) and when the travel stages of Sheba were measured out (34:18). The second word is important because it is not a word for deprivation. To restrict provision, in this vocabulary, is to measure it — to give a determined amount rather than an unrolled one. Neither state is described as a punishment or a reward. Both are described as acts of apportionment by the same hand, which is exactly what the elite's reasoning cannot accommodate.

**"To Whoever He Wills" — The Clause That Does the Work**

*Li-man yashāʾ*, for whom He wills, appears in the middle of the sentence and severs the link the opponents relied on. Had the verse said God expands provision for the grateful and restricts it for the ungrateful, the boast would have been refuted by substituting one signalling system for another, and the wealthy would merely have needed a new theory about who is approved. By declining to name a criterion, the verse removes the possibility of reading the distribution at all. This is not a statement that the distribution is arbitrary; the Qur'an calls the same Lord All-Wise in this sūrah's first verse (34:1). It is a statement that the criterion has not been published, which is a different thing. What follows from it is a rule of interpretation: no one may infer a verdict from a balance sheet, in either direction.

**The Formula Recurs Across the Book**

This sentence is one of the Qur'an's fixed statements and appears in nearly identical wording at (13:26), (17:30), (28:82), (29:62), (30:37), (39:52) and (42:12), as well as twice in this sūrah. Tracking its settings shows what it is for. Al-Qaṣaṣ places it in the mouths of those who had envied Qārūn's treasure the day before the earth swallowed him. Al-Rūm places it alongside the observation that people despair when restricted and grow arrogant when expanded. Al-Isrāʾ attaches it to a warning against killing children for fear of poverty. In every case the formula is deployed against a false inference drawn from someone's material condition — envy, despair, arrogance, fear. Sabaʾ uses it against the last of the sūrah's targets, the man who reads his own prosperity as a certificate.

**Why the Prophet Says "My Lord"**

The possessive in *rabbī* is worth noticing, because the verse could have said "Allah" or "your Lord". The Prophet is made to attribute the doctrine to his own Lord in a dispute with men who were wealthier than he was. This places the claim in the mouth of someone who loses by it socially and gains nothing from it materially — a speaker with less wealth and fewer sons than his opponents, asserting that wealth and sons prove nothing. The same first-person possessive returns at the end of this passage when the formula is repeated with an addition (34:39), and it recurs throughout the sūrah's closing sequence of *say* commands. The effect is to keep the argument personal. It is not an anonymous theological proposition being announced; it is one man's stated relationship with the One who apportions, offered against another party's stated relationship with their own accounts.

**"But Most People Do Not Know"**

The verse closes with the same clause that ended the announcement of the universal mission eight verses earlier (34:28), and the repetition is not filler. What most people do not know, in this setting, is specifically the thing just said — that the distribution carries no message. This is an accurate observation about human beings in any period. The reflex that reads success as merit and hardship as fault is close to universal, survives every argument against it, and reasserts itself in each generation's preferred vocabulary. The word is *yaʿlamūn*, know, rather than believe, which frames the error as a gap in understanding rather than an act of defiance.

**What the Doctrine Asks of the Poor as Well as the Rich**

A statement that cuts the link between wealth and divine favour has two edges, and the sūrah's audience contained people on both of them. For the elite, it removes a certificate they were relying on. For the believing poor of Mecca — the men and women the elite had just dismissed by comparison — it removes something too: the reading of their own hardship as a sign of abandonment. If an expanded provision proves nothing, a restricted one proves nothing either, and the verse's refusal to publish a criterion protects both parties from misreading their circumstances. Mary is shown answering exactly this question inside the sanctuary, asked where her provision came from, and replying that it is from God, *"Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit"* (3:37). The sūrah now turns from the false inference to the true one, and states what actually brings a person near.
