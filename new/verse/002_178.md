## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:178

> O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set for you in cases of murder— a free man for a free man, a slave for a slave, and a female for a female. But if the offender is pardoned by the victim’s guardian, then bloodmoney should be decided fairly and payment should be made courteously. This is a concession and a mercy from your Lord. But whoever transgresses after that will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Law Given Into a Feud**

The verse must be read inside the system it replaced. Pre-Islamic Arabia ran on the tribal vendetta: a killing was an injury to the clan, the clan's honor demanded blood, and the demand escalated far beyond the original crime — a tribe might answer one murder by killing many members of the killer's tribe, and might deliberately overbid the victim's status, taking a free man for a slave, a man for a woman, a notable for a person of low station. The feud thus served two economies at once: revenge for the past and prestige for the future, with the original crime quickly irrelevant as each retaliation justified the next. Into that escalating arithmetic this verse introduces the principle of equivalence: the retaliation is set — *kutila*, prescribed, written — as life for the life taken, person for person, and no more. The commentators are explicit that the verse's function is limitation: responsibility follows the nature of the crime precisely, so that retaliation can neither fall short of the crime nor exceed it. The feud's engine — the honorable escalation — is removed at the root.

**"A Free Man for a Free Man"**

The three pairs have been misread as a tariff of inequalities, and the classical exegetes took care to close that reading. The verse was not understood to mean that only a man may be executed for a man, or that a woman's murder goes differently unavenged; nor does it make anyone but the perpetrator responsible for his act. The pairs state the equivalence rule against the vendetta's status-inflation: for the free man killed, the free man who killed him — not his whole tribe, not a substitute of higher rank; for the slave, the slave; for the woman, the woman. Each pairing forbids the specific excess the tribes practiced in that category. The wording implies that the retribution for a crime against a woman could neither fall short of nor exceed what the crime warrants — her life carries the same protection of the law, and the law's arithmetic refuses both the feud's overkill and any discount of her blood. Read against the practice it answers, the verse is leveling rather than ranking: it is the first time Arabian blood-debt was put on scales that weighed only the deed.

**The Three Options of the Heir**

The verse's legal machinery places the homicide's outcome in the hands of the victim's next of kin, with the state holding the sword. Islamic law's developed doctrine gives the heirs three courses: demand retaliation (*qiṣāṣ*), carried out only by the authorities — the verse is understood to exclude vigilantism entirely; accept the blood-money (*diyah*), a monetary composition; or forgive outright. The options are mutually exclusive in effect: once one is executed, the other two are cancelled — an heir who has taken the diyah cannot afterward demand the life, and the reverse holds. Some jurists declined to treat retaliation and blood-money as equal alternatives in cases of clear, intentional homicide, holding that capital punishment must then follow; most left the choice with the heirs. A minority position recorded by Ibn Kathīr allowed the killer himself to refuse the composition and submit to the punishment against the heirs' will. The detailed literature that grew from this verse — direct versus indirect causation, standards of evidence, the manner of execution, the amount and payment schedule of the diyah, the state's role — is the measure of how much jurisprudence one sentence of revelation generated: the schools differ on the branches while the trunk, equivalence with the heir's option of mercy, is common to all.

**The Pardon and the Courteous Payment**

The second sentence legislates the mercy-case in two directions. *If the offender is pardoned by the victim's guardian* — the wording is notable: the pardoner is called the offender's *brother*, a term of kinship the law keeps alive at the moment of maximum enmity — then let the pardon be followed honorably and the payment rendered with goodness. The exegetes read the double adverb as binding both parties: the heir who pardoned for money must not harass or inflate, and the offender's side must not delay or diminish; the settlement is to be performed with the courtesy that makes it a reconciliation rather than a truce. And the verse names this arrangement itself a gift: *that is an alleviation from your Lord, and a mercy*. The alleviation, as the commentators specify, is the very existence of the blood-money option — that homicide, the gravest of crimes, admits of composition at all. The comparison with the Torah's own retaliation law was drawn by the exegetes: Deuteronomy instructs the eye-for-eye court to show no pity (Deuteronomy 19:21), while the Qur'an's parallel in the sūrah of the Table pairs the same lex talionis — a life for a life, an eye for an eye — with the sentence that whoever waives it charitably finds it an expiation for him (5:45). Rabbinic tradition too incorporated composition into its practice; the Qur'an's contribution is to make the merciful exit explicit, named, and rewarded.

**Transgression After the Settlement**

The verse closes by legislating against its own abuse: whoever transgresses after that has a painful punishment. The classical reading fixes the case with precision — the heir who accepts the blood-money and then kills the offender anyway. The settlement extinguished the claim; the killing after it is not retaliation but plain murder, and the jurists drew the consequence: for that killer, execution becomes mandatory and no blood-money may be accepted from him. The clause completes the verse's architecture by protecting the peace the payment bought. A composition system survives only if the composition is final — if the seller of his vengeance can resell it in blood afterward, no offender would ever pay, and the feud returns through the back door of the settlement. The transgression-after-pardon is therefore the one crime the verse punishes without option: the heirs lost their mercy-privilege the moment they took the money, and the state's sword becomes unconditional. The pain threatened here is not only the punishment of the Hereafter but the removal of the very mercy the verse called an alleviation — the transgressor exits the system of pardon and re-enters the system of the feud, with the law now against him instead of the feud.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Moral Sequence**

The placement of this legislation is argument. The verse before defined righteousness as creed, costly charity, established prayer, kept pledges, and patience in misfortune, hardship, and battle (2:177); the verse after will give the retaliation's rationale — life in the qiṣāṣ (2:179). Between the definition and the rationale, the sūrah installs the law that most needs both: the killing-law, given to a community of ex-feuders whose patience is about to be legislated for the battlefield. The sequence teaches that the retaliation is not an isolated penalty but a chapter of birr — the kept pledge includes the settlement signed over a grave, and the patience in moments of peril begins with the heir who could take blood and takes money, or who could take money and forgives. The Qur'an's criminal law here is continuous with its ethics: the same heart that gives wealth despite loving it is being asked to give vengeance despite loving that too, and the verse that demands the exchange supplies, in its own title for the arrangement, the reason it can be endured — an alleviation from your Lord, and a mercy.
