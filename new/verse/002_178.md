## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:178

> O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set for you in cases of murder— a free man for a free man, a slave for a slave, and a female for a female. But if the offender is pardoned by the victim’s guardian, then bloodmoney should be decided fairly and payment should be made courteously. This is a concession and a mercy from your Lord. But whoever transgresses after that will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Law of Retaliation**

The verse prescribes retaliation in cases of murder, and it states the principle in the form that the community of the time would understand: a free person for a free person, a slave for a slave, and a female for a female. The sentence then provides for pardon and for the payment of compensation.

The Book's treatment of the subject belongs to a body of legislation that organised the life of a society in which killings were followed by long feuds. The community's scholars described the passage as a limitation on a practice that had grown beyond the offence, and they read the sentence as an instruction about proportion.

**The Context of Tribal Feuds**

The commentators described the conditions the verse addressed. When a person was killed, his tribe would seek revenge, and the retaliation would often exceed the original killing. A group might kill several people for one, or take the life of a person of higher standing than the one who had been killed, and the exchange would continue until the original offence was forgotten.

The Book's sentence addresses that practice by naming the categories that correspond, and the community's teachers explained that the intention was to restrict the punishment to the offender and to the measure of the offence. What the reader receives is a picture of a society in which the Book's legislation intervened to limit the damage that a killing caused.

**The Categories Named**

The commentators discussed the three pairs, and they rejected the reading that would make the verse a command to kill a person of a certain category for a member of another. What the sentence establishes is the correspondence between the offender and the punishment, and the community's scholars explained that the rule prevented the killing of anyone other than the murderer.

The Book's legislation on the subject is developed further in other passages, and the community's jurists built their treatment of homicide on the verses. What the sentence in this place supplies is the principle that retaliation is limited by the offence, and the reader is shown how the Book approached a matter that had been governed by custom.

**Pardon and Compensation**

The sentence then states that a person who receives a pardon from his brother may be dealt with according to the terms that follow, with the payment made in a proper manner. The commentators explained that the pardon belongs to the family of the victim, who may accept compensation instead of retaliation.

The Book's use of the word brother in this context was noted by the community's teachers, who read it as an indication that the relationship between the two parties survives the offence. The sentence therefore opens a path between the families that does not require either the extinction of the grievance or the continuation of the feud, and the community's practice has preserved both the retaliation and the settlement as options.

**A Concession and a Mercy**

The sentence describes the arrangement as a concession and a mercy from God, and the community's scholars read the phrase as a statement about the legislation itself. The Book presents the law as something granted for the benefit of the community rather than as a burden imposed on it.

The two terms also indicate that the arrangement with the pardoning family is a relief from the obligation of strict retaliation. What the reader receives is a picture of a legal order that combines the justice of the response with the possibility of settlement, and the community's teachers used the passage when they spoke about the virtue of accepting compensation or of pardoning outright.

The community’s teachers read the phrase as a statement about the legislation rather than about the people it addresses, and they noted that the Book presents its rulings as provisions for the community’s welfare. The two terms indicate that the arrangement with the pardoning family is a relief from the strictest course, and the reader is shown that the law provides for both the claim of the victim’s family and the possibility of a settlement. The community’s practice has preserved the two options, and the sentence gives both of them the standing of a provision from God.

**The Warning at the End**

The sentence closes with the statement that a person who transgresses after the settlement will receive a painful punishment, and the commentators explained the transgression as exceeding what the agreement allows. A person who has accepted the compensation and then takes revenge has broken the terms of the settlement, and the Book states what follows.

The warning applies to both parties, since either may act beyond what the law provides. The community's scholars read the sentence as closing the loophole by which a feud might be renewed after an agreement had been reached, and the reader is shown that the Book's legislation provides for the settlement and then protects it.
