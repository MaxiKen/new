## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:25

> And as for those given their record in their left hand, they will cry ˹bitterly˺, “I wish I had not been given my record,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Left Hand Loses**

Shimāl — the left — inverts verse 19's whole triumph. Right hands brandished records joyfully; left hands receive them as sentences. The Book's hand-geography is absolute: companions of the right inherit gardens (56:8, 27, 38), companions of the left inherit fire. Anatomy preaches — right means rectitude, left means loss — and the Day confirms the metaphor eternally. Amma...fa ("as for") opens the second case after the first's six-verse celebration, prosecutorial symmetry: verdict for the saved, verdict for the damned. The passive "given" (ūtiya) stresses helpless reception — none chooses the hand; hands reveal lives. Left reception announces damnation before a page is read. The palm itself pronounces. Readers should feel the pivot's weight: feasting ends, wailing begins. What follows will mirror every right-hand joy into left-hand horror.

**Would That Concealment Held**

Yā laytanī lam ūta kitābiyah — "would that I had not been given my record!" Layta voices the impossible wish — optative of the irreparable. The damned soul begs unread pages, unopened books, unbraved audits. Concealment is the craving: had the record stayed sealed, shame might have slept. Al-Rāzī sharpens the pathos — the soul may dread witnessing its deeds more than suffering the fire, preferring pain to exposure. Shame outweighs searing. Such is guilt's psychology: pain punishes the body, but exposure annihilates the self. The verse's "my record" (kitābiyah) personalizes inescapably — this book, these deeds, no other's. Wishing it ungiven confesses its accuracy: none dispute the transcript, all rue its reading. Readers hold pens now: write pages you will beg to show, not beg to seal.

**Behind the Back**

A companion passage complicates the scene: some receive their books "from behind their backs," then cry for destruction (84:10-12). Exegetes reconcile — the left hand gets twisted behind the back to take its book. Ibn Kathīr, Qatādah, and others picture the contortion: arm wrenched rearward, record slapped into reversed palm. Posture performs disgrace — reception deformed as lives were deformed. Facing one's book would grant dignity; back-handed receipt denies it. The body testifies: twisted limbs for twisted conduct. Such physicality prevents abstraction — damnation is not status but posture, agony in anatomy. Readers should picture the wrenched shoulder: every joint confessing. What hands did (withholding, striking, signing falsehood) hands now display — behind backs that turned on truth. Contortion fits; disgrace suits.

**Bitter Crying Mirrors Happy Crying**

Yaqūlu — "he says" or "cries" — echoes verse 19's identical verb. Same speech-act, opposite tears: the saved cry "Here, read!" while the damned cry "Would it were unread!" One verb, two eternities. The sūrah's symmetry is surgical — reception, cry, confession on both sides (verses 19-20, 25-27). Mirroring teaches by contrast: joy's grammar inverted becomes horror's. Translation's bracketed adverbs ("happily," "bitterly") rightly voice the tears' flavor. Such parallelism also universalizes the scene: every soul cries on that Day; content divides them. Readers choose cries now — "here!" or "would that!" — by choosing conduct. Tears are certain; their taste is chosen. What throats will voice then, lives rehearse now. Cry "here" by readiness; never "would that" by neglect.

Layta heals nothing. Wishes on the Day purchase no revision — no second lifetimes, no amended records, no appeals. Regret's function expires at death: earthly remorse converts (repentance accepted), posthumous remorse merely torments. The verse thus preaches timing's tyranny — same wish ("would that!"), opposite value before and after. Living hearers can still convert layta into tawbah: "would that I had prayed" becomes prayer. Dead wishers cannot. Such is the passage's pastoral blade: regret now saves, regret then sears. Readers should voice their laytas early — every "would that" a summons to act. What the damned wail hopelessly, the living may whisper hopefully. The verse's cry is mercy's alarm wearing horror's voice. Hear it; heed it; never echo it.

**The Company of the Left Assembles**

Verse 25 musters damnation's company as verse 19 mustered bliss's. The companions of the left (56:9) gather — deniers, mockers, hoarders, back-turners — all left-handed. Company multiplies torment: shared shame, mutual blame, collective wailing. Yet solitude persists inside multitude — each soul's book is solitary ("my record"), each wish personal ("would that I"). Together yet alone: the Day's social hell. Such assembly also completes the sūrah's sorting — exposure universal (verse 18), reception divided (verses 19, 25). Readers stand pre-sorted: conduct now assigns companies then. No transfer window opens after. What hands practice (giving or clutching) pre-registers palms. Choose company by conduct. The left assembles from earthly choices; the right likewise.

No lottery assigns hands. Giving hands shaped right-reception; clutching hands shaped left. Pre-registration runs lifelong: each charity votes right, each withheld due votes left. The Day merely announces precincts tallied. Such causality preserves responsibility — hands received match hands extended. Complaining of left-reception indicts earthly fists. Readers still hold choosing hands: open them. What palms practice, palms inherit. Generosity pre-registers right; miserliness, left. The ballot is daily; the count, final. Vote with alms. Election Day displays what life elected. Choose hands now; receive them then.

**Exposure Feared More Than Fire**

Al-Rāzī's insight deserves its own chamber: the soul may dread witnessing its record more than suffering its sentence. Shame outweighs searing. Bodies endure pain routinely — illness, injury, labor — but selves shatter at exposure. Honor cultures grasp this instinctively: public disgrace outranks private agony. The Day stages disgrace absolutely — records read before multitudes, hidden deeds broadcast to saints and scoffers alike. No spin, no context, no character witnesses. What the soul hid for decades surfaces in moments. Such is guilt's true horror: not consequence but confession, forced and final. Readers should weigh their hidden files honestly: which pages would you pay fire to suppress? Pay repentance instead — cheaper now, priceless then. What exposure threatens, tawbah deletes. Hide sins from men if you must; never hide them from mercy. Confess privately; escape publicly.

**Ignorance Wished Next**

Unread record wished; unknown reckoning next. Verse 26 will deepen the lament — "nor known my reckoning!" — wishing ignorance past unreadness. Wishes escalate: pages sealed, then knowledge voided, then (verse 27) existence ended. Readers should brace for the staircase of layta — each wish more desperate. What follows will wish away awareness itself. The next verse wishes the reckoning itself unknown, deepening the cry of regret.
