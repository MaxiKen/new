## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:128

> Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You and from our descendants a nation that will submit to you. Show us our rituals, and turn to us in grace. You are truly the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prayer for Submission**

The first request is that the two men be made submitters to God, and the Arabic term is the one from which the name of the religion later revealed through Muhammad ﷺ derives. The Book uses the same word for Abraham elsewhere and states that he was neither Jew nor Christian but one who submitted, which is why the commentators described this verse as one of the passages that show the universal meaning of the term.

The prayer is for an ongoing condition rather than for a single act. Abraham had already submitted when he was commanded, as the next verses state, and the request here is that the state be maintained, which is how the Book presents the life of the faithful as a continuing orientation rather than a completed event.

**A Nation From Their Descendants**

The second request widens the scope from two individuals to a community, and the Book uses the same word for a people that it will later apply to the community of Muhammad ﷺ. The petition asks that the descendants of the two founders become a nation of submitters, and the sūrah will later state that the community was given a role among humanity.

The commentators noted that the prayer concerns descendants rather than all people, and they explained the limitation by reference to the principle that a person's kin have the first claim on his concern. The request is not exclusivity, since the promise to make Abraham a pattern for humanity had already been given; it is the natural petition of a father for the line that would continue his work.

**"Show Us Our Rituals"**

The third request concerns the rites of worship, and the Arabic term is the one used for the practices of the pilgrimage, including the stations, the sacrifice, and the movements performed at the House. Commentators also read the phrase more broadly as a request for the acts of worship in general, and the Book uses the term elsewhere for the rites appointed to every community.

The prayer presupposes that the manner of worship is something God teaches rather than something people devise, and the Book's accounts of the prophets repeatedly present the rites as given. The sūrah's later passages describe the pilgrimage in detail, and the request here presents the community's practices as an answer to Abraham's petition. The request also treats the forms of worship as something God gives rather than something people settle for themselves. That principle appears elsewhere in the Book when it states that every community was given its rites, and the prayer in this verse places the community's practices in the same category.

**"Turn to Us in Grace"**

The fourth request asks God to turn toward the two men, and the Arabic verb describes a reciprocal movement, since the same root is used for a person's turning to God in repentance. The commentators explained the term as describing God's acceptance of a servant who turns toward Him, so that the vocabulary contains both the human act and the divine response.

The request follows the petition for the rites, and the sequence suggests that even the performance of worship requires God's acceptance rather than merely His instruction. The Book's teaching about worship elsewhere makes the same point, and the Prayer shows it in its opening chapter, where the worshipper asks to be guided along the path of those who have received favour. The petition also acknowledges what the two men knew about their own work: that performing what is commanded does not oblige God to accept it. The request for grace at the end of their petitions is therefore not a formality but the heart of the matter.

**The Names at the End**

The prayer closes with two names describing God as the one who accepts repentance and the source of mercy, and both names correspond to the request that preceded them. The names supply the ground of confidence for a person who has asked for acceptance and knows that his own work cannot require it.

The Book pairs these names in other passages about repentance and about the community's failures, and it uses them to close accounts of severe offences. Their appearance at the end of this prayer indicates that the request for the rites and for descendants is offered in the same spirit as the request for forgiveness, and the reader is shown the founders of the sanctuary asking for what every worshipper after them would need.

**The Sequence of the Prayers**

The verses that follow continue the petition with a request for a messenger from among the descendants, and the sequence is worth noticing: submission, community, rites, acceptance, and then a teacher. The sūrah presents the community's founding as a series of requests for the conditions of worship, and it will later describe how each request was answered. The order of the petitions also reflects the order of the matters themselves: the two founders ask first for their own state, then for the community, then for the practice of worship, and only then for the teacher who would instruct it. The structure is a model of how the Book presents the relation between commitment and instruction.

**A Prayer That Shaped a Community**

The petitions in this verse were answered in the history the sūrah goes on to describe, and the community's own rites and identity were traced by its scholars to the requests Abraham and Ishmael made at the House. The book that follows records the messenger raised from among the descendants, the rites taught to the community, and the direction of prayer established at the sanctuary, and each of those corresponds to an item in the prayer. What the sūrah is doing with the material is therefore more than telling a story about the past. It is presenting the community's worship as the fulfilment of a request made by the man both earlier communities venerated, so that the practices the Muslims observed were not an innovation but an answer. The reader who follows the sequence from the building of the House to the coming of the messenger is being shown a single continuous purpose, and the prayer in this verse is where its content is set out.
