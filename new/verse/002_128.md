## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:128

> Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You and from our descendants a nation that will submit to you. Show us our rituals, and turn to us in grace. You are truly the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Petition to Be Made Submitters**

The builders' prayer continues, and its first petition is its most remarkable: *our Lord, make us both fully submit to You*. The request is for submission spoken by submitters — the patriarch who fulfilled every commandment (124) and the son laboring at the House asking to be *made* muslimūn, the participle of a transitive verb: the ones submitting, in the state the verb's form describes as produced rather than self-generated. The exegetes mark the word's significance precisely: *muslim* here is the active participle tied to *islām*, the submission that verse 132 will name as the prophets' bequest and the sūrah will legislate as religion's universal form — and the petition's transitive grammar carries the doctrine inside it. Submission is asked *from* Allah: the orientation of the self toward its Lord is itself a gift to be requested, not a possession to be declared. The prophets pray for what they already hold, which reads at three depths at once — increase of a state that admits of degrees, perseverance in a state exposed to trial, and the confession that the state's holder never owns it outright. The petition defines the prayer's whole theology in its first clause: the highest station in the recital's file — the tested, fulfilled imām — stands before Allah asking to be made what he is, and the asking is not deficiency but the station's proper posture. A submission that stopped asking to be made would already have become property, and property is the category the previous section's hundred verses dismantled.

**A Nation That Submits**

The prayer's second petition extends the first across generations: *and from our descendants, a nation that will submit to You*. The word *umma* names not a family but a community — a people gathered by a shared orientation, the term the Qur'an uses for the constituted bodies of religious history. The petition is the patriarch's answer to his own question at verse 124: asked whether the covenant would run through his offspring and told it does not extend to wrongdoers, Abraham now prays the only prayer that question permits — not that his descendants hold the office by blood, but that a nation of them be *made submitting*, the covenant's condition fulfilled in them by the same grace requested for himself. The exegetes ground the petition's scope in a general principle: kin hold the first claim on a person's care and concern — al-Rāzī's note, anchored in the Qur'an's own command to the believers: *protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones* (66:6). The patriarch's prayer models the principle at its prophetic height: the imām's first congregation is his line, and the line's good is not its precedence but its submission. The petition's wording also carries the recital's covenant-law forward: a nation *that will submit* — the qualifying participle attached to the community as its defining mark, so that the nation asked for is constituted by the posture rather than the genealogy, and every later member qualifies by the same clause. The prayer thus builds, inside the House-raising scene, the community that will inherit the House — an umma defined at its founding petition as submitters-made, descendants in form, muslims in content.

**“Show Us Our Rituals”**

The third petition asks for the liturgy itself: *show us our rituals* — *manāsik*, the word whose range the tradition sets out as the pilgrimage-rites, the sacrificial practice, and worship's forms in general. The verb — *show us* — is the petition's doctrine: the rites are seen before they are performed, given before they are invented. No prophet in the recital designs his community's worship; the forms descend, are assigned, are shown — as the House's site was assigned (22:26) and the standing-place legislated (125). Sūrah al-Ḥajj states the principle across the communities: *for every community We appointed a rite of sacrifice, so that they may pronounce Allah's Name over the livestock He has provided* (22:34) — appointment and provision from one side, pronouncement from the other, the liturgy's two-party structure fixed. The petition's humility is its instruction for the recital's audience: the patriarchs, custodians of the first House, ask to be shown how to worship in it — the builders requesting the liturgy of their own building. The question embedded in *our rites* is equally instructive: which forms belong to this House, this community, this covenant? The prayer leaves the answer entirely with Allah, and the answer arrived in the tradition's record as the complete ḥajj — the circling, the running, the standing, the sacrifice — held in Islam as Abrahamic institution, its forms received, its showing granted. Between the submission asked in the first petition and the rites asked in the third stands the prayer's complete order: orientation first, form second — the submitting heart before the shown ritual, because the rite without the orientation is the empty house of verse 114's indictment, maintained in stone and abandoned in function.

**“Turn to Us”**

The fourth petition is the prayer's most tender clause: *and turn to us in grace* — *tub ʿalaynā*, the tawba-verb in its divine direction. The tradition's grammar of tawba is reciprocal, and the reciprocity is the clause's whole content: the human being turns toward Allah in repentance, and Allah turns toward the human in acceptance — one word, two motions, the meeting named from each side. The sūrah filed the mechanism at its origin-station: Adam received words from his Lord and the turning was accepted (2:37) — the first repentance, its vocabulary given, its reception guaranteed. The patriarchs' use of the verb carries no claim of known sin; it is the servant-posture in its pure form — the creature asking the Lord to keep the relation turned, the face-to-face of grace maintained, whatever in the servants' state requires the turning. The two Names sealing the petition are chosen from the prayer's own request: *the Accepter of Repentance* — *tawwāb*, the intensive form, the One whose turning is abundant, repeated, ready; *Most Merciful* — *raḥīm*, the mercy that meets the turning where it lands. The pairing is the verse's theology of return in two words: the request to be turned to is addressed to the One whose Names already answer it, which is the recital's standing structure of duʿāʾ — the petition built on the attribute that grants it, the asking shaped by the Giver's self-description. For the community hearing the prayer, the clause legislates the liturgy's interior: rites shown, submission made, and over both the continuous turning — the worship that keeps asking to be received, at the House whose builders never stopped asking.

**The Prayer's Architecture**

Read as a whole, the petition sequence is a complete religious life in four requests, and the sequence is the verse's gift to every supplicant after it. Submission — the orientation asked as gift. A submitting nation from the descendants — the transmission asked as grace, the faith's crossing into the next generation held in the same petition as one's own. Rites shown — the form asked as revelation, worship received rather than composed. Turning accepted — the relation asked as mercy, the whole structure enclosed in continuous return. The four petitions cover the religion's four dimensions — the self, the community, the practice, the relation — and their order is instructive: the self's orientation precedes the community's formation, the community's formation precedes the liturgy's showing, and the liturgy stands under the turning that alone keeps it alive. The tradition connects the prayer's horizon to the petition the next verse files — the messenger asked for from within the submitting nation — and the connection shows the architecture's reach: the four requests build the community that the fifth petition will ask to be guided from within. The recital's audience received the sequence as a template. The Muslim supplicant praying at the House, or toward it, prays inside a liturgy whose founding prayer was for submission, for children who submit, for rites shown, for turning granted — and every clause remains petition, none of them possession, which is the posture the patriarchs modeled with their hands on the stones and the verse preserves with their words still unfinished.
