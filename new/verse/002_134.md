## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:134

> That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The File on Abraham Is Closed**

The long appeal to Abraham that began with his covenant and his imamate has just ended with his deathbed, and this verse draws the line under the whole account. "That was a community that had already gone before" — *qad khalat*, they passed, they are gone, their case is finished. The generation of the patriarchs lived, chose, believed, struggled, and died, and what they accumulated went with them into their own reckoning. The sentence is not a dismissal of the ancestors; the sūrah has spent the previous verses honoring them. It is a transfer of attention. The ancestors cannot act for you, so the question that remains is what the living will do. Every appeal to a golden past eventually reaches this floor: the past is a record, not a proxy, and records are read by those who must still write their own.

**Kasab: Deeds as Property**

The verb the verse uses for what the dead and the living alike hold is *kasaba*, to earn — and the same root, depending on context, carries the sense of committing or perpetrating. The double edge is deliberate. A deed in the Qur'an's moral economy is not an event that evaporates; it is an acquisition that attaches to its doer the way a purchase attaches to a buyer. What a person "earns" becomes his property in the only ledger that survives death, and the property can be a burden as easily as a treasure. The vocabulary of earning also explains why the verse phrases both halves identically — *for them* what they earned, *for you* what you earned. There is no qualitative difference between the ancestors' account and the descendants': the same verb governs both, the same ownership rules apply to both, and the same audit awaits both. Death closes the earning, not the ownership; what was acquired remains acquired, held by the dead in a custody no living relative can reach into. That is why the verse can say so flatly that "for them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned": the sentence structure of ownership. Their earnings are in their hands; yours are in yours. No transfer mechanism exists between the two accounts, in either direction.

**No Soul Carries Another's Load**

The principle stated here in the language of earnings is stated elsewhere in the language of burdens. The sūrah of the Star records, as if reciting a creed already known, *"˹They state˺ that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another, and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards"* (53:38–39). Two claims in two clauses: liability is non-transferable, and reward is proportional to effort. Together they seal the verse's argument. A sinner cannot offload onto his ancestor, and an heir cannot withdraw from his ancestor's balance. The Qur'an presses this point precisely because ancestral credit was a live currency in its first audience — lineage was identity, protection, and prestige, and the claim to have righteous forefathers functioned as a kind of moral insurance. The verse cancels the policy.

**A Community, Not a Clan**

The word for what has passed is *ummah* — a community defined by what it held together, its faith and its way, rather than by blood alone. That choice of word quietly redefines what the heirs inherit. From an *ummah* that has gone before, the living receive a direction: the creed it lived by, the pattern of its submission, the guidance it carried. What they do not receive is its credit. The distinction matters because the same sūrah has already fixed the rule at the top of Abraham's story: when God made him a leader for humanity, the imamate was explicitly withheld from wrongdoers among his own line — *"˹He said,˺ 'And ˹so will˺ some of your descendants ˹also˺.' ˹Allah˺ said, 'My covenant does not ˹extend to˺ the wrongdoers'"* (2:124). Rank in God's economy runs through conduct, not chromosomes. A clan boasts of its dead; a community continues their work.

**Why the Sentence Will Return**

This exact sentence appears again, word for word, seven verses later (2:141), and the repetition is structural rather than redundant. Between the two occurrences falls a dense argument: rival invitations to join one sect or another, the community's own full confession of faith, the exposure of the claim that the patriarchs were Jews or Christians. The verse here states the principle before the case is argued; its twin restates it after the case is closed. The effect is a bracket — the dispute about who owns Abraham is opened and shut between two identical judgments. When the second occurrence arrives, the sentence will no longer be information. It will be a verdict. The reader who notices the bracket understands that everything between its arms has just been ruled out of order.

**The Earned Inheritance**

If ancestral credit is unavailable, what remains of the ancestors is their example — and the example is itself an earned thing, offered for imitation rather than for claiming. Abraham did not bequeath righteousness the way a man bequeaths land; he demonstrated a path, and the path is open to whoever walks it. This is why the sūrah can honor the patriarchs so highly in one breath and strip their descendants of every inherited privilege in the next. Honor belongs to the dead for what they earned; obligation belongs to the living for what they will earn. The verse thus performs a double liberation: it frees the living from despair over ancestors who sinned, and it frees them from complacency about ancestors who were righteous. Both moods are refusals of work. What the verse demands is work — your own, beginning now, in an account no one else can touch.
