## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:134

> That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement That Ends an Argument**

The verse closes a section in which the sūrah has been addressing claims made about the patriarchs, and it does so with a single principle. The community in question has passed from the world, and the reckoning concerns each party's own deeds. What the sentence removes is the practice of arguing about one's standing by appealing to the conduct of ancestors.

The Book states the point in the form of a division: theirs is what they earned, and yours is what you earned. The two halves of the sentence face each other, and the symmetry is what makes the statement decisive. Neither party's record can be transferred to the other, and the question of who was right about the past does not alter what the living are answerable for.

**The Repetition Later in the Passage**

The same sentence appears again seven verses later, at the end of a passage about the direction of prayer and the claims of the People of the Book. The repetition brackets the material between the two occurrences, and the reader meets the principle before and after the arguments, so that the whole section is read within its limits.

The Book uses this arrangement when a subject has produced a long exchange of claims. The statement about individual accountability is placed at both ends of the dispute, and the material in between is thereby identified as material the reader should weigh rather than as material that decides the fate of the people being discussed.

**What the Word for Earning Conveys**

The verb used for earning carries the sense of acquiring and, where the object is a deed, of committing it. The Book uses it for the moral proceeds of a person's actions, and the sentence therefore treats deeds as a person's actual property, the only possession that travels with him to the reckoning.

That image appears throughout the Book, which repeatedly describes the Day of Judgment as the point at which every person finds what he has earned present before him. The sūrah has used commercial vocabulary in the same way in its account of the community that traded the Hereafter for this world, and the sentence here applies the vocabulary to the ancestors whose deeds are being used in an argument.

**No Accountability for the Deeds of Others**

The closing clause states that the living will not be questioned about what the earlier community did, and the commentators explained it as a denial of responsibility rather than a denial of interest. The deeds of the ancestors are not the burden of the descendants, and the merit of the ancestors is not their credit.

The Book states the principle in another chapter in the words that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another, and it adds that every person will be informed of his own differences on the day of return. The sūrah's use of the principle here is aimed at a specific habit: the practice of claiming a position on the strength of a forefather's record rather than on the strength of one's own conduct.

**Why the Correction Was Needed**

The sūrah has been describing communities that made their descent from Abraham and Jacob the ground of their claims, and it has answered with the narrative of what the patriarchs actually did. The verse now removes the premise of the argument altogether. Even if the ancestors were all that the claimants said they were, their standing would not supply the descendants with a position before God.

The Book never denies that the patriarchs were honoured, and the surrounding verses praise them. What the sentence denies is that honour is transferable as a status, since the religion the patriarchs practised consisted of submission and obedience, and those are acts rather than inheritances. A descendant who practises them belongs to the community of the patriarchs, and a descendant who does not has no claim on their record.

**Individual Accountability in the Book**

The Qur'an states this principle in several places, and it does so in the context of communities that relied on lineage. It tells its readers that no one will reap except what he sows, that every soul is held for what it has earned, and that a person's record will be read out to him on the day of judgment. The repetition indicates how important the Book considers the correction.

The sūrah's own treatment of this community follows the same line. The favours granted to the ancestors are recalled so that the descendants may be grateful, not so that they may be confident, and the covenant narrative shows the community's own response to what it received. What is inherited, on the Book's account, is the opportunity and the responsibility rather than the reward.

**The Bracket and the Sūrah's Method**

The two occurrences of this sentence give the reader a technique for handling long arguments about religious history. The Book presents the material, states that the parties are answerable for their own deeds, and returns at the end to the same statement so that the reader does not lose the point in the detail. The method assumes an audience that reads carefully rather than one that needs to be told the conclusion at every step.

The sūrah's later passages use the same approach when they address the Muslims about their own obligations, since the warnings given to the earlier community are turned on the new one in identical terms. What the reader receives from the sentence is a standard that applies to all communities, whatever their record claims for their ancestors.

**What the Verse Asks of Its Reader**

The sentence asks the reader to take up his own case rather than someone else's. A person who has inherited a religious tradition has been given a language, a history, and a set of practices, and the Book treats all of those as provisions for which he is grateful and answerable. What he cannot do is present the record of an earlier generation as a settlement of his own account, and the verse states the reason in a way that leaves no room for negotiation: the two cases are separate, and each will be examined on its own.

**The Tone of the Sentence**

The wording is matter of fact. The Book does not accuse the living of trying to steal the merit of the dead; it states that the accounts are separate and moves on. The effect of that calm is that the principle appears self-evident, which is how the Book often presents its corrections of inherited habits. The reader is left to draw the conclusion, and the following verses continue the argument with the direction of prayer and the claims of the two communities.
