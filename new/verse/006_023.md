## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:23

> Their only argument will be: “By Allah, our Lord! We were never polytheists.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Defence That Convicts Its Speaker**

Facing a question they cannot answer, the accused produce one sentence, and the sentence is a lie sworn in the name of the God they spent their lives ignoring. They do not plead that the idols were powerful, or that they had misunderstood the message, or that their ancestors misled them. They deny the charge entirely, and they do it under oath, in the presence of the One they are swearing by.

The verse presents the reply as their "only argument," which strips away every version of the defence a person might expect from a people who had reasoned about religion for decades. The skill they displayed in the marketplaces of Makkah, the rhetoric of their poets, and the traditions of their elders all disappear, and nothing remains but an oath. A city that had produced negotiators, genealogists, and keepers of shrine custom finds itself in front of the gathering with one sentence to say.

That sentence is also, on its face, a denial of the plainest facts about their own conduct. They had made pilgrimages to the stones, slaughtered for them, distributed shares of their harvests to them, and argued in the market about their standing. To call all of that by another name in front of the One who recorded it is to attempt a change of legal category at the last possible moment.

**Swearing by the God They Denied**

There is a bitter irony in the formula they choose. Their oath is taken in the name of Allah, and they attach the word "our Lord" to it, which is an acknowledgement of the lordship they had refused to live by. The Book notes elsewhere that the Makkans already knew who created the heavens and the earth and would say so if asked (31:25; 43:9). In the moment of judgment they say it again, with the name they had avoided, to deny the practice they had maintained.

That confession is wrung out of them and used against them. They have just testified to the authority of the God in whose presence they are standing, which makes the denial that follows harder to file as an error of judgement.

Swearing in God's name was not, for this audience, an unusual thing to do in a tight corner. The Book records that they swore their most solemn oaths that a warner would make them better guided than any other community, and that when the warner came it only drove them further away (35:42); they swore that God would never raise the dead (16:38); they swore that a sign would bring them to belief (6:109). The oath of 6:23 is the last of that series, and the sūrah of the Argument reports the same behaviour on the Day of Resurrection, when people will falsely swear to God as they used to swear to their leaders, thinking they have something to stand on, and are named in the report as the total liars (58:18).

**Why They Believe Their Own Denial**

A person who spends forty years among idols does not necessarily experience himself as a polytheist. The Makkan position, as the Book describes it, was that the stones were intermediaries — a position that allowed its holder to think of himself as honouring the supreme God while approaching him through lesser powers. Under that description, the accusation of associating partners with God can be sincerely denied, because the worshipper never thought of his intermediaries as divine in the same sense as the Creator.

The verse takes that self-image and holds it against the record of what the people did. Their hands performed the rites, their money funded the shrines, and their leaders opposed the Prophet on the ground that he was attacking their gods. Whatever their theory of the stones was, their actions had already filed the case.

**The Disclosure That Comes First**

The Book describes a moment before the lying begins: what they used to hide becomes clear to them (6:28). The verse at hand sits inside that disclosure. They are not being asked to remember an event from long ago; they are standing in front of a record that has been produced, and the denial is spoken against what they themselves now see.

That is the terrible shape of the scene. A person who has been shown the truth of his own case and still denies it has crossed out of the category of the mistaken and into the category of the liar, and the Book names the result plainly: see how they lie about themselves (6:24).

**The Gods Who Refuse to Back Them Up**

Elsewhere the Book lets the false deities speak. When the polytheists call on their associate-gods, the gods throw the accusation back at them and say that they are definitely liars (16:86), and on that Day they offer full submission to Allah while everything they had fabricated falls away (16:87). Even their allies from the unseen do not defend them. Satan himself announces at the end that he had no authority over them, that he only called and they answered, and that they should blame themselves (14:22).

The Makkans had expected supporters. What they get is a courtroom where every witness, including their own gods and their own leader, declines to speak on their behalf. The oath in the verse is therefore the sound of a man abandoned by everyone he had relied on.

**A Sentence That Describes a Permanent Human Habit**

The specific denial concerns idol worship, but the pattern is broader than the seventh century. A person who has arranged his life around something other than God will often insist that he has done no such thing, because his own definition of the offence excludes the thing he does. The Book does not argue with that definition. It records the oath, notes that the gods have failed, and leaves the reader to notice that the final verdict belongs to the One being sworn by.

The verse that follows turns to that discovery, and the sūrah uses the same formula later in describing a different audience. Here the courtroom is still in session, and the accused has just finished his testimony.
