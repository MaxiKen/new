## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:27

> You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night. You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And You provide for whoever You will without limit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Sentences About Time and Life**

The prayer continues, and it moves from authority to two things that every human being experiences and no human being controls: the alternation of night and day, and the passage between life and death. Both sentences are phrased as ongoing actions — you cause, you bring forth — rather than as past events.

The choice matters. A miracle can be dismissed as an exception that proves nothing about the ordinary; the alternation of darkness and light is so ordinary that nobody thinks to attribute it to anything. The Book presents it as an act of the same will that confers kingdoms in the verse before, which means the daily cycle and the fall of dynasties are being made into the same kind of event.

**The Merging of Night and Day**

The image in Arabic is of one period sliding into the next: the night drawn over the day and the day drawn over the night, with each stealing part of the other. The Book uses the same expression at 22:61, at 31:29 where the sun and moon are described as subservient and orbiting for a fixed term, and at 57:6, where the verse immediately adds that God knows what the heart conceals.

The progression of those passages is instructive. The physical observation belongs to everyone; the conclusion drawn from it is that the one who moves the hours also knows what happens inside them. Time in this Book is not a neutral container in which events occur. It is administered, which is why the Book so often connects the passing of days with the possibility of repentance: a person's remaining hours are borrowed from someone whose will governs the borrowing.

**Living From the Dead, Dead From the Living**

The second pair of clauses states a law that runs through the whole Book: God brings the living out of the dead and the dead out of the living. The commentaries took the first direction to include the sprouting of grain from seed and the birth of a child from a body, and the second to include the death of a living being and the consumption of the living by what grows from the soil.

The Book uses the same formula to answer the denial of resurrection: *“Indeed, Allah is the One Who causes seeds and fruit stones to sprout. He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. That is Allah! How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?”* (6:95), and again in a verse that closes with the resurrection itself: *“He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And He gives life to the earth after its death. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺.”* (30:19). The argument is elegant: the thing being denied is happening in every field and every womb, and has been acknowledged by the denier every time he ate.

**"Without Limit" — the Phrase in Its Two Directions**

The prayer ends with provision *without limit*, *bi-ghayri ḥisāb*. The Arabic can mean that the gift is given without measure, and also that God gives without rendering an account to anyone. Both senses are present in the Book's usage. Provision without reckoning is promised to the generous at 24:38, to the believers at 40:40, and to the patient at 39:10, where the reward promised for patience is precisely the absence of a limit.

The phrase is a deliberate inversion of the way people think about resources. Human beings distribute what they have according to accounts kept — measures, entitlements, ledgers. The Book says the distribution of provision is not run on that system, which is why the visible world contains inequalities that no moral ledger explains. A person may find the arrangement unjust until he remembers that the accounts are being kept for another Day.

**Why the Prayer Turns to Bread and Rain**

Provision enters a prayer about authority because the two are the same subject in the lives of the poor. A community under pressure from wealthier neighbours asks two questions: who rules, and who feeds? The verse answers both with one hand.

The pairing also keeps the prayer honest. It does not ask for the enemies' wealth or for the fall of their cities; it states that power, time, life, and food all come from one source, and it leaves the request to be framed by the one who says it. This is a prayer with no shopping list, which is what makes it repeatable in every circumstance.

**A Prayer That Reads the World Instead of Asking for Signs**

Most requests in the Book are for something specific: forgiveness, provision, protection, guidance. This one is constructed differently. Its clauses are statements of what God already does, arranged so that the person praying ends up agreeing with the world rather than asking for it to be changed.

That shape gives the prayer a use in every circumstance. A community whose situation has just changed — a defeat, a death, a reversal of fortune — is not handed a petition that must be adapted to the news. It is handed a description of the order that produced the news, and it recites the description. The prayer is therefore as true on the worst day as on the best, which is what makes it repeatable.

**The World as a Text About Its Owner**

Taken together, the two sentences of this verse are an argument from observation, and the argument is available to anyone who can see. A person does not need a prophet to notice that day follows night, that harvests come from buried seed, that the provision of a household appears from a direction nobody predicted.

The Book's habit is to turn such observations into testimony. What the eye reports about the world is evidence about the one who made it, and the person who recites this prayer is agreeing with the evidence rather than asking for a new sign. The next verses turn from the order of creation to the order of loyalty among the people who recite it.
