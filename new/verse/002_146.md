## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:146

> Those We have given the Scripture recognize this ˹Prophet˺ as they recognize their own children. Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Simile Taken From the Certainties of Blood**

The verse describes the knowledge of the Scripture-people with the strongest simile available in human experience: they recognize it — or him — *as they recognize their own children*. The comparison is chosen for its epistemic quality, not its warmth. A father among many children does not infer which is his; he knows by a familiarity that precedes argument, that survives any stranger's contradiction, that he could not be talked out of even if he wanted to. The recognition the verse attributes to the learned among the People of the Book is of that order — immediate, intimate, unmistakable. This is not the tentative conclusion of an investigation but the settled knowledge of long acquaintance: they have handled the descriptions in their own scriptures the way a parent has handled his child, and the features match. The simile simultaneously honors and convicts: it honors their knowledge by calling it certain, and it convicts their silence by making that certainty undeniable even to themselves.

**What Is Recognized: the Direction or the Man**

The exegetes divided over the object of the recognition. Al-Ṭabarī read it as the truth of the Kaʿbah's qiblah, following the previous verse's assertion that those given the Scripture know the new direction to be the truth from their Lord. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī read it as recognition of the Prophet himself — known through the prophecies in the Bible, and known further by his presence, his character, the signs he brought, and the truth of his message. The second reading subsumes the first: recognition of the qiblah's truth depends on recognition of the Prophet's truthfulness, since Jerusalem had been a legitimate direction for a time by his own practice, and nothing about the Kaʿbah's stones compels assent apart from the authority of the one who turns the community toward it. The translation's insertion of "this Prophet" follows that logic. Either way the verse's charge is unaffected: the knowledge exists, it is child-certain, and it is being managed.

**The Same Recognition, Stated Twice More**

The Qur'an returns to this claim with variations that confirm its centrality. The sūrah of the Cattle states it almost identically and draws the darker conclusion: *"Those to whom We gave the Scripture recognize him ˹to be a true prophet˺ as they recognize their own children. Those who have ruined themselves will never believe"* (6:20) — recognition that does not issue in faith is self-ruin, not a neutral state. The sūrah of the Heights locates the recognition textually: the followers of the unlettered Prophet are *"the ones whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel"* (7:157). The three verses together give the claim its full structure: the recognition is in the books they carry (7:157), it operates in their minds with parental certainty (2:146), and its rejection is a spiritual catastrophe rather than an honest error (6:20). This is the testimony whose concealment the previous argument denounced — the *shahādah* from Allah that the concealers hide (2:140) — and here the Qur'an describes the certainty behind the concealment.

**A Group, Knowingly**

The second sentence is calibrated with two qualifiers that do careful work. *A group of them* — the concealment is not attributed to all; the verse, like the sūrah throughout, distinguishes between the communities and a faction within them, and the distinction is what makes the later exceptions honest: some of the Scripture-people will believe, and the verse's own logic requires that they be the ones who acted on the recognition rather than against it. *Knowingly* — the concealment is deliberate, performed by people fully aware of what they are suppressing. This is the same phrase the sūrah used in its earliest addresses: do not hide the truth knowingly (2:42); why do you mix the truth with falsehood and hide the truth knowingly (3:71)? Knowledge here is not an excuse but the aggravating circumstance. An honest doubter who withholds because he is unsure is a different case entirely; the verse is describing people who are certain and silent, and certainty plus silence is what the Qur'an classifies as the peak form of wrongdoing against truth.

**The Rabbi Who Tested and Then Testified**

The recognition described in the verse has its most famous individual enactment in a report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (3938), narrated by Anas. When news of the Prophet's arrival in Medina reached ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām — a rabbi of the community — he came and said he would ask three questions whose answers only a prophet could know: what is the first sign of the Hour, what is the first food the people of Paradise will eat, and why does a child resemble its father or its mother. The Prophet answered all three, saying Gabriel had just informed him, and Ibn Salām immediately testified that there is no god but Allah and that he is the Messenger of Allah. Then came the detail that reads like a commentary on this verse: knowing his people to be given to slander, he asked the Prophet to question them about his standing before they learned of his conversion. They praised him extravagantly — the best of us, the son of the best of us. When he emerged and repeated his testimony, they reversed in the same breath: the worst of us, the son of the worst. The episode displays both halves of 2:146 in one man's story — recognition that acts on itself, and a group whose praise and whose slander are both instruments, deployed knowingly, against the truth they had just heard.

**Knowledge That Never Becomes Faith**

The verse's underlying theology of knowledge is severe and worth stating plainly: recognition without acknowledgment is not a halfway house but a condemned position. The Qur'an does not treat inner certainty plus outer silence as a private arrangement that harms only the silent person. The concealer injures everyone he might have guided — his community takes its cues from its scholars, and a scholar's silence about what he recognizes as his own child teaches the multitude that there is nothing to recognize. This is why the wrongdoer-superlative attached to concealing testimony (2:140) is not overstatement, and why the verse pairs the child-simile with the concealment clause rather than with an invitation: the point at which certainty is known to be certain and still suppressed is the point at which guidance becomes structurally unavailable from that quarter. The believers' consolation, which the passage will voice within two verses, is that the truth stands on its own Lord and does not depend on these witnesses speaking — but the witnesses' own case is settled by what they did with the certainty they could not deny.
