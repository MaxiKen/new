## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:30

> ˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” They asked ˹Allah˺, “Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?” Allah responded, “I know what you do not know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Council Announced, Not Consulted**

“Remember when your Lord said to the angels, ‘I am going to place a successive human authority on earth’” (2:30). The verb is announcement. Allah informs, not solicits permission. The language of lordship — Rabbika — frames the scene as master and servants. The angels are addressees of a decree that will shape the earth’s moral history.

Successive authority renders khalīfah — deputy, successor, one who comes after. The source notes that the word carries both meanings: khalīfah as stewardship of God in the world, and as succession within human history, generation after generation. Verses such as “He made you successors on earth” (6:165) and “We will make you successors on earth after them” (10:14) lean to the one or other nuance; 10:73, 27:62, 35:39 add further cases. Here, the first sense dominates: humanity as such is khalīfah — not a single figure only, but a kind endowed with responsibility that angels do not bear: to cultivate, to judge, to keep covenant, to mend.

One etymological gloss adds that khalīfah comes from khalafa, “to come after,” hinting that the human arrives after all grades of being and gathers them in one synthetic form. Whether or not that derivation delights linguists, it captures the sūrah’s portrait: the human as summary creature, heir to what preceded.

**Why Did the Angels Ask About Corruption and Bloodshed?**

“They asked, ‘Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?’” (2:30). The question has puzzled readers: how could angels know humans would corrupt and shed blood before humanity had acted?

The sources collect several answers. One says the angels had seen prior inhabitants — jinn — who had corrupted and shed blood, and they inferred likeness. The long report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās — preferred by al-Ṭabarī in narration — says jinn first inhabited the earth, corrupted, and were displaced by Iblīs and a host called al-ḥinn. On that reading, the question is analogical: if those earth-dwellers did so, will these?

Another answer — favored by al-Rāzī and others — reads the question not as suspicion but as wonder (taʿajjub), not doubt. Angels, by the majority Sunni view, cannot disobey; their speech is not objection but astonishment before a wisdom they do not yet see. A minority view noted by al-Rāzī, attributed to the Muʿtazilah, allowed disobedience for angels, but the received position preserved in this sūrah’s theology is that angels are obedient servants whose question seeks illumination, not veto.

The wording about glorifying praises and proclaiming holiness — nusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdik wa nuqaddisu lak — is rendered variously as “we hymn Your praise while proclaiming Your holiness” (cf. 32:15; 39:75; 42:5). The angels offer their own constant worship as contrast not to boast but to inquire: if the purpose is worship without corruption, why place a creature who will marry worship with blood?

**Iblīs in This Scene — What Is Firm and What Is Folklore**

The source’s long Ibn ʿAbbās report — that Iblīs was of a tribe called al-ḥinn, created from smokeless fire while other angels were created from light, that he was named al-Ḥārith and custodian of Paradise, that Adam’s clay body lay lifeless forty years while Iblīs entered and taunted it — is presented alongside the source’s own critique: details such as Iblīs being an angel are not reliable, since the Qur’an elsewhere states plainly that Iblīs was of the jinn (18:50), and the only other creation-order text is “We created man from dried clay… and the jinn We created earlier from scorching fire” (15:26–27). The report’s narrative additions — forty years, flying through the form, the name al-Ḥārith — are popular histories (isrāʾīliyyāt) that circulated early and must be handled as UNVERIFIED.

What is firm is the Qur’anic core: a command to prostrate before Adam (2:34), refusal by Iblīs with arrogance, and identification of Iblīs as jinn, not angel. The angels as a class obeyed; the disobedient among the jinn refused. That distinction preserves both angelic obedience and creaturely freedom: angels are made for obedience; jinn and humans are made for trial.

**“I Know What You Do Not Know” — The Answer That Opens, Not Closes**

“Allah responded, ‘I know what you do not know’” (2:30). The reply does not deny that corruption and bloodshed will occur. Indeed, human history will soon confirm the angels’ foresight in part. The reply says the angelic inference, while true as far as it goes, is incomplete. There will be of this khalīfah what the angels do not yet know — knowers, reformers, lovers who remember, prophets and martyrs and hidden saints, artisans of mercy, and a sealing prophet whose ummah embodies what this sūrah will legislate.

The answer also models how to hear qadar: not every harm proves a mistake in creation, and not every good erases the reality of harm. Divine knowledge encompasses what angelic conjecture cannot yet hold.

**From Earth Furnished to Earth Entrusted**

2:29 furnished the earth “for you” and formed seven heavens with perfect knowledge (2:29). 2:30 entrusts that furnishing to a khalīfah. Provision and responsibility are paired: what was made for humans is now placed under human care. The sūrah’s repeated word fasād — corruption — now gains its counter-term: khilāfah as repair under trust. To reduce khilāfah to domination is to miss the verse; to reduce it to lineage is to miss its universality. Every human is, by creation, a deputy for responsibility within his or her sphere.

**Reading the Angels’ Piety Without Romanticizing Them**

The angels’ “while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness” (2:30) states their constant worship. Yet it also shows the limit of their worship: it is beautiful, ceaseless, and — by Ibn ʿArabī’s gloss cited in the source for the next verse — bounded by what each angel is. Angels glorify God through the names they know; the human will be taught names the angels do not yet bear in comprehension. Angelic holiness is pure; human holiness, when chosen, is earned through trial.

**Living the Verse**

- Succession is not only political. The plainest khilāfah is in the house, the shop, the field, the classroom: to receive what is made “for you” and return it better than taken.
- Corruption and bloodshed are not surprises to the Qur’an; they are foreknown and still outweighed by hidden goods the angels had not yet been shown. That foreknowledge without cancellation steadies action: reform is neither naive about evil nor despairing because of it.
- The proper response to “I know what you do not know” (2:30) is not to claim hidden knowledge but to act on revealed knowledge, trusting that the unseen ledger is not blank.

**A Note on Sources and Grading**

Reports that detail a tribe al-ḥinn, creation of those angels from fire, custodianship of Paradise, or the forty-year lifeless clay should be treated as popular narrative, not established sunna. The Qur’anic text (2:30; 2:34; 15:26–27; 18:50) and the well-known hadith about the Messenger’s night prayer and prostrations are firm; the embellished taunting narratives are UNVERIFIED and should not be cited as prophetic.
