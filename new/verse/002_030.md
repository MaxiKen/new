## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:30

> ˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” They asked ˹Allah˺, “Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?” Allah responded, “I know what you do not know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Appointment Announced Before It Is Made**

The verse opens a scene that the sūrah presents as a remembered conversation, and the first thing said in it is a declaration of intent. God informs the angels of a decision to place an authority on the earth. The Arabic noun rendered here as successive authority is *khalīfah*, and it carries the sense of a successor or a deputy: something or someone that comes after and stands in place of what preceded it.

The word has held a fixed place in Muslim political vocabulary ever since, through the title given to the leaders who came after the Prophet ﷺ. In this verse the term is being used about the human being as such, and the sūrah will treat the appointment as the beginning of a history rather than as the founding of an office.

**Successor to Whom, According to the Commentators**

Two readings dominate. On one, the *khalīfah* is Adam, and the word describes his standing as God's representative on the earth, charged with carrying out what he has been commanded. On the other, the word is singular in form but describes a succession: human beings who come one after another, each generation replacing the last and taking responsibility for the world it inherits.

Both readings have support in the Book's usage. Where the term appears elsewhere in the same grammatical form, it tends to describe a whole people rather than an individual: *“He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you.”* (6:165), and *“Then We made you their successors in the land to see how you would act.”* (10:14). A third passage pairs the appointment with deliverance from distress: *“And ˹Who˺ makes you successors in the earth?”* (27:62). The two readings are not in conflict; Adam is the first of the line the verse describes, and the office is shared by all who follow him.

**The Question the Angels Asked, and Why They Asked It**

The angels do not simply accept the announcement. They ask whether the one being placed will spread corruption and shed blood, and the Book records the question without censuring it. Commentators offered two explanations of the motive. One treats the question as wonder at the divine wisdom — the angels know that bloodshed will occur and do not see how it serves the purpose — while affirming that God's decision is right. The other connects the question with experience: if the earth had previous inhabitants who ruined it, the angels would be speaking from what they had watched happen.

The reports that fill in that background are mixed in quality. One widely repeated account holds that the jinn lived on the earth before human beings, that they corrupted it, and that Iblīs was among those who fought them. Another describes Iblīs as belonging to a tribe of angels called *al-ḥinn*. The sūrah al-Kahf identifies Iblīs plainly as one of the jinn, which tells against the account that makes him an angel, and al-Rāzī preferred the simpler position that God addressed all the angels without reference to a prior population. What the Book itself supplies is the fact of the question and the fact of the answer.

**Glory and Holiness Named as the Angels' Own Work**

The angels support their question with a description of their own activity: they glorify God's praises and proclaim His holiness. They are not represented as claiming a status they do not have, and their words are not a demand for the position on their own behalf. What they offer is a comparison between a creature that will ruin the earth and creatures that do not, and the Book leaves the comparison standing without accepting it as a valid argument.

The Book's own descriptions of the angels elsewhere match their self-description. The Qur'an states that the heaven and the earth and everything in them glorify God, and that no creature is exempt from that glorification even if human beings cannot comprehend it (17:44). The verse here presents the angels as precisely that kind of creature, engaged in unceasing praise, which makes their question about the value of the new creation a natural one.

**The Answer That Declines to Explain**

The reply is a single sentence: I know what you do not know. It does not deny the corruption, and it does not offer a defence of the human record. It states that the account being kept contains information the angels do not have access to, and it leaves the matter there. The Book's most characteristic answer to a question it does not choose to answer fully is exactly this refusal, and it places the refusal in the mouth of God rather than in a commentator's note.

What follows in the sūrah supplies part of the missing information. The verses immediately after this one show a capacity in Adam that the angels do not share, and the scene closes with a demonstration that vindicates the appointment. The sentence at the end of this verse is therefore not a dismissal. It is a promise that the reasons exist, and the next verses begin producing them.

**The Weight the Word Khalīfah Carries**

The noun chosen for the appointment is one of the most consequential in Muslim thought. It names a creature placed on the earth to carry out a commission that is not his own, and the Book attaches it to the whole human species rather than to a single individual. The point of using it here, before the disqualifying question is even answered, is that the appointment is a responsibility rather than a crown.

Later passages use the word and its plural in the language of testing: the Book tells a whole community that they have been made successors in the land precisely so that it may be seen how they act (10:14), and an earlier verse says the same of those who followed a destroyed people (7:69). The term therefore carries judgment inside it from the start. To be placed on the earth as a deputy is to be observed in that role, and the sūrah's account of the first human being sets the terms for every successor who follows him.
