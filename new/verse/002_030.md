## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:30

> ˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” They asked ˹Allah˺, “Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?” Allah responded, “I know what you do not know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Khalīfah: A Word That Named an Office and an Era**

The announcement's key term is *khalīfah*, and its semantic range shaped Islamic political vocabulary for a thousand years. The word comes from *khalafa*, to come after, to succeed, to replace — and it carries both directions of the relation at once: one who comes *after* another, and one who stands *in for* another, a deputy. The title *khalīfat rasūl Allāh*, successor of the Messenger of Allah, shortened to *khalīfah* and anglicized as caliph, is the historical deposit of this verse's word. But the Qur'an's own usage is wider than any throne. Here and in al-Anʿām the term spans the species: *"He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you"* (6:165) — succession as the human condition, coupled to testing. In Ṣād it narrows to an office with a job description: *"O David! We have surely made you an authority in the land, so judge between people with truth"* (38:26). Both layers are present in the announcement: humanity as such succeeds — on the earth, after its prior orders of life — and particular humans deputize, administering justice in the name of the One who placed them.

**An Announcement Made, Not a Question Asked**

The divine speech here is declarative: *innī jāʿilun fī al-ard khalīfah* — I am placing, I am about to place, a vicegerent in the earth. The active participle states a matter resolved, and the angels are informed rather than consulted. The distinction was important to the commentators, because it frames everything that follows: the angels' reply is not a vote against the decree but a question within it, and the divine response treats it as such — answering, not rebuking. There is a model of speech in the scene worth noticing. Allah includes creatures in the knowledge of His decisions without transferring to them the authorship of those decisions; He makes angels and, later, prophets and communities, parties to information about what He will do. The announcement also fixes the theater: *in the earth* — the same earth verse 29 described as created for humanity, now receiving its announced heir. The gift and the heir are matched before the heir exists; the endowment of verse 29 reads backward as preparation for this sentence.

**The Angels' Question: Two Readings and a Weighing**

The reply is the scene's puzzle: *Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood?* The question quotes two specific crimes, and the exegetes split on where such specifics came from. The first reading gives the angels experience: the earth had prior inhabitants — the jinn-tribes of the Ibn ʿAbbās narrative, who spread corruption and shed blood before being driven off — and the angels generalize from a known record. The second reading, al-Rāzī's preference, makes the question an expression of wonder rather than doubt or objection: the angels marvel at a wisdom that wills a blood-shedder for vicegerent, without impugning the wisdom. Weighing them, the question's precision favors the first — corruption and bloodshed are named, not guessed — while angelic impeccability favors the second, since wonder cannot sin. The tradition harmonizes: they knew the type because Allah had disclosed it to them, and they asked to understand, not to object. Notably, the two crimes they name are this sūrah's own charges — corruption in the land (2:11, 2:27) and the shedding of blood it will legislate against — as if the angels quoted the file on humanity that al-Baqarah is in the middle of documenting.

**The Iblīs Material and How the Tradition Grades It**

The commentaries attach a body of narrative to this verse — Ibn ʿAbbās's report of Iblīs as al-Ḥārith, an angel of the tribe called al-ḥinn, custodian of Paradise, sent against the earth's earlier corrupters, filled with pride at the victory, taunting Adam's lifeless clay body for forty years, flying in and out of it: *you are nothing*. The reports are vivid and they are graded by the tradition itself. Al-Rāzī and others note that the angelic-tribe specifics cannot be relied upon — they carry the fingerprints of popular narrative — because the Qur'an settles the question of Iblīs's kind in its own text: *"but not Iblîs, who was one of the jinn, but he rebelled against the command of his Lord"* (18:50). An authority like Ibn ʿAbbās would have known that verse. The method lesson is preserved inside the exegetical tradition rather than imported into it: lore is weighed against the Qur'an's explicit statements, and where they diverge, the lore is demoted. What survives the grading is the order-of-creation datum the Qur'an itself supplies — the jinn fashioned earlier, from scorching fire, while man was made of sounding clay (15:26–27) — enough to ground the prior-inhabitants reading without the mythology.

**“While We Glorify and Sanctify”: The Credential in the Question**

Embedded in the angels' question is their self-description: *wa-naḥnu nusabbihu bi-ḥamdika wa-nuqaddisu lak* — while we glorify Your praise and proclaim Your holiness. The two verbs name the angelic office: *tasbīḥ*, declaring Allah beyond every defect, and *taqdīs*, the related sanctification — some commentators read the pair as one act, we glorify You while praising You. The credential sharpens the question into its real form: the sky already has worship in it — ceaseless, sinless, unanimous — so what does the earth gain by a worshipper who sheds blood? The Qur'an's own cosmology deepens the puzzle: *"The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him. There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises"* (17:44). If everything already glorifies, the angels' worship is not unique in kind. The answer the scene will develop — taught names, a tested creature, worship chosen where it could be refused — supplies what universal glorification lacks: the *tasbīḥ* of a being that could have corrupted and shed blood, and did neither. The credential in the question is therefore honest and incomplete, and the verse lets it stand unrebutted.

**“I Know What You Do Not Know”**

The divine reply is the scene's whole theodicy, and it declines to argue. *Innī aʿlamu mā lā taʿlamūn* — I know what you do not know: not a refutation of the record on corruption and blood, which the reply leaves standing, but a reserve entered against it. The commentators fill the reserve with contents: prophets, saints, martyrs, knowers of Allah, generations of the just — goods the announcement will produce that the angels' data cannot yet contain. Al-Rāzī's summary holds the logic: despite the inevitability of bloodletting, good will come of this creature, and what that good is, is known to Allah alone. The reply thus establishes the epistemics of human history in one sentence: the species is justified by hidden contents, its ledger balanced by entries no observer — angelic or human — can total. It also answers backward into the sūrah: the God who knows the unshown good in a blood-shedding species is the same who overheard the mockery in the closed room (2:14) and reads what hearts conceal (2:28's Lender). The angels were told, in effect, that their information was accurate and their conclusion premature — a distinction every reader of this verse is living inside.
