## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:166

> So what you suffered on the day the two armies met was by Allah’s Will, so that He might distinguish the ˹true˺ believers

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wound Attributed to God's Permission**

The verse states that what befell the community on the day the two hosts met happened by God's permission, and it adds that the event served the purpose of making the believers known. The sentence is the sūrah's summary of the battle's place in the divine arrangement.

The wording sits beside the answer given a verse earlier, where the community was told that the affliction came from itself. The two statements are not in competition; the Book holds them together, and the commentators treat the pair as the complete account of what happened on the field. The community's conduct produced the loss, and the loss occurred within a permission that God had granted. The sentence also guards the community against reading its defeat as abandonment, since what happened took place within a permission rather than outside the knowledge of God.

**Reconciling Permission With Responsibility**

The Book states in another passage that whatever good comes to a person is from God and that whatever evil comes to him is from himself, and it uses the same division in this sūrah. The two halves of the statement describe different aspects of a single event: the good is granted, and the harm is the consequence of conduct.

The commentators applied the distinction carefully, since a community that hears only the first half drifts toward fatalism and one that hears only the second drifts toward despair. The verse before the reader supplies the first half for a community that had already been given the second, and the pairing is the Book's usual way of teaching its people to hold both truths at once.

**What Permission Means in the Sentence**

The term rendered as permission describes the granting of leave, and its use here states that the event happened within the scope of God's will rather than outside it. The Book uses the term for many kinds of event, from the arrival of rain to the delivery of a message.

One passage states that no calamity or blessing occurs on the earth or in a person's own self without being recorded before it is brought into being. The verse before the reader makes the same claim about the day of fighting. The community is told that its losses were not an interruption of the arrangement but a part of it, and that the permission under which they occurred was not withdrawn afterward.

**The Purpose Stated in the Sentence**

The clause about knowing the believers uses the same expression that the sūrah applied to the events of the battle in an earlier passage, and the commentators explained it in the same way. The knowledge described is the kind that becomes evident rather than the kind that is acquired.

The community had learned from the day who among its members would hold a position under fire and who would not. The verse states that this disclosure was one of the purposes the event served. What the community received, along with its losses, was a clearer understanding of itself, and the sūrah treats the understanding as part of what the day was for.

**The Day the Two Hosts Met**

The phrase naming the two hosts is the sūrah's standard way of referring to Uḥud, and it appears in the passage about those who turned away. The wording keeps both sides of the engagement in view and presents the battle as a meeting of two bodies rather than as a disaster that struck the community alone.

The Book's naming of the event in this way keeps its readers from reading the day as an isolated misfortune. What happened was a battle between two gathered forces, and the fact that one of them suffered more than it expected is stated in the same passage that explains why.

**The Wound as a Means of Disclosure**

The verse's arrangement of the two clauses makes the disclosure a purpose of the wound rather than an incidental result. The community was tested, and the test was intended to show what it contained.

The Book describes trials in this language throughout, and the sūrah had already used it in its account of the alternation of days. The community that understood the sentence would stop reading its losses as evidence that it had been abandoned and start reading them as information about itself, which is the reading the rest of the passage supports.

**The Consolation in the Statement**

The sentence is not a reproach. The verse immediately before it had told the community where its affliction came from, and this sentence places the same event within a purpose that God had willed.

That combination is the sūrah's method of consolation after the battle. The community is not allowed to excuse itself, and it is not allowed to conclude that the loss was meaningless. The next verse completes the sentence by naming the other group the day had exposed, and the community is thus given the full picture of what its wounds had accomplished.

**The Sentence's Place in the Sequence**

The verse stands at the head of a short passage that runs through the statements about the hypocrites, the challenge to those who claimed the dead could have been saved, and the declaration about the martyrs. It supplies the frame for all three.

The passage as a whole answers the questions the community was asking after the battle, and it does so in an order that moves from the general arrangement to the particular conduct. The community's loss, its enemies' intentions, and the state of its dead are each placed within the sentence's scope, and the verse that follows names the first of the groups the day had exposed.
