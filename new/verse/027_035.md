## Sūrah an-Naml 27:35

> But I will certainly send him a gift, and see what ˹response˺ my envoys will return with.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third Option Between Fighting and Yielding**

Her council offered war. The letter demanded submission. She takes neither and invents a third move: send a gift and watch what comes back. Nothing in the situation as presented to her contained this option; she constructed it.

The gift is not a bribe, though it could be mistaken for one, and it is not tribute, though it resembles that too. It is an instrument of inquiry. The response to a gift tells you what kind of party you are dealing with in a way no letter can, because a gift creates an obligation and how a man handles an obligation reveals him. She has been asked to submit to a king she has never met, on the basis of a document. Before doing anything irreversible, she wants to know who wrote it. That is the same instinct Sulaymān showed when he refused to act on the hoopoe's report until it was checked, and the symmetry between the two rulers is exact.

**What a Gift Tests For**

Consider the possible outcomes she is buying information about. If Sulaymān accepts the gift and drops the demand, he is a king like other kings, wanting wealth, and his letter was an extortion dressed in religious language. If he accepts and keeps the demand, he is a conqueror and the matter is settled. If he refuses it outright, then the demand was not about wealth at all, and whatever he wants is something a gift cannot satisfy.

Only the third outcome is consistent with his letter being what it claims to be. She has designed a test where the sender's sincerity is the one hypothesis that survives a refusal, and she has designed it without committing herself to anything. The gift costs her treasure, not standing. Sulaymān's actual reaction, when it comes, is the third option delivered with some force, and it therefore gives her precisely the finding she needed: this is not a man who can be paid. Everything she does afterwards follows from that single result.

**"See What My Envoys Return With"**

The final clause repeats a structure the chapter has already used. Sulaymān told the hoopoe to deliver the letter and then observe what they send back. She now tells her court she will send a gift and observe what her envoys bring back. Two rulers, the same method, described in nearly the same words.

The repetition is deliberate and it establishes something about how the chapter ranks people. The queen and the prophet-king are shown reasoning alike: neither accepts a claim without testing it, and both design the test to produce information rather than to confirm a preference. That is why the narrative can end with her submission as a conclusion rather than a capitulation. She arrives at the truth by a method that was already sound; what she lacked was the data, and she goes and gets it. Pharaoh's court, in the first half of this chapter, had the data and had no method.

**The Commentators' Inventories of the Gift**

Classical commentaries supply extensive detail about what she sent — the metals, the quantities, the slaves, sometimes elaborate puzzles designed to test Sulaymān's wisdom. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī transmit such accounts, occasionally at length.

Al-Rāzī dismissed the whole category of detail as irrelevant to the story, and he was right to. The Qur'an says *hadiyyah*, a gift, and stops. There is no evident route by which the contents of a three-thousand-year-old diplomatic package could have reached an eighth-century commentator, and the accounts do not agree with each other — which is the ordinary signature of material generated rather than transmitted. Genuine reports converge as they multiply; invented detail diverges. The inventories are best classified with the other narrative expansions the story attracted, and al-Rāzī's refusal to engage them is the soundest handling in the classical literature. What the narrative needs is that something valuable was sent and refused, and the text supplies exactly that and no more. The reticence is characteristic. The Qur'an withholds her name, her city, her dates and the contents of her gift, and keeps every detail that bears on how she thought.

**Her Reasoning Has Moved in Three Steps**

Track the queen across four verses. She received a letter and judged it noble. She asked her council for a ruling and got a capability report. She reasoned from a general law about what war does to a place. Now she acts, and the action is designed to produce knowledge rather than to settle the question.

Each step is conditioned by the one before and none of them skips ahead. She has not yet decided anything about Sulaymān's God, has not conceded a point of religion, and has not committed her kingdom to a course. What she has done is refuse to decide while uninformed — three times, in three different ways. The chapter's opening verses described people whose deeds were made to look good to them so that they wander blindly, and she is the counter-example built at length: someone who keeps checking. Her envoys reach Sulaymān's court in the next verse, and the reception they get is not what she expected to purchase.

**Gifts as a Language Between Courts**

Diplomatic gift-exchange was the standard grammar of relations between ancient Near Eastern states, and every party to it understood the conventions. Accepting a gift opened a relationship between equals; refusing one closed a door; the scale of a gift signalled the sender's estimate of the recipient's rank.

That shared code is what makes her move intelligible as a message rather than merely as a payment. She is speaking to Sulaymān in the recognised language of kings, and she is doing it after receiving a letter that spoke a different language entirely. The gift therefore asks a precise question: will you answer me as one monarch answers another? His refusal is not rudeness within that code; it is a declaration that he is not operating inside it. The queen, who understood the code well enough to use it, would have understood the refusal immediately. What she learns from the exchange is not that Sulaymān is hostile but that the conventions she governs by do not apply to this case, and that discovery is what puts her on the road to Jerusalem.
