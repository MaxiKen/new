## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:137

> So if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed ˹to the truth˺. But Allah will spare you their evil. For He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Condition and Its Consequence**

The verse sets out the terms on which the two communities would be guided, and it does so without adding requirements beyond the confession the previous sentence has just supplied. What the Book asks of them is what it asks of the believers, and the condition is stated in the form of a comparison between one community's belief and another's.

Commentators explained the phrase so that it would not suggest that the object of belief is somehow duplicated. What is meant is that the two communities should affirm what the believers affirm, and the comparison concerns the content of the confession rather than the existence of two versions of it. Some commentators added that the earlier communities could hold what the believers hold by accepting an undisfigured form of their own scriptures, which would be the same in substance as the revelation given to the Muslims.

**Guidance Attached to Belief**

The sentence makes guidance the consequence of belief, and the Book uses this order consistently. Guidance in the Qur'an is God's gift, and the verse states that it is given to those who accept what He has sent, so that the condition and the gift are held together rather than separated.

That arrangement is important for the argument the sūrah has been conducting. The two communities had claimed guidance as a property of their identity, and the verse states the condition on which it is actually granted, which is the acceptance of the revelation as a whole. What the sentence removes is the assumption that guidance follows from belonging.

**Turning Away as Schism**

The alternative is described as turning away, and the Arabic term for the condition that results carries the sense of a fracture and of opposition. The Book uses the word in other passages for those who split from the truth and set themselves against it, and the term therefore describes more than a difference of opinion.

The choice of word implies that the two communities' refusal would not leave them where they were. The sūrah has described people who recognised the truth and concealed it, and the term used here presents the refusal as a break with the position their own scriptures had assigned them. What they would be opposing is not a community but the truth that had come to them.

**God Will Suffice the Believers**

The sentence then assures the Prophet ﷺ and the community that God will spare them the harm implied by that opposition. The Arabic verb carries the sense of being enough for someone, and the Book uses it in several places where the believers are told that God is sufficient as a protector, a reckoner, or a support.

The expression became one of the community's standing formulas, and the Book records it on the lips of the believers who faced threats and answered that God is sufficient for them and the best protector. The verse in this place supplies the ground of that confidence: the opposition being described does not have the power to determine the outcome, and the One who is enough for the community hears what is said and knows what is intended.

**The Two Names at the End**

The names that close the sentence correspond to the two halves of the situation. God hears what is said against the believers, and He knows what lies behind it, so that the community's position does not depend on its ability to answer every accusation. The Book frequently ends its assurances this way, and the names give the promise its substance.

The two names also appear at the close of a later verse in this sūrah where they follow the promise of divine support in a similar contest. The repetition is characteristic of the sūrah's style, which uses the same closing attributes for situations of the same kind so that the reader learns to recognise the pattern.

**The Tone of the Sentence**

The verse does not celebrate the prospect of division, and it does not threaten the two communities with anything beyond the consequence of their own choice. What it describes is a fork in the road, and the calm wording places the responsibility with the people who must decide.

The Book's treatment of the People of the Book in this passage is consistently of this kind. The sūrah states what they believe, reports what they say, offers the criterion by which the matter can be settled, and then leaves the outcome with them. The instruction to pardon and bear with them appears in the same chapter, and the two passages are read together within the community.

**The Verse as a Summary**

The sentence summarises the argument of the previous verses in a compact form. The confession of faith has been stated, the invitation of the two communities has been reported and answered, and the verse now presents the two outcomes: acceptance, which brings guidance, and refusal, which leaves the refuser in a position of opposition that God will manage on behalf of the believers.

**What the Community Learns**

What the community receives from the verse is a way of conducting itself in a dispute that it cannot settle. The Book does not instruct the believers to secure agreement by argumentative force, and it does not leave them anxious about the results. It states the condition, describes the alternative, and locates the protection where it belongs, and the community's task is to hold to the confession while leaving the outcome to God.
