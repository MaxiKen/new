## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:137

> So if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed ˹to the truth˺. But Allah will spare you their evil. For He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condition Stated Once, Plainly**

The verse converts the confession of the previous verse into a single conditional with two exits and no third door. If they believe *in the like of* what you believe, they are guided; if they turn away, they are in opposition. The phrase "in the like of" has been read carefully by the exegetes, because taken crudely it would imply that the object of the believers' faith has a "like" — and Islamic theology holds that nothing resembles God. Al-Ṭabarī therefore explains it as affirmation of the same things: the People of the Book should believe the very realities this community believes, God and all His revelations without distinction. Al-Rāzī adds a second layer with historical bite: believing "the like" can mean believing the Torah and Gospel in their undistorted state — the scriptures as originally given rather than as edited by the hands the sūrah has already accused (2:75). On either reading the condition is not a vague sympathy but a full assent, and the guidance promised is the guidance the believers already possess.

**Shiqāq: Turning Away as Fracture**

The word for what the refusers do is *shiqāq* — literally a splitting or fracture, and by extension an attitude of active opposition and hostility. The Qur'an uses the same root consistently in this hardened sense: those who differ regarding the Book are *"totally engrossed in opposition"* (2:176); the wrongdoers are *"totally engrossed in opposition"* (22:53); the disbelievers stand *"˹entrenched˺ in arrogance and opposition"* (38:2); and the one who denies what is from God has *"gone too far in opposition"* (41:52). The choice of word matters. Turning away from this creed is not classified as a legitimate alternative, a different path among paths. It is named as rupture — a deliberate break made against known truth. The verse thus refuses the comfortable modern category of "equally valid options": belief here yields guidance, refusal yields schism, and the two outcomes are not morally symmetrical.

**A Guarantee Before the Community Had Power**

Between the two exits the verse places a promise: "But Allah will spare you their evil." When this was revealed the Muslim community in Medina was small, surrounded, and internally infiltrated; its opponents had wealth, alliances, and rhetorical skill. The guarantee that their evil would be sufficed away was not a description of present strength but a pledge against a future the community could not yet see. The verb is *kafā*, to suffice — to be enough for a need. God is announced as enough against the opponents' evil: enough that their scheming will not destroy the community, enough that its members need not buy safety by diluting the creed. The promise is total because the sufficiency is divine; no quantity of opposition exhausts it, which is why the verse can state it in one clause and move on.

**The Family of Sufficiency**

The Qur'an returns to this sufficiency in register after register. God is sufficient as a Reckoner over the trusts of orphans (4:6); sufficient as Guardian and Helper against enemies (4:45); sufficient for the Prophet and the believers who follow him (8:64); sufficient in battle, when He drove back the confederates *"in their rage, totally emptyhanded"* (33:25). The believers' own liturgy of sufficiency is the sentence *"Allah is sufficient for me"* placed on the Prophet's tongue when the adversaries turn away (9:129), and the older formula *ḥasbunā Allāh* — God is enough for us — which Āl ʿImrān records the believers saying when told a great army had gathered against them (3:173). Each instance is a variation on the promise of this verse: whatever the form of the evil — financial, military, rhetorical — the sufficiency covers it.

**Two Men and Two Fires**

Ibn ʿAbbās linked that formula to two moments of extreme danger, and the report is preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (4563): "Allah is Sufficient for us and He is the Best Disposer of affairs" was said by Abraham when he was thrown into the fire, and it was said by Muhammad when the people told the believers that a great army was gathering against them — and it only increased their faith. The two men faced the same arithmetic: overwhelming force on one side, nothing visible on the other. In both cases the sentence was not a denial of the danger but a declaration about the size of God beside it. Read against this verse, the arc is exact. The promise that Allah will spare the believers their opponents' evil was made in Medina when the community was weak, and its first great test — the confederate army at the Trench — ended with the enemy driven back without a battle, the sufficiency demonstrated rather than argued.

**All-Hearing, All-Knowing**

The verse seals with two names: He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. The pairing is not decorative; it names the two faculties the besieged community most needs God to have. All-Hearing: the mockery is audible to Him, the taunts about the qiblah, the whispered "he follows our religion," the slander of a community too small to answer every voice. All-Knowing: the schemes are visible to Him, the private plotting that never reaches speech, the intentions behind the arguments. A guarantee against "their evil" would be worthless if God could be misinformed about it, so the names certify the guarantee. The enemies are not being spared attention — they are being heard and known with a completeness their victims cannot match, and the case is in hands that will not lose evidence. The community's job, in this verse's own logic, is not surveillance or counter-plotting but belief: hold the creed, and the sufficiency holds the rest.
