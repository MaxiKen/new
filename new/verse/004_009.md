## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:9

> Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind. So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Imagine Your Own Children Left Behind**

This verse asks the guardian to reverse perspectives. *Wa al-yakhsha alladhīna law tarakū min khalfihim dhurriyyatan ḍiʿāfan khāfū ʿalayhim* — let the guardians be as concerned for the orphans as they would if they were to die and leave their own helpless children behind. The image is specific: not children in general, but *dhurriyyatan ḍiʿāfan* — weak offspring, small children who cannot yet stand on their own, whose future depends on how others will treat them. Every guardian knows the fear that image brings. The Qur’an takes that natural fear and turns it into a rule for conduct. Deal with the orphan in your care the way you would want strangers to deal with your children tomorrow. That single substitution — put your child in the orphan’s place — does more work than a list of penalties, because it recruits love and worry on behalf of justice. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“do not kill your children out of fear of poverty”* (17:31) and praises those who prefer others over themselves, but here the appeal is quieter: remember your own vulnerability, and let that memory govern your hand when it holds another’s wealth.

**Be Mindful of Allah**

The command *fa al-yattaqū Allāh* — so let them be mindful of Allah — returns the sūrah to its opening word. Verse 1 began with *ittaqū rabbakum* — be mindful of your Lord who created you from a single soul — and verse 1 closed with *Allah is ever Watchful over you* (4:1). This verse’s call to *taqwā* therefore completes a short arch: creation to kinship to accountability. For the guardian, *taqwā* is not an abstract piety; it is caution in accounting, restraint in spending, promptness in testing and returning, and care in wording at distribution. The sūrah has already detailed those steps: return wealth without substitution (4:2), do not use marriage to discount dowries (4:3–4), do not hand wealth prematurely while still providing generously and speaking kindly (4:5), test and return with witnesses under God as Reckoner (4:6), honour fixed shares for both sexes (4:7), and offer provision to those present with decent words (4:8). This verse gathers those particulars into a posture. To be mindful of God here is to handle the orphan’s affair as one who knows he is watched while others cannot watch him.

**Speak Equitably**

The phrase *wa al-yaqūlū qawlan sadīdan* — and speak equitably — was read by early commentators as speech that is straight, sound, and hits the mark, like an arrow that flies true. *Sadīd* in Arabic conveys both correctness and directness, a word that lands where it should. In this context it covers several kinds of speech at once. To the orphan, it is the decent word that explains why property is being managed and when it will be returned, already commanded as *qawlan maʿrūfā* (4:5) and *qawlan maʿrūfā* at distribution (4:8). To oneself, it is the inner speech of intention — to mean to act justly and to commit to fairness when tempted to delay or to take. To others, it is the counsel a person would give about an orphan’s affairs if consulted by a dying friend about his children. The verse was also read as guidance for bequests: when a person with children advises another who is writing his bequest, or when he gives a deathbed instruction that would impoverish his own heirs, he should speak *sadīd* — straight — and advise equity, as in not bequeathing beyond one-third to the detriment of children. Al-Ṭabarī cites examples where a man on his deathbed wanted to give away so much that his children would be left needy, and the counsel was to say what is sound and balanced instead.

**Counsel About Bequests**

That bequest reading deserves attention because it ties verse 9 to the detailed inheritance fractions that follow. Some commentators understood the verse to address those who are present when a person is drafting a bequest and who might encourage him to dispose excessively. The fear-for-your-own-children test applies directly: would you want someone to encourage your father to leave you little? If not, then speak what is straight. The Qur’an elsewhere limits bequests to what does not harm heirs (4:12) and restricts the harms that can be done through wills. This verse therefore does double duty. For the guardian of existing orphans, it says handle their property as you would want yours handled. For the adviser of a future estate, it says counsel the testator toward equity as you would want others to counsel your own kin. In both cases the measure is the same: imagine your children weak behind you, and let that image straighten your words. The phrase *qawlan sadīdan* thus becomes more than politeness; it becomes the verbal form of justice, the counterpart to the just division of portions that the sūrah is about to quantify.

**A Verse That Completes Guardianship**

Read with verses 2 through 8, this verse finishes the sūrah’s opening ethic of trust. Verse 2 commanded return; verse 3 redirected marriage away from exploitation; verse 4 secured dowries; verse 5 taught sustaining provision without premature handover and with kind speech; verse 6 set testing, restraint, fair compensation, witnesses, and divine reckoning; verse 7 established obligatory shares for both sexes; verse 8 added voluntary provision for bystanders with kind speech. Verse 9 supplies the inner motive that makes those steps sincere: fear on behalf of your own weak offspring, *taqwā* of God, and straight speech. Without that motive, the procedures could be performed technically while the heart remains hard. With it, the procedures become acts of mercy. The sequence also shows how the Qur’an legislates through empathy as well as rule. It does not only say do this; it says feel this, remember this, imagine this, and then do this. That method keeps law from becoming a set of evasions. A guardian who can picture his own child asking for bread tomorrow will not easily tell himself that the orphan in his house will not need his property until later.

**A Standard the Community Can Apply**

The verse’s test is portable beyond individual guardians. A judge settling an estate, a committee dividing a legacy, a family meeting to agree on shares — each can ask the same question: if these were my children left weak, what would I want the table to decide? That question does not replace the fixed shares of 4:11–12, but it governs the discretionary spaces around them — timing, valuation, generosity to those present, tone of words, and speed of return. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“Allah is a Witness over all things”* (4:33) and *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6), and this verse places that watchfulness inside human imagination. Before the next verse warns that consuming orphans’ wealth is eating fire (4:10) and before the long arithmetic of inheritance begins, the sūrah secures the disposition that will make the arithmetic honest. It asks for fear that leads to *taqwā*, and for *taqwā* that leads to straight speech, and for speech that leads to action where the weak child, whether yours or another’s, is left neither unprotected nor unheard.
