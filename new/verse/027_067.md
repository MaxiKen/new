## Sūrah an-Naml 27:67

> The disbelievers ask, “When we and our fathers are reduced to dust, will we really be brought forth ˹alive˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Their Own Words, Quoted**

The chapter now lets the opposition speak. For two verses the Qur'an reports the Meccan objection in the first person, without paraphrase, and the objection is a question about dust.

Quoting an opponent accurately is a choice, and the Qur'an makes it consistently — Pharaoh's speeches are recorded, Iblīs is given his arguments, and the councils of the destroyed nations are reported in their own words. The practice has an effect on how the refutation lands. A reader who has heard the objection stated at full strength is in a different position from one handed only the answer. This particular question appears in near-identical form in half a dozen chapters, which indicates it was the standing Meccan reply rather than a remark made once.

**The Objection Is About Physical Bodies**

What they find incredible is specific. Not that God exists, not that He created them, but that a person reduced to *turāb*, dust, could be brought out again as himself. The verb they use, *mukhrajūn*, means taken out — the image is of extraction from a grave.

That specificity tells us the dispute was never theological in the broad sense. The Meccans conceded a creator; the Qur'an says so elsewhere. What they could not accept was a bodily resurrection, and the reason is visible in their phrasing. They are reasoning from an observation: decomposition is total, the material disperses, and nothing in ordinary experience reverses it. As an inference from available evidence it is entirely sound. It is also, the chapter has just argued, an inference drawn from the wrong evidence base, since the question is not what usually happens but what the one who began the process can do.

**"We and Our Fathers"**

They include their ancestors in the objection, and the inclusion is not incidental. It appears again in the next verse, where the promise is said to have been made to their fathers before them.

Two things are being done by the phrase. The first is rhetorical amplification: generations have gone into the ground and none has come out, which strengthens the empirical case. The second is social. Accepting the resurrection means accepting that the men whose way of life they inherited are also subject to it, and the Qur'an records elsewhere that the fear of implicating the fathers was a real obstacle to Meccan conversion. A doctrine that condemns your ancestors is harder to accept than one that merely inconveniences you. Their own wording exposes the pressure they were under.

**Al-Rāzī on What the Doubt Is Really About**

Al-Rāzī identifies the root of the objection: doubt about the resurrection comes from doubt about the power of God, and it amounts to a failure of ordinary human imagination. That diagnosis is exact and it locates the problem where the Qur'an locates it.

The Book answers this objection in several places by pointing at scale. If the one who created the heavens and the earth is asked whether He can create their like, the question answers itself — and the Qur'an puts it in precisely those terms in Yā Sīn. The argument is one of proportion. A person who can accept the larger act and balk at the smaller one is not applying a consistent standard; he is applying an imaginative one, in which what he can picture seems possible and what he cannot seems absurd. Al-Rāzī's phrase for this is the failure of ordinary imagination, and it is the correct description of an objection that rests on never having seen something happen.

**Dust Is the Qur'an's Own Word for the Beginning**

There is an irony in the term they chose. *Turāb*, dust or earth, is the substance the Qur'an repeatedly names as what the human being was made from in the first place.

Their objection therefore runs: how can we be brought out when we are dust? — and the Book's account of their origin gives the answer inside the question. They began as the material they are afraid of becoming. Whatever happened at the start is being denied as impossible at the end, using the same noun for both. The Qur'an does not point this out in this verse, and it does not need to; the vocabulary is shared and the audience knew both claims. What the chapter has already put in place, three verses earlier, is the principle that resolves it: the one who originates the creation is the one who brings it back, and the second act is not the harder of the two.

**Why This Objection Recurs Across the Qur'an**

The same question appears in nearly identical wording in ar-Raʿd, al-Isrāʾ, al-Muʾminūn, aṣ-Ṣāffāt and al-Wāqiʿah. Five chapters, one sentence, barely altered — which tells us the Qur'an was answering a fixed formulation rather than a shifting set of doubts.

A repeated objection usually indicates a settled talking point, something a community has reduced to a single line that closes the conversation. The Meccan version had all the marks of one: short, concrete, framed as a question, and apparently unanswerable by observation. The Qur'an's treatment of it is correspondingly consistent. It never disputes the premise that bodies decompose, and it never offers a mechanism for reassembly. What it does, every time, is relocate the question from what has been observed to who is being asked, which is the only place the argument can actually be settled. The objection is about the limits of nature; the reply is about who set them. That is why none of the answers in the Qur'an is technical. Each one asks the questioner to compare the difficulty of the second act with the difficulty of the first, and the comparison always runs the wrong way for the objector.
