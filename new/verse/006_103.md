## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:103

> No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision. For He is the Most Subtle, All-Aware.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Says God Is Beyond Sight**

No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision. The sentence states a limit on human perception and a corresponding attribute of God, and the Book's commentators treated it as one of the most important verses in the Qur'an on the subject of seeing God. The Book says that eyes cannot reach Him and that His perception reaches everything, and it arranges the two clauses as a contrast between what creatures can do and what the Creator does.

The two verbs are the same in the Arabic, which is why the sentence has the shape it has: no vision encompasses Him, and He encompasses all vision. The verse does not simply deny that God is visible; it denies that any act of perception can take Him in, and it states that the relation of knowledge runs in the opposite direction.

**How the Companions Used the Verse**

The earliest community read this verse as a statement about the vision of God, and the reports about ʿĀisha are the clearest example. When she was told that some people claimed that the Prophet had seen his Lord, she rejected the claim and cited this verse, saying that anyone who told her that Muhammad had seen his Lord had told a lie, because God says that no vision can encompass Him. The report is recorded by al-Bukhārī and it shows how the verse functioned in the community's theology from the beginning.

ʿĀisha used the same style of argument against the claim that the Prophet had been shown the unseen, citing the verse that says none has knowledge of the unseen but God and the report about the five keys. The two verses together, this one and the one on the keys of the unseen (6:59), furnished the early community with its account of the limits of created knowledge.

**The Debate About Seeing God**

The verse entered the later theological discussions as one of the central texts. Some held that God cannot be seen at all, and they cited this verse together with the answer given to Moses when he asked to see his Lord and was told that he could not see Him, though the mountain was levelled when God appeared to it (7:143). Others held that the verse refers to the perception of the eyes in this world and that the believers will be granted the vision of God in the Hereafter, and they cited other passages for that hope.

What the verse itself settles is the inability of human perception to contain God, and the Book elsewhere states that no vision can reach Him while He perceives everything. The passage about Moses adds the detail that even a prophet's request was answered with a limit, and the two verses together define the discussion for every generation that has taken it up.

**The Names at the End**

For He is the Most Subtle, All-Aware. The two names explain why the contrast in the first half of the verse holds. The Most Subtle describes a being whose knowledge penetrates what is hidden from every other observer, and the All-Aware describes a knowledge of the inner reality of things. Together they state the reason that God perceives what no eye can reach.

The sūrah has attached the same vocabulary to God throughout its account of the hidden and the seen. It said that God knows what is on land and sea and that not a leaf falls without His knowledge (6:59); it described His knowledge as encompassing everything in Abraham's argument (6:80); and it stated that He has the keys of the unseen (6:59). The names that close this verse are the summary of that whole account.

**The Creation and the Knowledge of It**

The sūrah of the Kingdom puts the two halves of this verse's logic together in a single sentence, asking how the One who created could fail to know what He has made, and naming Him the Most Subtle, the All-Aware (67:14). That is the reasoning that stands behind this verse: the maker of a thing knows it in a way no outside observer can, and the knowledge described in the second clause is the knowledge of the one who formed what He sees.

The Book states the same principle through the image of the mustard seed in the counsel of Luqmān, where the father tells his son that a deed the weight of a seed, hidden in a rock or in the heavens or the earth, will be brought out by God, who is the Most Subtle, the All-Aware (31:16). The closing names of this verse carry the same scope, and the sūrah of the Women and the sūrah of the Confederates attach the pair to the recording of what people do. A perception that runs through rock and seed and sky is the perception the sūrah has claimed for God throughout, and this verse states it as the ground of the account that follows.

**What the Verse Does for the Sūrah's Argument**

Read in place, the verse answers a question the previous verses had raised. The sūrah had presented the audience with God as the Creator and Maintainer of everything, and the natural response of a person hearing that description is to look for the object described. The verse states that the search will not succeed and that the perception which matters runs from God toward the world rather than the other way.

That arrangement is what makes the record described in the previous verses possible. A God who is seen by His creatures could not know them as this sūrah has described Him knowing them, and the verse's second clause is the ground of the first. The Book's arguments about the leaf, the grain, and the deeds of human beings all rest on the fact that nothing is beyond His awareness.

**The Transition That Follows**

The next verse turns from the description of God to the responsibility of the people who have been given this knowledge, saying that insights have come from the Lord and that whoever chooses to see does so for his own good while whoever chooses blindness does so to his own loss (6:104). The verses function as a pair: the true account of God is stated, and the reader is told that the response to it is his own.

The sūrah has been making the same point since its account of the blind and the seeing (6:50), and the pairing in these two verses is its final statement of the principle in this section. What follows is a series of further signs, and the audience is left with a choice that the Book describes without pressure.
