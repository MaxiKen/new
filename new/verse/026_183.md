## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:183

> and do not defraud people of their property. Nor go about spreading corruption in the land.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Other People's Property Is Not Yours to Reduce**

The command names the victim as well as the act. People possess property, and the person who deals with it must not quietly turn part of it into his own gain. Defrauding is more than a mistake in arithmetic. It is a knowing reduction of another person's right through deceit, pressure, or misuse of trust.

Quran 2:188 applies the same principle to wealth taken through false means and even mentions using authorities to consume part of another person's property. The reference shows why Shuʿayb's warning cannot be reduced to marketplace weights. A false claim, a corrupt ruling, a hidden fee, and a short measure can share one moral structure: taking what belongs to someone else while pretending that the taking is legitimate.

**Corruption Travels Beyond One Transaction**

The verse then turns from a single act of fraud to a course of conduct in the land. To go about spreading corruption suggests movement, persistence, and public reach. Classical explanations connect the Forest people's wrongdoing with predatory acts such as banditry, raiding, attacks on travelers, and damage to crops. These are not merely private sins. They make ordinary life unsafe for everyone who depends on roads, fields, and markets.

Corruption grows when a gain made by one person becomes a fear carried by many. A trader who cheats one buyer harms that buyer; a group that intimidates travelers changes how an entire region lives. The verse joins property and land because private greed can become public disorder.

An isolated dishonest act is serious, but corruption becomes more destructive when leaders protect it, institutions normalize it, or the strong make resistance costly. The victim then loses more than property. He loses the expectation that law, custom, or community will protect him.

This is why 7:85 pairs the prohibition of defrauding property with the prohibition of spreading corruption after the land has been set right. The command does not leave justice to private goodwill. It requires a public order in which trade, authority, and security do not cooperate in the removal of people's rights.

**The Land Is a Trust, Not a Playground for Predation**

The expression land gives the warning a common setting. People meet there, travel there, cultivate it, and build their livelihoods there. Corruption is therefore not an invisible defect in one heart only. It appears in roads made dangerous, harvests destroyed, agreements made meaningless, and communities forced to organize around fear.

A person may claim that his gain is small, but repeated small acts can change the character of a place. When dishonesty becomes expected, honest people pay more to protect themselves and the vulnerable withdraw from useful activity. Restoring the land begins with restoring the rule that no one may use another person's weakness as a resource.

**Justice Requires Repair, Not Only Restraint**

To stop defrauding is the first duty, not the whole of social repair. Stolen property must be returned where possible, false accounts corrected, and people harmed by corruption given protection. A community cannot praise order while leaving the victims to carry the cost of its old practices.

Shuʿayb's command keeps worship tied to this practical work. Allah is not honored by ceremonies that leave people exposed to theft and intimidation. The land becomes sound when property is respected, power is restrained, and those who were made vulnerable can live without expecting the next deception. That is the opposite of a society built on corruption.

Repair begins with naming the actual loss. A community cannot say that corruption is finished while it praises the person who gained through fraud and leaves the victim to absorb the damage. Accounts must be opened, testimony heard, and stolen rights restored as far as possible. Where complete restoration cannot be made, the responsible authority must not hide behind the difficulty. It should provide protection and a fair remedy.

The command also separates lawful profit from predation. Trade is not corruption merely because one party gains and another pays. Corruption appears when the gain depends on false information, coercion, theft, or the destruction of the conditions that let others live and trade safely. This distinction prevents both greed and a careless condemnation of every economic difference.

A society can spread corruption through speech as well as through goods. False accusations can turn neighbors against one another, and fabricated claims can make honest people lose work or property. Public officials can worsen the harm when they repeat rumors or punish without evidence. The verse's wide wording keeps justice from being narrowed to a measuring cup.

Shuʿayb's command is therefore a program of restraint and responsibility. Do not take what belongs to people, do not make the land unsafe, and do not treat public trust as a resource for private gain. The community's prosperity is genuine only when people can keep their rights without bribery, intimidation, or deception.

A community should therefore ask where its convenience is coming from. Cheap goods may conceal an unpaid worker, a stolen resource, or a producer forced to accept an unfair price. Fast growth may conceal land taken without consent or public money diverted from those who need it. The verse does not require suspicion of every benefit, but it does require the courage to trace benefit back to the rights of the people who carried its cost.

Spreading corruption can also mean making good conduct appear foolish. If honest merchants are mocked and officials are expected to accept bribes, the public order trains people toward wrongdoing. Reform must then praise integrity, make complaints safe, and refuse to reward the person who harms others most skillfully. Justice is not complete until truthful conduct is possible without extraordinary bravery.
