## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:96

> There the deviant will cry while disputing with their idols,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fire Produces Argument, Not Escape**

The deviant cries while disputing with the idols. The scene shows suffering becoming speech. The condemned do not meet the idols in peaceful devotion; they argue with them. The relationship that once promised comfort has become a source of accusation. Their cries reveal that false worship did not merely fail to save them. It left them with a history they now have to face.

The word deviant describes the people who chose a direction away from God, while the idols represent the objects around which that direction was organized. Their dispute is therefore about more than a statue. It is about the authority they granted, the hopes they placed, and the allegiance that shaped their choices.

**Dispute Reveals the Collapse of Trust**

A person argues with someone from whom he expected an answer. The condemned speak to the idols as though the objects could bear responsibility, but the very act of disputing exposes the failure of that expectation. The idols have no reply and no power to change the scene. The worshippers are left speaking into the emptiness they once called sacred.

This is the final form of a broken relationship. In worldly life, a person may protect an idol from criticism because family, priesthood, or custom surrounds it. In the Hereafter, those defenses have disappeared. The worshippers can no longer preserve the illusion through ritual. Their words meet objects that cannot respond.

**Crying Shows Recognition Arriving Too Late**

The deviant cries because the truth has become unbearable. They recognize the loss of every false refuge, but recognition after judgment does not function like repentance during life. The difference between the two moments is crucial. A person can use awareness now to change direction; the condemned can only express grief over a direction that has ended.

The verse does not mock the tears. It displays their tragedy. A warning that produces repentance is mercy, while a truth recognized only after the final unveiling becomes evidence against the one who rejected it. The reader is invited to hear the cry early enough to avoid its cause.

**The Idols Are Treated as Responsible Partners**

The deviant disputes with the idols as though the objects had participated in the wrong. This language may be rhetorical, because the idols are powerless, but it captures how worshippers experienced their own attachment. They gave the objects authority and relied on them as partners beside God. At judgment, they address the objects in the language of a broken alliance.

The complaint cannot make the idols responsible in the way human beings are responsible. The idols did not choose to be worshipped. The people chose to worship them and chose to obey the voices that defended them. The dispute therefore exposes a moral reversal: the worshippers try to transfer their own responsibility to the objects they invented as lords.

**A Parallel Shows Satan Rejecting the Blame**

In 14:22, Satan speaks after judgment and denies possessing authority over his followers beyond calling them and receiving their response. That parallel illuminates the dispute here. Those who followed false powers may blame the objects or the tempters, but the existence of influence does not erase the choice to answer it.

The two scenes share a warning about misplaced blame. A leader may invite, an idol may be honored, and a tempter may decorate sin, yet the servant remains accountable for the allegiance he accepts. The argument in the Fire cannot undo the decision made in the world. It only reveals how badly the condemned wish that someone else could carry it.

**Dispute Cannot Restore the Lost Object's Power**

The deviant may raise accusations, but argument does not give the idols ears. The objects remain what they were: created things unable to help themselves. Their presence in the dispute is humiliating precisely because the worshippers still address them while knowing, at some level, that they cannot answer.

This is the danger of confusing emotional intensity with truth. A person may feel that an object has guided or protected him because the practice surrounding it is powerful. The feeling can be real while the object remains powerless. The final dispute tears apart that confusion. Emotion does not create divinity, and regret does not create a late rescue.

**The Group's Cry Exposes Shared Responsibility**

The deviant are not described as silent individuals. They cry while disputing with their idols, and the plural setting gives the argument a public dimension. The people who once supported one another in false worship now share the distress produced by it. Social agreement had made error feel secure; the same collective setting cannot save them from its result.

This does not make the group responsible in one undifferentiated way. Each person had a distinct role, level of knowledge, and choice. Yet the scene shows that communities shape worship. A culture that praises false objects can make deviation normal. Its members must resist that pressure rather than treating common practice as proof.

**The Verse Turns a Shrine Into a Scene of Accusation**

The shrine in worldly life was a place where people expected benefit and protection. The Fire turns it into a courtroom of words. The idol is no longer surrounded by offerings or caretakers. It is surrounded by claims, tears, and the evidence of failure. The change reveals what the shrine had hidden: the objects were never sources of salvation.

A believer should therefore examine not only what he calls sacred but what his sacred practice produces. Does it lead to truth, humility, mercy, and obedience to God? Or does it protect an object, leader, or custom from every question? The dispute in the Fire is the end of a worship that refused examination while life still offered time.

**The Cry Is a Warning to the Living**

The verse gives the reader access to a conversation that the condemned cannot use for escape. Hearing it now is a mercy. It teaches that false worship will not remain an abstract mistake. It will become a personal crisis when the heart discovers that its chosen objects cannot answer.

The right response is not to wait for certainty in the Fire. It is to settle allegiance while repentance remains possible. Abandon false trust, seek God alone, and take responsibility for the choices no idol or tempter can carry. The cry of the deviant is a final testimony that no created object deserves the heart's ultimate dependence.
