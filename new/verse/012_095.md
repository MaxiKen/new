## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:95

> They replied, “By Allah! You are definitely still in your old delusion.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Verse in the Chapter's Final Movement**

Four words of dialogue occupy a verse of their own, and the allocation is a marker of weight. The chapter has used the same technique throughout its closing section: a promise given its own verse (12:61), a question given its own verse (12:71), a report given its own verse (12:81). Here a dismissal receives the same treatment. The brevity is not a lack of content but a concentration of it. Everything the family has believed about Joseph for decades, everything they have said to Jacob about his grief, and everything they are about to have corrected is contained in a single sentence, and the chapter lets the sentence stand alone. Its brevity gives the correction weight. A longer verse would have reasoned with the household, explained the scent or appealed to Jacob's record; this one asserts that they will find him neither senile nor deluded, and leaves the assertion to be tested by what arrives. The chapter is confident enough in the coming event to introduce it in a single unadorned sentence.

**"Your Old Delusion"**

The adjective is the verse's sharpest word. The speakers do not say their father is mistaken now; they say he is in his old, long-standing error — a condition they date to the past and present as continuous. The commentators gloss the reference as Jacob's excessive love for Joseph and his hope of meeting him again, which those around him considered unreasonable given what they took to be the facts. The adjective therefore does double work. It identifies the duration of the grief, and it identifies the speakers' long acquaintance with it: they have watched this hope for decades and have concluded that it is a fixed feature of an old man's mind rather than a belief about the world. Treating it as a feature rather than a belief is what makes the charge unanswerable at the time. A mistaken belief can be corrected by evidence; a disposition cannot, because every piece of evidence offered will be read as a symptom of it. Jacob's household has reached the point of dismissing his claims by reference to his age rather than examining them, and the verse records the dismissal in their words.

**An Oath Used to Dismiss a Prophet**

The reply opens with *tallāh*, by God — the same formula used in verse 91 by the brothers confessing their sin, in verse 73 denying the theft, in verse 85 predicting their father's death from grief, and in verse 16 supporting a false report. The chapter has used the formula five times and it now uses it for a dismissal. The accumulation is worth noticing without being moralised: an oath is a form of emphasis, and this family reaches for emphasis whenever it is most confident, including at the moments when it is most wrong. Verse 96 will show the confidence collapsing inside a single verse. The collapse is immediate and total. The household that swears by God that Jacob is mistaken receives a traveller, a shirt and a restoration of sight inside a single verse, and no further argument is addressed to them. The chapter sets the oath and its refutation beside each other with nothing in between, which is the most economical rebuttal available to it.

**Who Is Speaking, and When**

The identity of the speakers is disputed, and the dispute matters. Some commentators attribute the reply to those of Jacob's tribe who were with him in Canaan, which fits the timing: the caravan has only just departed Egypt (12:94) and the sons cannot yet be present. Others attribute it to the brothers, reading the exchange as part of a conversation that occurs on their return. The chapter does not settle the question, and both readings leave the sense intact: the people closest to Jacob dismiss his perception of Joseph. On the first reading the dismissal comes from a household that has never had cause to reconsider; on the second it comes from men who have just met the person being discussed.

**Two Charges in Two Verses**

Verse 85 accused Jacob of a grief that would kill him. Verse 95 accuses him of a delusion that has lasted decades. The two charges are different in kind: the first concerns the effect of his sorrow on his body, the second concerns the truth of his belief. Together they describe a family that has concluded both that their father is being destroyed by his mourning and that the object of the mourning is not real. The chapter does not have anyone answer these charges in argument. Verse 96 answers them with an event — a bearer of good news arrives, a shirt is cast over a face, and sight returns. Answering with an event is the chapter's characteristic method. Senility and delusion are claims about a mind, and no argument about a mind settles them; a blind man recovering his sight when a shirt is cast over his face settles them. This verse reports the charges, the next reports the arrival, and the juxtaposition performs the work of a rebuttal.

**The Verse Is the Low Point of Jacob's Position**

Read in sequence, verses 83 to 95 trace a steady deterioration. Jacob rejects a report, declares beautiful patience, hopes for a return of all three sons, turns away and weeps until his eyes fail, is rebuked for the grief, redirects his complaint to God, claims knowledge his family does not have, commissions a search, perceives a scent, anticipates dismissal, and is dismissed. Every step is recorded without relief and without support from anyone in his household. Verse 95 is the end of that descent, and it is the last thing said to Jacob before the reversal begins. The chapter gives it a verse of its own so that the reader marks the point. Marking it is an act of honesty about belief. For the length of one verse the sūrah shows a prophet who is not believed by his own household, whose claim cannot be checked and whose only support is a knowledge he cannot explain. That is where Jacob stands before the caravan arrives, and the chapter does not soften it by anticipating the arrival.

**Verse 96 Answers in Jacob's Own Words**

The reversal is immediate and it is total. The bearer of good news arrives, the shirt is cast over Jacob's face, his sight returns, and he says to his children: did I not tell you that I know from God what you do not know (12:96)? The reply is not an argument against the charge of delusion. It is a reminder that the charge was made against a claim already stated in verse 86 and now verified. The chapter's method is to answer a dismissal with an event and then let the dismissed person note that he had said so. Nothing in verse 95 is refuted directly; it is simply overtaken, in the space of one verse, by the arrival of what it denied.

**The Delusion Was the Only Accurate Belief in the Room**

The verse's irony is exact and the chapter states none of it. The people dismissing Jacob as deluded are either his household, who have believed for decades that a wolf ate his son, or his sons, who have just met that son in an Egyptian granary and watched him forgive them. On either reading the accusation is false in a way the accusers either cannot know or have just been shown. What the dismissed man believed — that Joseph was alive and that God would return all three sons to him (12:83) — is the only belief in the exchange that the sequel confirms. The chapter therefore closes the family's argument not by persuading anyone but by arriving at the father's position from a direction nobody in Canaan expected.
