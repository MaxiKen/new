## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:123

> The people of ’Âd rejected the messengers

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿĀd Enters the Pattern of Prophetic Rejection**

The people of ʿĀd are introduced with the same verdict used for Noah's people: they rejected the messengers. The story now changes its nation, messenger, and social setting, but the basic problem remains. A community receives a call from God and decides that its inherited power and customs deserve protection from it.

The opening sentence does not begin with their buildings or strength. It begins with their response to revelation. That choice establishes the moral center of the account. ʿĀd will be remembered not merely as a people who possessed remarkable resources but as a people whose power could not overcome their rejection of the messengers.


Although the immediate messenger is Hūd, the verse uses the plural messengers. The wording can mean that rejecting one true messenger is rejection of the shared prophetic call. Messengers may appear in different times and languages, but the central demand remains worship of God, reverence, and obedience to revealed guidance.

The plural also places Hūd within continuity rather than isolation. He is not inventing a private religion for ʿĀd. He stands in the same line as Noah and the messengers who came before and after. A community that rejects him is resisting a universal pattern of warning, not simply declining one individual's advice.

**The Community's Strength Does Not Validate Its Faith**

ʿĀd is associated in other Qur'anic passages with great stature and power. Yet this verse introduces them through rejection, not through praise of their achievements. Strength can make a people confident that its culture is self-sufficient. The prophetic call exposes the weakness beneath that confidence.

A society may build roads, fortresses, monuments, and systems of control while remaining spiritually blind. Material achievement answers some worldly needs but cannot answer who deserves worship or whether the powerful will be judged. ʿĀd's story refuses to let civilization become proof of divine approval.


Quran 7:65 describes Hūd as the brother sent to ʿĀd and records his call to worship Allah and fear Him. The parallel confirms that the people were not confronted by an outsider with no connection to their lives. Their rejection was a refusal of a familiar messenger who understood their world and addressed their own society.

Quran 46:21 likewise calls him the brother of ʿĀd and describes his warning among the sand-hills. These passages establish that the plural messengers in the present verse includes Hūd as part of a known prophetic mission, not as a symbolic figure detached from history.

**Rejection Can Hide Behind Cultural Confidence**

A people that believes its customs prove its superiority can treat warning as an attack on identity. Hūd's message asks ʿĀd to judge its worship and conduct by God rather than by the age or grandeur of its civilization. That request threatens any culture that has made its own achievement the measure of truth.

The problem is not love of language, place, or inherited memory. The problem comes when those things become shields against correction. A people can preserve its culture while submitting to God, but it cannot make culture an authority above the messenger. ʿĀd's rejection shows what happens when pride turns heritage into a rival lord.


The plural also carries a moral warning for selective belief. A person may claim respect for earlier messengers while rejecting the one whose message challenges present interests. The Qur'anic presentation treats that division as incoherent. Truth does not change its status because the messenger appears in a different generation.

Hūd's people are not allowed to say that his call is too local to matter. His message belongs to the same line of guidance that confronted earlier peoples. The people who reject him reject the authority of that line, even if they have never encountered every messenger personally.

**The Opening Refuses to Let Power Write History**

Powerful societies often preserve their own account of themselves. They celebrate victories, buildings, and ancestry while hiding the harm done to others. The prophetic record begins differently. It names their response to God before recording their public glory. Their first historical fact is moral accountability.

This does not deny their achievements. It places them in the right scale. A monument may outlast its builder for a time, but it cannot outlast God's judgment. The community's real legacy is determined by whether it accepted truth and used its strength justly, not by how impressive it appeared to later admirers.



The verse says that ʿĀd rejected. The wording assigns agency. They were not created unable to recognize the warning, and their strength did not force them to deny it. A community can inherit pride, but it still chooses whether pride will govern its response.

That choice is why the story can warn later peoples. If ʿĀd were only a strange ancient exception, its account would have little force. Their rejection follows a recognizable human pattern: a people trusts its own standing, dismisses the messenger, and discovers that power cannot shield it from the Lord.


The account of ʿĀd begins where Noah's account ended: with a people deciding whether to believe. The repetition invites comparison without erasing difference. Readers should watch how Hūd addresses pride, how ʿĀd interprets warning, and whether their strength changes the result.

For this verse, the central claim is already complete. ʿĀd rejected the messengers, and that rejection defines the community's relation to its power. No civilization is secure because it is old, strong, or admired. Its security depends on whether it accepts the Lord's guidance.

The opening verdict thus asks the reader to examine the first response to truth. Before a people debates consequences, it has already revealed its posture toward the one who warns it. Rejection turns strength into evidence against itself.
