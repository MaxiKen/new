## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:122

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah's Story Ends With Power and Mercy**

The refrain names God as the Almighty and the Most Merciful after the flood. The two names interpret the rescue and the drowning together. Divine power explains why the Ark was protected and why the rejecting people could not escape the judgment. Divine mercy explains why Noah was sent, why the warning continued, and why a path of safety was made available.

Neither name should be isolated. Power without mercy would be misunderstood as arbitrary domination, while mercy without power would offer no assurance that God can protect the faithful or end oppression. The refrain holds both truths in one sentence. The Lord is strong enough to judge and gentle enough to save.


The people of Noah judged believers by social status. They considered the humble unworthy of following the messenger. God's might overturned that judgment. The community's chiefs could control public opinion, but they could not control the Ark, the flood, or the final distinction between belief and rejection.

This title therefore frees the servant from fear of elites. A person may lose approval, position, or material security for following truth, but the power that threatens him is not ultimate. The Almighty can preserve a servant whom people despise and can remove a society that imagines itself secure.

**Mercy Was Present Before the Flood**

The flood is often remembered as the dramatic act, but mercy came first. Noah was sent as a warning, and the people had time to listen. The believers were not saved by a secret rescue withheld from everyone else. They followed a public instruction and entered the means God provided.

Mercy does not require that the warning be pleasant. A serious danger requires serious speech. Noah's warning was merciful because it made repentance possible. The people could have changed their worship and joined the safety prepared for them. Their rejection cannot be blamed on a lack of divine care.



Noah spent his mission facing denial and threats. The final names remind him that his work is held by God's power and mercy, not by the people's response. Their refusal cannot defeat the Almighty, and their stubbornness does not exhaust the Merciful's knowledge of the case.

This is consolation without denial of pain. A messenger may grieve over a people and still know that God's judgment is right. The servant's duty is to warn faithfully, protect believers, and trust the Lord with the outcome. Success is not measured only by the number who accept immediately.

**A Parallel Account Shows the Two Names in Action**

The account in 46:21–26 describes Hūd warning ʿĀd, their rejection, and the destructive wind that followed. That parallel shows that the refrain after Noah is part of a wider pattern. God sends warning as mercy and exercises power when a people persists in denial.

The same account also recalls that ʿĀd had been given hearing, sight, and understanding, yet those faculties did not benefit them when they denied God's signs. Divine mercy supplied means of recognition; divine might then judged the refusal. The two names remain joined across prophetic histories.


Some people treat mercy as though it required leaving injustice untouched. The flood rejects that idea. Mercy toward the believing community included ending the power of those who threatened and rejected it. A ruler or society that harms the vulnerable cannot claim that continued power is owed to it in the name of compassion.

At the same time, human beings must not imitate God's final authority. They can oppose oppression and seek lawful protection, but they do not know the full hidden account of every person. The Almighty judges with complete knowledge. The servant combines firmness toward harm with humility about the final verdict.

**The Names Direct Gratitude to God**

The Ark could have become a symbol of Noah's skill, and the believers could have treated their survival as proof of their own superiority. The refrain prevents that pride. God is the Almighty who made rescue possible and the Merciful who chose to preserve them. The saved community owes worship to Him, not to the instrument or the messenger as an independent power.

This is the proper way to remember deliverance. Recognize the human effort and honor the messenger, but place ultimate gratitude with the Lord. The names keep memory from becoming hero worship and keep rescue from becoming a new form of tribal vanity.


A true prophetic warning carries both seriousness and hope. The Almighty gives the warning weight because judgment is real. The Merciful gives the warning purpose because return is possible. If either side is removed, the call becomes distorted: fear without hope produces despair, while hope without fear produces carelessness.

Noah's message had this balance. He called people to worship God, warned them of the consequence, and continued to offer a path of obedience. The refrain confirms that his call reflected the Lord's character. God's power is not opposed to mercy; it makes mercy dependable.

**The Refrain Makes the Story a Living Reminder**

The words are brief, but they keep Noah's story from being sealed in the past. Every later community faces the same Lord. He remains powerful over human arrogance and merciful toward those who turn to Him. The forms of disbelief change, but the divine response remains governed by the same character.

A believer can carry this refrain into danger without pretending that difficulty is small. The Almighty can protect and judge; the Merciful can guide and forgive. That knowledge gives courage to reject false authority and humility to seek mercy. Noah's story ends with a Lord whose might and mercy explain everything that happened. The refrain keeps the rescued community from mistaking survival for independence from its Creator.
