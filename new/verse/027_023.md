## Sūrah an-Naml 27:23

> Indeed, I found a woman ruling over them, who has been given everything ˹she needs˺, and who has a magnificent throne.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Report Begins With Governance, Not With Religion**

The hoopoe's account of Sheba opens with three facts about the state: it is ruled by a woman, she is comprehensively provided for, and she possesses a great throne. Nothing about worship appears until the following verse. The bird describes the kingdom before he describes its error.

That order is a piece of reporting discipline. A witness who leads with his objection tells you about himself; a witness who leads with the situation tells you about the situation. The hoopoe establishes the facts on the ground first — who governs, how well, with what resources — and only then raises the problem he flew back to report. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the description of her provision means everything a kingdom requires, which reads the phrase as an administrative assessment rather than a flourish. The bird is, in effect, filing a briefing, and it is structured the way a competent briefing is structured.

**A Woman Ruling, Reported Without Comment**

The Arabic says *imra'atan tamlikuhum* — a woman, ruling over them. The Qur'an reports this and attaches no evaluation to it. There is no criticism of Sheba for being governed by a woman, no note of surprise from the hoopoe, and no reaction from Sulaymān when he hears it.

This silence is significant because of what surrounds it. The chapter is perfectly willing to criticise Sheba: the very next verse condemns the kingdom's worship in direct terms. So the absence of any comment on her rule cannot be read as diplomatic restraint. The one thing the Qur'an faults in this kingdom is what it worships, and the queen herself is about to be shown acting with more caution, consultation and intelligence than most of the male rulers in the Book. By the end of her story she submits to God and is given the last word in her own narrative. Pharaoh, who occupied the first half of this chapter, refused what he knew to be true. The contrast between the two monarchs is the structure the chapter is building, and gender is not one of its terms.

**The Same Phrase Applied to Two Rulers**

The words used of her provision — *ūtiyat min kulli shayʾ*, given of all things — are the same words Sulaymān used of himself seven verses earlier when he told his people they had been given everything they needed. The repetition is exact in the Arabic and cannot be accidental.

Al-Qurṭubī reads her portion as everything her kingdom requires, and the same partitive grammar governs both statements: a share of every kind of thing, not the totality of things. Setting the two rulers side by side with identical language invites the comparison the chapter wants. Both are well-supplied monarchs with impressive courts. What will distinguish them is not resources but what each does with the knowledge that arrives. Sulaymān responded to a report by testing it; she will respond to a letter by consulting her council, then by sending a gift to probe the sender's motive, and finally by revising her position when the evidence requires it. Two rulers given all they need, and the chapter measures them by their handling of information rather than by their holdings.

**"A Magnificent Throne" and Where the Phrase Leads**

The third item is *ʿarsh ʿaẓīm*, a mighty throne. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī and al-Thaʿlabī all take the description to cover both senses at once: the physical object was genuinely enormous and richly made, and it also stood for the power of the one seated on it. Arabic allows the throne to mean the reign, as English does, and the commentators saw no need to choose.

A seat of state in the ancient world was a political instrument as much as a piece of furniture, built to be seen and to settle questions of rank before anyone spoke.

The detail is not decorative, because this throne will return. Sulaymān will later have it brought to his court before she arrives, and its transportation becomes the chapter's most dramatic demonstration of what his kingdom could do. The hoopoe's passing mention plants the object that the narrative will pick up twenty verses later. There is also a deliberate echo waiting at the end of the bird's speech. He has just called her throne mighty; three verses on he will call God *the Lord of the Mighty Throne*, using the same adjective. The bird sets up his own comparison, and it is the comparison his whole report exists to make.

**Firsthand Testimony, Stated as Such**

Twice in two verses the hoopoe says *I found* — *wajadtu*. He does not say it is reported, or that the kingdom is said to be so. He locates himself at the scene and speaks from observation.

Arabic could have given him a verb of hearing or of learning, and the narrative chose the verb of finding, twice, in successive clauses. Repetition of that kind in a short speech is emphasis rather than habit.

That framing matters given what he was asked for. Sulaymān demanded proof with authority behind it, and the strongest thing a single witness can offer is direct sight. The hoopoe's whole defence rests on having been somewhere and seen something, which is also exactly what made him absent from the parade. His offence and his excuse are the same act, and he presents it accordingly: I was not at the muster because I was two thousand kilometres away, looking at this. The Qur'an's narratives are full of characters who report what they have not witnessed; this one is careful to establish that he has. He now turns to what he saw the people of that kingdom doing.
