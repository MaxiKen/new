## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:8

> and the moon is dimmed,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lesser Light Goes First**

Having opened with the failure of human sight, the sūrah turns to the lights in the sky, and it takes them in descending order: the moon, then the sun. The verb used of the moon, *khasafa*, belongs to a root meaning to sink, to be swallowed, or to lose light — the same word from which the term for a lunar eclipse is derived. The moon does not crash and it does not explode in this verse. It goes dark. That detail matters more than it first appears. A body that is extinguished rather than destroyed has had something withdrawn from it, and the withdrawal is the point: the moon was never the source of its own light, and on that Day whatever arrangement supplied it is switched off at the source.

**Two Lights With Two Different Jobs**

The Qur'an is precise about the difference between the two great lights, and the distinction is repeated wherever they appear together. *“He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺.”* (10:5). The sun is a lamp; the moon is a measure. The moon's function is to mark out time — *“As for the moon, We have ordained ˹precise˺ phases for it, until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk.”* (36:39) — and human beings were given it as a calendar. The two are also described as running on tracks that never interfere: *“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own.”* (36:40). Order, measure, and reliability are the qualities being emphasised every time.

**What Is Being Undone: the Clock, Not the Lamp**

If the moon is the instrument of timekeeping, then darkening it is the removal of the world's clock before the removal of the world. That reading fits the sūrah's argument, which is about a people who asked *when* and were given no date. The object they used to measure their intervals is the first thing dismantled. Time in this life is what makes delay possible; it is the substance the denier was spending while he put off his repentance. Taking the measure away is therefore not incidental scenery. It is the end of the postponement, stated physically: the interval in which one might have changed has closed, and the instrument that counted it has gone dark.

**The Calendar the Deniers Relied On**

The first audience lived by a lunar calendar. Months were tracked by the moon's phases, and the same moon regulated the times of pilgrimage, the waiting periods of divorce and inheritance, and the season of the fast. A community that ordered its commercial and ritual life by that light could not have missed the force of the image. The thing they treated as the fixed frame of their affairs was itself a creature with an appointed term, and the Qur'an had already said so: the sun and moon are *“each travelling in an orbit”* (21:33), *“each orbiting for an appointed term”* (13:2), *“each orbiting for an appointed term”* (39:5). A term is a thing that ends. The stability people assume when they look up at the sky is the stability of a schedule, not of a necessity. Nor is the point that timekeeping was somehow wrong — the Qur'an itself instructs believers to reckon their appointed times by the moon. The point is that a measured thing is a created thing. Living by the phases is lawful; assuming that the phases guarantee themselves is to mistake a schedule for a necessity.

**Eclipse, or Erasure**

The commentators glossed this verse in company with the next, and many understood the moon's fate as part of the enfolding described in the sūrah of the Rolling Up: *“When the sun is put out, and when the stars fall down.”* (81:1–2). On that reading the moon is not merely eclipsed but folded away, its light taken and its form undone. The difference between the two readings is a difference of severity rather than of kind. An eclipse implies a light temporarily blocked; an enfolding implies an arrangement brought to its end. The sūrah's own wording sits deliberately between them, and the reader is left holding both meanings at once, which is the effect the whole passage is aiming for: the world's most reliable object has become unrecognisable.

**The Order Was Never Self-Sustaining**

The theological point underneath the imagery is that the regularity of the heavens is not a machine left running. Every description of the sun and moon in the Qur'an attaches them to a term and to a Will that set them on their courses, and the purpose of their regularity is stated as a sign: *“He makes the signs clear so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord.”* (13:2). The order in the sky was installed as an argument for the Day that is now arriving, and its dissolution is that argument's conclusion. The One who split the moon as a sign to a generation that demanded one (54:1) is the One who now puts its light out, and nothing in between was ever held in place by anything but His command. For the denier, this is the closing of the case he had been arguing. He had treated the regularity of the world as the strongest evidence against the Hour, since nothing in his observation suggested that any day would differ from any other. The darkening answers him in his own terms: the pattern was never a proof of permanence but a sign, and a sign is a thing that points beyond itself to the One who set it.
