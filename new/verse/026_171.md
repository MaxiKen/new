## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:171

> except an old woman, who was one of the doomed.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Family Relation Does Not Guarantee Salvation**

The previous verse said that Lot and his family were saved; this verse identifies an exception. An old woman from his household is counted among those doomed. The distinction is essential. Divine rescue is not distributed by bloodline alone. A person may live in the home of a prophet and still choose the side of the community that rejects God.

The verse keeps family affection from becoming a false assurance. Lot loved his household and prayed for its protection, but God's judgment remained based on faithfulness and response. The saved group is defined by allegiance to truth, not by a name shared with the messenger.


The woman's old age is mentioned without turning age into an excuse. Human beings deserve care when they are weak or elderly, but age does not make deliberate opposition to God righteous. Vulnerability calls for compassion in human dealings; it does not erase the moral account before God.

This balance prevents two errors. A society must not neglect the elderly because they are weak, and a family must not assume that an elder's position makes every choice beyond question. God knows the circumstances, intentions, and degree of responsibility of each person more fully than any human observer.

**A Household Can Contain Different Allegiances**

Lot's family is not morally uniform simply because it shares a home. One household can contain people who encourage faith and another who identifies with the surrounding culture. The verse teaches the reader to distinguish domestic proximity from spiritual solidarity.

That distinction can be painful. A believer may pray for relatives who do not share his commitment, warn them with wisdom, and still be unable to bring them to faith. Love remains real, but it must not become an argument against God's justice. The Lord does not wrong a person by judging the choice that person knowingly makes.


Quran 7:83 repeats that God saved Lot and his family except his wife, who was among the doomed. Quran 11:81 likewise says that the wife would suffer the fate of the others. These parallels confirm the moral point without requiring later stories to supply details absent from this verse.

The repetition also shows that the exception is not an incidental family dispute. It belongs to the revealed account of how divine rescue distinguishes faith from outward association. The household is mentioned together, but judgment remains attentive to each member's allegiance.

**The Exception Protects the Meaning of Prophetic Kinship**

A messenger's relative is not saved by proximity to the messenger. If kinship guaranteed salvation, people could treat faith as inherited privilege and ignore their own conduct. The exception prevents that distortion. Lot's mission points the family to God, but it cannot substitute for their response.

This also protects prophets from being treated as owners of divine mercy. Lot cannot command God to save someone who has chosen the opposing side. He is a servant who prays and warns, while the Lord decides with perfect knowledge.


The woman's age may make the exception especially sobering. A long life can create a deep attachment to a community's customs, and years of proximity to a prophet do not automatically produce obedience. Time spent near truth is an opportunity, not a guarantee that truth has been accepted.

The verse therefore asks the reader to examine not only how long he has lived or how close he has been to religious people, but what he has chosen. Familiarity with sacred language can coexist with resistance in the heart. Salvation requires more than environment; it requires sincere submission.

**Divine Judgment Is More Precise Than Human Labels**

Human beings often judge by family name, age, reputation, or public role. God's judgment can distinguish what those labels conceal. The exception warns against assuming that every member of a group shares one moral status, while also warning against denying the reality of collective choices.

This precision should make the believer humble. He may recognize the outward direction of a community, but he should not claim to know every person's hidden account. He can oppose public wrongdoing and protect people from harm while leaving final judgment to God.


The verse is severe, but it does not say that a relative of a prophet was doomed merely because she belonged to a certain family. The decisive issue is allegiance. That means repentance remains meaningful for anyone who turns to God before death and judgment.

A person who has inherited a corrupt identity can leave it. A household member who has resisted guidance can return. The warning is against relying on association, not against seeking mercy. God's justice is exact, and His mercy remains open to the one who sincerely changes.


Lot's household could not be protected by sentiment alone. Faithful love tells the truth, seeks rescue, and accepts that God judges each person. It does not conceal an ally's wrongdoing to preserve the appearance of family unity.

For the reader, the verse makes a difficult demand: love relatives deeply, but do not make relatives an authority above God. Encourage them, protect them from injustice, and invite them to repent. Yet trust the Lord's judgment when outward kinship and inward allegiance diverge.

The exception also warns against measuring faith by access to sacred company. Someone may hear a prophet daily, live near a righteous family, and still nurture loyalty to the people who oppose God. Proximity gives opportunity for belief, but it cannot perform belief on another person's behalf.

This is why the verse should produce self-examination rather than speculation about others. Ask whether personal attachments are making God's command easier to obey or easier to evade. Family affection is honorable when it leads toward truth; it becomes a barrier when it asks truth to yield to attachment.
