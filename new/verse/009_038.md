## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:38

> O believers! What is the matter with you that when you are asked to march forth in the cause of Allah, you cling firmly to ˹your˺ land? Do you prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter? The enjoyment of this worldly life is insignificant compared to that of the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Body Can Cling Before the Mind Admits It**

Verse 38 asks what has happened to believers who are called to march in Allah's cause and cling heavily to the land. The image is physical: a person who should rise remains pressed down, as if the ground has become a weight. The body exposes an attachment the tongue may still deny. They may say they believe in sacrifice, yet the summons reveals that comfort has become their true master.

The immediate setting is the expedition to Tabūk, with its heat, distance, cost, and fear of the Byzantine frontier. The command was not a demand that every person ignore genuine incapacity. Later verses exempt the weak, sick, and those without means. Verse 38 addresses those capable of responding who prefer the settled life because the march threatens their ease. The distinction between inability and reluctance is central.

**The Question Is About Preference, Not Possession**

The verse asks whether they prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter. It does not say that worldly life has no value. Food, homes, family, trade, and safety are gifts from Allah. The problem is ranking the temporary gift above the eternal relationship with Him. A believer may enjoy the world while using it for the Hereafter. He becomes blameworthy when enjoyment decides which command he will obey.

Preference is often revealed by what a person protects under pressure. Someone may sincerely love Allah in easy circumstances but choose profit when truth becomes expensive. The verse's question cuts through stated intention and asks what the heart actually selects. The answer can be painful, but pain at diagnosis is better than comfort inside a hidden disease.

**The World Is Called Little by Comparison**

The verse calls the enjoyment of worldly life insignificant compared with the Hereafter. This is a comparison, not a denial. A cup of water is precious to a thirsty traveler, yet small beside a river. Worldly joy can be real and good, but its duration, reach, and security are limited. The Hereafter is not merely a longer version of this life. It is the lasting result of how this life was used.

The comparison corrects distorted fear. A person may refuse a duty because he imagines the immediate loss as enormous. Revelation places that loss beside the reward and asks him to calculate again. A career opportunity, a profitable contract, or a comfortable weekend may matter, but none deserves the authority to cancel Allah's command. The world feels large when it is the only horizon; it becomes small when the Hereafter enters the scale.

**Tabūk Tested More Than Courage**

The Tabūk expedition demanded resources at a difficult season. The distance made preparation expensive, and the community could not rely on a nearby battle to stir immediate excitement. A person who marched had to spend before knowing whether there would even be an encounter. This uncertainty revealed whether obedience depended on visible reward.

The verse therefore tests more than battlefield courage. It tests the willingness to act for Allah when results are hidden. A person may be willing to support a cause if success is likely, applause is guaranteed, or the sacrifice is short. The harder obedience asks whether Allah's command remains enough when the worldly outcome is unclear. The believer's answer is not careless optimism. It is trust in the One who knows the unseen result.

**The Command Has Legitimate Exceptions**

The chapter's next legal clarifications show that verse 38 is not an accusation against every person who stays behind. The weak, sick, and those unable to provide a mount or equipment are not blamed when they are sincere. The Qur'an does not ask bodies to perform what they cannot perform. It asks capable believers not to turn comfort into an excuse.

This distinction is useful in every religious duty. A person must separate genuine limitation from reluctance dressed as limitation. Illness may require rest; fear may require counsel; poverty may restrict a person's contribution. But a person with strength, wealth, and opportunity who repeatedly chooses ease over a clear duty is hearing verse 38. Allah judges the actual capacity and intention of each person, not a comparison with someone else's capacity.

**Marching in the Present**

Modern believers may not be called to Tabūk, but they are called away from forms of settled comfort. A person may need to leave an unlawful income, spend time serving the vulnerable, study when entertainment is easier, speak truth when silence protects status, or join a community effort that brings no recognition. These acts are not identical to the expedition, but they share its question: what has become too heavy to leave?

Verse 38 does not ask the believer to despise the earth. It asks him not to cling to it when Allah calls him forward. Use the land, family, and wealth as trusts. Do not let them hold the feet when obedience requires movement. The Hereafter is not a distant idea added to life. It is the scale that tells the believer what the present enjoyment is worth. The person who sees the larger horizon can leave comfort without believing that he has left everything. He gives up one form of security because he trusts that Allah knows how to return what is better, in this life or the next. That trust is not a promise that every worldly loss will be repaid in the same shape. It is confidence that no sacrifice made for Allah is outside His knowledge or mercy.
