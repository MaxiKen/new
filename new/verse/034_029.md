## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:29

> And they ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Designed Not to Be Answered**

*Matā hādhā al-waʿdu in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — when is this promise, if you are truthful? The sentence has the grammar of an enquiry and the function of a taunt, and the two halves of it work together to produce that effect. The first half asks for a date. The second half attaches a condition that turns the request into a test of the speaker's honesty rather than of the claim's truth. Nothing about the structure invites information. A man who genuinely wanted to know when something would happen would not append a clause implying that failure to name a date proves he is being lied to. This is one of the recurring voices in the Qur'an's record of its opponents, appearing at (10:48), (21:38), (27:71), (36:48) and (67:25) in nearly identical words. The consistency across chapters suggests a stock objection in actual circulation rather than a literary device.

**Why the Demand for a Date Is a Trap**

The logic the question relies on is worth exposing, because it looks stronger than it is. If no date is given, the questioner concludes the threat is empty. If a date were given and passed without event, he would have his refutation. And if a date were given far in the future, he would point out that nothing testable had been asserted. The demand is therefore unanswerable by design, and this is precisely why the Qur'an never meets it on its own terms. The Book states repeatedly that the timing of the Hour is withheld from everyone including the Prophet, which the questioners took as evasion and which the sūrah has already explained on other grounds: knowledge of the unseen belongs to God, as the failure of Solomon's jinn demonstrated (34:14). Refusing to supply a date is not a weakness in the position. It is a consequence of the position.

**"This Promise" — A Word That Cuts Both Ways**

The noun they use is *al-waʿd*, translated here as *threat*. Arabic distinguishes between *waʿd*, a promise of something good, and *waʿīd*, a promise of something bad, though *waʿd* covers both in general usage. Their choice of the neutral term is a small piece of evidence about how they heard the message. To them the coming Day was a threat and nothing else, an item of hostile rhetoric produced to frighten people into compliance. The sūrah has given a different account of the same event from its fourth verse onward, where the Day is first described as the occasion on which believers receive forgiveness and honourable provision (34:4). The opponents' word choice therefore reveals something they did not intend to disclose: they had understood only half of what was being said to them, and the half they missed was the half addressed to people unlike themselves.

**Who Is Being Asked**

The verb is plural and the condition is addressed to a group — *if you are truthful*, plural *ṣādiqīn*. The translation supplies the referent: they are asking the believers, not only the Prophet. This detail matters more than it seems. A challenge put to a small community rather than to its leader is a challenge aimed at the weakest point of a new movement, which is the confidence of ordinary members who have staked a great deal on a claim they cannot demonstrate. Every such member had relatives asking exactly this question at exactly this volume. The sūrah's answer in the following verse is accordingly given to the Prophet to relay, which supplies the community with a reply it could repeat. Scripture here is functioning as it often does in the Meccan period: not as abstract doctrine but as material for people under social pressure.

**Certainty About the Fact, Silence About the Hour**

The Qur'an's position on this question is unusual and consistent: maximum confidence that the Day will come, complete refusal to say when. Both halves are held at full strength, and the sūrah has already displayed the pattern in its third verse, where the Prophet is made to swear an oath that the Hour will certainly arrive while adding nothing at all about its timing (34:3). The combination is not a compromise between belief and ignorance. It reflects a claim about what kind of knowledge is available: the structure of the world is knowable, including the fact that it is the sort of place where accounts are settled, while the schedule is not. A questioner who treats the withheld schedule as evidence against the structure has confused two different kinds of claim.

**What the Question Reveals About the Questioner**

There is a diagnostic value in this sentence that the sūrah exploits. A man who asks when a danger is coming, and who treats the absence of a date as permission, has told you how he makes decisions: he responds to deadlines, not to risks. Anyone who has watched a person postpone a medical appointment because no symptom has yet arrived recognises the reasoning. It is not stupidity; it is a preference for the tangible over the probable, and it works well enough in ordinary life to feel like wisdom. The Qur'an's alternative is stated in the very next verse and is worth noting in advance only as the shape of a reply — the date exists and is fixed, and its being unknown to him changes nothing about it. Behind the taunt lies the assumption the sūrah named in its opening exchange, that the Hour will simply never come to these particular people (34:3), and the taunt is that assumption in its confident public form. Ridicule, as the chapter has shown before, is how an unexamined assumption defends itself when it cannot produce an argument.
