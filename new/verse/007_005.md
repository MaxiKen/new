## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:5

> Their only cry—when overwhelmed by Our torment—was, “We have indeed been wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Cry Left to Them**

The verse states that the communities had nothing to say when the punishment came upon them except to confess that they had been wrongdoers. The Book gives the condemned several such confessions, and it places them at the moment when the outcome is already settled. The sūrah of the Prophets describes the wrongdoers saying that they have been unjust when the punishment touches them (21:46), and the sūrah of the Pen reports the owners of the garden saying that they were transgressors once their harvest was destroyed (68:29).

The Book's account of the people of Lot at the end of their sūrah reports a similar admission, and the sūrah of the Believers describes the condemned confessing their misery before their Lord. The verse in front of us states the pattern in its plainest form: one sentence, spoken at the moment of ruin, and it is the admission of the guilt that the community had denied all its life.

**Why the Confession Arrives Late**

The confession is not accepted as repentance, and the Book's treatment of the matter distinguishes between turning to God in the time of action and admitting the truth in the time of punishment. The sūrah of the Women states that repentance is not accepted from those who persist in evil until death comes upon them (4:18), and the sūrah of Jonah describes Pharaoh's profession of faith as he was drowning and states that it came too late to profit him (10:90). The confession of the destroyed towns belongs to the same category.

The sūrah states the sequence plainly: the punishment arrives, the community admits its guilt, and the punishment proceeds. What the confession does in the narrative is to establish the justice of the outcome. The condemned confirm the truth of the charge against them, and the reader sees that the destruction was not arbitrary.

**The Meaning of the Word Wrongdoers**

The term the verse places in the mouths of the condemned describes those who have wronged themselves, and the Book uses it for the people who have brought harm upon their own souls. The sūrah of the Cattle states that no soul bears the burden of another and that the wrongdoers are those who have forfeited their own selves. The Book's repeated phrase that God does not wrong people in the least, but that people wrong themselves, runs through the sūrah of the Family of Imran and the sūrah of Yūnus, and the confession in front of us uses the same vocabulary.

The confession is therefore accurate, and it is the truth the audience's own traditions about the destroyed towns preserve. The ruins the people of Makkah passed on their journeys stood as testimony to communities that had denied their messengers, and the verse in front of us supplies the words those communities spoke when they understood what they had done.

**The Silence of the Ruins**

The Book's treatment of the ruins uses their silence to make the same point. The sūrah of the Bee instructs the audience to travel through the earth and observe the end of those who denied the messengers, and the sūrah of the Ant describes the dwellings of the destroyed peoples as empty. The verse in front of us adds the one utterance that the ruins cannot reproduce, and it places it in the narrative as the last word of the condemned.

The sūrah's audience lived among people who boasted of their descent from tribes whose cities had been destroyed, and the verse turns that history into a warning. The confession of the ancestors is reported to the descendants, and the descendants are placed in the position of choosing whether they will also need it.

**The Pattern the Sūrah Will Repeat**

The middle section of the sūrah recounts the destruction of the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, and the people of Shuʿayb, and each account follows the sequence stated in the opening verses: the messenger is sent, the people deny him, the punishment arrives, and the denial is abandoned too late. The verse in front of us states the sequence before the narratives supply their detail.

The Book's arrangement of these accounts in other sūrahs follows the same pattern, and the sūrah of Hūd presents the sequence of prophets and peoples in a form that culminates in the destruction of Pharaoh's hosts. The verse in front of us supplies the closing sentence of each of those stories, and its brevity gives it the force of a formula.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands fifth in the chapter's opening section, and it supplies the response that followed the destruction described in the verse before it. The pair of verses states the fact of the punishment and the words of those who suffered it, and the two together close the first movement of the sūrah's introduction.

The verses that follow turn to the questioning of those who received the message and of the messengers themselves, and to the weighing of deeds on the day of judgment. The theme of accountability is thus opened by the confession of the destroyed communities, and the reader passes from the ruin of the world to the reckoning of the Hereafter.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that God will question both those to whom the message was sent and the messengers themselves (7:6). The passage moves from the admission of guilt in this world to the formal accounting of the next, and it names both parties in the transaction of revelation.

The two verses together state the two sides of the account: the recipients who were warned and the messengers who warned them. The sūrah has already described the sending down of the Book and the duty of the messenger to warn, and the verse that follows completes the arrangement by stating who will be asked about the delivery.
