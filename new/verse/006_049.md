## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:49

> But those who deny Our signs will be afflicted with punishment for their rebelliousness.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mirror of the Previous Verse**

The sūrah has just named the promise attached to belief and good conduct (6:48), and this sentence supplies the counterpart. The structure is exact: two conditions on the favourable side, and on the other side the denial of the signs together with the reason given for the punishment, which is a word the translation renders as rebelliousness. The Book frequently pairs the two outcomes in this way, so that the reader receives mercy and warning in the same breath.

The pairing is not a literary symmetry for its own sake. It tells the audience that the two roads being described are the only ones available, and that each has been marked with the same clarity. The sūrah has spent thirty verses assembling evidence and answering objections; this sentence states the consequence of ignoring all of it.

**The Word Behind "Rebelliousness"**

The Arabic term describes the conduct of someone who steps outside a boundary he was meant to keep, and the Book uses it for people who break covenants and corrupt what they touch. The same root appears where the Book describes those who spread corruption in the land and violate their pledges (2:27), and where the people who cross the limits set for them are described as the ones who ruin themselves (2:229). The word indicates conduct rather than an inherited condition.

That distinction matters in a sūrah that has been arguing that denial is an activity. The verse does not say that the deniers were born outside the boundary; it says that they put themselves there and remained, and the punishment is attached to the choice rather than to a label applied from the outside.

**Denial and Conduct Held Together**

The verse names two things about the people it describes: they deny the signs, and they have crossed the boundary. The Book's habit is to join belief with righteous action and denial with transgression, and it does so because the two belong together in its understanding of a human being. Faith that has no effect on conduct is not the faith the verse is describing, and misconduct that has no connection to belief is not the fault it is naming either.

That view is stated plainly in the passage where the Book describes the righteous as those who keep their pledges, are patient in hardship, and give from what they have (2:177), and where it describes those who break the covenant, cut the ties God ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption as the true losers (2:27). The pairing runs through the whole Book, and this verse is a compact instance of it.

**Why the Punishment Is Attached Rather Than Described**

The verse does not describe what the punishment consists of, and this restraint is typical of the Makkan passages. Earlier in the sūrah the description was more explicit, with the wrongdoers at the point of death surrounded by angels stretching out their hands (6:93), and later the sūrah will describe the scene where the wrongdoers are held before the fire (6:27). Here the sentence ends at the fact.

The restraint keeps the focus on the cause rather than on the penalty. What the audience needed to understand was the connection between the way they were living and the outcome, and the Book describes that connection with a single word before moving to the wider argument about what the messengers are and are not.

Elsewhere the same sūrah fills in what is left out here. The wrongdoers are told at the point of death that they will be repaid with the torment of disgrace for lying about God and for being arrogant toward His signs (6:93), and those who take their faith as play and amusement are described as facing a boiling drink and a painful punishment for their disbelief (6:70). The absence of such detail in this verse is a matter of placement rather than of information. The sūrah states the two roads first and describes the destinations later, once the audience has been brought through the argument that makes the description bearable to hear.

**The Proportion Between the Two Sentences**

The favourable sentence in the previous verse describes an inner state and an outward conduct, and then removes fear and grief. The unfavourable sentence describes denial and a crossing of boundaries, and then states that a punishment follows. Nothing in either sentence mentions quantity, and nothing suggests that the two outcomes are comparable in weight.

The reader is left to work out the proportion for himself, and the Book helps him elsewhere when it says that God's mercy encompasses all things (7:156) while His punishment strikes whoever He wills. The pairing in these two verses is deliberately unequal in tone, and the inequality is part of what the sūrah is telling its audience about the One they are dealing with.

**What the Verse Requires the Reader to Decide**

Both sentences in this pair are about groups, and neither one asks the reader to place himself in a group. The sūrah treats the reading itself as the point at which a person begins to decide, and the verses that follow turn immediately to the practical shape of the community being addressed, including the Prophet's treatment of the poor among his followers.

That shift is not a change of subject. Having stated the two outcomes, the sūrah shows the audience what the two roads look like inside a living community, where the worth of a person is measured by God rather than by his family or his wealth. The next passages handle that question directly.

The sentence also matches the Book's description of what happens when the same promise is given from the other side. In the sūrah of the Heights, the children of Adam are told that whoever shuns evil and mends his ways will have no fear and no grief when messengers recite God's revelations to them (7:35), and in the sūrah of the Cow the promise is attached to belief, good deeds, prayer, and alms (2:277). The two halves of the contrast are composed of the same materials in every passage: inward belief, outward conduct, and the consequences that follow from each.
