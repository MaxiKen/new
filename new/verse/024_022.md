## Sūrah an-Nūr 24:22

> Do not let the people of virtue and affluence among you swear to suspend donations to their relatives, the needy, and the emigrants in the cause of Allah. Let them pardon and forgive. Do you not love to be forgiven by Allah? And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Emotional Crisis of Abū Bakr and the Oath of Retaliation**

The historical occasion for this verse represents one of the most poignant emotional and ethical crossroads in early Islamic history. Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq had long provided regular financial maintenance to his impoverished maternal cousin, Misṭaḥ ibn Uthāthah, a destitute emigrant and veteran of the Battle of Badr. When it emerged that Misṭaḥ had actively participated in circulating the heinous slander against ʿĀʾishah, Abū Bakr was wounded to the depths of his fatherly heart.

Overwhelmed by natural human grief and righteous indignation at such betrayal from a dependent kinsman, Abū Bakr swore a solemn oath by God: By Allah, I will never spend anything on Misṭaḥ, nor will I benefit him with a single dirham ever again! This verse descended directly to address Abū Bakr's personal oath, transforming a private family crisis into an eternal monument of transcendent spiritual generosity and unconditional forgiveness.

**Honoring the Moral Stature of the Giver: ulū al-faḍli minkum wa-al-saʿah**

The divine address opens with words of extraordinary gentleness and reverence toward Abū Bakr: *wa-lā yaʾtali ulū al-faḍli minkum wa-al-saʿah*. Even while correcting his emotional reaction, God explicitly confirms his supreme spiritual standing, addressing him as possessing both spiritual excellence (*al-faḍl*) and material abundance (*al-saʿah*).

The divine voice does not scold or condemn his righteous anger; it acknowledges his natural pain, yet summons him to rise above ordinary human reciprocity. Ordinary human beings give charity to those who praise and thank them; the elite of spiritual nobility are called to give even to those who have deeply injured them. The title *ulū al-faḍl* served as a divine tribute to Abū Bakr's unmatched preeminence in faith, inviting him to demonstrate that his charity was directed purely toward the Creator rather than contingent upon human gratitude.

**The Triple Dignity of the Errant Recipient**

The text describes Misṭaḥ through three profound categories that highlight his enduring claims upon communal mercy: *ulī al-qurbā* (kinsfolk), *al-masākīn* (the indigent poor), and *al-muhājirīna fī sabīli Allāh* (emigrants in the path of God). Despite his catastrophic moral blunder in the matter of the slander, Misṭaḥ remained a close relative, a starving believer, and a heroic companion who had sacrificed his home in Makkah for the cause of Islam.

The Qur'an teaches the community that a believer's lifelong sacrifices and genuine human vulnerability must not be erased by a single grievous stumble. Misṭaḥ had received the statutory eighty lashes, repented before God, and undergone purification. To cut off his livelihood would be to reduce his family to starvation and push a fallen brother into permanent social alienation. Divine justice insists that past righteousness and human need must remain visible even in the shadow of major sins.

**The Twin Commands of Pardon and Forbearance: wa-lyaʿfū wa-lyaṣfaḥū**

The divine decree issues an uncompromising double imperative: *wa-lyaʿfū wa-lyaṣfaḥū*, and let them pardon and forbear. In classical Arabic semantics, these two terms describe complementary stages of emotional and legal resolution. *Al-ʿafw* denotes waiving a penalty, releasing a debt, or relinquishing the right to retaliate. *Al-ṣafḥ* represents an even higher spiritual station: turning a completely new page, overlooking the offense entirely, and cleansing the heart of lingering bitterness and reproach.

The Qur'an commands Abū Bakr not merely to resume his financial disbursements mechanically, but to restore internal warmth and familial affection. To forgive outwardly while maintaining cold distance is an incomplete virtue. True spiritual nobility demands the total erasure of resentment, welcoming the repentant offender back into the embrace of genuine brotherhood without reminding them of their past failure.

**The Unforgettable Rhetorical Interrogation: a-lā tuḥibbūna an yaghfira Allāhu lakum**

The verse delivers the most moving and effective rhetorical question in the entire revelation: *a-lā tuḥibbūna an yaghfira Allāhu lakum*, do you not love that Allah should forgive you? The divine voice places human relationships before the mirror of cosmic reality. Every human being, regardless of their piety, stands in desperate, trembling need of divine pardon for their own hidden sins, shortcomings, and oversights before the Lord of the worlds.

How can a mortal servant plead with God for infinite mercy, yet refuse to extend a tiny fraction of that mercy to a fellow creature who has wronged them? The measure of mercy one shows to others is the measure one will receive from heaven. When Abū Bakr heard the Messenger ﷺ recite these words, tears streamed down his face, and he exclaimed with immediate devotion: Yea, by Allah! Indeed, our Lord, we love that You should forgive us! He immediately restored his financial stipend to Misṭaḥ, vowing: By Allah, I will never withhold it from him forever!

**The Ethic of Repelling Evil with Good**

The legal and moral philosophy enshrined in this passage represents the absolute summit of Qur'anic ethics. Many religious and secular legal codes endorse reciprocal justice: an eye for an eye, kindness for kindness, and withdrawal for offense. The Qur'an commands the faithful to transcend reciprocity through proactive grace: repelling evil with that which is better.

By commanding the father of the victim to continue feeding the repentant slanderer, divine law breaks the endless cycle of generational grievances, blood feuds, and domestic vengeance. It demonstrates that the health of a religious community depends upon the capacity of its righteous leaders to absorb personal injury and transform pain into healing generosity. The verse seals this sublime ethic with the divine attributes *wa-Allāhu ghafūrun raḥīm*, anchoring human forgiveness in the infinite, everlasting mercy of God.
