## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:44

> This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian, nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ghayb: What "the Unseen" Covers**

The verse describes what it has just narrated as news of the unseen, and the Arabic word behind the phrase is *ghayb*, which covers everything absent from human perception: what is hidden in space, what is past, what is still to come, and what belongs to another order of being altogether.

The Book uses the term for revelation's content throughout. At Sinai, the Prophet is told that he was not present on the western side of the mountain when the commandments were entrusted to Moses, nor was he there in his time (28:44). The story of Joseph is introduced as one of the narratives of the unseen which God reveals to him, adding that neither he nor his people knew it beforehand (12:102; 11:49). The same construction frames the narrative of these verses about Mary. What the Prophet recites is knowledge he has no means of possessing, and the Book presents that fact as part of its claim.

**"You Were Not With Them" — an Argument From Absence**

The proof is stated as a fact about the Prophet’s biography, and its plainness is the argument. He was not among the priests of the sanctuary when they made their decision, and he was not present at their dispute. He was born centuries later, in a region where these events were not recorded and where the surviving traditions of the Jewish and Christian communities were not available to him in their original languages.

The argument that follows from this is not that a man could never have heard a story at second hand. It is that this particular narrative, in this detail, with its cast of characters, its procedure of casting lots, its setting, and its outcomes, did not exist in his environment. The Book's repeated use of the formula turns a narration into a testable claim: a reader may ask where the material came from, and the text itself invites him to.

**The Casting of Lots in a Sanctuary**

The verse refers to a procedure: the men concerned cast lots to decide which of them would take charge of the girl. The Book mentions a similar practice in the story of Jonah, who drew straws with the passengers of a ship and lost, and was thrown overboard (37:141).

The practice was well established in the region, and the accounts of it in other scriptural traditions show it used for a range of decisions — the allotment of duties among priests, the selection of animals for sacrifice, and the choice of a king. What concerns the verse's readers is the underlying conviction: a matter is referred to God by a procedure that removes human preference from the outcome. The guardian of a girl in the sanctuary was chosen that way, which means the community's most desirable responsibility was settled by something other than influence.

**Who Was Disputed, and on What Grounds**

Commentators recorded two views about the dispute. The majority held that the priests of the sanctuary wanted to be Mary's guardian and cast lots to determine who would have the privilege, and that God's will gave her to Zachariah. Al-Ṭabarī preserved a second view in which Zachariah's position was not contested at all, since he was related to Mary's mother, and the dispute concerned other aspects of her care or of who would serve her in the sanctuary.

The first view is supported by the narrative's emphasis on the lots and by the role that Zachariah's care plays in the surrounding verses. The second view rescues the story from an apparent awkwardness, since a prophet competing by lot for a child's custody invites questions. The majority position is the stronger reading of the text: the verse states plainly that they cast lots and that they disagreed, and it treats the disagreement as a fact of the story rather than as something to be explained away.

**Why the Detail Is Preserved**

The dispute adds something to the portrait of Mary that the preceding verses had already drawn. A girl whose care was worth competing for, in a community that dedicated men to the temple's service, was a person of value before God ever sent angels to her.

The mechanism of the decision also keeps the narrative honest about the human arrangements. Mary was not placed with her guardian by a family negotiation or a purchase; the men responsible referred the matter to God in the customary way, and the outcome is presented as God's choice. The verse's final clause about their arguing is not a smear on her guardians; it is a record that the assignment happened in a public, contested setting, which makes the protection she received afterwards more remarkable.

**What the Verse Proves About the Book Being Recited**

The claim that this is unseen news functions as evidence for the sūrah's message, and the Book repeats the device so that readers learn to weigh it. Narratives in the Qur'an often end or begin with a note that the Prophet could not have been present, and the notes accumulate into an argument about the source of the material.

A person who accepts the sūrah as revelation reads the note as confirmation. A person who does not must account for where the stories came from, and the sūrah's answer is already on the page: the Book is being sent down by one who knows what no human witness could report. The verse's opening phrase and its closing clause are the two ends of that argument.

**The Frame Around a Human Story**

Everything in the passage has been domestic — a vow, a birth, a girl in a room, a priest standing at prayer — and this verse draws back from the scene to say how the reader has come to see it.

The effect is to place the smallest details of a household inside a claim about the source of knowledge. A dispute among temple priests over a child's custody, preserved for a community in Arabia centuries later, is being offered as evidence of the unseen, which is a way of saying that nothing in the narrative is too small to have been considered worth revealing.
