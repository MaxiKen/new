## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:13

> Indeed, there was a sign for you in the two armies that met in battle— one fighting for the cause of Allah and the other in denial. The believers saw their enemy twice their number. But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills. Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sign With a Date and a Place**

The verse points to an event its audience had lived through: two armies that met, one fighting for God and one refusing Him. Muslim tradition identifies the encounter as Badr, in the second year after the migration, the community's first full battle against the Quraysh, and the verses that follow in this sūrah keep the memory of it in view.

What happened at Badr is remembered as a battle with the numbers stacked against the believers and an outcome nobody could have predicted from the material facts. The Qur'an's treatment of it is not triumphalism. It is evidentiary: the event is called a sign, and the word *āyah* in this Book means something put there to be read. The report of the day is offered to teach, and its teaching is intended for readers who were not present.

**Muslim 1763 and the Night of the Supplication**

The Prophet's own state before the fighting is remembered in a report that is difficult to read without stopping. He turned to the *qibla*, stretched out his hands, and pleaded with God to bring about what had been promised to him, adding: *O Allah, if this small band of Muslims is destroyed, You will not be worshipped on this earth* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1763). His cloak slipped from his shoulders while he prayed; Abū Bakr picked it up, wrapped it round him, and told him that his Lord would fulfil what He had promised.

Set beside this verse, the report removes any impression of a community marching on its own confidence. The one who conveyed the certainty of verse 12 was pleading, half a sūrah earlier in the timeline, with a mantle falling from his shoulders. The victory the sūrah calls a sign followed a prayer that confessed the possibility of being erased from the earth.

**"Twice Their Number" — the Art of Seeing Numbers**

The verse reports what the believers saw: their enemy at double their strength. The concrete figures behind it are given in other accounts — the Quraysh said to number about a thousand, the Muslims a little over three hundred — and the discrepancy between "double" here and "triple" there has occupied the commentators.

One explanation is that what a man sees on a battlefield is not an inventory. An army three times one's own, seen at a distance, may register as twice the size; and the Qur'an's language is carefully phrased as what the believers saw rather than as the census. Another suggestion is that "twice" carries the sense of double again, approaching the traditional ratio. What the verse does not do is present the count as the cause of anything. The clause that follows it has already redirected the reader: victory belongs to God, who gives it to whoever He wills. The numbers were part of the scene, not part of the reason.

**A Lesson for People of Insight**

The sūrah closes the verse with *ʿibra li-ulī al-abṣār*, a lesson for the possessors of insight — literally, of eyesight, but used in the Book for the faculty that reads what the eye supplies. The Qur'an uses the expression for people who can take a visible event and see what it demonstrates.

The task set for that faculty here is straightforward. Two forces meet; the smaller wins; nothing in the equipment of the smaller party explains the result; a conclusion follows about who governs outcomes. The verse does not argue that God intervenes around the clock in the affairs of every group that prays. It argues that the day of Badr is on record, that its outcome is not accountable to military arithmetic, and that a mind willing to look can draw the inference.

Insight in this vocabulary has a cost attached. The reader who accepts the inference has accepted that his own calculations are not the measure of what will happen to him, and the sūrah will keep pressing that conclusion on the community for the rest of its length. What the two armies display is not simply the power of God in the abstract; it is the difference between a group whose plans depend on their own numbers and a group whose plans depend on a promise. Both were present at Badr. Only one of them was reading the day correctly.

**What the Sign Actually Demonstrates**

Taken alone, a victory by a smaller force proves nothing about God. What gives the event its force in the Qur'an's account is the whole sequence: a promise given before the battle, a supplication made in public, precise numbers attached to the day, and an outcome that matched the earlier announcement. The sign is the coherence of the whole thing, not a lucky upset.

That is why the verse's confidence is so quiet. It does not describe a miracle in the sky; it says the reader has been *given* a sign in the meeting of two armies, which is to say the event has already been delivered as evidence and remains available for weighing. The Book's reader is handed no less than the men who stood on the field — the account of what was said beforehand, and the record of what followed.

The sūrah's other references to the day fill the picture without changing its point. The believer is told elsewhere that the enemy was made to appear few in his eyes so that a purpose already decided could be carried out (8:44), and the believers are told how their cry for help was answered with angels (8:9–10). The reinforcements, the arithmetic, and the outcome are one story. Whoever reads only the count of troops reads the smallest part of what the day contains, and the verse's final word — insight — is a reminder that such readings are available only to those willing to look past the surface.
