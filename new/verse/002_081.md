## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:81

> But no! Those who commit evil and are engrossed in sin will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Balā: The Word That Overturns**

The verse opens with a single particle that answers the claim before it: *balā* — rendered here *But no!* — a word whose whole function is to reverse a preceding denial. The numbered days have just been quoted and rebutted by question; now the rebuttal becomes pronouncement. The dialogue form drops — there is no *Say* here, no argument addressed to arguers — and the recital speaks in the register of settled fact: the schedule is void, and the actual rule is filed in its place. The particle's force is corrective rather than merely negative: *balā* does not only deny the tariff, it asserts the opposite of what the denial implied. They said the Fire's touch would be brief and bounded; *balā* — the Fire's residence is real and, for the persons about to be described, without end. The shift from question to pronouncement marks the passage's judicial structure: verse 80 cross-examined the claim and found it unwarranted; verse 81 delivers the finding. And the delivery begins by defining, with surgical narrowness, exactly whom the forever binds — which is the verse's mercy to every reader not inside the definition, and the reason Islamic theology has argued over its two conditions for fourteen centuries.

**Two Conditions: Earning and Encompassment**

The persons the verdict names are defined by a double formula, and each term is load-bearing. First: *those who commit evil* — the verb is *kasaba*, to earn, to acquire, the sūrah's commercial idiom turned on the self: sin as something a person produces and takes ownership of, wages in the exact sense, the same earning-language verse 79 used for the proceeds of forgery. Second: the sinner *engrossed in sin* — the Arabic *aḥāṭat bihi khaṭīʾatuhu*: his sin has encompassed him, surrounded him, closed over him, the imagery of water over a drowned man or walls around a besieged city. The two conditions are not redundant; the second narrows the first. Not every earner of evil is encompassed by it — the formula describes a completion, and the exegetes' glosses converge on that point: the sin that encompasses is, in Ibn Kathīr's terms, the sin of one who dies in rejection; in al-Ṭabarī's reported reading, the sin of shirk, of one who meets Allah having set up rivals with Him; in another gloss, the sin that has left no good deed standing to counterbalance it. What the conditions together describe is not a lapse but a settled estate: a person whose earning has become an enclosure, whose choices have completed a character with no opening left in it. The verdict that follows — residents of the Fire, forever — is pronounced on that estate and only on it, and the precision is why the verse's first word can be *balā* without contradicting every promise of mercy in the Book.

**Residents, Forever**

The verdict itself uses the sūrah's standing eschatological formula: evildoers become *the residents of the Fire*, and they are *there forever*. The same sentence closed the recital's opening portrait of denial — those who disbelieve and reject the signs are the Fire's residents, there forever (2:39) — and its reappearance here ties the numbered-days claim to the denial it sprang from: the doctrine of a bounded Fire was a privilege claimed by people whose position the sūrah has already ruled on. *Residents* — *aṣḥāb*, companions, the people of a place, belonging to it as it belongs to them — is the verse's psychology in one word: this is not visitation but citizenship, and citizenship in the Fire is the fixed form of the character the two conditions described. *Forever* — *khālidūn… abadan* — doubles the permanence, and the doubling answers the claim's arithmetic exactly: against days numbered, duration without number. The justice of the formula reads through the encompassment: what the person built around himself in a finite life is made the shape of his infinite one, the enclosure he earned becoming the residence he keeps. The Qur'an's eschatology does not present the Fire as a caprice imposed on strangers to it but as the consummation of an encompassed self — which is also why the door the verse leaves open, in the word *encompassed*, has been the hope of every sinner who has not yet been closed over.

**The Verse in the Theological Wars**

Few Qur'anic sentences were argued over as fiercely in Islam's formative centuries, and the argument defines the verse's reception. Read maximally — *evil* meaning any grave sin — the verse became the Khārijī and Muʿtazilī proof-text: the unrepentant major sinner, they held, is the Fire's resident forever, the Muʿtazilah assigning him an intermediate station in this world and perdition in the next. The mainstream position read the two conditions together and refused the maximization: the evil that encompasses is disbelief and association, not the failures of a believer struggling inside his faith, and the Qur'an's own texts bound the reading. *"Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating others with Him ˹in worship˺, but forgives anything else of whoever He wills"* (4:48) — the unforgivable named, everything below it placed inside the divine will. And the recital's own open door: *"O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins"* (39:53) — a verse addressed to the exceeded-limit people precisely so that no encompassment short of final rejection would be read as sealed. The mainstream synthesis keeps both edges: the believer's grave sin does not make him the Fire's resident forever, and no sinner may presume the mercy while continuing the earning — because the verse's terror is real, its conditions are exact, and the space between a lapse and an encompassment is the space repentance exists to keep open.

**The Anatomy of Encompassment**

The image at the verse's center — sin closing over its owner — describes a process the sūrah has already documented from the adjacent side. The hardened hearts of verse 74 were hardened *after* the signs, layer upon layer, each unresponded evidence depositing stone; the encompassment of verse 81 is the same accumulation seen from inside the water. A first sin is a wound; repeated, unrepented, defended, it becomes a habit; the habit becomes a character; the character becomes an enclosure — the point at which the person no longer has his sins but is had by them, and the verse's second condition is met. The tradition's moral psychology takes the image literally as diagnostic: a ḥadīth of Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim pictures trials presented to hearts as a reed mat is woven stick by stick — every heart impregnated by them takes a black mark, every heart that rejects them a white one — until hearts sort into two kinds, and the engrossed state is reached not by a single enormity but by the refusal of many small breaches (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 144a). This is why the verse's terror is pastoral rather than merely judicial. It is not filed to make readers despair of their lapses — a lapse is not an encompassment — but to make them afraid of the direction: of the earning that continues after the conscience protests, of the breach defended instead of repaired, of the slow hydraulic work by which a sinner's own choices close over his head. *Balā* overturns the numbered days, and the overturned claim's lesson survives in inverted form: there are no cheap schedules, and the only number that matters is how long the encompassment is allowed to continue before it is no longer possible to leave.

**The Audit of Earning**

The verse closes the numbered-days episode by installing its permanent law: the Fire is not a tariff but a wage, and wages follow work. The *kasaba* that opens the formula is the recital's whole economics of the self in one verb — every person earning, daily, the residence he will occupy, the sūrah having just indicted a community for earning its religion as revenue (79) and its eschatology as comfort (80). The encompassed sinners of verse 81 are the final case of the same principle stripped of its excuses: no forged warranty, no national exemption, no schedule — only the closed account of a self that earned enclosure and receives it. The verdict's symmetry with the promise the recital keeps beside it is the verse's balance for its audience: the same formula, the same forever, stands ready on the other side of the ledger for belief and righteous work, and the sūrah will file it immediately. For the reader under audit — and the recital addresses every reader as audited — the verse's function is to destroy the two false comforts it was revealed against. Against presumption: identity purchases no term, and no people has a pledge it cannot produce. Against despair: the encompassment is not the state of the struggling, and 39:53 remains open until the water actually closes. Between the two, the verse leaves the only ground the recital ever offers — the earning, today, in whichever direction the hands are moving.
