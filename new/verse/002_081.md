## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:81

> But no! Those who commit evil and are engrossed in sin will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word That Cancels What Came Before**

The verse opens with an emphatic correction that the translators render "but no," and its function is to cancel the claim reported in the previous sentence. The Fire is not limited to a fixed number of days for the people described here, and the exemption they supposed does not exist. The Book uses this construction when a false position has been stated and must be cleared away before the true one is given.

The opening word is therefore retrospective as well as forward-looking. Everything the reader has just heard about numbered days is set aside, and the sentence that follows supplies the test that actually applies to a person's standing.

**The Two Conditions in the Sentence**

The verse sets out two requirements, and both must hold for its description to fit. The person commits evil, and he is engrossed in his sin. The second condition carries the weight, and the Arabic verb behind it describes something that closes around a person from all sides until no part of his conduct remains outside it.

The commentators read the enclosure in two ways that do not exclude each other. Some took it as the state of a person whose sins surround him completely, so that his whole life is governed by them. Others read it as the particular sin that places a person beyond forgiveness, connecting the verse to the passage in the women's chapter stating that God does not forgive the association of others with Him while forgiving what is less than that for whom He wills (4:48). Both readings keep the emphasis on the totality of the engulfing rather than on an isolated act.

**Sin That Becomes a Wall**

The Book employs this image of encirclement for people whose deeds accumulate into a barrier. It states that the evil of what they earned surrounded them, and it warns the believers not to let their actions come between them and the mercy of God. The word also appears in the account of Pharaoh's household, who were engulfed by what they used to earn (40:45 in substance).

The metaphor is exact about how ruin arrives. No single act builds a wall around a person; the wall is raised by accumulation, and the person inside loses sight of what lies beyond it. That is why the verdict here attaches to the surrounded condition rather than to an individual offence, and why the next verse names the opposite condition for those whose conduct went the other way.

**Faith and Deeds Held Together**

The following verse pairs belief with good conduct and promises the Garden as a permanent home. Read together, the two sentences state the Book's standard in the form this sūrah applies throughout: conviction expressed in action, or action without conviction that eventually closes in on the person doing it.

The pairing is not a claim that works save anyone independently of faith, and it is not a claim that a profession of faith settles a person's fate independently of his conduct. The Book uses the same structure in its repeated descriptions of those who believe and act rightly, and the community's scholars drew from the two verses the position that a person's final standing is determined by both together.

**The Early Controversies**

Later schools read this verse and its partner in diverging directions. The Khawārij treated the commission of a major sin as grounds for declaring a Muslim outside the faith, taking the "engrossed" clause as applying to anyone who fell into grave sin without repenting. The Murjiʾa moved the other way and detached faith from action, holding that a profession of belief settled a person's fate whatever his conduct. The Muʿtazila held that the two together decided reward and punishment and that neither could substitute for the other.

The Book's own answer lies in its repeated treatment of sin and repentance: it commands turning back to God and states that He forgives those who do so. The mainstream position of the scholars therefore read the permanence described here as applying to a person who dies in the condition named, since a life whose sins have closed in around it is by definition a life without the repentance that opens a way out.

**Permanence Stated in Both Directions**

The sūrah assigns the same eternity to the residence of the believers in the next verse that it assigns here to this group. The symmetry is part of the passage's argument: a claim that the punishment of a community would be brief is answered by a statement of permanence the Book applies to those who have enclosed themselves in wrongdoing.

The verse names no community, and its conditions are phrased so that any reader can apply them to himself. That is deliberate, since the sūrah's readers include the believers, and every warning addressed to the People of the Book in this passage will be turned on the Muslims in one form or another before the sūrah is finished.

**A Standard Rather Than a Verdict on Others**

The sentence states a criterion, and the criterion is not the identity of the person being described. A reader who uses the verse to settle the fate of a community has converted a test into a label, and the Book's own usage resists that move. The two destinies are described in general terms, and the entries are moral rather than genealogical.

What the passage then does is return to the covenant the community had ratified and broken, and it measures their conduct against their own undertaking. The verse's function in that sequence is to make the outcome explicit before the evidence is set out, so that the reader knows what is at stake when the covenant's terms and the breach of them are presented in the verses that follow.

**Where the Verse Places Responsibility**

The sentence describes a condition rather than a category of person, and it places responsibility for reaching that condition on conduct. The Book's order is consistent throughout: a person acts, the action accumulates, and the accumulation eventually surrounds him. Nothing in the verse suggests the conclusion arrives without the conduct that produced it, which is why the Qur'an's commands to repent are addressed to people in every state short of death. The verse is therefore not a sentence passed on a group but a warning about a trajectory, and the sūrah states the opposite trajectory in the next line so that the two directions can be compared. A community that reads the passage as an account of somebody else's fate has missed the function the Book assigns to it in this place.
