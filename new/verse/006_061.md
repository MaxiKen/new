## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:61

> He reigns supreme over all of His creation, and sends recording-angels, watching over you. When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, never neglecting this duty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement the Sūrah Makes Twice**

The opening clause of this verse repeats almost word for word a sentence the sūrah used earlier, where God is described as reigning supreme over His creation and as being All-Wise and All-Aware (6:18). The repetition is not accidental, since the two statements sit inside the same argument, and the second adds the machinery that makes the first concrete: not only does God hold authority over what He has made, but there are appointed agents watching what every human being does.

The Arabic phrase describes a mastery that leaves nothing outside it, and the Book uses the same root for the overwhelming of a people by water, for the descent of an irresistible punishment, and for the crushing realization of a truth one had denied. The verse's claim is that God's authority over His creatures is of that order: complete, present, and unopposed.

**The Angels Who Write**

Then the verse describes the recorders: vigilant, honourable angels who note everything and know what people do (82:10–12). The sūrah of Qāf describes two of them sitting on the right and the left and states that no word is uttered without an observer ready to write it down (50:17–18). The Book refers to the same system when it says that every soul has successive angels before and behind it, protecting it by God's command (13:11).

Commentators discussed whether these are the same angels as the scribes of a person's deeds, and the common view is that the record of deeds is kept by two witnesses at all times. What matters for the verse is that the watching is described as an office assigned by God rather than as a power the angels hold by themselves, so the whole apparatus leads back to the One the audience was being asked to acknowledge.

**Protection and Observation in One Sentence**

Read carefully, the clause describes two functions that pull in opposite directions: the angels guard, and they record. The Book holds both ideas without tension, since the same presence which notes a person's conduct also shields him from harms he never knows about, and the sūrah of the Cave speaks of protection when it says that God brought the truth before anyone could see it.

For the Makkans, this sentence carried a correction to a familiar belief about the jinn and the invisible world. The Book's position is that the unseen is administered, not chaotic, and that no spirit has a private claim on a human being's fate. The angels in this verse do nothing on their own initiative, which is why the account they keep is trustworthy.

**The Moment of Death**

When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, never neglecting this duty. The verse moves from the ongoing record to the single moment in which the record is closed, and the phrasing about the angels never neglecting their task is a reassurance to the friends of God and a warning to everyone else. No one dies unattended, and no one dies at a moment other than the one that was fixed.

The Book describes the taking of the souls of the wrongdoers in harsher terms elsewhere. When the angels seize the souls of those who wronged themselves, they ask them what they were doing, and the reply from the people who claim they were oppressed in the land is met with the question of whether God's earth was not wide enough to move within (4:97). Another passage describes the angels striking the faces and backs of those who disbelieve (47:27), and the sūrah itself will describe the same scene with the angels stretching out their hands (6:93).

**A Reassurance That Cuts Both Ways**

The sentence about the angels fulfilling their duty is addressed to a community in which the manner of a person's death carried heavy social meaning. A death far from home, or an early death, or a violent death, was read as a judgment on the person and his tribe. The verse removes that reading by stating that the taking of the soul is performed by appointed agents who never fall short, which means the circumstances of a death are not a verdict on the one who dies.

That is the mercy in the sentence. The same clause also establishes the point the Book repeats in several places: the moment cannot be moved. The sūrah had said earlier that every community has its appointed term and cannot delay or advance it (7:34; 10:49), and here the application is made to a single person. Neither the physicians of the city nor the idols at the shrine have any part in the timing.

**What Follows the Angel**

The verse stops at the taking and leaves the next sentence to describe what happens after it, which is where the sūrah's argument about accountability reaches its conclusion. The reader is left with a chain of events that has no gap in it: keys with God, knowledge of every leaf, souls taken at night, deeds known by day, an appointed term completed, then the return.

The word the verse uses for the return is a term of ownership, and it opens the question the next verse answers in four words about who the Master is. The record kept by the two angels and the moment of death are the two ends of a single process, and the sūrah is now ready to describe what comes after both.

**What the Sentence Teaches About the Hidden World**

The Book mentions the angels in this verse and in the two on either side of it, and it never once describes them as acting on their own authority. They record, they guard, and they take the soul when the order comes. The uniform picture is a world in which every invisible power is an employee rather than a partner, and the sūrah's audience, which had assigned shares of the unseen to the jinn and the idols, is being shown the actual chain of command.
