## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:110

> Establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. Whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will ˹certainly˺ find ˹its reward˺ with Allah. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Commands in the Middle of an Argument**

The sūrah interrupts its account of the People of the Book with an instruction to the believers, and the instruction consists of the two practices that the Book treats as the standing markers of a life turned toward God. The interruption is deliberate. The passage has described communities that broke covenants and discarded their scriptures, and the sūrah now tells its own community what to hold on to.

The pairing of the prayer and the alms appears throughout the Book in descriptions of the prophets and their peoples, and the sūrah itself listed them among the terms of the covenant given to the children of Israel (2:83). The commands here therefore echo the obligations whose neglect the passage has been documenting. The chapter has been recounting how earlier communities broke their covenants and set their scriptures aside, and its own community is now told what to keep. The commands echo the obligations whose neglect the passage has been documenting.

**Sending Good Ahead**

The verse describes good deeds as something a person sends forward, and the image treats a person's actions as provisions dispatched to a destination he has not yet reached. The Book uses the same figure in the chapter of the wrapped one, where the believers are told to send ahead whatever good they can and are promised that they will find it better and greater (73:20).

The metaphor answers the attachment to life described a few verses earlier, where a community wished for a thousand years instead of preparing for what follows. What the Book offers in place of that wish is a transaction whose return is guaranteed and whose terms are available to anyone, and the sentence states that what is sent ahead will be found.

**Found With Allah**

The promise is that the good will be found with God, and the commentators understood this to mean that the reward is stored and delivered on the Day of Judgment rather than in the present. The Book describes that day as the moment when every person finds what he has done present before him, and the sūrah's repeated promise in this passage uses the same vocabulary.

The phrase also identifies the security of the deposit. What a person sends ahead is not exposed to the hazards that attend property in this world, since the One who receives it does not lose what is entrusted to Him, and the sūrah's closing attribute states that nothing given in this way escapes His notice.

**The Prophet's Practice in Recitation**

A companion reported that when the Prophet ﷺ recited this verse he would follow it by affirming that God sees all things, and the report illustrates a practice of pausing during recitation to respond to the content of a verse with supplication or praise. The same practice is described for verses of glorification and verses of prostration, and it reflects a way of reading that treats the revelation as a conversation rather than a text to be completed.

The report also supplies a model for the reader of the passage. The verse's final clause is a statement about God's awareness, and the companion's account shows the earliest community answering such statements directly rather than passing over them. It also leaves an example of how the passage should be received. Since the verse ends with a statement about God's awareness, the report shows the first community responding to such statements aloud instead of passing over them.

**Instructions Between Arguments**

The sūrah's placement of a devotional command inside a long dispute has a pedagogical purpose. The passage has been analysing the conduct of communities that possessed revelation and failed it, and the risk for the new community is that analysis will substitute for practice. The sūrah answers by issuing two commands that are entirely within its own readers' control.

The commands are also the counterweight to the envy described in the previous verse. The believers cannot change what others wish for them, and the verse gives them work they can do while the matter is in God's hands. The two sentences that follow, one about the claims of the two communities and one about the submission that the Book accepts, continue the passage's argument about what actually counts before God.

**Fasting, Prayer, and the Chapter's Pattern**

The sūrah will return to these two obligations at intervals as the chapter continues, and it lists them among the marks of the believers in its description of the community's character. The obligations are given here without further qualification and without an explanation of their forms, since the community was already practising them.

What the reader receives from the verse is a pair of anchors set in the middle of a difficult passage. **The Closing Attribute**

The sentence ends by stating that God sees what the believers do, and the sūrah has used the same ending a few verses earlier in its description of the community that clung to life. The repetition places the two endings side by side: the same seeing that exposed the earlier community's attachment witnesses the deeds of the believers. The attribute is a comfort and a warning at the same time, and the Book leaves both implications with the reader. **Prayer and Alms as the Believers' Answer**

The two obligations named in the verse are the ones the sūrah listed among the terms of the covenant given to the children of Israel, and the passage has been describing what became of that covenant. The instruction therefore has a historical edge: the community now receiving the revelation is told to do what the earlier community was told and abandoned. The commands are also a practical answer to the envy and hostility described in the surrounding verses, since they give the believers a discipline that no opponent can interrupt and a form of wealth that no rival can take from them. What the sentence promises is that whatever good is sent ahead will be found with God, and the promise is stated in the language of deposit rather than of expenditure. The verse leaves the reader with two acts and one assurance, and the sūrah's later passages build on both.
