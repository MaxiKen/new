## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:174

> So they returned with Allah’s favours and grace, suffering no harm. For they sought to please Allah. And surely Allah is ˹the˺ Lord of infinite bounty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return Described**

The verse states that the community returned with blessing and bounty from God and that no harm had touched it, and it adds that the men had pursued the contentment of God. The sentence describes the outcome of the expedition that the previous verses introduced.

Al-Ṭabarī explains that the community came back unharmed by the enemy, since no second battle took place. The sentence supplies the outcome of the decision reported in the previous verse, and it does so in the language of gift rather than of achievement. On the reading that connects the passage with the appointment at Badr a year later, the return was from that meeting, where no fighting occurred at all. Both readings describe a homecoming without losses.

**Blessing and Bounty Distinguished**

Al-Rāzī drew a distinction between the two words: the blessing may refer to goods of this world, such as the safety and the provisions of the journey, while the bounty refers to the good of the Hereafter. The sentence therefore covers both portions of what the Book promises to those who obey.

The pair had appeared in the earlier description of the martyrs, and the repetition links the two groups. Those who died in God's cause and those who came back safely from the pursuit are described with the same vocabulary of divine generosity, and the passage presents both outcomes as gifts.

**Pursuing the Contentment of God**

The phrase about pursuing God's contentment appears earlier in the sūrah, where the verse contrasts the person who seeks it with the one who earns anger, and the two verses are connected by the same words. The men who went out after Uḥud are described by their aim, and the aim is the one the sūrah has named as the mark of the righteous.

The Book's use of the same phrase in both places gives the passage a symmetry. The community that had been instructed about trust and reward is shown in action, pursuing the same contentment that the earlier verse described as the object of a good life.

**The Book's Promises to Those Who Obey**

The Book attaches both kinds of good to the obedience it commends. In one passage it tells the believers that God will give them help and a victory that is near, describing the gift as something they long for. In another it tells them not to falter in pursuing the enemy and reminds them that they have hopes their opponents do not share.

The verse before the reader fits this pattern. The community's decision to march was answered with safety and with generosity, and the sūrah records both as a single outcome of a single decision. The promises in the two passages come from different moments in the community's history, and the Book states them with the same confidence that obedience and gift belong together.

**What "Untouched by Evil" Covers**

The phrase covers safety from the harm that had been feared, and the commentators read it as a statement about the community's condition rather than only about its bodies. The men had gone out wounded and returned without further losses, and the sūrah notes the fact without elaborating it.

The detail matters in the passage's economy. The community had been told that it would be tested and that its conduct would determine the outcome, and the expedition after Uḥud was the first occasion on which it could apply what it had learned. The verse records that the application went well.

**The Name That Closes the Verse**

The sentence ends with the declaration that God is possessed of tremendous bounty, and the phrase echoes the close of the earlier verse about the pardon the community had received. The same generosity that covered the failure at Uḥud is named here for the success of the pursuit.

The repetition gives the two moments the same ground. The community's pardon and its reward both come from a giver whose generosity is not measured by the merit of the recipients, and the sūrah's readers are expected to hold both statements together when they consider what the community had been through.

**The Confidence That Was Restored**

The two expeditions stand side by side in the sūrah: the march that ended in defeat, and the march that ended in safety and gain. The community had lost confidence in the first, and the second restored it.

The Book uses the pair to show that a community's condition is not fixed by a single day. The sūrah had explained the first outcome in terms of the community's conduct and had promised pardon; the second outcome follows the corrected conduct and is presented as what such conduct produces. The reports describe the men setting out with their wounds still fresh only days after the defeat, and the safe return gave the community a second memory to place beside the first.

**The Pattern the Passage Teaches**

Read with the verses before it, this sentence describes a community that had been told its losses came from its own hands, that had accepted the correction, that had answered a call while still wounded, and that had returned with what the Book calls blessing and bounty.

The sequence is the sūrah's answer to the frustration of the earlier passage. It shows that the community's position with God had not been forfeited by the defeat, and it gives the readers of the sūrah a model of recovery that does not depend on the specifics of the campaign. The next verses leave the fighting behind and take up the sūrah's wider arguments, and the account of the expedition stands as the community's working example of what it had been taught.
