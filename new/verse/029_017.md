## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:17

> You worship besides Allah nothing but idols, simply creating lies ˹about them˺. Those you worship besides Allah certainly cannot give you any provision. So seek provision from Allah ˹alone˺, worship Him, and be grateful to Him. To Him you will ˹all˺ be returned."

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naming the Objects by What They Are**

Abraham's next sentence strips the local religion of the names it uses for itself. He does not call the objects of worship deities, intercessors, or honoured ancestors; he calls them idols, and he places the word at the head of the verdict. Naming is an argument in the Book, because a practice is often protected by a vocabulary: call a stone a god and it acquires a claim, call it a stone and the claim disappears. Abraham's people knew perfectly well what stood in their temple; what they had done was to arrange their lives around it and give it a title. The Book repeatedly performs this operation on idolatry, describing the objects by their material and their incapacity rather than by their reputation. The first service Abraham renders his people is to say what they are actually dealing with.

**The Charge That They Manufacture Their Own Religion**

The clause that follows is a charge of fabrication, and it belongs to the rhetoric of the previous verses, where the sūrah indicted men who invented a religion and sold it as a service. Abraham's people are described as creating a lie about the objects they worship and about the standing of those objects. The lie has two parts: that the idols possess what only God possesses, and that the worship offered to them is a transaction that will pay. Neither claim can be checked against anything, which is the definition of fabrication the Book has been using. The charge is not that the idolaters are stupid about stones; it is that they have built a worldview on assertions they cannot support and then staked their conduct on it. The sūrah treats that manufacture as the common crime of every generation that prefers an unverifiable god to the One who makes claims on it.

**The Argument From Provision**

Abraham then selects a single test case and presses it: the objects of worship cannot provide for their worshippers, so provision should be sought from the One who can give it. The Qur'an returns to this argument often, stating that those who worship besides God cannot afford anyone any provision from the heavens and the earth and have no power to do so (16:73). The reasoning is practical rather than metaphysical. A worshipper asks his god for what he needs — rain, food, children, security — and the man who carves or buys his god is petitioning something that depends on the same supply chain he does. The Book's test exposes the absurdity without mockery: address your requests to the source of the supply. Abraham's people were asking for provision from things that had never provided anything, and the sentence tells them where the warehouse is.

**The Nearest-to-God Defense, Answered**

The Book records the counterargument that idolaters in every era make, and it answers it directly. They say they worship these beings only so that they may bring them closer to God (39:3), and the Book replies that sincere devotion belongs to God alone and that those who persist in the claim are, in its own words, liars and deniers. The defence fails because a mediator has to own something to mediate with. An object that neither hears nor sees nor benefits cannot carry a request upward, and the Book's alternative is the one Abraham states in this verse: seek provision from God, worship Him, and give thanks to Him. The instruction removes the middle layer entirely and leaves the creature addressing his creator without an apparatus. Abraham is not denying the existence of a distant God; he is demolishing the office of the intermediaries his people have installed.

**Gratitude as the Only Honest Response**

The command to be grateful is placed at the exact point where the argument about provision ends, and the placement is deliberate. Gratitude is the proper reaction to a gift that has already been received, and it is also the test of whether the recipient understands where the gift came from. The Book pairs thanks with worship in one breath when it tells believers to eat from the good things provided for them and to give thanks to God if it is Him they truly worship (2:172). Ingratitude, by contrast, is presented as the characteristically human failure: people remember God when a need presses and forget Him when the pressure lifts. Abraham's three imperatives — seek, worship, thank — describe a life ordered around a single provider, and the gratitude is what keeps the order honest.

**Provision as a Subject of Worship**

The Book joins the supply of livelihood to the practice of worship in a way that is easy to pass over, and one verse states the connection with unusual directness: the Prophet is told to command his people to pray and to be constant in it, and then to say that God asks no provision of him, since it is God who provides for him, with the final outcome belonging to the righteous (20:132). The order in that sentence is the same as in the verse under discussion: the command to worship comes first, and the question of supply is answered by the One giving the command. Abraham's instruction to his people was that they seek provision from God and give thanks to Him, and the placement of the injunction inside a missionary speech tells the reader that the argument about food was, from the first, an argument about worship.

**The Return That Closes the Case**

The verse ends by naming the destination every party in the argument shares: to Him you will all be returned. The idolaters will not be returned to their idols, and the man who argued with them will not be returned to his own reasoning. Everyone arrives at the same court, which is why the Book can be patient with the disagreement in the present. Abraham's speech does not end in an ultimatum about the city; it ends with a fact about the future that both sides will verify at the same time. That structure will be repeated in sūrah after sūrah, and its effect is to make the day of return the moment when every claim about gods is finally checked against what the claim was worth to the person making it. The verse's movement is also worth tracing as a whole: it starts by identifying the objects, exposes the fabrication behind them, tests them against provision, redirects the request to God, and closes with the destination everyone shares. Five steps, none of them longer than a line, and together they dismantle a religion without insulting the people who practise it.
