## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:27

> If only you could see when they will be detained before the Fire! They will cry, “Oh! If only we could be sent back, we would never deny the signs of our Lord and we would ˹surely˺ be of the believers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Scene Shown to the Prophet**

The Book several times asks the Prophet to picture a moment he cannot witness, so that its weight enters his argument. If you could see when the wrongdoers are in the throes of death while the angels stretch out their hands (6:93); if only you could see when they will be detained before their Lord (6:30); if only you could see the wicked hanging their heads in shame before their Lord, crying that they have now seen and heard and asking to be sent back (32:12). The device takes a claim about the future and gives it the force of a scene already in progress.

Its placement here matters. The verse before it described leaders who turned people away from the Prophet and kept their distance while ruining themselves, and this scene shows where that road ends. Nobody in the picture is debating theology, and nobody is guarding a shrine.

**Detained, Not Yet Thrown In**

The Arabic verb describes being held back and made to stand in front of something, which shapes the whole passage. These people are not yet inside the punishment, and they are not on their way back to the ordinary world either. They are held at the point where the truth about their lives is visible in front of them and nothing can be done about it, which is precisely the position the sūrah has been telling its audience they refuse to imagine.

Standing rather than burning means the scene is about knowledge before it is about pain. What is recorded in it is not a sensation but an understanding, and the request that follows is produced by that understanding rather than by the heat.

**The Reading That Changes the Grammar**

The sentence they speak admits two vocalisations, and the difference matters. On the reading followed by the translation, they promise: if we were sent back, we would never deny the signs of our Lord, and we would surely be of the believers. On the other reading, the sentence is a regret about the past rather than a pledge about the future: would that we had not denied the signs of our Lord, and that we had been among the believers.

Commentators who weigh the two prefer the first, because the verse that follows explicitly rejects the pledge: even if they were sent back, they would certainly revert to what they were forbidden. A regret about the past would leave that rebuttal without a target, while a promise about the future gives it something to refuse. The disagreement is grammatical, and its resolution is that one reading supplies the next sentence with its subject.

**The Request Every Generation Makes**

Begging for a return is a fixed feature of these scenes, and the Book repeats it because the request never becomes less common. The wrongdoers plead, as they face the torment, to be shown any way back (42:44); people scream to their Lord to take them out so they may do good rather than what they used to do (35:37); the wicked ask to be sent back, promising good conduct and claiming sure faith (32:12); and the misled followers, seeing the fate of the leaders they obeyed, cry out that with a second chance they would disown them as they were disowned (2:167).

The repetition shows what the discovery of truth does to a person who lived around it without submitting to it. It also shows the reader this scene in advance, since the request is granted to nobody in the Book.

**Why the Wish Cannot Be Honoured**

Nothing in the scene is arbitrary. The life being asked for was already given, and the answer to a direct request for return is recorded: were you not given lives long enough for whoever wished to be mindful to be mindful, and did the warner not come to you (35:37)? A second term would add no information to the case, because the information had been delivered. What it would add is a different motive, and a virtue produced by the sight of the Fire is not the virtue the test was designed to produce.

That is the difference between a life built on conviction and a life adjusted to consequences. The sūrah's argument has been that the signs were sufficient for anyone willing to weigh them; a second term would replace weighing with arithmetic.

**The Voice of Panic, Not of Faith**

Commentators observe that the promise is made out of fear of the Fire rather than from any real desire for belief, which is why it carries no benefit. That judgement sounds harsh until the words are examined. A person who wants to be sent back so that his situation may be improved is asking for relief; a person who wants to be a believer is asking for a change in himself, and the sentence in the verse is the first kind of request.

The Book has already recorded what happens to a promise of belief made at the sight of punishment, in the case of Pharaoh as the sea closed over him. He declared his faith, and was told that it came too late, after a lifetime of disobedience and corruption (10:90–91). The verse in front of us is that moment, multiplied into a crowd, and spoken before the reader has to reach it.

There is one further difference worth noticing between the two scenes. Pharaoh spoke when the water arrived and there was still a heartbeat in which to speak, while the men in this verse are detained in a place where the ability to act has been withdrawn entirely. Both confessions cost their speakers nothing, which is precisely why the Book reports them and refuses to count them as faith. A confession whose price is zero cannot be evidence of anything about the person making it.
