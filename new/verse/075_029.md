## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:29

> and ˹then˺ their feet are tied together ˹in a shroud˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Leg Joined to Leg**

The verse is four words in Arabic and it has produced a long list of readings. *Al-sāq* is the shank, the part of the leg between knee and ankle, and *iltaffa* means to become entwined or wrapped around. The plainest picture is of the two legs brought together and bound, and the question the commentators argued over is what that binding means. The sūrah gives no explanatory clause, which is why the readings multiply: every interpreter has to supply a context, and each supplies the one his reading of the surrounding verses suggests.

**The Reading That Fits the Sequence**

The majority took it as the shroud. The legs of the dead are brought together and wrapped in the winding sheet, and the verse then belongs to a sequence that is easy to follow: the soul reaches the collarbones, the question is asked, the certainty arrives, and the body is wrapped. This reading has the immediate context on its side, since the sūrah has been describing the physical progress of a death from v. 26 onward, and the binding of the legs is the next physical thing that happens to that body. It also has the idiom on its side: Arabic poetry uses the phrase for the wrapping of the corpse, and the early authorities reported that sense without hesitation. On this reading the verse completes the deathbed scene and hands the body over to burial.

**The Readings That Read It as Hardship**

A second group of commentators took *al-sāq* in its idiomatic sense of severity, since Arabic uses the baring of the shank for a matter that has become grave, and read the verse as the joining of one hardship to another: the agony of leaving this world entwined with the agony of entering the next. Variations on this reading make it the last day of this life joined to the first day of the next, or death joined to life, or the general confusion of a day on which people's legs are tangled and they cannot move. The strength of this family of readings is that it takes the idiom seriously and gives the verse a moral rather than a merely physical content. Its weakness is that it must supply the hardships being joined, since the text names none, whereas the shroud reading needs nothing supplied.

**Weighing Them**

The shroud reading should be preferred, and the reason is contextual rather than lexical. Every verse from 26 onward has been tracking the body: collarbones, the question at the bedside, the certainty, and then, three verses later, the driving to the Lord. A description of the corpse being wrapped sits naturally in that series, while a moral abstraction about two hardships arriving together does not. The objection to discarding the second reading entirely is that the Qur'an itself uses *sāq* in the sense of a grave and unavoidable matter: *“˹Beware of˺ the Day the Shin ˹of Allah˺ will be bared, and the wicked will be asked to prostrate, but they will not be able to do so,”* (68:42). The lexicographers connect that usage to the image of a man rolling up his garment to his shank for a task that has become serious, which is the same idiom the commentators invoked here. That said, the two are not exclusive in effect. The Qur'an frequently uses a physical detail that carries a moral charge, and the binding of a dead man's legs in a shroud is not an image anyone finds neutral. The body is being prepared for the grave at the very moment the sūrah is insisting that the grave is not the end.

**The Body Is Not Discarded**

This is the point the sūrah's argument has been building since v. 3. The objector asked whether the bones could be gathered, and the reply was that even the fingertips could be restored. Everything since then has assumed the importance of the body: the soul leaves it at the collarbones, the certainty is located in the dying person, and the body is wrapped with care and buried. The Qur'an does not treat the corpse as a husk to be shed. It is the same body that will be raised, which is why the sūrah can move without embarrassment from a man's legs being tied in a sheet to his being driven to his Lord. Resurrection is not the replacement of a body but the reassembly of one. The sūrah said as much at the outset, when it met the objection about crumbled bones with the claim that even the fingertips can be restored, and the Qur'an shows the same principle enacted for a man who doubted it: *“And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!”* (2:259). The corpse being wrapped in this verse is therefore not a discarded shell. It is the material of the argument the sūrah opened with, and the body that will answer for the life lived in it.

**Burial, and What Follows Immediately**

The sequence also compresses time in the way the Qur'an does elsewhere. Between the shroud and the driving there is, in ordinary experience, an interval that may last centuries; in the sūrah's account the two are joined by *on that Day*, and the barrier that is set behind the dead until the resurrection (23:100) is not an interval the dead experience. The grammar is doing theology here. A person dies, is wrapped, and is driven to his Lord, and nothing in the sūrah's telling allows the reader to insert a comfortable delay between the grave and the reckoning.
