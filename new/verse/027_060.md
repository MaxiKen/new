## Sūrah an-Naml 27:60

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who created the heavens and the earth, and sends down rain for you from the sky, by which We cause delightful gardens to grow? You could never cause their trees to grow. Was it another god besides Allah?” Absolutely not! But they are a people who set up equals ˹to Allah˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Largest Thing to the Smallest**

The question begins with the creation of the heavens and the earth and ends with a tree. That movement is the verse's design, and it is more persuasive than either end would be alone.

Cosmic origins are easy to concede and easy to keep at a distance; a man can agree that God made the universe and continue his religious practice unchanged, because nothing in his week depends on the answer. The verse therefore does not stop there. It follows the chain down — heavens and earth, then rain from the sky, then gardens grown by that rain, then the specific trees in them, then the flat statement that the listener could not grow one. By the last clause the argument has arrived in the orchard the audience owns, and the concession made at the top has become a claim about their own property.

**Rain Placed Immediately After Creation**

The second item in the chain is water sent down from the sky. Placing it directly after the creation of the heavens and the earth gives it extraordinary weight, and the placement makes sense for the audience addressed.

Mecca is a dry valley. Agriculture in Arabia depended on rainfall that arrived unpredictably and could not be compelled, which is precisely why rain-making featured in the religious practices of the region. A question about who sends the water is not abstract theology in that setting; it concerns the single variable that decided whether a season ended in surplus or in famine. The Qur'an returns to rain constantly as its standard example of a sign, and the reason is this combination: it is universally necessary, entirely outside human control, and visibly arriving from somewhere else.

**"Delightful Gardens" and What the Words Mean**

The Arabic is *ḥadā'iqa dhāta bahjah*. A *ḥadīqah* is not a garden in the general sense; al-Ṭabarī defines it as an enclosed, walled garden, and al-Qurṭubī specifies gardens of date palms. The word denotes cultivated ground with a boundary around it — a property.

*Bahjah* is beauty of the kind that gladdens. Al-Ṭabarī connects it here to what is edible, the flowering and fruiting from which people and animals eat, so the delight is not purely visual. Taken together the phrase names something an Arabian audience knew as the highest form of agricultural wealth: a walled palm garden in fruit. The verse's argument is then addressed to the owner of exactly such a property. He built the wall, dug the channels, planted the offshoots and pruned the crowns, and the verse is about to tell him what he did not do.

**"You Could Never Cause Their Trees to Grow"**

The clause is emphatic in Arabic: *mā kāna lakum an tunbitū shajarahā* — it was not for you to make their trees grow. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss is exact and practical: if God did not send the water, these trees would not flourish.

Al-Rāzī presses the point further, arguing that the verse removes the idea that a human being grows anything at all, since every aspect of a tree's existence and survival originates elsewhere. That is the harder claim and the verse supports it. A farmer's contribution is real and it is entirely rearrangement — he moves a seed, directs water, removes competitors. Nothing he does makes the seed open. The Qur'an puts the same argument about crops in another place, asking whether the sower or the one who sows is responsible for what is reaped. Both passages isolate the identical gap between the work a person genuinely performs and the outcome he takes credit for.

**"Was It Another God?" — the Refrain Begins**

The question closes with *a-ilāhun maʿa Allāh* — is there a god along with God? This is the refrain that will end each of the questions in this section, and its form is worth attention.

The preceding clause set up a comparison and never delivers one. The grammar leads the listener to expect something placed against God and then arrives at a question about whether anything can be placed there at all. That structural refusal is itself the argument: the power and generosity just described are of an order that should not be offered a comparison. The answer follows immediately as *bal*, a particle that cancels what came before — no, rather, they are a people who set up equals. And the verb chosen, *yaʿdilūn*, is ambiguous in a way the commentators noticed. Without a preposition to fix it, it can mean to make equal or to swerve away. Both fit. A person who assigns God an equal has, in the same act, turned aside from Him.

**The Argument Uses Only Agreed Facts**

Nothing in this verse asks the audience to accept a new premise. The heavens exist, rain falls, gardens grow, and no farmer has ever made a tree come up by willing it. Every element was available to a Meccan merchant without any revelation at all.

That restraint is what makes the question hard to answer. The Qur'an is not appealing to a miracle, a scripture or a private experience; it is pointing at the shared world and asking who is responsible for it. Pharaoh's court refused signs that were specially produced for them. The audience here is given nothing special, only the ordinary furniture of their lives, arranged so that the question becomes unavoidable. This is the method the chapter opened with when it called the Book's verses *āyāt*, signs, using the same word it uses for rain and growth. The evidence was always the world; the Book claims only to be pointing at it.
