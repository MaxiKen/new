## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:42

> Allah surely knows that whatever ˹gods˺ they invoke besides Him are ˹simply˺ nothing. For He is the Almighty, AllWise.

**Expanded Commentary**

The complaint against the town's devotion is put in terms of knowledge rather than of power, which is a more damaging charge. A weak ally who has been told what is needed can still be useful, whereas a thing that cannot be informed of the request cannot respond to it at all. Every act of worship is a claim to have been heard, and the verse removes that claim by naming the one party in the relationship who is described as knowing everything. The Book's argument against the idols therefore does not depend on what they could do; it depends on what they were.

**The Verse States an Empty Set**

The sentence names the objects of invocation and then states outright that they are nothing. The reader who has followed the chapter recognizes the claim, since the sūrah has already described idols that cannot provide, patrons who will disown their dependants, and protectors who cannot protect. What is new here is the form of the statement: the Book is not saying that the gods of the disbelievers are weak, inferior, or subordinate. It reports that they are nothing at all, while at the same time acknowledging that people invoke them and organize their lives around them. The verse's construction holds both facts, since the first half is about what people do and the second half is about what actually exists. The God who is addressed knows the difference even when the worshippers do not.

**Why the Verse Begins With Knowledge**

The sentence opens with an assertion about what God knows rather than an assertion about what the gods are, and the ordering is deliberate. The people addressed have their own account of the matter, and it is not a philosophical one; they call upon these beings, they feel their needs met by them, and they conclude that something is there. The verse does not argue with that experience. It places the judgement with the one who knows the situation entirely, and it leaves the audience to consider which of the two assessments is the one that will hold. The Book uses this structure repeatedly in its arguments about idolatry, and it appears again in the same sūrah when it tells the reader that God knows what is in the breasts of all beings.

The Book leaves the point where it stands rather than drawing out the consequence. Delay turns the same sentence into a warning to those who live in safety and into a comfort to anyone waiting for relief, and both readings are available from one line of text. What the verse rules out is the assumption that the arrangement is neutral: a power that holds everything and knows everything is either a reason to be careful or a reason to be patient, and the reader is left to decide which.

**The Idols That Cannot Hear**

Other chapters supply the reasoning that stands behind this brief verdict. Abraham asks his father why he worships what can neither hear nor see nor benefit him at all (19:42), and the Book tells the Prophet to ask the disbelievers how they can worship besides God those who can neither harm nor benefit them, when God alone is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing (5:76). The incapacity is total: no perception, no agency, no effect of any kind, whether for good or ill. The argument is not that the idols are weak deities but that they do not belong to the category of agents at all, and the verse's claim that they are nothing is the conclusion of that argument stated in its shortest form.

A person who relies on a thing that has no knowledge of him has placed his affairs in a relationship that runs in one direction only. The verse states the fact as part of an argument that runs through the whole sūrah: worship is a transaction with a party who hears, and the Book's practice of answering supplications is unintelligible without a hearer. When God is described as knowing everything, the sentence quietly removes the alternative, since the objects of the town's devotion were being asked for things they could not be informed of.

**The Names That Close the Sentence**

The verse ends with two divine names, and their placement answers the two possible objections to everything that has been said. The Almighty answers the question of power: the One who actually holds the matter is capable of doing what He wills, so the comparison between Him and the objects described is not a comparison within one scale. The All-Wise answers the question of judgement: nothing in His administration is arbitrary, so the exposure of the empty protectors is not an expression of rivalry but a statement of fact. The sūrah has paired these names before, and the pattern suggests that the Book's case against false reliance rests on authority and intelligence together rather than on force alone.

**What the Sentence Implies About the Worshippers**

If the objects invoked are nothing, the labour spent on them is spent on nothing, and the sentence quietly leaves that conclusion standing. The sūrah has already described the outcomes of such wasted effort in its account of the destroyed nations, and it will return to the theme in the verse that follows about parables and who understands them. The people in the parable are not described as victims of a deception imposed on them from outside; the verse simply records what they are doing and who is watching. That the observers of their conduct outlive their objects is the point on which the whole image turns.

The two names answer the two assumptions the sentence has just removed. Power closes the question of escape, since nothing in the heavens or the earth can be out of range of the one who holds it, and wisdom closes the question of fairness, since what the reckoning produces will not be arbitrary. Placing them at the end also keeps the verse from resting on sheer capacity. The sentence tells the reader what kind of power is being described, and the second name is what distinguishes it from the force of an object that happens to be larger than the objects around it.

**A Sentence That Reads Differently in Different Mouths**

The verse is addressed to a Prophet engaged in a dispute, and its confidence is that of a man who has been told the outcome. For the audience in Makkah it functioned as a warning, since it names the fate of everything they were relying on. Read by later communities, it keeps its edge, because the objects of invocation change while the structure of the mistake does not. What the Book consistently supplies in such passages is not an argument that the reader must win in a debate but a fact that the reader may verify in the ruins of the nations described earlier in the chapter. The next verse explains why so few readers act on what the parables show them.

**The Difference Between an Object and a Distraction**

One more distinction belongs to this verse, and it concerns the attitude of the people described. The Book does not say they deny God; the sūrah has already reported that the idolaters acknowledged Him as the maker and the provider while associating others with Him in worship. Their problem was not a missing creator but a crowded ledger, and the sentence's word "besides Him" identifies the specific error. What they call upon in addition to God turns out to be nothing, and the addition is what the verse is examining. It is a narrow charge, and it is sharper for being narrow: everything they have added to their devotion has no counterpart in existence, and the One they acknowledge beside their additions knows precisely that. The Book makes the same case elsewhere when it tells the Prophet to ask the idolaters whether they are informing God of something He does not know in the heavens or the earth (10:18), and when it invites them to invoke those they claim as divine and observe that they have no power to remove harm from them or transfer it to another (17:56). The additions were real only as activities, and activities without objects leave the ledger exactly where it was.
