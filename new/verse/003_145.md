## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:145

> No soul can ever die without Allah’s Will at the destined time. Those who desire worldly gain, We will let them have it, and those who desire heavenly reward, We will grant it to them. And We will reward those who are grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Death and Its Appointed Term**

The verse states that no soul dies except by the permission of God and at a term that has been fixed. It follows the announcement of the Prophet's mortality with the principle that governs every death, and the two verses belong together.

The Book states the same teaching in many places. One passage declares that every soul will taste death, and another declares that everything is bound to perish except God. The sentence in this verse adds the element of appointment: the time of a person's death is set, and no human precaution changes it.

**The Enjoined Term and the Implicit Verb**

Al-Ṭabarī noted that the phrase rendered as an enjoined term works with an implied verb, so that the sense is that God has decreed a term that is fixed. The construction is one of the ways the Book compresses a clause by leaving out what the listener will supply.

The doctrine is stated plainly in the sūrah of the Iron and elsewhere, where the Book tells people that no misfortune befalls them without being written before it happens. The purpose of such statements in the Book is consistently practical: they remove from people's conduct the calculations that fear and greed produce, and the verses that follow in this passage apply that removal to the case of fighting.

**Two Desires and Two Outcomes**

The verse contrasts those who desire the reward of this world with those who desire the reward of the next, and it states that each will be given what he seeks. The Book makes the same contrast in several passages, and the wording there is often more pointed than it is here, with the worldly seekers receiving their portion in this life and having no share in the next.

One passage states that whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter will have it increased for him, while whoever desires only the harvest of this world will be given some of it but will have no share in the Hereafter. Another states that whoever desires the fading world alone will be hastened toward whatever is willed for him and then consigned to the fire. The contrast in this verse is stated with a similar economy, and it addresses the motives that were in play when the fighting began.

**The Spoils and the Wider Meaning**

Al-Rāzī read the reference to those who desire the reward of this world as pointing to people whose motive in the fighting was spoil, glory, and reputation. Al-Qurṭūbī read the sentence more broadly, as a statement about human motives in general.

The wider reading suits the verse's place in the sūrah. The community had left a position for the sake of what could be gathered on the field, and the clause names the kind of desire that produced that decision without accusing anyone in particular. The sentence's general form is deliberate: it gives the community a way to examine its own motives whenever it takes up a cause.

**Courage, Fear, and What They Change**

Al-Rāzī drew from the verse a lesson about fear and courage, observing that a person's anxiety and his cowardice do not preserve him and that the term of his life is not in his hands. The conclusion he drew is that a person should act for what is good without letting the fear of death govern him.

The argument is precisely the one the verse supports. If the appointment is fixed, then calculation of the risk cannot extend a single life, and the only variable left is the quality of what a person does with the time he has. The Book's commands about fighting are grounded on this reasoning throughout, and the sentence before the reader states the premise in one line.

**The Hypocrites' Argument Silenced**

The commentary tradition noted that the verse also closes an argument that the hypocrites among the community were using. Their claim was that the men who died at Uḥud would not have died if they had stayed home, and they used it to discourage the believers from taking part in further fighting.

The Book answers the argument directly elsewhere by telling the men who said it about their brothers to try to avoid death themselves if what they said was true. The principle in this verse is the deeper answer: the term is fixed, and remaining behind provides no more protection than going out does. The two statements work together to remove the calculation from the community's thinking.

**A Report About Three Men**

The tradition preserves a report that bears on the verse's contrast between motives. Abū Hurayrah related that among the first people whose cases are decided on the Day of Judgment is a man who died as a martyr, who is made to recount the blessings he received and admits them, and who is told that he fought so that it might be said he was brave, and that it was said (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1905a).

The same report describes a man who acquired knowledge and recited the Book, and a man who was given wealth and spent it, and each is told that his motive was the reputation he gained rather than the pleasure of God. The report makes the verse's distinction concrete by showing that the same act can belong to either category according to the intention behind it, and the community's literature has used it for that purpose ever since.

**The Verse's Closing Promise**

The verse ends with the statement that God will reward the thankful, the same clause that closed the sentence about the Prophet's mortality in the verse before it. The repetition ties the two sentences together and identifies the quality that carries a person through both the fear of death and the temptation of gain.

The Book's treatment of gratitude elsewhere describes it as the perception of the source of what one has, and it promises increase to those who give thanks. In this passage the word has a specific force: the people who acknowledge that their lives and their cause belong to God are the ones whose conduct holds steady when the days turn against them, and the sūrah closes the account of the battle on that note.
