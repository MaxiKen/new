## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:140

> Or do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants were all Jews or Christians?” Say, “Who is more knowledgeable: you or Allah?” Who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah? And Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim About the Patriarchs**

The verse reports a claim that the ancestors were Jews or Christians, and it asks the claimants to consider what they are asserting. The figures named lived long before the scriptures and the communities that bear those names existed, so the claim assigns them a religious identity that came into being centuries later.

The Book addresses the same point in another chapter when it asks the People of the Book why they dispute about Abraham when the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until after him. The question there and the question here belong to one argument, and the sūrah states it in the form of a choice between two claims about the same past.

**Retroactive Identity**

What the verse identifies is the practice of reading earlier figures into a later community's self-definition. The patriarchs had been made into members of communities that did not exist in their lifetimes, and the purpose of the retroactive reading was to give those communities a claim on the greatest names in the tradition.

The Book's answer separates the person from the label. Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, as the sūrah states elsewhere, but one who submitted in uprightness and was not a polytheist. What he practised was the religion described in the earlier verses of this passage, and the labels that came later describe communities that formed around the same tradition at different times.

**"Who Is More Knowledgeable: You or Allah?"**

The question is put in the form of a comparison, and its force lies in the fact that the answer is already known. The claimants were offering an account of the past on the strength of their own tradition, while God's account of the same past had been given in revelation.

The Book uses questions of this kind when a claim about history has no support and the alternatives are unequal. The reader is asked to weigh two sources, and the sentence states that the matter is settled by the comparison rather than by the number of people who hold a position. The question is the Book's way of saying that the record belongs to the One who knows what happened.

**Concealing the Testimony**

The verse then describes the wrongdoing of those who hide the testimony they received from God, and the term for testimony carries the sense of a witness borne to something known. The commentators explained it in two ways: as the religion brought by the Prophet ﷺ, which the earlier scriptures are said to have announced, and as God's own testimony that these prophets were not members of communities that arose later.

Under either reading the offence is the same. The claimants possessed material that answered their own question, and they withheld it because the answer did not suit the position they had taken. The sūrah has made this charge repeatedly in the passage, and the verse brings it to the point of the dispute.

**The Seriousness of the Offence**

The question about who does greater wrong places the concealment of testimony alongside the worst conduct the Book describes. The sūrah has used the same form for obstructing worship and for distorting scripture, and the reader learns that suppressing what one knows about God is treated as a crime of the first order.

The Book's vocabulary for the offence is consistent across the sūrah: the forgers wrote with their own hands, the group that heard God's word altered it knowingly, and those who were given the Book cast it behind their backs. The verse here names the motive for all of those actions, which is the refusal to let a known truth affect one's standing.

**The Closing Attribute**

The sentence ends with a statement that God is never unaware of what the people being addressed do, and the sūrah attaches this attribute to several of its charges against this community. The attribute removes the possibility that concealment succeeds; what is hidden is known, and the conduct of the hiders is recorded.

The repetition of the attribute at the close of so many verses in this passage gives the reader a sense of completeness. Each account of the community's behaviour ends with the same assurance about God's knowledge, so that the passage functions as a series of statements made in the presence of the one who sees.

**What the Verse Asks the Reader to Do**

The verse is addressed to the disputants, and its argument is available to every reader who holds a tradition. What it asks is that a person examine whether the position he holds about the past can be supported by what he actually knows, and whether the answer has been adjusted to protect a claim. The Book's standard here is not the intensity of a person's conviction but the honesty of his account of the evidence.

**The Shape of the Argument**

The sentence moves from the historical claim to the question about knowledge, then to the charge of concealment, and it closes with the statement about God's awareness. The Book builds the case in that order so that the reader sees the claim, the test, and the offence in a single sequence, and the passage that follows returns to the community that has passed away and to the principle that each party is answerable for its own deeds.

**The Consistency of the Passage**

The verse is one of a series in which the sūrah examines the claims of the two communities about the patriarchs, and each examination uses material the communities themselves accepted. The Book appeals to chronology, to testimony, and to the record of what the ancestors said, and it does so without needing to introduce a new authority. The reader is shown how the argument looks when it is conducted on the opponent's own ground.
