## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:140

> Or do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants were all Jews or Christians?” Say, “Who is more knowledgeable: you or Allah?” Who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah? And Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Claim the Calendar Refutes**

The verse puts the sectarian argument of the preceding verses to its simplest test: were Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes Jews or Christians? The question is not rhetorical decoration — it is a chronology. Āl ʿImrān presses the same point with the calendar made explicit: *"O People of the Book! Why do you argue about Abraham, while the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him? Do you not understand?"* (3:65). The Torah is the book of Moses; the Gospel is the book of Jesus; both came centuries after the patriarchs lived and died. To call Abraham a Jew is to attach him to a law he never received; to call him a Christian is to attach him to a gospel he never heard. The verse's own conclusion, stated in the companion sūrah, is the flat denial: *"Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian; he submitted in all uprightness and was not a polytheist"* (3:67). The labels arrived late, and the patriarchs predate them all.

**"Who Is More Knowledgeable: You or Allah?"**

Between the question and the condemnation the verse inserts what may be the sharpest sentence in the whole passage. Who has better knowledge — you, or God? The question admits exactly two answers, and both are fatal. If God knows better, then His testimony about who Abraham was stands, and the claimants are overruled by the only authority competent to speak. If the claimants know better, they have set their knowledge above God's — a position no believing Jew or Christian can occupy without abandoning his own creed. The force of the sentence lies in its brevity: it does not argue the chronology again or cite the patriarchs' practices; it simply forces the opponent to choose between deferring to divine knowledge and claiming to exceed it. Every dispute about revelation eventually arrives at this fork, and the verse's placement of it here makes it the hinge between the historical argument and the moral charge that follows.

**Words That Did Not Yet Exist**

The anachronism can be sharpened with the history of the labels themselves. "Jew" derives from Judah, the son of Jacob whose name passed to his tribe, then to the southern kingdom, then to the religious community that took shape around the Torah centuries after Moses — the identity is downstream of events Abraham could not have witnessed. "Christian" is younger still: the book of Acts records that it was in Antioch that the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:26) — a name coined by outsiders, in a city, in the generation after Jesus, more than a millennium after Abraham's death. Neither word existed while the patriarchs lived, and neither community's founding document had been revealed when they died. The Qur'an's question — were they Jews or Christians — is therefore not a theological puzzle but a factual impossibility, and the fact that both sects nonetheless claimed the patriarchs shows what the claims were really doing: not reporting history but annexing it.

**A Testimony Hidden From Allah**

The charge that follows names the mechanism of the annexation: who does more wrong than those who hide a testimony they hold from Allah? The *shahādah* — testimony — is understood by the exegetes in two converging senses. It is the testimony God placed in their own scriptures: the descriptions of the coming prophet and the records showing that the patriarchs lived before the sectarian identities existed, so that the Qur'an testifies to the same truths their books uphold. And it is God's own testimony, borne in revelation, that these prophets were neither Jewish nor Christian. Concealing either is the same act, and it is the act the sūrah has been building toward since its early addresses: do not mix truth with falsehood or hide the truth knowingly (2:42); why do you mix the truth with falsehood and hide it knowingly (3:71)? The wrongdoer superlative — who does *more* wrong — marks concealment of known testimony as a peak category of injustice, because it injures not the concealer but everyone he might have guided.

**Why the Patriarchs Were Worth the Fight**

The intensity of the verse is proportionate to the stakes. Abraham is the founding figure of monotheism itself; whoever owns him owns the legitimacy that flows from him. Both sects claimed him because both needed their faith to be primordial rather than novel, and the claim to Abraham was the claim to be the original religion. The Qur'an's answer does not merely deny the claim; it relocates the inheritance. The people closest to Abraham, in Āl ʿImrān's formulation, are those who followed him, and this prophet, and those who believe (3:68) — proximity by following, not by labeling. That is the substance of the ḥanīf answer two groups of verses back (2:135): the patriarch's legacy belongs to whoever walks his road of upright devotion, and it is forfeited by whoever merely files him under a party name he never knew.

**"And Allah Is Never Unaware"**

The verse closes with the refrain of surveillance: Allah is never unaware of what you do. The concealment charge and the closing line interlock. Hiding testimony works only where witnesses can be managed — where the audience does not know what was hidden, and the hider can count on the gap. The refrain annuls the gap: the One from whom the testimony comes is watching the act of concealment, and the case file is complete on His side even when the public record is doctored on theirs. The same clause ends the qiblah verdicts that follow in this passage (2:144, 2:149), which is why the refrain functions as the passage's signature: every dispute about who is guided, who owns Abraham, and which direction is true is being conducted in full view of a Judge who cannot be deprived of evidence. For the concealer it is a warning with no appeal; for the community accused and outargued, it is the assurance that its case does not depend on winning the argument in the street.
