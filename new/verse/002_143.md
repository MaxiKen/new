## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:143

> And so We have made you ˹believers˺ an upright community so that you may be witnesses over humanity and that the Messenger may be a witness over you. We assigned your former direction of prayer only to distinguish those who would remain faithful to the Messenger from those who would lose faith. It was certainly a difficult test except for those ˹rightly˺ guided by Allah. And Allah would never discount your ˹previous acts of˺ faith. Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wasaṭ: The Middle That Means the Best**

The community is made *ummatan wasaṭan* — a middle community, rendered here "upright" — and the middle in Arabic idiom is not the mediocre but the choicest. A ḥadīth cited by the commentators reports the Prophet explaining "middle" as "just," and a saying current enough that some counted it a ḥadīth declares the choicest part of anything to be its middle. The social image behind the idiom is concrete: when a people gather around their leader, he stands at the center, so to name a man the middle of his people is to name him one of their finest. Al-Rāzī adds that the *wasaṭ* of anything is its choice part. The verse therefore confers a rank, not a location: this community is placed at the just center of the religious possibilities ranged around it, and the center is where the best of each extreme survives and the distortion of each is refused.

**The Ethics of the Center**

The commentators worked out what the middle means in practice, and their examples are the classic pairs. Courage is the mean between recklessness and cowardice; generosity is the mean between prodigality and miserliness — virtue as the disciplined midpoint of two failures. Applied to the religious traditions, the same schema yields the community's defining claim: where Judaism is read as emphasizing law and Christianity as emphasizing love and mercy, Islam is presented as holding the balance between them; where one tendency collapses into the external observance alone and another into the interior alone, the middle community keeps exoteric and esoteric together, this world and the Hereafter in one ledger. Some extended the image to geography, noting the lands of Islam across the middle belt of the inhabited world. The theological core is the first pairing: the center is a discipline, maintained against the pull of every excess, and it is what qualifies the community for the office the verse next names.

**Center of the Earth, Center of the Peoples**

The opening word "and so" — *kadhalika* — ties this rank to what preceded it, and the commentators read the tie two ways. Either: just as the Kaʿbah is the center of the earth, so you are the middle people — the community's position among nations mirroring its House's position among places. Or: just as We guided you to the straight path, so We made you a middle community — the uprightness of the creed producing the uprightness of the collective. Both readings converge on a single claim: centrality is given, not achieved. The community did not earn the middle by negotiation between the sects it declined to join; it was made middle by the same act that assigned its direction of prayer. The verse's own logic supports this — the very next sentence describes God assigning the qiblah as an instrument of His knowing and testing — so the community's character and its compass are one gift, dispensed together.

**Witnesses Over Humanity**

The purpose of the middle position is an office: *so that you may be witnesses over humanity and that the Messenger may be a witness over you*. To be a witness is to hold real standing in the judgment of a thing, by virtue of knowing it — the same weight the Qur'an gives bodily testimony on the Last Day, when hands speak and feet testify to what their owners earned (36:65). The commentators connect the office to the Day of Judgment directly: this community will testify to the faithfulness of earlier prophets in delivering their missions, even where those prophets' own peoples will not confirm it — the witness structure of 4:41, where a witness is brought from every community and the Messenger as witness over his. The sūrah of the Pilgrimage states the same arrangement in nearly the same words: *"so that the Messenger may be a witness over you, and that you may be witnesses over humanity"* (22:78). A chain of testimony runs through the office: God's knowledge to the Messenger, the Messenger's life to the community, the community's account to the nations.

**Allah's Witnesses on Earth**

The witnessing is not wholly deferred to the Last Day. In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (1367), Anas ibn Mālik narrates that a funeral procession passed and the people spoke well of the deceased, and the Prophet said, "It has been affirmed." Another passed and the people spoke ill, and again he said, "It has been affirmed." When ʿUmar asked what had been affirmed, the Prophet answered: this one you praised, so Paradise is affirmed for him, and that one you spoke ill of, so Hell is affirmed for him — *you people are Allah's witnesses on earth*. The report shows the community's witness-function operating at the scale of a single funeral: the collective judgment of those who knew a life carries evidentiary weight before God. Read against 2:143, the ḥadīth is the earthly installment of the eschatological office — the middle community testifies now, in its streets, and will testify then, over humanity.

**The Qiblah as the Sorting Instrument**

The verse then explains what the change of direction was for: *We assigned your former direction of prayer only to distinguish those who would remain faithful to the Messenger from those who would lose faith*. Al-Ṭabarī preserves the reactions the test extracted. The hypocrites jeered: what is wrong with you? You adopt one direction, then you swap it. The Jews read the turn toward Mecca as nostalgia: Muhammad longs for his homeland; had he stayed with our direction, they said, we would have taken him for the awaited one. The Meccan idolaters drew the opposite comfort: Muhammad has wavered in his faith. And within the community itself some asked the anxious question the verse answers: what about our brothers who died facing Jerusalem — what of their prayers? To all of it the verse responds at once: the direction was never the point; it was the instrument of separation, and God would never discount your faith — the prayers already offered toward the old qiblah remain valid, believed, and recorded. The test was hard, the verse concedes — except for those God guided.

**"That We May Know"**

The phrase "only to distinguish" renders a construction that says literally *that We may know* — and the theologians handled it with care, because God's knowledge is eternal and nothing can add to it. Al-Ṭabarī records the readings. The first: "so that We may observe" — knowledge of a thing as existing requires the thing to exist, and God knows eternally what will happen but observes it happening in time; the trial produces the known thing, not the knowledge. The second: "so that My friends and partisans may know" — actions are attributed to a leader whose followers perform them, as people say ʿUmar conquered Jerusalem though he never besieged it; the divine first person covers the community's own seeing. Al-Rāzī adds a third with a pastoral motive: "so that you will all know," with God's use of the first person softening the verdict — *We shall know* falls less harshly than *you will know* when what is known is someone's disbelief. The underlying problem is the one the Qur'an poses wherever it speaks of testing — do people think they will be left to say "we believe" without being tried (29:2) — and the answer is the same: the outcome is known to God before the test, and the test is real anyway, because justice requires the deed to exist, not merely to be foreseen. The verse closes on the character of the Tester: Ever Gracious, Most Merciful to humanity — the sorting is severe, and the sorter is kind.
