## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:2

> Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him —the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence That Clears the Field First**

The line has three movements, and the order matters. First the name alone; then the removal of every rival — a denial that is stated before anything is affirmed about the one who remains; then the two attributes that explain why He can be trusted with the whole of existence.

*“He is the Ever-Living. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. So call upon Him with sincere devotion, ˹saying,˺ ‘All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds.’”* (40:65). The same architecture appears at 2:255, the verse of the Seat, and at 112:1–2 in the shortest possible form: *“He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺; Allah—the Sustainer ˹needed by all˺.”* (112:1–2). The Qur'an does not introduce God by arguing with the objects people worship. It states who He is, and the rivals fall away as a consequence of the description.

This is the sūrah's first sentence after the letters, and the sūrah will spend much of its length in dispute — with the People of the Book, with the hesitant inside Madinah, with those looking for signs. Fixing the identity of God at the door means no later argument in the sūrah can be read as a merely local quarrel over community boundaries.

**Al-Ḥayy: Life That Was Never Given**

*Al-Ḥayy* says more than "living". Every living thing in the sentence's shadow received its life as a loan; God did not. His life is not a quality added to Him at some point or maintained by anything outside Him, which is why the verse that calls Him *al-Ḥayy* calls Him at the same time imperishable: *“Put your trust in the Ever-Living, Who never dies, and glorify His praises. Sufficient is He as All-Aware of the sins of His servants.”* (25:58).

The Qur'an draws the practical conclusion from the name rather than leaving it as a metaphysical decoration. If life cannot be drained from Him, then the one who leans on Him is leaning on something that cannot fail — not because He is strong, but because the failing He would have to undergo does not exist in Him. The declaration at 20:111 turns the same name into an image of the last hour: *“And all faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. And those burdened with wrongdoing will be in loss.”* (20:111). The proud faces of this life will be lowered in front of the name in the next.

**Al-Qayyūm: The One Who Stands and Holds Others Up**

*Al-Qayyūm* is built on the verb *qāma*, to stand, in an intensive form, so the name carries two directions at once: He stands by Himself, owing His existence to no one, and He keeps everything else standing. The translation "All-Sustaining" follows the second direction; the first is not lost in the Arabic.

The Qur'an spells the job out in the verse of the Seat: to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and *“the preservation of both does not tire Him. For He is the Most High, the Greatest.”* (2:255). The statement is an answer to a very human worry. If the universe were held up by something that could weary, everything in it would be living on borrowed stability, and the borrowed kind eventually runs out. The two names together cover what a creature cannot supply for itself: life, and support under the weight of being alive.

**Why the Two Names Are the Sūrah's Foundation**

Nothing in this sūrah can be understood without the pair. Claims will be made about a prophet born without a father, about a people who received books and then disputed, about wealth and children as securities, about death as a bridge rather than a wall. Each of those subjects is a question about what sustains what. The answer stands in the second verse: there is a Living who was not given life and a Sustaining who is not carried.

That is why the sūrah repeats the clause. At 3:18 the same words return with a body of witnesses attached, and the repetition is the sūrah's way of holding its own line. A text that says the same thing twice in eighteen verses is telling the reader which sentence the rest of the argument is standing on.

**Two Names, Two Human Needs**

Put the pair beside the human condition and its coverage becomes clear. A person is weak and needs strength; a person is dependent and needs support. The first name addresses the first need, the second the second, and the order in which they are given is the order in which the needs are felt: first the fear of dissolving, then the fear of falling alone.

The rest of the Book handles the same pair from the side of the creature. The Prophet is told that the mind should not treat God as a requirement of the intellect but as the ground of it, and the servant is told to *“put your trust in the Ever-Living, Who never dies”* (25:58) precisely when everything else can be taken away. Whatever is written later in this sūrah about loss — of wealth, of children, of victory — has already been answered here in a sentence of four words.

One further observation belongs to the history of reading. These two names sit at the head of a sūrah in which Muslims, Christians, and Jews disagree about the identity of God and about who may speak for Him — and the Book begins by settling the question without referring to any of the parties. The names answer no controversialist and address no denomination. They establish a fact about God that no tradition in the conversation can revise, and they leave the coming dispute to be judged against that fact rather than against the standing of the disputants.
