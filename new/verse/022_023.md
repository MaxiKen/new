## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:23

> ˹But˺ Allah will surely admit those who believe and do good into Gardens, under which rivers flow, where they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls, and their clothing will be silk,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Is Built as a Mirror of Verses 19 and 20**

The passage has been describing the disbelievers' sentence: garments of fire cut to measure, scalding water poured over the head, melting the insides and the skin. Verse 23 answers item by item.

Where they had garments cut out for them, these have garments of silk. Where boiling water ran over their heads, these have rivers running beneath them. Where their skins were melted, these are adorned with gold and pearl. The symmetry is exact and it is the whole design of the passage: two destinations described in the same vocabulary, so that the reader measures the difference rather than being told about it.

This is why the sūrah gave the punishment in such physical detail. It was never description for its own sake; it was the setting-up of a contrast, and the contrast is the point.

**Gold and Pearl**

*Yuḥallawna fīhā min asāwira min dhahabin wa-luʾluʾan* — they are adorned in it with bracelets of gold, and pearl. The verb is passive: they are adorned. Nobody in the Garden decorates himself.

The two materials carry a history. Gold, in this world, is what a person hoards; pearl is what a person dives for at risk. Neither is useful — you cannot eat gold and a pearl is a wound that hardened — and both are valued precisely because they are useless and hard to get. That is what makes them ornaments, and the verse puts them on the inhabitants as ornaments rather than handing them over as assets. The transfer from possession to decoration is the difference between wealth and honour.

The parallel descriptions elsewhere confirm the register: *“They will enter the Gardens of Eternity, where they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls, and their clothing will be silk”* (35:33), and in the fuller account, *“There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade, reclining there on ˹canopied˺ couches”* (18:31).

**Silk**

*Wa-libāsuhum fīhā ḥarīr* — and their clothing in it is silk. The word is *ḥarīr*, and the same fabrics are named in the descriptions of the Garden elsewhere: *“dressed in fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another”* (44:53), and *“The virtuous will be ˹dressed˺ in garments of fine green silk and rich brocade, and adorned with bracelets of silver”* (76:21).

Two observations are worth making. The first is that clothing in the Qur'an is consistently the outward sign of an inward state, which is why the garments of fire in verse 19 and the silk in this verse are doing the same work in opposite directions. The second is that the material is described by texture rather than by cut or colour. What is promised is how it feels against the skin — the exact inversion of a punishment delivered to the skin.

**Why the Reward Is Described in the Language of This World**

The obvious philosophical difficulty is that a spiritual reward is being described in material terms, and the obvious answer is the one the tradition gives: the Qur'an can only describe what it has vocabulary for. A pleasure never experienced cannot be named, so the text names the nearest known thing and marks the description as approximate.

There is a second reason, though, and this sūrah has earned it. A sūrah that has argued for the resurrection of the body from embryos and dry fields cannot then describe the reward in terms that have nothing to do with bodies. The consistency is deliberate: the same creature that was raised is the creature that is dressed.

The descriptions are therefore neither literal in the flat sense nor merely symbolic. They are the truth about a state, rendered in the only images available, and addressed to a creature who will have a body when he gets there.

**How the Qur'an Builds These Pairs**

The matching-description technique used here is not unique to this sūrah, and recognizing it elsewhere sharpens the reading here. Sūrah al-Dukhān puts the two destinations four verses apart using the same method: for the punished, *“Like molten metal, it will boil in the bellies, like the boiling of hot water”* (44:45–46); for the rewarded, *“dressed in fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another”* (44:53). Belly and silk, in that order.

The consistency of the method tells us something about what the descriptions are for. They are not reports filed from another world; they are a rhetorical instrument for making a decision visible. A reader who is told only that the godly are rewarded and the wicked punished has received information. A reader who sees the garment of fire and then the garment of silk has been shown a difference he can hold in his mind, and the Qur'an prefers the second.

It also guards against a common error. Because the two descriptions are built from the same materials, no reader can suppose that one of them is literal and the other figurative. They stand or fall together.

**"Admit" — the Verb Returns**

*Yudkhilu* — He causes them to enter. The same verb the sūrah used in verse 14, in the same form, with the same condition attached: those who believe and do sound deeds.

The repetition settles a question that the preceding verses might have raised. The punishment of verses 19 to 22 was described almost entirely in the passive — garments cut, water poured, no agent named — which leaves a reader wondering who is doing this. The reward is not left in that form. God is named as the one who brings them in, and the asymmetry is intentional: judgment is the machinery, mercy has an author.

That is also why the verse's conditions have not changed since verse 14. Belief and sound action, and entry as something done to you by the One who wills it.
