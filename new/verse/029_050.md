## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:50

> They say, “If only ˹some˺ signs had been sent down to him from his Lord!” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Signs are only with Allah. And I am only sent with a clear warning.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Demand That Returns in Every Chapter**

The request recorded here appears again and again in the Book, which suggests that it was a settled feature of the dispute rather than a passing remark. The disbelievers repeatedly call for a miraculous sign to confirm the messenger: they ask why no sign has been sent down to him (10:20; 13:7), they demand that he produce a spring from the ground and a garden of palms and grapes (17:90–91), and Sūrah Ṭā Hā records them demanding a sign from his Lord even while noting that they had already received the clear confirmation of earlier scriptures (20:133). The Book gathers all these challenges into the single sentence of this verse, and its answer is equally compact. The demand was not new, and neither was the response to it.

**What the Demand Actually Asks For**

Behind the request lies an assumption about who controls the demonstration. The challengers propose a test, expect it to be administered on their terms, and reserve to themselves the decision about what would constitute proof. The Book treats that arrangement as the heart of the matter, and it answers by relocating the authority to grant signs from the messenger to God. Nothing in the reply disputes the audience's ability to evaluate evidence, and nothing in it denies that signs have been given to earlier prophets. What it denies is the premise that a messenger carries a supply of miracles which he can distribute on request, and that a refusal to do so indicates a shortage of evidence.

The request is also more specific than it appears. The audience is not asking for an argument it can examine; it wants a display that would end the discussion without requiring anything of it, and the demand recurs in the Book precisely where the case has already been made and the hearers have declined to act on it.

**"Signs Are Only With God"**

The phrase places the signs in the treasuries of the unseen, and the Book states the point more fully elsewhere in the instruction given to the Prophet to say that he does not claim to possess God's treasuries or to know the unseen, and that he only follows what is revealed to him (6:50). The sentence therefore refuses a promotion. The messenger is not offered the status of a wonder-worker whose arrival can be certified by performance; he is given a message and told to deliver it. The Book's insistence on this division of labour runs through its accounts of every prophet, and it protects both the messenger and the audience from a relationship built on spectacle rather than on submission.

**Why the Signs Were Not Simply Provided**

The Book gives its reasoning in another chapter: nothing prevents God from sending the requested signs except that earlier peoples denied them, and it cites the example of Thamūd, which was given a she-camel as a clear sign and wrongfully rejected it, adding that signs are sent only as a warning (17:59). The logic is uncomfortable and precise. A demonstration that arrives on demand, and is refused, leaves the audience with nothing except a heavier account to settle, and the Book treats the withholding of further spectacle as restraint rather than as withholding of evidence. The signs already provided were of that kind: the she-camel was clear, and the people who rejected it did so after seeing it.

**"I Am Only Sent With a Clear Warning"**

The title the verse assigns the Prophet is one the Book repeats in many places: a warner, and nothing more in the way of status (22:49; 35:23; 38:70; 46:9; 67:26). The adjective attached to the warning in this verse describes both its content and its tone. The message is clear, which means it does not require a wonder to be understood, and its function is to alert rather than to compel. A warner who could force acceptance would not be warning anyone; he would be operating a gate. The Book's conception of the prophetic office keeps the messenger's role within the bounds of announcement, and the sentence under discussion is one of its most economical statements of the boundary.

The reply also defines the messenger's jurisdiction. He delivers what he was given and answers for the delivery, while the production of signs rests with the one who chose when to send them, and the closing verses of the sūrah depend on that division being understood by the people making the demand.

**The Evidence That Was Already in Circulation**

The demand in this verse is answered elsewhere by pointing to what the audience was already holding. The Book asks those who want a sign whether the clear verses they have received are not sufficient for people of firm faith (2:118), and it presents the revelation itself as the standing demonstration that accompanies the messenger wherever he goes. The verse before us stops short of naming that answer, and the sentence that follows it in the sūrah supplies the reply in full. The pause between the two is where the argument lives. A community that has been reciting a book for years is in a different position from an audience that has heard nothing, and the Book treats its own recitation as the sufficient sign for the situation.

The recitation itself was the evidence under discussion, delivered in the town's own language by a man the audience had known for decades, and the Book asks its readers to consider what kind of proof could be required beyond something they were already able to hear and understand.

**What the Challenge Reveals About the Challengers**

The request for a sign is not a request for information, and the Book's handling of it shows that its authors understood the difference. A person who wants to believe in a message can examine the message; a person who wants to hold a verdict in reserve asks for something the messenger cannot supply. The Book's answer is not contempt for evidence; it records that earlier communities received signs of the most concrete kind and refused them anyway, and it draws the conclusion that the demand was never about proof. What the sūrah's opponents were seeking was a bargaining position, and the sentence removes it without rudeness and without conceding anything.

A demand of this kind also reveals what the challengers thought a prophet was for. Their request assumes a messenger who arrives with a spectacle and settles matters by force of display, whereas the Book presents messengers who argue, warn, and leave the response to the people standing in front of them.

**The Difference Between a Warning and a Compulsion**

A clear warning respects the person who receives it, and the verse's choice of that description over the language of proof or demonstration is exact. A warning supplies a person with what he needs to make a decision and then leaves the decision with him. An irresistible sign would do the opposite: it would remove the possibility of refusal and with it the possibility of the loyalty the Book is asking for. The sūrah has been teaching its readers since its first verses that faith is tested, and a test administered in the form of a spectacle is no test at all. What the Prophet is told to announce is therefore the narrowest claim: a message, a summons, and a consequence for those who ignore the summons.
