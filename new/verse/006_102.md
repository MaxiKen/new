## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:102

> That is Allah—your Lord! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. ˹He is˺ the Creator of all things, so worship Him ˹alone˺. And He is the Maintainer of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conclusion Drawn From the Argument**

That is Allah, your Lord! The sentence gathers the whole preceding account of the created order and attaches a name to it, and the Book's demonstrative form is the same one it used when it described the splitting of the seed and asked how the audience could be deluded (6:95). The verse identifies the subject of the signs: the One who originated, provided, guided, and knew, and it states the identity as a conclusion rather than as a new claim.

The passage has been building to this sentence for several verses. The sūrah described the sky, the human origin, the rain and the harvest, and then answered the claims about the jinn and about offspring, and the verse in front of us states what the audience should have concluded from all of it. The Book's method is to let the accumulation of evidence produce a declaration, and the declaration comes at the end.

**The Formula of Exclusive Worship**

There is no god worthy of worship except Him. The sentence is the Book's central confession, and it appears in this sūrah in the context of an audience that had many gods. The formula does not merely reduce the number; it denies the category, since the Book's claim is that everything the audience called divine lacked the standing the word implies.

The Book attaches the same formula to its conclusions about the created order in other passages, and it uses it as the boundary of the community's identity. The sūrah has already used it twice in its first hundred verses, and its reappearance here marks the end of a section of argument rather than the beginning of one.

**The Creator Who Is Worshipped**

He is the Creator of all things, so worship Him alone. The verse moves from the fact of creation to the command to worship, and the connection is the sūrah's persistent argument. The Makkans already accepted that God had created everything; their error lay in the conclusion they drew from the fact, and the verse states what the conclusion should be.

The Book makes the same connection in the sūrah of the Troops, where it states that God is the Creator of all things and the Maintainer of everything (39:62), and in the sūrah of the Thunder, where the acknowledgement of God as the Creator of all things is followed by the declaration that He is the One, the Supreme (13:16). The command to worship in this verse is the practical form of a fact the audience had already granted.

**The Maintainer of Everything**

And He is the Maintainer of everything. The Book's term describes the one who manages and arranges the affairs entrusted to him, and the sūrah has applied it to God at several points. The sūrah of the Troops uses the same title for God at the head of a passage on provision (39:62); the sūrah of Hūd states that God is the Trustee of all affairs (11:12); and the sūrah of the Confederates says that God is sufficient as a Trustee of affairs (33:3).

Attached to a passage about creation and worship, the title states that the arrangement described in the previous verses was not a single act that has since been left alone. The stars keep their courses, the rain comes, the harvest ripens, and the provisions of every creature arrive because the One who made the world continues to run it, and the verse names that ongoing management in a single phrase.

**Maintenance and the Everyday Evidence of It**

The title the Book attaches to God in this verse appears elsewhere in the context of provision. The sūrah of Fāṭir asks whether there is any creator other than God who provides for the audience from the heavens and the earth, and it follows the question with the same declaration of exclusive divinity that stands in this verse (35:3). The sūrah of Consultation says that God is kind to His servants and provides abundantly for whoever He wills, and it names Him the All-Powerful, the Almighty (42:19). Both passages join the act of providing to the title of maintainer, which is the pairing this verse makes.

The Book treats that pairing as the answer to the audience's practical worries. The sūrah of the Spider states that many creatures cannot secure their own provisions and that God provides for them and for the people who worry about them (29:60), and the sūrah of Hūd says that no creature on earth lacks a provision guaranteed by God and that everything is written in a perfect record (11:6). The Makkans who heard this verse lived from trade caravans and from the produce of the oases, and the Book's claim was that the arrangement behind both was the same arrangement described in the verses about the rain and the harvest.

**What the Verse Means for the Sūrah's Audience**

For the Makkans, the sentence was a direct challenge to their practice. They had their own answer to the question of creation, and the verse takes their answer and draws the conclusion they refused to draw. It does not ask them to accept a new fact about the origin of the world; it asks them to worship the being they already acknowledged as its maker.

The Book's argument in this chapter has proceeded in exactly that way throughout. It asked who provides, who rescues, who gives hearing and sight, and it recorded the audience's answers. The verse in front of us is the conclusion the sūrah has been working toward, and the verses that follow it turn to the question of whether God can be seen and to the responsibility of the people who have been shown the signs.
