## Sūrah an-Naml 27:5

> It is they who will suffer a dreadful torment, and in the Hereafter they will ˹truly˺ be the greatest losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Falls in Two Places**

The verse divides one outcome across two locations, and the division is precise. A dreadful torment is stated without a time attached; the greatest loss is explicitly assigned to the Hereafter. Since the second half specifies the next world, the first half most naturally refers to this one. The people described in the previous verse do not wait for the accounting to begin paying.

Commentators split on how far to press that reading. Al-Rāzī allows the punishment to be general, covering suffering in either world, since the term itself is unqualified. Al-Zamakhsharī prefers a concrete reference and connects it to the defeat of the Quraysh leadership at Badr, a loss suffered by the same men who had been most confident that no reckoning existed. The distributed reading is stronger on grammatical grounds: naming the Hereafter in the second clause would be redundant if the first clause already meant it. Something is being said about now. The chapter itself will supply the evidence later, when Thamūd is destroyed in place and Pharaoh's people are drowned, both before any Day of Judgment arrives.

**A Verdict Written in the Language of Trade**

*Al-akhsarūn* — the greatest losers — comes from *khusr*, a word that belongs to commerce before it belongs to theology. It names the merchant who returns from a venture with less capital than he set out with. Arabic used it for spoiled goods, short measures and failed expeditions, and the Qur'an borrows the term without softening it.

The metaphor runs through the Book and shapes how disbelief is described. Guidance is treated as a commodity a person can trade away: *“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.”* (2:16). The image is exact and unflattering. A man who denies the Hereafter has not made an intellectual error in this framing; he has made a bad deal. He handed over something of permanent value and received something that expires. Al-Ṭabarī reads the superlative here against that background, and the reading fits: calling them the *greatest* losers ranks them within a market where everyone is trading, which is what makes the label sting. They were not passive. They were active, confident, and wrong about the price.

**What Makes This Loss the Largest of All**

A superlative invites the question of what it is measured against. Al-Qurṭubī gives the answer that has been most widely repeated: many people lose in this world and gain in the next, trading comfort now for security later, and that is a good trade. The people described here have made the exchange in reverse. They spent the permanent to purchase the temporary, and when the temporary ends they hold nothing.

The arithmetic explains the superlative without any rhetorical inflation. A man who loses his wealth has lost something replaceable. A man who loses a life has lost something finite. The loss named here is of a different order because the thing forfeited was unlimited and the thing bought was guaranteed to end. There is also a second layer, which the previous verse set up: these are people whose deeds were made to look good to them. They will arrive at the accounting expecting a balance. The Qur'an elsewhere describes exactly this shock — those whose efforts in this life were wasted while they thought they were doing well. The greatest loss is not merely the largest; it is the one least anticipated by the person who suffers it.

**"It Is They" — a Construction That Excludes**

Both halves of the verse use an emphatic Arabic construction, *ulāʾika lahum* and *wa hum*, which the translation renders with *It is they* and *they will ˹truly˺ be*. This is not decoration. The form restricts the predicate to the subject named, in the way English does by stressing a pronoun: *they* are the ones, meaning the others are not.

The restriction is doing real work in a chapter about misread evidence. In the world the Qur'an's first audience lived in, the men who denied the Hereafter were not obviously losing. They held the trade routes, the sanctuary and the social power, and the believers were the ones visibly short of everything. Verse 5 reverses the apparent ledger and does so with grammar rather than argument, refusing to let the category of loser stay attached to where it visibly seemed to belong. Al-Zamakhsharī, who was attentive to these constructions, noted that the emphatic pronoun functions as a correction of an assumed opinion. Somebody had a list of losers. This verse hands back a different one.

**Where the Threat Sits in the Chapter's Design**

Five verses in, the opening block closes. The Book has been named and described, its beneficiaries have been listed by their practice, the opposing party has been diagnosed, and now the consequence is stated. Nothing further is said about this group; the next verse turns to the Prophet and the source of what he is receiving, and after that the chapter moves into narrative for most of its length.

That placement tells the reader how to take the stories that follow. The pattern of arrival and refusal will repeat four times — Mūsā before Pharaoh, Ṣāliḥ before Thamūd, Lūṭ before his people, and the queen of Sheba, who breaks the pattern by submitting. Each episode is a demonstration of the principle this verse states flatly. The threat is announced first in the abstract so that the narratives are not read as ancient history with no bearing on the audience. The chapter states the rule, then spends ninety verses showing it operate.
