## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:175

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Names Close Lot's Account**

After the rescue of Lot's household and the destruction of the rest, the verse names the Lord as the Almighty and Most Merciful. The pairing interprets the entire account. God's power explains why the corrupt community could not protect itself, while His mercy explains why Lot was warned, supported, and delivered.

The refrain prevents the story from becoming either a tale of arbitrary force or a promise that mercy means no judgment. God is mighty enough to punish persistent corruption and merciful enough to provide a path of repentance before judgment arrives. Both names are needed to read the event correctly.


Lot's people threatened to expel him, as if their local authority could determine his safety and future. The Almighty shows that the power of a community is temporary. It can threaten a messenger, but it cannot prevent God from saving him or ending the order that opposes His command.

This title also corrects Lot's own vulnerability. The messenger has no need to pretend that he can manage the outcome by himself. He speaks faithfully and turns to the Lord whose power exceeds the crowd's. Courage grows from dependence on the Almighty, not from an illusion of personal invincibility.

**Mercy Came Before the Rain**

The punishment is preceded by Lot's warning, his call to fear Allah, his refusal of the community's practice, and his prayer for rescue. Mercy was present at every stage. The people were not denied guidance, and Lot was not abandoned to face their hostility without divine knowledge.

The fact that the people rejected mercy does not mean it was absent. A gift can be refused and still remain a gift. The warning was compassionate because it made return possible, even though the community chose to defend what God had forbidden.


Some imagine that a powerful God must be distant from human suffering. The refrain rejects that assumption. The Almighty acts to protect the faithful and to end a public order that harms and threatens. His power is not a cold abstraction; it is exercised with knowledge of the people, the messenger, and the consequences of their choices.

At the same time, compassion does not require leaving injustice untouched. Mercy toward victims includes ending the power of those who persist in wrongdoing. The believer can oppose harm firmly while remembering that he is not God and cannot claim complete knowledge of every heart.

**A Parallel Shows Rescue as Grace**

Quran 54:34 describes the punishment of Lot's people and the delivery of his family before dawn as an act of grace. That parallel makes the two names visible in the narrative. Judgment displays divine power, while deliverance displays mercy granted to those who remained with Lot.

The word grace also prevents the saved household from boasting. They were not preserved because they controlled the city or defeated the crowd. God made a way out. The faithful respond with gratitude, not with the belief that their own strength produced the rescue.


The same pair of names closed the stories of Noah, ʿĀd, and Thamûd. Lot's account belongs to a single pattern of prophetic history. Different communities receive different warnings, but the Lord remains consistent: He sends guidance, allows a response, protects those who obey, and judges persistent rejection.

This continuity also teaches that no age can claim exemption. Lot's people had its own customs and desires, just as earlier nations had theirs. The forms of resistance change, but the Almighty remains above every society and the Most Merciful remains near to those who return.

**Mercy Does Not Mean Approval of the Practice**

A false idea of mercy would ask God to ignore the harm done by the people of Lot. The refrain gives a better definition. Mercy includes warning the community, giving it time, rescuing those who refuse participation, and ending a system that has become destructive.

This protects the believer from confusing compassion with moral surrender. Be patient with people, invite repentance, and avoid cruelty. But do not call the forbidden good merely because correction is uncomfortable. The Most Merciful is also the Almighty whose judgment gives the moral boundary weight.


Those who follow Lot's example need both divine names in their own conduct. Remember the Almighty when a crowd threatens, so that fear of people does not make you compromise. Remember the Most Merciful when correcting others, so that truth does not become an excuse for contempt.

This balance makes moral witness credible. A person who speaks of power without mercy becomes harsh, while a person who speaks of mercy without power becomes permissive. The servant holds firmly to God's command and softly seeks the return of those who have gone astray.


Lot's people have been judged, but the reader is still being invited. The refrain comes after the sign so that later generations can respond before repeating the pattern. The Almighty can end false security, and the Most Merciful can accept a sincere turn while life remains.

The proper response is therefore practical. Fear God, protect the household from corruption, speak truth without cruelty, and seek rescue through obedience rather than through social approval. The story closes with the Lord whose power and mercy make every warning meaningful.

The names also teach the believer how to speak about punishment. Do not deny that God judges, and do not speak as though judgment belongs to your anger. Let the Almighty make you brave before pressure and let the Most Merciful make you gentle toward those who can still repent. Truth without mercy becomes pride; mercy without truth becomes surrender.

Lot's account therefore closes not with a fascination for catastrophe but with a discipline for living. Receive warnings early, guard the household, refuse corrupt belonging, and trust God with the outcome. The One who saved the faithful is powerful enough to preserve obedience in every age.
