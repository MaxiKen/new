## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:228

> Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can remarry˺. It is not lawful for them to conceal what Allah has created in their wombs, if they ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day. And their husbands reserve the right to take them back within that period if they desire reconciliation. Women have rights similar to those of men equitably, although men have a degree ˹of responsibility˺ above them. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Waiting Legislated**

The verse opens the divorce-code with its central institution: the divorced women wait — three qurūʾ, three courses — before the divorce is final and remarriage possible. The waiting period, the ʿiddah, is the type of divorce the verse governs: the ṭalāq, declared by the husband — a period in which marital intimacy is suspended and the declaration stays reversible. The jurists' first question concerned the course itself — where the course's boundary lies: the flow itself, its stopping, or the full ablution that reopens prayer — a disagreement recorded by the commentators and left, characteristically, as a school-level detail. The waiting's functions are multiple and the verse legislates them in order. It is a period of clarity: the marriage's end is not instantaneous but processual, giving the decision time against the heat that may have produced it. It is a period of possibility: the reconciliation the clause that follows preserves. And it is a period of knowledge: the womb's truth established before any new marriage begins — the paternity-function the next clause addresses directly.

**The Womb's Truth**

The second clause legislates the waiting's evidentiary heart: the divorced woman may not hide what her womb contains — a pregnancy possibly begun — and the prohibition is enforced at the level of faith itself: belief in God and the Last Day is named as its sanction. The clause's structure is remarkable: the prohibition is enforced not by a procedure but by a faith-statement. Only the woman knows what her womb holds in those first weeks; no court, no former husband, no family can audit the knowledge; and the verse addresses the only auditor who can — her belief. The conditional *if they believe in God and the Last Day* makes the concealment's impossibility a matter of creed: the woman who believes stands before the Knower of the womb's contents and the Reckoner of the concealed truth, and the belief itself is the enforcement mechanism. The legal stakes are concrete: a concealed pregnancy would confuse the lineages — a child attributed to a new husband who is the former husband's, an inheritance misdirected, a blood-relation rendered strangers to each other — the whole genealogical order the marriage-legislation exists to protect. The ʿiddah's three courses serve exactly this certainty, and the clause binds the one person whose truthfulness the certainty depends on, binding her at the deepest level the scripture possesses: her faith.

**The Right of Return**

The third clause preserves the reconciliation the waiting exists to make possible: their husbands have the better right to restore them in that period, if they desire peace — the maʿrūf reconciliation, the restoration of the marriage. The right's logic follows the divorce's origin: the husband — initiator of the ṭalāq — holds the initiative of its undoing — the declaration was his, and within the waiting-period its withdrawal is his, conditioned on the desire for reconciliation rather than for harm. The condition matters and the verses that follow will enforce it: the return-right exists for peace, and its abuse — retaining to harm, the cycling of divorce and revocation as captivity — is legislated against at 2:231. The wife's position within this architecture is the jurists' noted asymmetry: her options for ending the marriage do not run through the ṭalāq but through the khulʿ, the divorce she initiates with a ransom, legislated in the next verse (and at 4:20). The waiting-period thus holds the marriage in a reversible state under the initiator's hand, with the reconciliation desired as the outcome the period prefers — the law's tilt toward preservation visible in the structure of the right itself.

**The Reciprocity Clause**

The verse's fourth movement is its most far-reaching: the women have obligations the like of what is owed to them, in what is honorable — and the men have a degree over them. The reciprocity-clause generated a range of readings the commentators preserve, and the range is the clause's wealth. Some read it as mutual duty: each side owes what the other owes — wives their part, husbands theirs, in honorable exchange. Some read it as the mutuality of intimate right: the wife's claim on her husband in this respect resembling his on her (al-Ṭabarī). Some read it as mutual protection from harm: the husband not harming the wife through endless cycles of divorce and reconciliation, the wife not harming the husband by concealing her womb — the two harms the verse itself has just legislated against, gathered into one principle of reciprocity. Others grounded it in the marriage's purpose: closeness, love, and peace shared between the spouses — with al-Rāzī's striking verdict that in this sharing, one might even say the woman's portion is more abundant. And Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have lived the clause in its most domestic sense: I adorn myself for my wife as she adorns herself for me. The clause installs, at the center of divorce-law, the marriage's own constitution — reciprocal rights and duties in maʿrūf — and makes the waiting-period's conduct a matter of that reciprocity rather than of the husband's prerogative alone.

**The Degree**

The degree-clause — a degree named for men over women — has been read by the commentators in directions that share a common structure: the degree is load-bearing rather than dignifying. Ibn ʿAbbās's reported reading is the most cited: the degree consists, he explained, in men's fostering of good relations and their largesse toward women in property and conduct — the upper hand is bound to incline against its own interest. On this reading the degree is noblesse oblige legislated: the advantage carries the obligation of self-disadvantaging generosity, the headship measured by what it gives up. Other readings the commentators record ground the degree in men's economic advantages and their corresponding obligations — the maintenance and clothing the marriage-law assigns — in their role in the community's defense, and in qualities such as physical strength. Al-Qurṭubī gathers the positions without collapsing them, and the verse's own closure — Almighty, Wise — places the degree's measure with God rather than with the degree-holder: the Mighty enforces the reciprocity the degree sits inside, and the Wise has ordered the household's asymmetry as an ordering whose wisdom is His to know. The degree, in the verse's architecture, never escapes the reciprocity-clause that precedes it: the obligations owed and owing are mutual and honorable, and the degree stands over that mutuality as responsibility, not exemption.

**The Soul's Waiting**

The commentators' spiritual reading extends the verse's whole architecture inward, following the Sufi exegetes' method: the marriage legislated here has its counterpart in the soul's own household. The nafs — the soul, the self — holds claims owed to it and claims it owes; the spirit (rūḥ) dominates the soul's powers, yet spiritual progress takes place precisely through the soul, not around it; and the discipline the verse legislates for the estranged couple applies to the person estranged from his own depths — gentleness with the soul, rest when rest is needed, the waiting-period against hasty dissolution, the reconciliation preferred over the divorce of the self from its own capacities. The wedding of the soul and the spirit is a standing motif of the tradition's spiritual literature, and the domestic verse is read as its legal shadow: as the marriage is kept honorable in its rights and degrees, so the soul's marriage to the spirit is kept in its mutual dues. The reading does not displace the law — the qurūʾ, the womb, the return-right, and the degree remain legislation for actual households, enforced by actual courts — but it completes the verse's reach: the same principles of bounded estrangement, honest knowledge, preferred reconciliation, and load-bearing asymmetry govern the community's marriages and the soul's interior, under the same Mighty and Wise.
