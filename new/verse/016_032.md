## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:32

> **those whose souls the angels take while they are virtuous, saying ˹to them˺, “Peace be upon you! Enter Paradise for what you used to do.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Souls Taken While Virtuous**

Al-ladhīna tatawaffāhum al-malā'ikatu ṭayyibīn — those whose souls angels take while they are virtuous, good, pure. Tatawaffāhum al-malā'ikatu, angels take them, same verb as in verse 28 for wrongdoers, but state differs: ẓālimī anfusihim vs ṭayyibīn. Ṭayyibīn means good, pure, virtuous, from ṭ-y-b, to be good, pure, pleasant. Al-Ṭabarī explains ṭayyibīn as those who are pure from shirk, sincere in faith, good in deeds, or those whose souls are taken in easy, gentle manner, without hardship. The same root ṭayyib is used for good things, pure provisions. The contrast with verse 28 is deliberate: same angels, same act of taking souls, but different states and different greetings. One group wronged themselves, offered false submission, told they know what they did, commanded to enter Hell. Other group is virtuous, greeted with peace, told to enter Paradise for what they did. Death reveals what life cultivated.

**Peace Be Upon You**

Yaqūlūna salāmun ʿalaykum — saying, peace be upon you. Angels say salām, peace, security, greeting. Salāmun ʿalaykum is greeting of Paradise, also greeting among believers in world, and greeting from Allah, as 36:58 says *Peace, a word from a Merciful Lord* (36:58). Al-Qurṭubī says angels greet righteous at death with peace to calm fear, give glad tidings, and convey that they are safe from punishment. The greeting is in present tense, indicating ongoing peace, not temporary. It also indicates that angels are not harsh to righteous; they are gentle, welcoming. The same greeting will be heard in Paradise from angels and inhabitants, as 13:24 says *Peace be upon you for what you patiently endured* (13:24). The death greeting is foretaste of Paradise greeting.

**Enter Paradise For What You Used To Do**

Udkhulū al-jannata bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn — enter Paradise for what you used to do, because of what you used to do. Bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn indicates cause: because of your deeds, your faith, your patience, your good works. Al-Ṭabarī explains that entry to Paradise is by Allah's mercy, but deeds are cause and evidence of sincerity, and reward is according to deeds. The phrase does not mean Paradise is earned solely by deeds without mercy; it means deeds are sign of faith that Allah rewards. Al-Rāzī notes that bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn includes both inward and outward deeds: belief, gratitude, patience, trust, as verses 41-42 will mention for emigrants. The same phrase appears in other descriptions of Paradise entry, as in 16:32 and 52:19. It shows that Paradise is not arbitrary; it is for those who did what was required.

**Why This Verse Follows Gardens Description**

Verse 31 described Gardens of Eternity with rivers and fulfillment of desires as reward for righteous. Verse 32 explains who those righteous are: those whose souls angels take while virtuous, greeted with peace, told to enter Paradise for what they did. The sequence moves from reward to recipients to manner of transition. It also contrasts with verses 28-29 which described wrongdoers' death and Hell entry. The two death scenes are placed close to show two paths: one ends with peace and Paradise, other with denial and Hell. The listener is invited to choose which death he wants by choosing which life he lives. The sūrah's blessings catalogue was meant to cultivate ṭayyib, virtuous state, so that death will be while virtuous.

**The Meaning Of Ṭayyibīn**

Ṭayyibīn can mean pure from shirk, pure in speech and action, or those whose death is easy, without hardship. Al-Ṭabarī reports both interpretations and says they are compatible: those who are pure in faith have easy death. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that ṭayyib is opposite of khabīth, foul, impure, used for shirk and evil deeds. The righteous are ṭayyib because they avoided khabīth of shirk and ingratitude. Their souls are ṭayyibah, good, so angels take them in ṭayyib manner, with peace. The word also suggests that their lives were ṭayyibah, good life, as 16:97 says *We will surely give them good life* (16:97). Good life leads to good death leads to good home.

**Linguistic Note On Salām And Bi-mā**

Salām from s-l-m means peace, safety, wholeness, from same root as islām, submission that brings peace. Salāmun ʿalaykum is greeting that wishes peace upon addressee. Udkhulū from d-kh-l means enter, imperative plural, indicating invitation to group. Bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn, for what you used to do, uses bi- for cause, mā for what, kuntum for you were, taʿmalūn for doing continuously. The phrase indicates habitual deeds, not single act. The verse's structure — those whom angels take while virtuous, saying peace, enter Paradise for what you did — moves from state at death to greeting to command to reason, creating a complete, comforting picture of righteous death that contrasts sharply with harsh death of wrongdoers described earlier.

**How Virtuous State Is Cultivated**

Ṭayyibīn state is not accidental; it is cultivated through gratitude for blessings, reflection on signs, and patience in hardship. The sūrah has listed uncountable blessings to cultivate ṭayyib heart that recognizes Giver. It has warned against arrogance and denial that make heart khabīth. It has described emigrants who patiently endured and trusted Lord, as verse 42 will say. All these cultivate ṭayyib. At death, angels recognize that cultivation and greet with peace. The greeting salāmun ʿalaykum therefore is not only for that moment but for life that led to it. It says, your life was peaceful in submission, so your death is peaceful, and your next life will be peace.

**Enter Paradise As Invitation**

Udkhulū al-jannah, enter Paradise, is imperative of invitation, not command of compulsion. Angels invite righteous to enter what they have been promised, what they worked for, what Allah prepared. The invitation is gentle, with reason bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn, for what you used to do, acknowledging their effort. This contrasts with fa-udkhulū abwāba jahannama, so enter gates of Hell, which is command of humiliation. Both use same verb udkhulū but tone differs: one is honor, other is disgrace. The righteous enter Paradise for what they did, meaning their deeds are recognized and rewarded, not ignored. This recognition is part of reward: to be told your deeds mattered.
