## Sūrah an-Naml 27:55

> Do you really lust after men instead of women? In fact, you are ˹only˺ a people acting ignorantly.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection Is Stated Plainly**

Where the previous verse used a definite article as a euphemism, this one names the matter directly: approaching men with desire *instead of* women. Lūṭ has moved from pointing at the practice to describing it.

The phrase that carries the argument is *min dūni al-nisāʾ* — to the exclusion of women, in place of women. The same construction appeared in verse 24, where Sheba's people prostrated to the sun *min dūni Allāh*, instead of God. The Qur'an uses this formula for substitution: something has been put in the position belonging to something else. That is a consistent way of framing what the Book objects to, and it recurs across quite different subjects. The complaint is not that a desire exists but that an arrangement has been displaced.

**Al-Rāzī's Argument From Design**

Al-Rāzī states the reasoning the tradition generally rests on: the practice runs against the wisdom of God, who did not create men sexually for men. The argument is teleological — it reads a purpose off the structure of the thing.

Whether that reasoning persuades a given reader depends on premises the Qur'an assumes and does not argue for in this passage, chiefly that creation is purposive and that its purposes are legible. Within those premises the argument is straightforward and it is the one the classical commentators make. It is worth being clear about what kind of claim it is. Lūṭ's objection as the Qur'an frames it is not primarily about harm to others or about social order; it is about a design being set aside. That is also why the word chosen for the people's condition is *ignorance* rather than cruelty or injustice, which are the charges laid against Thamūd's nine.

**"A People Acting Ignorantly"**

The verdict is *qawmun tajhalūn* — a people who are ignorant, or who behave ignorantly. The root *j-h-l* is important in Qur'anic vocabulary and it does not mean a simple lack of information.

*Jahl* in Arabic usage is the opposite of *ḥilm*, forbearance and self-possession, as much as it is the opposite of knowledge. The pre-Islamic period is called the *Jāhiliyyah* not because the Arabs of that era lacked learning — they had poetry, genealogy and astronomy — but because their conduct was governed by impulse, pride and unchecked appetite. Applied here, the word names a way of acting rather than a gap in what is known, a failure of self-government rather than of education, which is consistent with the previous verse's charge that they did this *while seeing*. They are not uninformed. They are operating without the restraint that information should produce.

**Two Charges That Fit Together**

Put the two verses side by side and the accusation has a shape. Verse 54 said they did it openly, in each other's sight, knowing. This verse says they are ignorant. Those look contradictory and are not.

Knowing something and being governed by it are different capacities, and the Qur'an keeps them apart throughout this chapter. Pharaoh's court was certain the signs were true and denied them anyway. Thamūd's nine swore by God while planning murder. Lūṭ's people know the practice is indecent — that is what *while you see* asserts — and the knowledge does not reach their conduct. *Jahl* names precisely that disconnection. It is the condition of a person whose information and whose behaviour have come apart, which is a more accurate description of most human failure than simple error is.

**A Question Asked Rather Than a Sentence Passed**

Lūṭ's second question ends the part of the exchange that belongs to him. He has asked twice and characterised once, and there is no threat in either verse, no announcement of punishment, and no demand backed by a consequence.

The restraint is worth registering because of what happens next. His people's answer is not an argument but a motion to expel him and those with him from the city — the response of a community that regards an objection as an unbearable presence. The disproportion between his two questions and the reply they draw is the narrative's whole point, and it is a pattern the chapter has established. Ṣāliḥ asked why they were hurrying and was told he was bad luck; nine men then planned to kill his household. Sheba's queen received a demand and answered with a test. The chapter has been measuring communities by what they do with an objection, and the people of Lūṭ answer in the next verse.

**Where This Sits Among the Qur'an's Accounts**

Lūṭ's people appear in several chapters and the emphases differ. Hūd gives the scene at his door and his desperate attempt to protect his guests. Al-Ḥijr supplies the angels and the timing of the departure. Ash-Shuʿarāʾ places his story in a sequence of five prophets with a repeating refrain.

An-Naml keeps only the exchange, and reading the versions together shows how deliberate that selection is. This chapter has no angels, no guests, no negotiation and no dawn deadline. What it takes is the two questions and the answer they received, because the chapter's subject is the reception of a message rather than the machinery of a rescue. The same editorial logic governed its treatment of Mūsā, where years of confrontation in Egypt were compressed into one clause about signs arriving and being called sorcery. Each narrative in an-Naml is cut to the moment where a community decides, and the material that does not bear on the deciding is left to the chapters that want it. The result is four episodes that can be read as one argument. What the phrasing isolates is the deliberateness of the act, and deliberateness is what the rest of the passage goes on to judge.
