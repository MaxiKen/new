## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:279

> If you do not, then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger! But if you repent, you may retain your principal—neither inflicting nor suffering harm.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The War-Declaration**

The consequence of non-compliance is stated in the Qur'an's most severe formula: *then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger*. The commentators mark the declaration's singularity — no other sin in the Qur'an is met with a war announced by God and His Messenger together, and the pairing is read as the measure of the ribā-persistence's gravity: an economic practice that convulses households and absorbs estates is treated as an armed posture against the divine order and its envoy at once. Ibn ʿAbbās's reported gloss carries the declaration into the eschaton: on the Day of Resurrection it will be said to the one who took ribā — take up your sword for war (al-Ṭabarī). Others read the formula's direction as descriptive rather than prospective: whoever persists has already made himself hostile to God and His Messenger, the war being the state his persistence constitutes (al-Qurṭubī). And the jurists drew from the declaration its this-worldly edge, recorded across the four exegetes: persistence in ribā after the warning can be cause for the legitimate authority to take up arms against the persisters — compelling the surrender of the ribā-gains, and fighting those who refuse to surrender them or desist. A sin legislated with imprisonment, confiscation, and campaign behind it: the declaration is the sūrah's statement that the devouring-economy is not a private vice but a public war.

**If You Repent**

Against the war stands the verse's other half, and the tradition reads the two halves as one instrument of mercy: *But if you repent, you may retain your principal*. The repentance named is the previous verse's abandonment performed — the remainder left, the claims rewritten — and its reward is the amnesty's exact content: the raʾs al-māl, the head of the wealth, the capital itself, retained in full. The commentators' jurisprudence is precise about what the retention means for both parties: the debtor remains liable for the original loan — the principal is not forgiven by the repentance but confirmed — while every fee, increase, and deferment-charge that accrued on it is cancelled. The creditor who repents is thus made whole in his capital and stripped in his surplus; the debtor who is released keeps his obligation to the amount actually received. The verse converts the repentance from an interior state into a settlement with arithmetic: here is what you keep, here is what you release, here is the war you exit — the same structure the Farewell-declaration carried (the ribā of the Age of Ignorance remitted, the principal retained) now stated as the standing terms on which any ribā-holder may come in from the war.

**Neither Wronging Nor Wronged**

The settlement's formula is the verse's most quoted clause: *neither inflicting nor suffering harm* — lā taẓlimūna wa-lā tuẓlamūn, you shall not wrong and shall not be wronged. The commentators note the formula's double fairness with care. The creditor is not wronged: his capital returns to him intact, and the repentance-clause exists precisely so that the prohibition does not become a confiscation — the religion does not make the abandonment of ribā a punishment of the lender's estate. The debtor is not wronged: the increase that was absorbing his possessions — the doubling and redoubling of 3:130 — stops at the principal, and the wrong of compounding is lifted from him forever. The two negations thus state the settlement as a symmetry, and the symmetry is the ribā-block's moral core: the forbidden transaction is the one that requires a wronging — the increase taken from the debtor's need is a wrong inflicted, and its cancellation without capital-loss ensures no wrong rebounds onto the creditor. The tradition reads the same formula as the economy's standing principle, the Prophetic declaration at the Farewell Pilgrimage using it as the ribā-amnesty's own closing words: you have your principal — neither wronging nor wronged — and the jurists extended the principle to the whole of muʿāmalāt: no transaction in the Islamic order may be built on either party's ẓulm.

**The Principal and the Loan's Nature**

The retention-of-principal ruling carries an implication the commentators draw out in the block's wider context: the legitimate loan, in this legislation, is a loan that returns exactly what was given. The Islamic tradition's ideal of the qarḍ — the loan — is therefore charitable in structure: money lent for a term, repaid in the same amount, the time-value surrendered by the lender as an act of the giving the section has been legislating (the invitation to turn the difficult debtor's respite into outright charity follows at the next verse). The profit-seeking uses of capital are not forbidden by this structure but channeled — into the trade God permitted (2:275's permission-clause), the credit-sale with its negotiated price, the lease, the partnership where profit and loss are shared — instruments in which the return compensates real risk or real exchange rather than time alone. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both frame the principal-rule this way: the creditor's capital is protected because capital is legitimate property; the increase is cancelled because the increase was the wrong. The verse thus legislates not the abolition of lending but its purification — the loan restored to the form in which it can be, as the tradition insists, an act of charity with a contractual body.

**The Sequence's Position**

This verse and its neighbors form the block's enforcement-triad, and the triad's order is deliberate: the portrait of the pillar-community (2:277), the command to the believers to fear God and abandon the remainder (2:278), the war-and-repentance alternative (this verse), the respite-and-charity treatment of the struggling debtor (2:280), and the Day-return that seals the whole (2:281). The commentators read the movement as descending from the community's identity to its ledgers to its debtors to its final audit — each verse narrowing the frame: believers, creditors, debtors, souls. Within the triad this verse is the alternative-statement, the point where the command's two exits are named in full: persistence is war, repentance is settlement, and the settlement's terms are fixed at the principal. Nothing between the two exits is legislated — no partial compliance, no negotiated retention of some increase — and the binary is the verse's rhetorical force: the ribā-holder stands, in the moment of the command, at the fork the verse describes, and the fork's two roads have been named with their destinations, war on one side and neither-wronging on the other, with the principal waiting intact on the road of return.
