## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:99

> And none led us astray other than the wicked.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condemned Try to Identify Their Misleaders**

After admitting that they made idols equal to the Lord of all worlds, the speakers name the wicked as those who led them astray. The statement recognizes that false worship was not maintained by ideas alone. People transmitted it, beautified it, defended it, and directed others toward it. Misguidance can be organized through voices that present corruption as wisdom.

The term wicked is broad enough to include those who actively call to evil and those who preserve it for power, habit, or gain. The speakers are identifying agents of misguidance, but the confession does not yet prove that naming those agents removes their own responsibility. A leader can mislead, while a follower can still choose to follow.

**The Sentence Raises the Question of Blame**

The word none sounds like an attempt to place the whole cause outside the self. The condemned may be telling the truth about the influence of the wicked, yet the truth is incomplete if it turns into an excuse. They made the idols equal; they responded to the calls; they participated in the practice. Their misleaders mattered, but so did their consent.

This tension is central to moral accountability. Human beings are shaped by parents, teachers, rulers, friends, and culture. Those influences can be powerful, especially when they make falsehood appear normal. Still, influence is not compulsion in every case. The final confession must include both the evil of the leaders and the choice of those who accepted their direction.

**Forefathers Can Be Among the Wicked**

The source tradition allows the wicked to refer to forebears who handed idolatry down. That reading connects this verse with the earlier appeal to ancestors. A practice can be inherited through family and still be a form of misguidance. Respect for age does not turn transmission into proof.

Calling an ancestor wicked is serious, and it should not become a license for careless insult. The point is moral function. If a person knowingly leads others away from God, protects false worship, or makes error seem sacred, his influence is wicked even if his name is honored. Later generations must judge inherited teachings by truth rather than by family pride.

**Iblîs and His Agents Can Also Be Included**

Another reading connects the wicked with Iblîs and those who follow his path. The meaning fits the wider scene, where satanic influence is associated with the spread of false allegiance. Yet even here, the existence of temptation does not erase human choice. Iblîs calls; the servant answers. A tempter can decorate an error, but the heart must still consent to it.

The distinction protects against two extremes. One extreme denies spiritual temptation and imagines that every wrong is purely self-created. The other blames Iblîs for every choice and treats the human being as helpless. The verse's moral force lies between them: wicked influences are real, and people remain accountable for following them.

**A Parallel Makes the Follower's Responsibility Plain**

In 14:22, Satan denies possessing authority over his followers beyond calling them and receiving their response. That passage clarifies the present confession. A misleader may invite a person toward error, but the invitation does not become an irresistible command. The follower's response is part of the account that judgment uncovers.

This does not make every follower equally guilty. Knowledge, pressure, opportunity, and role can differ. God's judgment is precise. The principle is that blaming a misleader cannot erase the need for personal repentance. A person must leave the wicked direction rather than wait for the leader to carry the consequence.

**The Wicked Can Mislead Through False Protection**

Not every misleader speaks openly against God. Some promise safety if people remain loyal to a group, ruler, idol, or custom. They may call submission unity and call dissent betrayal. Such language hides the spiritual cost of following them. The person accepts a false lord in exchange for belonging or protection.

The verse gives a way to test those claims. Does the leader direct people toward worship of God, truth, justice, and repentance, or toward obedience to the leader's own authority? The wicked often become visible when they demand the reverence that belongs to God and punish those who refuse it.

**Shared Error Does Not Become Innocent**

A community may say that everyone was taught the same practice. That fact explains the strength of the error but does not transform it into guidance. Widespread misdirection can make a wrong path easier to enter and harder to leave. It cannot make the path lead to God.

The condemned recognize that wicked people helped lead them, but the recognition comes after the cost is fixed. The living can interrupt the chain. They can examine inherited teaching, refuse a corrupt authority, and stop passing the error to others. Breaking a chain of misguidance may be lonely, but repeating it makes the person a link for someone else.

**The Verse Does Not Let Leaders Hide Behind Followers**

The sentence also condemns those who lead others astray. A person who uses influence to strengthen idolatry or disobedience cannot say that each follower chose independently and therefore the leader bears no responsibility. Teaching, advertising, ruling, and persuading all carry moral weight when they direct people toward false worship.

Leadership is a trust because it multiplies effects. A wicked teacher may create many imitators, and a corrupt ruler may make injustice seem lawful. The verse names this danger in one word. The wicked are not only wrong in themselves; they become causes through which others are drawn away.

**The Correct Response Is Responsibility With Mercy**

A believer should identify harmful influences without becoming consumed by blame. The purpose is not to spend life cursing ancestors or leaders while refusing to examine the self. It is to stop the misguidance, repent from participation, and protect others from repeating it. Mercy can be shown to people without excusing the doctrine or system that harmed them.

The condemned say that the wicked led them astray, but the living can say something better: we recognized the influence, tested it against God's truth, and turned away. The final verse of their complaint becomes a warning to choose that response while the door of change remains open.
