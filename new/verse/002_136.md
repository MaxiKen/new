## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:136

> Say, ˹O believers,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us; and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them. And to Allah we all submit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Confession Given in the Imperative**

The verse instructs the Prophet ﷺ to state what the believers affirm, and the instruction places the community's creed in a single sentence. The Book often gives its teaching in this form, and the content is arranged so that the reader can recite it as a whole.

The confession begins with God and then lists the revelations, and it ends with a statement about the community's own posture. The structure is important: what the believers affirm is not a set of propositions about themselves but a relation to what God has sent down, and the final clause states the shape of that relation.

**The Revelations Named**

The list moves from the revelation given to the community itself to the revelations given to the patriarchs and then to the prophets of the later line. Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, and Jacob are named as recipients, and the term for Jacob's descendants covers the twelve tribes that descended from him.

The mention of Moses and Jesus then brings the list down to the two figures whose communities the sūrah has been addressing, and it closes with the prophets in general. By naming the recipients in this order the verse draws a single line of revelation through the whole of sacred history, and it places the community reading it at the end of that line rather than outside it.

**The Descendants of Jacob**

The Book refers to the twelve tribes descended from Jacob in another passage where it describes them being divided and given water in the desert, and the commentators explained the term as referring to Jacob's grandchildren. The mention of the tribes in a confession of faith serves the passage's argument, since the communities that claimed descent from them are being told that their ancestors received revelation as well.

What the verse establishes is a shared history rather than a competition over it. The revelation given to the tribes, to Moses, and to Jesus is affirmed by the believers in the same sentence as the revelation they received, and the Book presents the whole sequence as one provision from a single source.

**No Distinction Between the Messengers**

The middle of the confession states that the community makes no distinction between the messengers, and the sūrah states the same principle again in its closing verses, where the Prophet and the believers affirm their faith in God, His angels, His books, and His messengers without dividing them. The Book's point is that accepting some messengers and rejecting others is not a form of discrimination within belief; it is a rejection of the authority that sent them.

The sūrah has already used this principle to indict the community that believed in part of its scripture and rejected the rest, and the confession here supplies the alternative. What the believers affirm is the whole of the revelation, and the statement is made in the first person plural so that the community recites it together.

**Continuity Across the Scriptures**

The Book states in another chapter that God has ordained for the believers the same religion He decreed for Noah and revealed to Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, commanding that the faith be upheld and not divided. The confession in this verse belongs to the same teaching, and it presents the community's religion as the continuation of a single command rather than as a new departure.

That continuity is what the sūrah's narrative of Abraham has been establishing, and the confession gathers the result into a formula. The believers are told to affirm the revelation given to the patriarchs, the revelation given through Moses and Jesus, and the revelation given to them, and to do so without ranking the recipients in a way that would divide what God sent.

**Submission as the Closing Word**

The sentence ends with the community's submission to God, and the Arabic form places the pronoun before the verb so that the statement reads as a dedication of the community rather than as a description. The word chosen is the same one used of Abraham and Jacob in the preceding verses, and the confession therefore closes by identifying the believers with the religion the passage has been describing.

The verse thus serves double duty in the sūrah's argument. It answers the invitation recorded in the previous sentence by stating what the community holds, and it supplies the content of the faith that the following verses will test against the responses of the two communities.

**The Verse as a Criterion**

The confession is also a criterion. A community that recites it and then treats one part of revelation as acceptable and another as inconvenient has contradicted what it says, and the sūrah's warnings to its own readers make use of the same test. What the verse gives the community is a standard by which its own conduct can be measured, and the Book states it in the language of worship so that the recitation itself becomes the commitment.

The confession works in the sūrah as a rule for weighing claims about the prophets. A claim that assigns one of them to a community formed centuries later is measured against the words the believers recite, and the criterion stays available to any reader who has the verse in front of him. The sūrah does not leave the matter to scholarly arbitration; it supplies a statement both the People of the Book and the believers possess, and it asks them to hold the claims they hear against it.

**Where the Confession Sits**

The sentence stands between the answer given to the two communities and the statement about guidance and schism that follows. The arrangement leads the reader from the claim about Abraham, through the confession of what the community believes, to the consequence of accepting or refusing it, and each step develops the same argument.

The sentence comes after the account of Abraham’s religion and before the passage on the direction of prayer, and it gathers the material that lies between them. Readers have been shown what the ancestors believed, what the two communities claimed about them, and what the believers are told to say; the confession states the position the community holds once those claims have been examined. It is therefore a conclusion placed in the mouth of the community rather than a summary offered by a narrator.
