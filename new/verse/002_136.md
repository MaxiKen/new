## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:136

> Say, ˹O believers,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us; and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them. And to Allah we all submit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Creed That Leaves Nothing Out**

This is the community's confession of faith in a single sentence, and its architecture is deliberate. It moves in widening circles of revelation: God Himself first; then what has been revealed to this community; then what was revealed to the patriarchs — Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and Jacob's descendants; then what was given to Moses and Jesus; then what was given to the prophets as a class, unnamed and unnumbered. No era of revelation is skipped and no prophet is dropped. The confession is spoken in the first-person plural — *we* believe — because it is not a private opinion but the standing statement of a community, recited by each generation in its own voice. Its final clause folds the whole inventory into a single posture: and to God we submit. Belief is enumerated; then it is enacted.

**The Descendants: Grandsons and Tribes**

The word rendered "his descendants" is *asbāṭ*, whose singular *sibṭ* means grandchild — a precise genealogical note, since these are Jacob's grandchildren through his sons, the ancestors of the twelve tribes of Israel. The sūrah of the Heights records the division itself: *"We divided them into twelve tribes— each as a community"* (7:160). Placing the asbāṭ inside the creed accomplishes something the sectarian argument of the previous verses could not: the tribes that the Torah's guardians prized as their exclusive inheritance are here enrolled as recipients of revelation alongside everyone else. They are honored — revelation came to their line — but they are not privileged. In a confession that ends with "we make no distinction," even the twelve tribes hold their place as peers of Ishmael, the ancestor the Arabs claimed, and of the prophets who never had a tribe to name after them.

**No Distinction in Believing**

"We make no distinction between any of them" is a statement about belief, not about rank. The Qur'an elsewhere acknowledges that God *"chosen some of those messengers above others"* and raised some high in rank (2:253), and the believers' creed later in this same sūrah repeats the no-distinction clause in the same sense (2:285). What is excluded here is selectivity: believing some prophets and disbelieving others, accepting the ones your tradition kept and discarding the ones it lost. That selective habit is exactly what the sūrah denounced earlier, rebuking those who believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest (2:85). A creed that admits Moses but refuses Jesus, or honors Jesus but refuses Muhammad, has not made a theological judgment; it has made a tribal one. The confession closes that door: every prophet is believed, because every prophet came from the same Lord.

**One Religion From Noah Onward**

The creed's comprehensiveness rests on a theology of continuity stated explicitly elsewhere: *"He has ordained for you ˹believers˺ the Way which He decreed for Noah, and what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what We decreed for Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, ˹commanding:˺ 'Uphold the faith, and make no divisions in it'"* (42:13). One religion, four named stations, one command — and the prohibition of division inside the command itself. On this account the confession of this verse is not the membership oath of a new party competing with older parties. It is the same faith every prophet brought, recited by the community that refuses to divide it. The sects fractured the inheritance; the creed gathers it back up, which is why it can name Abraham, Moses, and Jesus in one breath without embarrassment or appropriation.

**The Same Words, Again and Again**

The formula proved to be the community's fixed statement rather than a one-off sentence. Āl ʿImrān carries a nearly identical confession — *"We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants"* (3:84) — and the sūrah of the Women commands belief in God, His Messenger, the Book sent down to him, and the Scriptures sent down before (4:136). The repetition across sūrahs, in Mecca and Medina, shows the creed functioning as the believers' answer whenever faith is demanded of them: when the People of the Book say join us, when the pagans ask what you worship, when the community defines itself in prayer. A statement repeated this many times in the Book ceased to be commentary and became liturgy — the shortest complete account of what a Muslim believes, recited in the plural because it is held in common.

**Submission as the Seal**

The creed ends where it began in God, but with a verb instead of a noun: "And to Allah we all submit." The inventory of revelations could have closed with a summary — this is our faith, this is our book. Instead it closes with an act. *Islam* here is not a second belief added to the first; it is the first belief standing up. To believe in God and every prophet He sent is to owe Him the whole of one's direction, and the confession pays the debt in its last four words. The placement also answers the sects implicitly. The Jews said follow our faith, the Christians said follow ours, and each named an institution; the confession names a posture — submission to the Lord of all the prophets, without distinction, without a party between the servant and his God. The verse after this one will state the consequence: whoever believes as this creed believes is guided.
