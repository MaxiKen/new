## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:100

> O believers! If you were to yield to a group of those who were given the Scripture, they would turn you back from belief to disbelief.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Turns to the Believers**

The three verses before this one addressed the People of the Book and asked them why they denied the revelations of God, why they turned the believers from the path, and what they intended by it. This verse turns to the believers themselves and warns them about the consequences of listening.

The change of address is the sūrah's method at the close of an argument. Once the conduct of a party has been described and its grounds examined, the Book instructs its own community about how to respond, and the instruction here concerns obedience rather than belief. The verse does not tell the believers to refute an argument; it tells them what follows from complying with the guidance of a particular group among the People of the Book.

**"A Group" and the Refusal to Generalise**

The word used for those in view is one that means a section or a party, not an entire people. The choice matches the sūrah's care elsewhere in the same passage, where it states that the People of the Book are not all alike and that some among them are upright, reciting the revelations of God through the night and prostrating themselves (3:113).

The Book also says in this sūrah that some among them are faithful while most are rebellious (3:110), and near its end it praises those who believe in God, in what was revealed to the believers, and in what was revealed to them, and who do not trade away God's revelations for a small gain (3:199). A warning directed at a group is therefore consistent with the sūrah's own distinctions, and the reader is protected from turning a judgment about conduct into a verdict about a people.

**The Incident the Commentators Record**

Commentators connect this part of the sūrah with an occasion in Madinah, when one of the Jews of the city came upon a gathering of Muslims from the tribes of Aws and Khazraj. Those two tribes had been enemies before Islam, and the man began to recall their wars and the insults they had exchanged, until the gathering grew heated and the former enemies nearly came to blows. The Prophet came out to them and asked whether they would return to the practices of the days before Islam while he was among them and while God had honoured them with Islam and reconciled their hearts; at that they laid down their weapons, embraced one another, and went with him.

**What "Yielding" Produces**

The sentence states a consequence rather than an accusation. If the believers were to follow the direction of that group, they would find themselves turned back from their belief, which describes a process with stages rather than a single decision.

The sūrah states the same outcome near its end in a warning addressed to the same community, telling the believers that if they obey the disbelievers they will be dragged back to disbelief and become losers (3:149). The image of dragging in that verse matches the sense of the sentence here, where the result is reached by following rather than by arguing. A community is not talked out of its faith in one exchange; it is guided away from it in a series of small compliances, and the verse names the end of the road so that the first step can be recognised for what it is.

**Envy as the Plain Motive**

The Book supplies the motive in another passage, where it says of many among the People of the Book that they wish they could turn the believers back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them (2:109). The wording is direct about what drives the wish, and it removes the possibility of mistaking the behaviour for an honest disagreement.

Naming the motive does two things in the passage. It warns the believers about the source of the pressure they will feel, and it prevents them from treating the dispute as an intellectual puzzle to be solved. The Book's counsel in the verse that follows the one just quoted is to pardon and bear with them until God brings about His decision, which is a policy for living alongside neighbours whose intentions have been stated rather than a licence for retaliation.

**The Remedy the Sūrah Prescribes**

The Book's answer to the danger of being drawn apart is stated a few verses later in this sūrah, where the believers are told to hold firmly to the rope of God all together and not to be divided, and to remember the favour by which they were enemies and then became brothers (3:103).

The remedy is therefore the opposite of what the stirrer of quarrels was doing. He revived old enmities; the Book commands the community to remember the reconciliation and to guard it. The present verse's warning about listening to a particular group belongs with that command, since a community that has been made brothers is vulnerable precisely through the memories that once divided it, and the sūrah presents the two instructions as parts of one policy.

**The Balance the Passage Holds**

The warning in this verse is followed by a question put to the believers about how they could disbelieve while the revelations of God are being recited among them and while His messenger is in their midst. Read together, the two verses locate the community's safety in what it has been given rather than in its own strength.

The balance of the passage is worth stating plainly. It warns against a specific conduct, it names a group rather than a people, and it holds before the community the presence of the message and the messenger as its protection. The sūrah's later verses praise the believers among the People of the Book and describe their reward with their Lord, and the two statements belong to the same book. What the community is being taught is to be careful about whom it follows without becoming unjust about whom it judges.
