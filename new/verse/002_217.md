## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:217

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about fighting in the sacred months. Say, “Fighting during these months is a great sin. But hindering ˹others˺ from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is ˹a˺ greater ˹sin˺ in the sight of Allah. For persecution is far worse than killing. And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith—if they can. And whoever among you renounces their own faith and dies a disbeliever, their deeds will become void in this life and in the Hereafter. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question About the Sacred Months**

The verse reports a question about fighting during the sacred months and answers that fighting then is a grave matter, while listing offences that are graver. The Book has already treated the sacred months in the passage on the pilgrimage, and the sentence here applies the subject to the conduct of the community's conflicts.

The answer's structure gives the reader two things at once: an acknowledgement of the gravity of the act, and a comparison that places it within a larger account of wrongdoing. The community's teachers read the sentence as a statement about proportion, and the reader is shown that the Book answers the question without excusing the community.

**The Occasion Reported**

The commentators connect the sentence with a scouting party that was sent out before the community's first major battle, and they describe the events in detail. At an oasis between Ṭāʾif and Makkah the party killed a man and took two prisoners, and they returned with the spoils believing that the day was the last of one month rather than the first of a sacred one.

The reports state that the Prophet ﷺ refused the share of the spoils that was offered to him and told the party that he had not commanded them to fight during the sacred months. The Quraysh condemned the breach and accused the Prophet ﷺ of violating the norm that the Arabs observed, and the matter was eventually settled through an exchange of prisoners and the payment of compensation. The reader is given the account of an incident that became the occasion for the ruling.

**The Offences Compared**

The sentence states that hindering people from the way of God, disbelieving in Him, preventing access to the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people are graver in the sight of God than fighting in a sacred month. The list names the conduct of the community's opponents, and the community's teachers read it as the statement of a proportion rather than a justification.

The comparison places the practices of the Quraysh above the offence that the community had been asked about, and the community's scholars noted that the Book commits its assessment to the sight of God rather than to the standards of the parties. Readers are shown that the passage's argument concerns the relative weight of the offences, and the sentence's list supplies the account of what the opposing party had done.

**The Persecution Named**

The sentence states that persecution is worse than killing, and the phrase repeats a statement made earlier in the sūrah. The term describes the pressure that drives people from their religion, and the community's scholars connected it with the treatment the believers had received in Makkah.

The recollection is part of the passage's argument, since the community's migration and its losses were the result of that pressure. The sentence therefore places the community's conduct beside the conduct that produced the conflict, and the reader is shown why the Book treats the matter as it does. The community's teachers used the verse when they explained the circumstances of the community's early years.

**The Warning About Renunciation**

The sentence states that the opponents will not stop fighting until they turn the community from its religion if they can, and it then states that whoever renounces his faith and dies in disbelief loses his deeds in both worlds. The warning addresses the community itself, and the community's scholars noted that the passage turns from the opponents' conduct to the danger it presents.

The clause's concern is steadiness under pressure, and the reports record the attempts that were made to induce the believers to abandon their faith. Readers are shown that the passage's central anxiety is the community's own perseverance, and the sentence's warning belongs to the theme of trial that the previous verses have opened.

**The Sacred Month and the Sacred Precinct**

The sentence answers an objection about fighting in a month that the Arabs held sacred, and the report places the occasion in the expedition that the community sent toward the caravan routes in Rajab. The skirmish that followed became the subject of the claims that the community had violated the month and the sanctuary.

The answer states a measure that the community's teachers returned to often. Wrong done to a people on account of their faith is heavier than the killing that the objectors named, and the sentence adds the greater offence of barring the believers from the sacred house. The comparison places the protection of belief above the sanctity of a time and a place, since the first belongs to the purpose for which the others were established.

The jurists discussed the standing of the sacred months after the revelation, and the schools differed about the application of the rule while agreeing on the principle that the sentence states. The passage's concern is the order of values by which the community judges its affairs.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the gravity of fighting in a sacred month, the greater gravity of the offences that the community's opponents had committed, the standing of persecution among those offences, and the warning against abandoning the faith under pressure. The reader is given the ruling and the comparison together, and the community's teachers used the verse when they spoke about the situations in which the community's conduct is to be judged as a whole. The passage continues with the promise made to those who believed and struggled.

The verse's comparison has been read as a statement that the offence against the person's faith outranks the offence against a sacred time, and the community's teachers applied the principle to the disputes of their own day. The sentence also keeps the sanctuary and the months in view, so that the answer corrects the objection without dismissing what it invokes.
