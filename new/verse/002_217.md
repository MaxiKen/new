## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:217

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about fighting in the sacred months. Say, “Fighting during these months is a great sin. But hindering ˹others˺ from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is ˹a˺ greater ˹sin˺ in the sight of Allah. For persecution is far worse than killing. And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith—if they can. And whoever among you renounces their own faith and dies a disbeliever, their deeds will become void in this life and in the Hereafter. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Expedition at the Oasis**

The verse answers a crisis, and the reports preserve its details. Roughly eighteen months after the Hijrah — before Badr — a scouting party under the Companion ʿAbd Allāh ibn Jaḥsh went out against Meccan movements. At a watering-stop between Ṭāʾif and Mecca they encountered a caravan: a man was killed — ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī — two captives taken, and the caravan's goods brought home. The killing, it emerged, had fallen on Rajab's opening day — sacred time — though the party had supposed Jumādā's final day still running — a confusion the crescent-based calendar made possible, since the month's beginning depended on the sighting the sūrah's moon-verse had legislated (2:189). The Prophet's response was immediate and severe: he declined the share of caravan-goods brought to him, and told the party that fighting in holy time was no order of his. The Quraysh seized the incident, condemning the breach and accusing the Prophet of transgressing the Arabian norm of the sacred months — spilling blood in the forbidden time. The affair was eventually resolved when the captives were ransomed back and ʿAmr's blood-price paid; and the verse descended, addressing all parties with a hierarchy of sins that neither side had calculated.

**The Scales: Grave, and Graver**

The verse's answer is a weighing, and its first weight concedes the accusation: fighting in the sacred month *is* grave — kabīr, great, a serious sin. The concession is total and comes first; the community's error is not minimized, excused by intention, or absorbed into the enemy's greater crimes. Only then does the scale take the other side: but turning people from the path of God, disbelieving in Him, excluding the faithful from the sanctuary, and driving out its people — these are graver in God's sight. The four named crimes are the Meccan system's actual record: the thirteen-year campaign of persecution, the boycott, the torture, the expulsion of the believers from their homes and their sanctuary, and the standing bar against the pilgrims the treaty had just enforced. Against that record the oasis-killing is weighed, and the verse's arithmetic holds both truths without collapsing either: the sin is real, and it is outweighed. The method is the Qur'an's moral calculus at its most disciplined — no side's crimes canceled by the other's, every act weighed in a single scale where the persecution of faith outranks the violation of a month, however sacred the month.

**Fitnah Is Graver Than Killing**

The weighing's summary clause repeats the war-code's foundational ranking: persecution — fitnah — is graver than killing. The same sentence stood inside the field-legislation (2:191), and its repetition here, in a case about an actual killing, is the verse's sharpest edge: the death of one man at the oasis is set against the campaign to destroy faith itself, and the campaign is named the greater evil. Fitnah in this usage carries the meaning the exegetes fixed — the trial and torture inflicted to make believers renounce their religion, the persecution whose end is apostasy, and in the commentators' gloss the idolatry the persecution aimed to restore. The ranking is not a discount on killing; it is a statement about hierarchies of harm: the killing of a body is terrible, and the killing of a soul's allegiance is worse, because it reaches past death into the eternity the war-code's whole legislation protects. The clause thereby answers the Quraysh's propaganda at its root: the party loudest about one bloodshed in a sacred month was the party operating a standing system of bloodshed against faith — and the verse measures the month's violator and the faith's persecutor in the same sight of God.

**They Will Not Cease**

The verse then states the enemy's objective as a permanent fact: the campaign against the community runs on, without pause, until apostasy succeeds — if it ever can. The clause is the war-legislation's intelligence report — the persecution named as not an episode but a program, bounded only by the enemy's ability, aimed not at territory or tribute but at the believers' faith itself. The conditional *if they are able* is not a softening but a realism: the objective stands whether or not the power currently matches it, and the community is instructed to plan against the objective rather than the momentary capacity. This is the verse's contribution to the whole war-code: 2:190–193 had legislated the fighting's limits and its end — fight until fitnah ceases; this verse confirms from the enemy's side that fitnah will not cease on its own, that the other program runs in the opposite direction with the same persistence. The two programs — the persecution aimed at apostasy, the fighting aimed at ending persecution — cannot both stop voluntarily, and the verse tells the community which one it is facing.

**The Apostate's Ledger**

The verse's final movement legislates the consequence of the enemy's success: whoever renounces his religion and dies in disbelief — the ledger of his deeds annuls itself across both lives, and the Fire becomes his lasting residence. The voiding is comprehensive: the whole ledger of a believing life — its prayers, its charity, its hijrah, its patience under trial — is annulled by apostasy carried to death. The exegetes note what the verse equally implies: the renouncer *can* return, and return restores him to the community, though some jurists held that certain obligations — notably the hajj — must be performed anew. And the tradition weighed carefully what the verse does and does not legislate about the community's treatment of the renouncer. The word irtadda is often translated as apostasy, but the commentators stress that mere change of religion is not, for many classical and contemporary scholars, a cause for communal punishment — in light of the Qur'an's own freedom-statements: the truth is from your Lord, so let whoever wills believe and whoever wills disbelieve (18:29); there is no compulsion in religion (2:256); to you your religion, and to me mine (109:6). On this reading the punishment the verse states is the Hereafter's, and the historical wars of Abū Bakr against the seceding tribes were directed at sedition — at apostasy amounting to a political act of destabilization, the changing of sides in a community where religion and polity were one — rather than at belief alone. The early authorities' practice points the same way: Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿAlī, Abū Ḥanīfah, and al-Thawrī barred executing a woman for renunciation alone, a distinction that makes sense if the capital matter is the treason and the sword, not the private turn of belief the verse leaves to the Fire's ledger.

**The Verse's Full Answer**

Read whole, the verse answers four parties at once. To the community it concedes the sin of the sacred month and refuses to let the concession become despair — the greater crimes of the persecutors stand in the same scales. To the Quraysh it returns their propaganda measured: the month you invoke, you violate daily in graver ways, by barring the Mosque's people and hunting their faith. To the enemy's program it names the objective — persecution until apostasy — and legislates the community's endurance against it. And to the believer it states the one loss that outweighs every other: the renunciation that voids the ledger, the death in disbelief that makes the abiding Fire the residence. The passage's whole war-theology stands compressed here — the fitnah ranking, the permanence of the enemy's aim, the sacred things' inviolability, the freedom of belief with its eternal consequences — and the verse leaves the community with the scales in its hands: weigh the month's violation against the faith's persecution, weigh the deeds against the death, and know which losses the legislation exists to prevent.
