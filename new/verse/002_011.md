## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:11

> When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word for Damage and the Word Set Against It**

*Fasād* names the ruin of something that was sound: the spoiling of land, the breakdown of an arrangement, the corruption of a community. Its opposite in the Qur'an is *iṣlāḥ*, the act of setting something right, and the Book uses the pair as moral opposites throughout. *“Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done, so that Allah may cause them to taste ˹the consequences of˺ some of their deeds and perhaps they might return to the ˹Right Path˺.”* (30:41).

The same pair appears in a warning aimed specifically at the people under discussion: *“Now if you ˹hypocrites˺ turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship!”* (47:22). The Book also names the activity as a standing characteristic rather than an accident: *“they strive to spread corruption in the land. And Allah does not like corruptors.”* (5:64). The command in this verse is not a general appeal to good behaviour. It forbids specific damage that the Qur'an expects from this group.

**The One Who Rebukes Is Left Unnamed**

The verse says *when they are told* without saying by whom. Commentators offered several candidates: the Prophet ﷺ, the believers, or any person who suffers from the damage and speaks up against it. Al-Rāzī noted the openness and treated it as an extension of the verse's reach, since a rebuke can come from many mouths and still carry the same content.

The device also keeps the verse's focus on the reply. The Book is not reporting a particular conversation; it is documenting a reflex. Wherever the warning comes from, the answer is the same, and the sameness is the point. The verse reads like a law of group behaviour: a rebuke that names the damage, and a defence that renames it.

**"We Are Only Peace-Makers"**

The noun *muṣliḥūn* describes people who set things right, and *innamā*, rendered *we are only*, restricts the claim to that role alone. The speakers do not deny that something is in dispute. They claim to be the ones repairing it. Their answer concedes the need for repair while placing themselves on the side that supplies it, and the move is familiar in any community where factions form: the mediator's position is the most respectable place to stand.

What the verse exposes is the difference between a role and a result. The Book does not question their intention; it questions the outcome, and it does so in the next verse with a verdict rather than an argument. A person can sincerely believe he is holding a community together while the activity he calls mediation is the thing pulling it apart, and the sūrah gives that possibility a name.

**Corruption as a Public Condition**

In the Qur'an, *fasād* is often a matter of the land and of the community rather than of private conduct. The verse at 30:41 links it to what people's hands have earned and to the ecological damage the expression describes; 5:64 links it to hostility that continues *“until the Day of Judgment”*; 47:22 joins it to the severing of kinship ties. Contemporary readers have applied the term to the destruction of the environment, and the application follows the vocabulary rather than departing from it.

Applied to the hypocrites of Madinah, the same word covers the work of men who carried messages between the city and its enemies, discouraged the community at moments of danger, and profited from every division. None of that activity looks like corruption to the person performing it. It looks like prudence, and the difference between prudence and corruption is precisely what the verse's reply is unable to see.

**A Report About a Later People**

Al-Ṭabarī records a statement attributed to Salmān al-Fārsī that the people described in these verses had not yet appeared when the verses were revealed. The commentators accepted the report, and their acceptance shows how they understood the passage: as a description of a recurring type rather than a snapshot of a few individuals in Madinah.

The implication is uncomfortable and deliberate. A verse that can be fulfilled by later generations cannot be filed away as history, and the traits it names are not the property of one faction in one city. The Book's description of a community's inner corrosion is written so that any community that reproduces the conditions can find itself inside the description.

**What a Person Thinks He Is Doing**

The verse sets a person's account of his own conduct against the effect of that conduct, and it refuses to treat the account as evidence. That refusal is the epistemological claim at the centre of the sūrah's portrait of the hypocrites. The Book is not describing villains who know themselves to be villains; it is describing people whose self-image and whose impact have come apart.

The reply *we are only peace-makers* is stated as a quotation, which means the Qur'an preserves the voice of the defence and places the verdict elsewhere. In the next verse the verdict arrives in a single clause, and the reader who has followed the argument has already been shown what it rests on. The measure is not what a person claims to be doing but what is done.

**A Prohibition Still Addressed to Its Hearers**

The command in this verse is phrased as a prohibition with continuous force: stop doing the thing you are doing. The Book does not address the hypocrites as an alien party to be expelled, and it does not treat the damage they cause as beyond repair. They are told, which means they are still being spoken to, and the address is preserved inside a portrait written mainly for the believers.

That double audience explains the tone of the passage. A revelation that had finished with these people would not need to argue with them, and it would not need to report their replies at such length. The sūrah keeps their words on the page because the warning is still being issued, and because a community that reads the exchange learns to hear the same reply in its own mouth.
