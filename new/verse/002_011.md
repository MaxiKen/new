## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:11

> When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Who Is Speaking to Them**

The verse does not say who issues the rebuke, and the openness is deliberate. The speaker could be the Prophet, could be any believer who has watched what they do, or could be one of their own victims. By leaving the address anonymous, the Quran makes the sentence available to anyone in any generation who finds himself saying it.

The form matters as much as the words. This is not a court summons; it is the ordinary, unremarkable protest of people who live alongside corruption and can name it. The sūrah is recording something that happens before authorities and doctrines get involved — the point at which neighbours tell a man to stop.

**What Counts as Corruption**

The Quran pairs two words throughout: *fasād*, corruption, and *iṣlāḥ*, setting a thing right. Corruption is whatever takes a thing out of the state it was meant to be in, and the Quran applies the word at two scales at once. It names the moral act — open disobedience, the breach of what God has set — and it names the social wreckage that follows from it: *Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people’s hands have done* (30:41).

The two are not separable in the Quran's account, which is what makes the charge here precise. Corruption in the land is not a metaphor for private sin; it is what private sin produces when it is done at scale and in public. A sūrah will later name the shape of it in a single line: *perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship!* (47:22). Broken kinship, broken agreements, a community that cannot trust its own members — that is what the word denotes on the ground.

**The Answer, and What It Claims**

They answer with the opposite word: *We are only peace-makers* (2:11), using *muṣliḥūn*, the very term the Quran uses for setting things right. Commentators have read the reply in two ways, and the second is the more instructive. On the first reading, they simply believed their own practice to be correct. On the second, they were acting deliberately as brokers — people whose lukewarm commitment allowed them to keep a foot in both camps, and who presented that as service rather than as evasion.

What makes the claim effective is that it is not obviously false. A person who stands between two hostile parties and refuses to commit to either can describe himself as a peacemaker, and in a conflict that description will get a hearing. Their fault is not that they invented a false name for what they were doing; it is that the name fits well enough to be believed, by others and by them.

**Why a Vice That Imitates a Virtue Is Worse**

Corruption that announces itself is manageable. A man who says he means to do harm can be answered, opposed, and if necessary stopped, and everyone involved knows where they stand. Corruption that presents itself as repair cannot be opposed without appearing to oppose repair, which is why it survives longer and does more damage.

The dynamic is not peculiar to seventh-century Madinah. Any community has a use for the person who can reassure both sides, and the community rarely asks what the reassurance costs. The broker is the last person anyone suspects, because his whole value to each side is that he is not suspected by the other. He is never the one who broke anything; he is the one who was trying to hold things together when they came apart.

The sūrah's answer in the next verse is therefore sharper than it first appears, because the only way to break the manoeuvre is to name the outcome and let the outcome decide the question. Intentions are not available for inspection and would not settle the matter if they were. What is available is the state of the thing the person claims to be mending: is the community more trustworthy than it was, or less?

**Not Confined to One Generation**

A report attributed to Salmān al-Fārsī holds that the people described in this passage had not yet come. That limitation is worth keeping. A passage about named hypocrites in Madinah can be read as history; the same passage read as a standing diagnosis is being applied to whoever fits it. The text supports the wider reading through its own grammar — the indefinite *when they are told*, the unnamed speaker, the unlocated *land* — none of which is pinned to a year or a city.

**Corruption and the Natural World**

Contemporary readers have taken the environmental destruction of the earth as one of the central meanings of these verses, and the reading has more support than novelty usually does. The Quran's language about the world is consistently one of provision: the earth made for settlement, water sent down, fruits brought forth for sustenance (2:22). To wreck the thing that was given for sustenance is to take it out of the state it was meant to be in, which is the definition of *fasād*.

The caution is that the word's primary sense in this sūrah is moral and social, and a reading that jumps straight to ecology loses the hypocrisy the passage is about. The two belong together rather than in competition: the same disposition that ruins a community's trust ruins the ground it stands on, and both are corruption because both spoil something entrusted.
