## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:11

> When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Arrives Before the Accusation Is Digested**

The verse is staged as dialogue, and its tempo is part of its content. "When they are told" — the speaker left indefinite, the charge delivered: do not spread corruption in the land. The reply comes at once, complete and unexamined: *we are only peace-makers*. No pause, no question about which acts are meant, no offer of account. The syntax of instant defense is on display: the accused answers the accusation not with facts but with a self-portrait. The reflex matters because it exposes where these people live — inside a prepared narrative, where every rebuke has a script waiting. An honest person hears "you are corrupting" and first asks *how*; the hypocrite hears it and first reasserts who he claims to be. The verse records the smallest unit of the passage's diagnosis: the disease of verse 10 speaking in real time, converting correction into an occasion for more of the costume.

**Fasād: Un-Making, Not Mischief**

The charge uses *fasād*, and the word is heavier than "mischief." From a root meaning to spoil or rot, it names the undoing of *ṣalāḥ* — the soundness, right order, and fertility of a thing. Corruption in Quranic usage is entropy with a moral cause: what disobedience does to the fabric that holds a land together. Ibn ʿAbbās defined corruption in the land as open disobedience against Allah, and the definition extends to its consequences, since the moral state of inhabitants registers in their world. The Qur'an states the registration at planetary scale: *"Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done, so that Allah may cause them to taste ˹the consequences of˺ some of their deeds and perhaps they might return ˹to the Right Path˺"* (30:41). Land and sea are not neutral stages on which sins occur; they carry the sins, spoil with them, and testify. A charge of *fasād* is therefore never a charge of mere rule-breaking — it is a charge of un-making the world's order from inside.

**The Speaker Deliberately Left Unnamed**

Classical exegesis noticed what the verse withholds: who says "do not spread corruption"? Al-Rāzī canvases the options — the Prophet ﷺ himself, a party of believers, or a victim of the corruption speaking rebuke to the corrupters — and the openness is the point. With the speaker indefinite, the scene becomes universal: every generation's corrupters hear this sentence from someone, and a society that still produces people who say it has not wholly died. The passive construction — *idhā qīla lahum*, "when it is said to them" — makes the rebuke ambient, a standing feature of the moral atmosphere rather than one prophet's campaign. The verse thus captures a permanent confrontation: the land's corruption generates its own protest somewhere, and the corrupter's reply, in every age, follows the same script. What is being tested is not the identity of the speaker but the reflexes of the one accused.

**“Only Peace-Makers”: Two Ways to Read the Claim**

The reply admits two readings, and the commentators supply both. On the first, the claim is sincere self-perception: they genuinely regard their course as reconciliation — mediators between camps, keepers of a workable peace, men of moderation in a polarized city. On the second, associated with al-Rāzī, the claim is strategy: positioned between the believers and the disbelievers, they present their lukewarmness as bridge-building, their hedging as peacemaking, and charge the price of the bridge to both sides. Weighing them, the next verse decides: the divine retort calls them corrupters *who fail to perceive it* (2:12) — correcting a perception rather than unmasking a known lie. That verdict fits the first reading: at this stage the blindness is real. The two readings are not rivals so much as layers — the strategic position of the bridgekeeper, maintained long enough, generates the sincere belief that bridgekeeping is righteousness. The costume of verse 8 has grown into the skin.

**Salmān's Warning: The Verse Has Not Peaked Yet**

An interpretation attributed to the Companion Salmān al-Fārsī runs through the commentaries: the people this passage fully describes have not yet come. Al-Ṭabarī records it, and the tradition accepts it in the sense that these verses are not confined to the Prophet's ﷺ contemporaries — the description awaits fuller populations in later ages. The reading has modern extensions: some contemporary Muslims find in *fasād* upon the land a direct verdict on the destruction of the natural environment, and 30:41's coupling of land and sea with the works of human hands supports the extension. The combined effect lifts the verse out of its Madinan scene without emptying the scene. There were hypocrites there, named in the histories, rebuked in these words; and there will be — are — corrupters elsewhere answering the same rebuke with the same self-portrait. A verse with no historical floor would float free; a verse with no future would be dead. Salmān's reading keeps both.

**A Virtuous Word Worn as Camouflage**

*Muṣliḥūn* — the word they claim — means those who set things right, who repair and put in order; the exegetes caution that it is not "reform" in the modern programmatic sense but the active maintenance of *ṣalāḥ*. The claim is therefore a seizure of vocabulary: the corrupters dress in the language of repair, and the dressing is tactical. Whoever controls the word "peace-maker" controls the verdict before any evidence is weighed. The Qur'an's answer, in the next verse, strips the label and reinstalls the test — claims are read against consequences, words against acts. The pattern this verse installs recurs wherever power learns moral language: oppression announced as order, plunder announced as development, betrayal announced as realism. Against all of it the verse preserves the oldest rebuke in the land's own defense — *do not spread corruption* — and records, for the diagnostician, the exact reflex by which the corrupter betrays himself: instant, total, self-portraiture.
