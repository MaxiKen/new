## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:11

> When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rebuke Met With a Counter-Name**

The sūrah has diagnosed a saying (2:8), an attempted deception (2:9), and a disease that is increased (2:10). The first outward symptom it names is a response to correction: *“When it is said to them, ‘Do not spread corruption upon the earth,’ they say, ‘We are only those who set things right’”* (2:11). The rebuke — *lā tufsidū* — “do not corrupt” — is met not with denial of the act but with a renaming of the act. They do not say “We did not do it”; they say “What we did was *iṣlāḥ*.” The root *ṣ-l-ḥ* denotes setting in proper order, repairing, making wholesome — the opposite (*ḍidd*) of *fasād*, which denotes rotting, unravelling, putting out of joint. By answering a prohibition of *fasād* with a profession of *iṣlāḥ*, the hypocrites do not merely excuse; they invert. The sickness of (2:10) now shows itself as a capacity to call the disease by the name of the cure.

**Who Says “Do Not Corrupt”? The Open Speaker**

The source notes that the identity of the speaker of *“Do not spread corruption”* is left open — it could be the Prophet, a group of believers, or any injured party who confronts the injury (R). Commentators who hear it as a general moral address rather than a historically singular rebuke note the passive *qīla* — “when it is said” — which in Qur'anic idiom often signals a standing address rather than a one-time utterance. That openness universalizes the verse. Any community that has a practice of commanding the good will, at some point, say to those who destabilize it, “Do not corrupt,” and the response that makes this verse perennial is the response that does not contest the facts but contests the names. Contemporary readers who have seen environmental ruin defended as “development,” or exploitation defended as “stability,” will recognize the same renaming without needing a Medinan map.

**Corruption as Disobedience, and Disobedience as Corruption**

What counts as *fasād fī al-arḍ* — corruption upon/in the earth? The early interpreter Ibn ʿAbbās and others gloss it as open disobedience against Allah, and others extend it to the *result* of such disobedience (47:22): when the bond between creature and Creator is severed, the bonds among creatures follow. The Qur'an’s own pairing of *fasād* with its opposite confirms this breadth. *Fasād* is not petty misconduct; it is the unmooring of the order that *iṣlāḥ* would secure. That is why the same opposite appears in the summons to the Prophet Shuʿayb’s people to give full measure and not to corrupt after reform (*lā tufsidū fī al-arḍ baʿda iṣlāḥihā*, 7:85-86), and why the Qur'an can later summarize an entire age as *“corruption has appeared on land and sea for what people’s hands have earned”* (30:41). The hypocrite’s corruption is not limited to a single lie in a single assembly; it is a way of handling the earth — its trusts, its contracts, its commons — as if the earth had no Lord.

**We Are Only Peacemakers — The “Only” That Excludes**

*“We are only peacemakers”* — *innamā naḥnu muṣliḥūn* — uses the restrictive *innamā* (“only,” “merely”) to exclude any other description. The profession is totalizing. It does not say “We do some reform among other things”; it says reform exhausts who we are. That exclusivity is the linguistic trace of the disease. A heart that has learned to hold “We believe” without believing (2:8) will learn to hold “We only reform” without reforming, and the *only* performs the same function in both sentences: it seals the profession against qualification. Classical rhetoricians note that *innamā* is the particle of confinement; here it confines identity to the very activity that the rebuke has just denied. The more intense the rebuke, the more intense the confinement, so that correction does not soften the claim but hardens it.

**Two Ways the Claim Can Be Meant**

The source distinguishes two plausible intentions behind *“We are only peacemakers.”* On one reading, they sincerely believe they are reforming — their lukewarm mediation between believers and outright rejectors is, in their eyes, a virtue, a bridging, a keeping of peace by keeping both options open. On the other, they know they are not reforming but find it useful to be seen as bridge-builders, so that neither side expels them. The sūrah does not need to decide between sincerity and cynicism because the disease produces both by turns. At times the hypocrite believes his own renaming; at times he deploys it. The sūrah’s next verse will cut through the distinction with a divine repudiation that does not inquire into motive: *“No, it is they who are the corruptors”* (2:12). The verdict attaches to the act, however it was intended, so that motive is not permitted to launder effect.

**“Have Not Yet Come” — A Report That Generalizes the Portrait**

A report traced to the Companion Salmān al-Fārsī, noting that the people meant by this passage “have not yet come” (Ṭ), is transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and accepted by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr (IK) not to deny the Medinan referent but to prevent the portrait from being archived [UNVERIFIED: Salmān al-Fārsī “lam yaʾtū baʿd” — Companion tafsīr report in al-Ṭabarī, graded as exegetical athar without prophetic chain; treated as tafsīr, not hadith]. The acceptation — that the *description* fits any who replicate the *behavior* — is the normal hermeneutic for the hypocrisy passages. The verses were revealed about a known group around Medina; they remain recited so that any community can recognize the type when it reappears. That is why contemporary interpreters who extend *fasād* to include despoliation of the natural environment are not importing a foreign concern; they are applying the verse’s own definition — corruption as disobedience made manifest in the earth that disobedience was meant to steward.

**Why the Rebuke Uses “Earth” and Not “City”**

*Fī al-arḍ* — “upon the earth” or “in the land” — chooses the widest possible scene. The corruption at issue is not a municipal dispute but an earthly one. The earth (*arḍ*) in the Qur'an is the stage of *isti khlāf* — viceregency — and of *rizq* — provision that is to be spent from (2:3). To corrupt upon the earth is to mishandle the very field that (2:5) had described as cultivated by the mindful (*falāḥ*/*filāḥah*). The hypocrite who says “We are only reformers” therefore not only misnames his act; he misnames his stage. He acts as if the earth were a neutral platform for private advantage, while the sūrah has already defined it as a trust whose tilling determines flourishing. The rebuke *“Do not corrupt upon the earth”* is, in that light, the practical corollary of “do not deceive Allah” (2:9): to corrupt the earth whose provision was given to be spent is to attempt to deceive the Provider in the very matter of His provision.

**The First Public Test of an Interior Disease**

The sūrah has moved from interior (saying, deception, disease) to public: being told, answering. That move is deliberate. A disease that remained private could be pitied; a disease that, when addressed, renames itself as health has become contagious. The hypocrite who answers correction with “We only reform” is not merely self-deceived; he is now teaching his community to call corruption by a name that will make future correction harder. The next verse will therefore not merely deny his claim but expose its imperceptibility to its author: *though they are unaware* will be widened into *but they fail to perceive it*, so that the community learns not to be reassured by the smoothness of the answer.
