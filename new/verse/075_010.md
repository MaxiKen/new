## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:10

> on that Day one will cry, “Where is the escape?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Description to a Human Voice**

Six verses of cosmic signs end here, and the passage turns without warning to direct speech. Up to this point the sūrah has described what will happen to the sky and the eyes; now a person speaks, and the sentence is a cry rather than a statement. The shift is the point of the whole sequence. The collapse of the heavens matters in the Qur'an not as spectacle but because of what it does to the people standing under it, and the sūrah moves from the event to the voice in a single conjunction. Everything described so far was preparation for this one sentence. It is also the answer to the mockery in v. 6: the man who asked *when* now asks *where*, and the two questions could not be more different in tone.

**Al-Mafarr: a Place, Not a Plan**

The word cried out is *al-mafarr*, from a root meaning to flee. The noun is a place-noun: not the act of escaping but somewhere to escape *to*. That distinction carries the desperation of the sentence. The speaker is not asking for advice, a strategy, or a way of buying time, which is what a person in danger normally wants. He is asking for a destination, and the form of the question concedes that flight itself is assumed — only the destination is missing. The same root produces the description of that Day elsewhere, when people are portrayed running from the people they love most: *“on that Day every person will flee from their own siblings, and ˹even˺ their mother and father, and ˹even˺ their spouse and children.”* (80:34–36). Flight is universal there, and it is useless there too.

**A Question With No Addressee**

Nobody is named as the person being asked. The cry is not directed at God, at the angels, or at the other people in the scene, and that anonymity is part of its horror. A question addressed to someone presupposes that someone might answer and that an answer would help. This question is addressed to the situation. The Qur'an uses the device elsewhere to show a person who has passed beyond negotiation — the disbelievers detained before the Fire cry out to be sent back (6:27), and the wrongdoers ask for a short delay (14:44), and in both cases the plea is met with the fact that the time for pleading has closed. What distinguishes this cry is that it is not even a plea. The Qur'an addresses the same cry to people who have not yet reached that Day, and the preposition changes everything: *“Respond to your Lord before the coming of a Day from Allah that cannot be averted. There will be no refuge for you then, nor ˹grounds for˺ denial ˹of sins˺.”* (42:47). The word for refuge there is of the same family as the one in the next verse, and the notice is served in advance. Nothing about that Day is presented as a trap sprung on the unprepared; the exits are described as closing long before they close.

**The Escape Routes That Used to Exist**

In ordinary life there is always somewhere to go. A man under pressure can leave a city, change his company, occupy himself with work, or simply refuse to think about the thing that frightens him. The Qur'an treats the availability of these exits as the real content of the test, and it names the day they run out in several places: *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53); *“If only you could see when they will be horrified with no escape ˹on Judgment Day˺! And they will be seized from a nearby place.”* (34:51); *“Whatever ˹idols˺ they used to invoke besides Allah will fail them. And they will realize that they will have no escape.”* (41:48). The repetition is deliberate. The Qur'an is training its hearers to recognise that the exits they rely on are borrowed, not owned.

**What Is Actually Being Fled**

The object of the flight deserves attention, because the sūrah is precise about it. What arrives on that Day is not a place of punishment but a record and a reckoning, and what the speaker wants to escape is being seen. This is consistent with the Qur'an's account of why people resisted the message in the first place: not because the argument was weak but because accepting it would require them to answer for how they had lived. The horror is therefore not physical. It is exposure. A person who has spent a life arranging to be thought well of is presented with the only audience whose opinion he could not manage, and there is nowhere to stand where he is not visible.

**The Reversal of the Two Questions**

The structure the sūrah has built is a reversal, and it is worth laying out plainly. In v. 6 the denier asked a question he did not want answered, in a tone of contempt, about a date he had no intention of preparing for. Here he asks a question he needs answered, in a tone of panic, about a place he would give everything to find. The first question was a way of avoiding the subject; the second is the subject. Between them stand four verses in which the world he trusted comes apart, and the sūrah's insistence on the suddenness of that collapse is the answer to his original demand for a timetable. He wanted to know when, and the answer is: sooner than his arrangements assume. There is a final irony in the pairing. The question he asked in contempt was designed to make the Day look distant and unreal; the question he asks in terror is designed to make it stop. Both questions are about time, and the sūrah has spent the verses in between showing that time is precisely what is being taken away.
