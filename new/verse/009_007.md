## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:7

> How can such polytheists have a treaty with Allah and His Messenger, except those you have made a treaty with at the Sacred Mosque? So, as long as they are true to you, be true to them. Indeed Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Exposes a Contradiction**

The verse begins with how can, a question that does not seek information. It asks how a continuing treaty can be imagined with people whose conduct has made the treaty meaningless. The question directs attention to the contradiction between a formal promise and a record of betrayal. A covenant with Allah and His Messenger is not a phrase that protects a party while that party destroys the conditions under which it was made.

Yet the question is immediately qualified by an exception. Some treaty partners remain outside the general judgment because they have kept their word. The verse therefore does not say that no polytheist can have a treaty. It asks how such a treaty can continue for those who have made the relationship empty through their own actions, then protects those whose behavior has remained sound. The rhetoric is severe toward treachery and precise toward the faithful.

**A Covenant With God Has a Human Form**

The verse speaks of a treaty with Allah and His Messenger. This wording lifts a political agreement above ordinary convenience. When the Messenger makes a binding arrangement, the believers do not regard it as a private contract that can be adjusted whenever public opinion changes. Allah is named as the ultimate witness and guarantor. Breaking such an agreement is therefore not merely a failed negotiation; it is a violation of an obligation made under divine oversight.

The Qur'an uses similar language for allegiance: Those who pledge allegiance to you pledge allegiance only to Allah; Allah's Hand is above their hands (48:10). That verse does not turn human beings into gods. It teaches that a promise made to the Messenger carries a higher accountability because the Messenger's authority is entrusted by Allah. Verse 7 applies the same principle to a treaty. The parties deal with one another, but the covenant is recorded in a court larger than both parties.

**The Exception Is Based on Steadfast Conduct**

Classical exegetes differed over the historical group identified by the exception. Some associated it with members of Banū Bakr who did not participate in the breach connected to Ḥudaybiyah. Others mentioned Banū Khuzāʿah or particular Qurashī groups who received a period of notice and then changed their position. The reports vary, but the rule that unites them is stated in the verse itself: as long as they remain true, remain true to them.

The reciprocal verbs make the principle memorable. Their upright conduct is answered by upright conduct. This is not a demand that the Muslim side imitate every belief or custom of the partner. It is a demand that the Muslim side match fidelity with fidelity. A community does not become less principled by honoring a promise made to someone of another creed. It becomes more principled because its conduct is governed by Allah rather than by tribal resemblance.

**Taqwā Is Visible in Keeping Faith**

The closing reference to Allah's love for the reverent explains why the verse treats diplomacy as worship. Taqwā means guarding oneself from what displeases Allah. In a treaty, that guarding includes restraint from opportunism. The believer may be tempted to interpret another people's weakness as permission to ignore the agreement. The verse says the opposite: weakness in the other party does not remove the obligation that still stands.

A promise kept only while profitable is not the form of reverence praised here. Taqwā appears when the community honors the agreed duration despite changing calculations. This does not mean that a party must allow an actual breach to continue without response. It means that the response follows evidence, not appetite. Where the terms are being observed, the believer observes them. Where the terms are broken, the believer proceeds under the rules for breach. Precision is part of piety.

**The Sacred Mosque Does Not Cancel the Rule**

The mention of the Sacred Mosque recalls the setting of the treaty and the pilgrimage proclamation. The sanctuary was a place where old tribal hostilities were restrained and agreements could be declared before many witnesses. The location gives the verse a public and solemn background. A treaty connected with that setting cannot be treated as casual speech made in an alley. It had been attached to a recognized place, a recognized season, and a public relationship between communities.

At the same time, the sacred location does not make every arrangement permanent regardless of conduct. The verse's exception clause shows that sanctity and accountability work together. The Mosque is not a charm that turns bad faith into good faith, and the treaty is not a burden that binds one side after the other has openly destroyed it. What remains sacred is the obligation to judge honestly and to keep faith as long as faith remains.

**The Rule Travels Beyond the Original Dispute**

Verse 7 provides a standard for contracts, alliances, and personal promises. Before ending an agreement, ask whether the other party has actually breached it. Before making an accusation, distinguish direct action from rumor and indirect support from mere association. After finding a breach, communicate the change openly rather than taking secret advantage. These habits are not modern additions to the verse; they are the practical shape of its command.

The verse also protects believers from a tribal morality in which loyalty is offered only to one's own. Allah's love is attached to the reverent, not to a particular ethnicity or faction. A person who keeps faith with a non-Muslim treaty partner may be closer to the verse's ideal than someone who speaks constantly about religious loyalty while breaking promises to those under his protection. The question of verse 7 remains sharp: if the treaty is being honored, why would you dishonor it? The answer must be based on evidence, not resentment.
