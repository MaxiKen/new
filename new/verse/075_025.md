## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:25

> anticipating something devastating to befall them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Expectation Is Part of the Punishment**

The gloomy faces are not merely dark; they are described as knowing what is coming. The verb is one of reckoning and expectation, and in the Qur'an's eschatological vocabulary it is used for a confidence about the future rather than for a guess. These faces are fixed in an expression because their owners have already worked out what is about to happen to them. Nothing is left to hope for and nothing is left to find out. The significance of this detail is easy to miss: the punishment described here begins before it is administered. The interval between the certainty and the event is itself part of the sentence, which is why the verse attaches the anticipation to the faces rather than to the sentence that follows.

**Ẓann: Reckoning, Not Conjecture**

The word deserves a note, because in ordinary usage it can mean opinion or suspicion, and a reader might take it to mean that these people are merely worried. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the confidence of the blessed, which settles the sense: when the context is the Hereafter, the verb describes a settled expectation, whether of good or of harm. The people of the record given into the right hand say, *“I surely knew I would face my reckoning.”* (69:20). The same certainty, the same verb, applied in the other direction, is what darkens the faces in this verse. What distinguishes the two groups is not that one knows and the other guesses. It is what each has become certain about. There is a further detail in the grammar: the expectation is attributed to the faces themselves, in the feminine singular, matching the singular noun for face used in the previous verse. The dread belongs to the person, not to the scene. This is not a description of a frightening situation viewed from outside but of a condition the sufferer carries with him, which is the only way the Qur'an ever describes punishment: as something a person has become rather than something happening around him.

**Al-Fāqira: the Back-Breaking Calamity**

The noun is built on a root meaning to break the spine, and it belongs to the vocabulary of catastrophic disaster rather than of ordinary misfortune. The image is of a blow that leaves a person unable to stand, and its application here is to something that will befall the faces rather than merely be seen by them. The Qur'an uses physical severity in this way deliberately: an abstract penalty would not convey the total character of the event. This is not a setback to be absorbed and survived. It is the kind of catastrophe after which the person who suffers it is not the person he was, and the faces in this verse are turned toward it in advance. The spine is what holds a person upright, both physically and in the Qur'an's idiom of dignity, so a blow that breaks the back is a blow that removes the ability to stand. The same people describe their condition once the calamity has landed, and their account of it is instructive: *“My wealth has not benefited me! My authority has been stripped from me.”* (69:28–29). What breaks is precisely what they had been standing on.

**The Pain of Knowing Beforehand**

Scripture is candid that the interval is part of the suffering, and it describes the physiological shape of the dread in more than one place: *“Warn them ˹O Prophet˺ of the approaching Day when the hearts will jump into the throats, suppressing distress. The wrongdoers will have neither a close friend nor intercessor to be heard.”* (40:18). A second passage gives the same moment from the standpoint of those who can see where the path ends: *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53). Knowing, in these scenes, is not a mitigation. It is the beginning of the thing known, and there is no longer any route by which the knowledge can be put to use.

**Why Certainty Is Not Mercy Here**

A person might suppose that certainty would be kinder than suspense, since at least the waiting would be over. The Qur'an's answer is that certainty is only a relief when it can be acted on. Knowledge of a verdict one cannot appeal, cannot flee, and cannot bargain with does not shorten anything; it removes the last occupation the mind had, which was the arrangement of arguments and hopes. The faces in this verse have run out of those. *“And warn the people of the Day when the punishment will overtake ˹the wicked among˺ them, and the wrongdoers will cry, ‘Our Lord! Delay us for a little while. We will respond to Your call and follow the messengers!’”* (14:44) — and the reply they receive is a reminder that they had once sworn none of this would happen.

**The Two Certainties Side by Side**

The sūrah has now put the two classes in front of the reader with their inward states attached, and the parallel is exact. The radiant faces are looking at their Lord; the gloomy faces are looking at what is about to break them. Both groups possess certainty, both are past the stage of argument, and both have arrived at the state produced by what they did. The difference is everything, and it was settled long before the Day. The pairing also rules out a sentimental reading of either side. The radiant faces were not spared the reckoning — they were radiant *at* it, and the sūrah of the Heights shows them saying that the promise was true and that they had been given an inheritance for what they did (7:43). The gloomy faces are not unfortunate. They are accurate. That is the last thing the sūrah will say about the Hereafter before turning to the deathbed, where the same two destinies are decided one person at a time.
