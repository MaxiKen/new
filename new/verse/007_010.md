## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:10

> We have indeed established you on earth and provided you with a means of livelihood. ˹Yet˺ you seldom give any thanks.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Established Upon the Earth**

The verse is addressed to people generally, and it states that God has established them on the earth. The phrase carries both a spatial and a political sense, since the establishment of a people can mean the provision of a dwelling and also the granting of a measure of authority in the world. The Book uses the language of establishment for the communities that were given a place and a term, and the sūrah of the Cattle states at its close that God has placed people as successors on the earth (6:165).

The Book's account of the establishment of the first human being in the Garden is followed by his descent to the earth, where the sūrah of the Cow reports the statement that he would find in it a residence and provision for a time (2:36). The verse in front of us states the same arrangement for the audience, and it places the provision of the earth in the same sentence as the establishment upon it.

**The Means of Livelihood Placed Therein**

The verse names the provision that has been placed in the earth for the people who live on it, and the Book's accounts of the created order describe that provision at length. The sūrah of the Cattle lists the produce of the earth, the livestock, and the fruits of the palm, and the sūrah of the Bee describes the rain that brings out every kind of crop. The verse in front of us summarises the arrangement in a single phrase, and the chapter's later passages on food and livestock fill it in.

The provision is described as something made ready rather than something achieved. The Book's passages about the harvest and the rain present them as gifts, and the sūrah of the Prophets states that God has made the earth a place of settlement and the sky a canopy. The verse in front of us places the audience in a world already supplied, and the rebuke that follows rests on that fact.

**An Introduction to the Story of Adam**

The verse serves as the introduction to the account of Adam's creation and fall that begins with the following verse, and the Book joins the two themes in its other accounts of the same story. The sūrah of the Cow reports the announcement of a deputy on the earth before it recounts the prostration of the angels, and the sūrah of Hūd states that God established people on the earth and gave them provision after the destruction of Noah's people. The place of the verse in the sūrah's arrangement is therefore deliberate.

The account of Adam that follows explains how the earth came to be inhabited by the audience that is addressed here. The command to the angels, the refusal of Iblīs, the temptation, and the descent are all presented as the opening of the history that the sūrah has already begun with the destroyed communities. This verse states the position in which that history leaves the present generation.

**Gratitude and Its Absence**

The verse closes with the rebuke that the people seldom give thanks, and the Book uses the complaint in the same contexts elsewhere. The sūrah of the Ant states that most people do not give thanks, the sūrah of the Sheba repeats the complaint in its account of David's family, and the sūrah of Yūnus states that God is full of grace toward people but that most of them do not give thanks.

The complaint is directed at the failure to draw the connection between the gift and the Giver. The Book's passages about the created order present the rain, the crops, and the livestock as evidence of the One who provides, and the verse in front of us states that the people seldom make the connection. The sūrah's later passages return to the same complaint in their treatment of the harvest and the herds.

**The Provision and the Signs**

The Book places the provision of the earth among its signs, and the sūrah of the Cattle describes the variety of the crops and the livestock as a sign for people who believe. The passage in front of us supplies the premise of that argument: the audience lives on an earth that has been prepared for it, and the preparation is the work of the Lord whose revelation it is being asked to follow.

The signs are therefore not confined to the sky. The Book's habit of drawing evidence from the crops, the herds, and the fruits places the argument in the audience's daily experience, and the sūrah of the Bee describes honey, oil, and the fruits of the palm as signs for people who reflect. The verse under discussion begins that sequence for this chapter.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands tenth in the chapter's opening, and it closes the introductory section that runs from the letters to the account of the reckoning. The following verse begins the narrative of Adam's creation and Iblīs's refusal, and the verses after that continue the story to the descent from the Garden and the mission of the messengers.

The reader has been given the Book, the command, the warning, the reckoning, and now the world he inhabits. The provision in front of him is stated as a gift, and the gratitude that it calls for is named as the thing that is missing.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that God created the people and shaped them, and then commanded the angels to prostrate before Adam, and that they all prostrated except Iblīs, who refused (7:11). The verse opens the account of the first human being, and it follows the mention of the earth and its provision.

The two verses together establish the position of the audience within the created order: the earth has been made habitable for them, and their first ancestor was honoured above the angels. The sūrah then recounts how the honour was tested, and the reader is given the story of the refusal that the audience is being warned against repeating.
