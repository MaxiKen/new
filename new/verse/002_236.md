## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:236

> There is no blame if you divorce women before the marriage is consummated or the dowry is settled. But give them a ˹suitable˺ compensation—the rich according to his means and the poor according to his. A reasonable compensation is an obligation on the good-doers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Unconsummated Marriage**

The code now takes up a case at the margin of its own subject: a marriage contracted but never consummated, and possibly never priced — the bridewealth undesignated — dissolved by divorce. The opening clause lifts blame: there is no fault in this divorce. The exemption is worth pausing on, because divorce elsewhere in the code carries the tone of a permitted thing that God dislikes, hedged with counts, waiting periods, and retained-or-released obligations. Here the apparatus falls away almost entirely. No waiting period attaches — the sūrah of the Confederates legislates the same case with the same conclusion: a believing woman divorced before she has been touched has *no waiting period for you to count* (33:49) — because the waiting's function, the womb's clarification, has no object where consummation never occurred. The verse's blame-lifting and the Confederates' count-lifting are the same mercy stated twice: where the marriage never became a shared life, its ending is not a shared catastrophe, and the law declines to manufacture one.

**What Remains: The Provision**

Blame is lifted, but the woman does not leave empty-handed. *But give them a ˹suitable˺ compensation* — the mutʿah, a consolatory grant distinct from the bridewealth, and the verse's legislation is entirely about its scaling: *the rich according to his means and the poor according to his*. The formula appears twice in the sentence, and the doubling is the point — the obligation is universal and its amount is personal, measured against the giver's capacity rather than against any fixed schedule. The same bi-māʾūn logic governed the nursing provision two verses earlier (2:233) and the code's whole treatment of maintenance: the law binds the conscience at full strength and binds the purse proportionally. The jurists add a fallback for disputes: where the couple cannot agree on the amount, the measure becomes half of what a woman of similar standing would have received as bridewealth — the compensation anchored to the social arithmetic of the marriage that never was.

**An Obligation on the Good-Doers**

The verse's closing formula names the addressee's character rather than his legal status: *A reasonable compensation is an obligation on the good-doers* — ḥaqqan ʿalā'l-muḥsinīn. The word choice has been read two ways that reinforce each other. Legally, it confirms the obligation: the mutʿah in this case is not a courtesy but a ḥaqq, a claim — and the sūrah's later general ruling, *Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women—a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺* (2:241), restates the same duty on the register of taqwā. Spiritually, it describes the payer: the man who pays honorably is enacting iḥsān, the excellence the Qur'an consistently places beyond the minimum — and the verse assigns the minimum itself to the excellent. The effect is characteristic of this code's drafting: the floor of obligation is installed at a height that only the good-doer's conscience will recognize, so that the law's least demanding clause still calls its addressee upward.

**Why No Blame Attaches**

The commentators read the blame-lifting against the case's social reality. A marriage contracted without consummation and without a settled bridewealth was, in the community's circumstances, often a tentative arrangement — concluded at a distance, interrupted by death of enthusiasm or of a party's circumstances, or entered by proxy and never activated. To attach full divorce-consequences to its dissolution would penalize formality where no shared life existed to break. The verse's structure confirms the reading: blame is lifted, the waiting is lifted, the half-bridewealth rule of the designated-dowry case does not reach here — and what replaces them is a scaled gift. The law distinguishes, with unusual clean lines, between the ending of a marriage that was lived and the canceling of a contract that was not, and it prices the difference honestly: the woman is owed compensation for the dissolved expectation, not support for a dissolved household.

**The Rich and the Poor, One Sentence**

The scaling clause deserves one more look at its rhetoric. *The rich according to his means and the poor according to his* places both men inside one obligation with one sentence — the code refuses the two moves that legal systems commonly make here. It does not exempt the poor man, treating the compensation as a rich man's courtesy; his grant is smaller but it is owed. And it does not flatten the rich man's duty to the poor man's measure, treating a token as sufficient from everyone; his grant scales with his capacity. Between the exemption and the flattening lies the verse's justice: identical in kind, differentiated in amount, judged by capacity — the same architecture the sūrah will state as a principle in its closing verses, *Allah does not task any soul beyond its capacity* (2:286), here applied not to the soul's burden but to the purse's. The dismissed wife receives from both men; each gives what his giving means.

**The Seam in the Code**

Positioned where it sits, the verse functions as the code's seam-piece. Behind it stand the divorce verses proper — the twice-counted ṭalāq, the retention in honor or release in grace, the waiting periods and the reciprocal rights (2:228–232). Ahead lies the general provision-ruling (2:241) and then the pivot to prayer (2:238) that carries the sūrah out of domestic law. The unconsummated-marriage case tests the code at its edge and demonstrates its method: the same instruments — blame, waiting, compensation, capacity — recalibrated for a case where the marriage's substance never materialized. The exegetes' cross-reference to the general bridal-payment legislation (4:24) completes the picture: the Qur'an's marriage law knows the price of every configuration, including the configuration that ended before it began. No woman leaves this code without a settled claim, and no man leaves it obligated beyond his means — the two guarantees, in the smallest of the code's cases, stated together.
