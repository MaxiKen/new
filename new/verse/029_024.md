## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24

> But the only response of Abraham’s people was to say: “Kill him or burn him!” But Allah saved him from the fire. Surely in this are signs for people who believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Town With Nothing Left to Say**

The verse reports the town's reply and marks it as the only reply they had. Abraham had laid out an argument in the previous verses: the idols they worshiped could not see, hear, provide, or benefit them, and the One who made them was the One to be worshiped. The Book records no attempt by the townspeople to answer any part of that case. What they produce instead is a decision about the man, and the decision is to remove him. This pattern recurs throughout the Book and is treated as a recognizable signal, since a position that cannot be defended in argument is defended by force. The sentence is therefore a verdict on the debate as much as on the men: the case was closed, and violence is what the losing side had left.

Their resort to force also marks the end of the argument. The Book links the two moves in a single sentence, reporting that the only answer of Abraham's people was a call for his death, and the sequence shows a public case being lost and then overruled by the crowd that lost it.

**The Two Options They Offered**

"Kill him or burn him" — the alternatives move from the ordinary to the spectacular. The Book fills in the scene in other chapters. In Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ the conclusion is recorded as an appeal to burn him in order to avenge their gods, if they must act at all (21:68), and in Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt they are described building a furnace and casting him into the blazing fire (37:97). The language of vengeance tells the reader what the idols meant to their worshipers: an insult to a god was a public injury, and the community's honour was at stake in the response. The Book is precise about the motive because the motive explains the scale. This was not a judicial proceeding against a heretic; it was the defence of a shrine, and the shrine could not defend itself.

The choice between the two methods is also a measure of how the debate had concluded. A killing would have been an act of private vengeance and could be argued about afterwards; a public burning was a demonstration, staged in front of the community, addressed to the gods as much as to the man. Commentators note that the town resolved the matter by consensus, which is the last resource of a society that has exhausted its reasoning. The sūrah has been tracking collective decisions throughout its narratives: a market that agreed on its scales, tribes that agreed to treat their messengers with contempt, a court that agreed to build a tower. In each case the agreement is presented as the crime, since it implicates the community in a decision no individual would have needed to defend alone. Abraham's town reached the same point, and the fire was the form its agreement took.

**The Fire That Was Ordered to Be Cool**

The rescue is reported in the fewest possible words here — God saved him from the fire — and expanded elsewhere with a command that has no parallel in the Book: the fire is addressed directly and told to be cool and safe for Abraham (21:69). The created element obeys its maker rather than its nature, which is the point the sentence makes about God's authority over the physical world. What burned everything else became, by decree, temperature and safety for one man. The Book's narration is careful not to describe Abraham performing a miracle of his own; he is the passive recipient of the act. When the same scene appears again, the outcome is stated in terms of the plotters rather than the plot: they sought to harm him, and they were made the worst losers (21:70).

**Rescued From the Fire, Not From the Test**

The deliverance granted here is not a removal from conflict, and the reader should not read it that way. Abraham's difficulties continue after the fire; the chapter will report his migration from the town and the family news that reaches him afterward. What the fire episode establishes is the pattern that the whole sūrah has been describing: God's intervention does not spare the believer the trial, and it does not remove him from the danger. It holds him inside it, the way the ark held Noah's passengers inside a drowning world. The sentence is placed here as evidence for the chapter's central claim, not as an escape hatch from it, and the man who walks out of the furnace still has a journey ahead of him.

The qualifier also explains why reports of these events divide their audiences. Two people can be told the same account of a fire that did not burn its occupant, and one takes it as evidence while the other files it away as a story, and the Book attributes that difference to what the hearer already brings to the hearing.

**Why the Signs Are Said to Be for Believers**

The Book says the signs in this rescue are for people who believe, and the phrase is chosen rather than the broader word that would include everyone who thinks. A sign functions only in relation to a stance. The townspeople who watched the fire become harmless saw an event, and their prior commitment prevented them from reading it as anything; the believer sees the same event as an act of God. This is not a claim that evidence is available only to those already convinced; the Book has just spent several verses arguing that evidence is available to anyone who travels and looks. The distinction is about what a person does with the evidence, and the Book consistently locates the difference in the disposition of the observer rather than in the clarity of the sign.

**The Same Scene in Another Sūrah**

Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ gives the scene with its dialogue intact: the people call for the fire to avenge their gods, God commands the fire to be cool and safe for Abraham, and the verse that follows reports that they sought to harm him and were made the worst losers (21:68–70). Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt supplies the means — a furnace prepared for the purpose — and the same conclusion that the plotters came out inferior (37:97–98). Three chapters recount one attempt from three angles, and none of them describes Abraham negotiating, defending himself, or escaping. The pattern is consistent with everything the sūrah has said about trials: the servant endures, God acts, and the outcome is recorded in a way that reminds the reader whose story it actually is. The town that could not answer a single argument is remembered for what it tried to do with fire, and the man it condemned is remembered as the one delivered from it. That is a lesson about the relative weight of arguments and force, and the sūrah has arranged for its readers to see both endings at once. The fire that was intended to close the argument became the evidence that settled it, and the town that demanded a demonstration received one it could not survive.
