## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:198

> There is no blame on you for seeking the bounty of your Lord ˹during this journey˺. When you return from ’Arafât, praise Allah near the sacred place and praise Him for having guided you, for surely before this ˹guidance˺ you were astray.

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Blame in the Market**

The verse's first clause lifts a scruple: there is no blame on you in seeking bounty from your Lord — that is, in trading during the pilgrimage. Al-Qurṭubī records the ruling the clause establishes: commerce during the hajj is permissible and does not nullify the rite, though refraining from it and devoting oneself wholly to the spiritual dimension remains the better course. The permission answers a real tension in the Arabian hajj: the pilgrimage season was also the peninsula's great fair — the markets of ʿUkāẓ and Dhū'l-Majāz ran alongside the rites, and the pre-Islamic pilgrim was merchant and worshiper at once. Early Muslim scrupulosity appears to have doubted whether the two could coexist inside the consecrated journey, and the verse resolves the doubt in the direction of permission — with a wording that keeps the priority straight: what the trader seeks is *bounty from your Lord*, the market's gain reframed as divine provision rather than secular interruption. The clause belongs to the same legislation as the provision-command before it (2:197): the hajj is a journey of bodies through a real economy, and the revelation sanctifies the economy's honest use instead of demanding its pretense of absence.

**The Pouring Forth from ʿArafāt**

The second clause places the pilgrim in the hajj's central geography: when you pour forth from ʿArafāt. The plain of ʿArafāt, east of Mecca, hosts the pilgrimage's essential rite — the standing (wuqūf) on the ninth of Dhū'l-Ḥijjah, when the assembled pilgrims spend the afternoon in prayer and remembrance before the plain; the tradition regards this standing as the hajj's sine qua non, the rite without which there is no pilgrimage. The word for the departure — *ifaḍah*, a pouring out — describes the mass movement at sunset, when the whole assembly flows westward toward Muzdalifah in a single current of people. The verse legislates what happens at that flow's arrival: remember God at the sacred monument — al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām. The exegetes located the monument in two readings: some identify the hill named Quzaḥ at Muzdalifah, where the prayer-leader stands to lead the combined maghrib and ʿishāʾ prayers after the pilgrims come from ʿArafāt; others, al-Rāzī among them, take it as Muzdalifah itself — the place of prayer and remembrance where the pilgrims go after the standing, which the Qur'an's own phrase elsewhere calls the sacred monument. Both readings fix the same night: the pilgrim's first station after the plain, in the dark, before the return to Minā.

**Remember God at the Sacred Monument**

The remembrance commanded at Muzdalifah has a defined content in the tradition's practice: the pilgrims remember God there through the standing formulas — al-ḥamduli'Llāh, praise be to God; Allāhu akbar, God is greatest; subḥāna'Llāh, glory be to God — recited through the night of gathering and at the prayers. The command's timing is instructive. The pilgrim has just come from the hajj's overwhelming moment — the standing on the plain, the sight of the whole ummah gathered — and the verse stations remembrance at the journey's next stage, so that the peak is not followed by emptiness but by named praise. The night at Muzdalifah is thus legislated as the plain's echo-chamber: what ʿArafāt held in presence, the sacred monument holds in words. The same structure of remembrance-after-rite governs the whole passage — the numbered days at Minā will be days of remembering God (2:203), and the rites' completion will end in the remembrance that outdoes the fathers' (2:200). The hajj, in this legislation, is a sequence of stations each capped by dhikr, so that the body's journey is continuously converted into the tongue's and the heart's.

**Remember Him as He Guided You**

The verse's second remembrance has a different object: remember Him *as He guided you* — or, in the reading the translation follows, praise Him for having guided you. The first remembrance is placed (at the monument); this one is motivated (by the guidance). The two together define the pilgrim's gratitude: God remembered at the place God appointed, and God remembered for the leading that brought the pilgrim there — to Islam, to the ability, to the road, to the plain. The clause *though before you were astray* gives the memory its personal history, and its addressees are the Prophet's own generation: the Arabs who had performed this very pilgrimage for centuries in idolatry, who had stood on ʿArafāt calling on Hubal and the stones, and who now stand there calling on God alone. The guidance remembered at Muzdalifah is therefore the guidance of a whole people — the hijrah-generation's passage from the jahiliyyah's rites to the Abrahamic rite restored — and each pilgrim after them inherits the clause: everyone who arrives at the monument has been guided there from somewhere else, and the verse makes the remembering of that somewhere the night's second duty.

**The Bounty and the Guidance in One Verse**

The two halves of the verse, market and monument, are joined by a single logic. The seeking of bounty is permitted because the journey is God's — the trade itself is reframed as seeking from the Lord; the remembrance is commanded because the guidance is God's — the arrival itself is reframed as His leading. In both clauses the worldly element of the pilgrimage (its commerce, its travel, its crowds) is not suppressed but re-owned: the market becomes bounty-seeking from the Lord, the night's arrival becomes guidance remembered. This is the verse's method with the whole hajj — a rite of bodies in a real world, legislated so that nothing real is denied and everything real is referred. The better course al-Qurṭubī notes — abstaining from trade for total devotion — stands inside the same method as the higher rung of a ladder whose floor is permission: the pilgrim may trade and may not trade, but he may not forget, at the monument, in the dark after the plain, that both his market and his worship are bounty and guidance from one Lord. The verse that opens with the blameless purse closes with the blameless tongue, and between them the sacred place waits.

**The Station in the Pilgrimage's Arc**

Within the passage's sequence the verse holds the middle station. The legislation began with the rites' completion and their concessions (2:196), moved to the months and the consecrated conduct (2:197), and now reaches the pilgrimage's heart — ʿArafāt and the night that follows it. What comes next in the sequence is the movement onward with the people (2:199), the remembrance after the rites' completion (2:200–202), and the numbered days of Minā (2:203). This verse is therefore the arc's summit and its pivot: the plain where the hajj's essence stands, and the monument where its meaning is spoken. The tradition's own geography confirms the pivot's weight — the pilgrim's whole journey moves toward the standing, and everything after it is descent into completion, the rites winding down through Minā's days toward the farewell circuit of the Kaʿbah. At the summit, the verse gives the pilgrim the two things to carry down: the permission that keeps his worldly life unbroken, and the remembrance that keeps his guidance unforgettable — bounty sought from the Lord on the road out, and praise rendered to the Lord at the sacred place, for having guided one who was astray.
