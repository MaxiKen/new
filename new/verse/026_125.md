## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:125

> I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hūd Grounds His Call in Trustworthiness**

Hūd describes himself as a trustworthy messenger. The statement identifies both the quality of the bearer and the authority of the mission. He is not presenting a personal theory about ʿĀd or seeking influence through a new religious program. He has been entrusted with a message from God and is committed to conveying it faithfully.

Trustworthiness matters because the call requires the people to challenge their worship, power, and way of life. They need to know whether Hūd is manipulating them for money, status, or private control. His declaration answers the suspicion before it becomes an excuse. He speaks as a servant under God, not as an ambitious rival to the chiefs.


Hūd's brotherhood means the people have had the opportunity to observe him. He is not an unknown voice whose character they cannot assess. His life among them gives weight to the claim of trustworthiness. The community can compare his public conduct with the message he now brings.

Familiarity does not automatically prove a person's truth, but it removes the argument that Hūd appeared without a history. A messenger's known honesty creates a fair basis for hearing him. If the people reject him, they must address the message rather than invent a motive that his life does not support.


**Trustworthiness Is Not Mere Social Respectability**

A person can be respected by society and still be morally false. Hūd's trustworthiness is deeper than good manners or social approval. It includes fidelity to God, accuracy in conveying the warning, and freedom from the desire to turn revelation into personal gain.

That distinction is important for every religious claim. A charming speaker may attract people without being trustworthy, while a difficult messenger may speak truthfully despite lacking popularity. Character supports the message, but the message must still be tested by its content and source. Hūd asks ʿĀd to listen to both.


Hūd does not say that his own wisdom makes him the source of truth. He calls himself a messenger. The title means that his authority is received and conveyed. He can be trusted because he does not secretly replace God's word with his own desire.

This also limits obedience to Hūd in the right way. The people follow him as the representative of God's command, not as an independent object of worship. A true messenger points beyond himself. His trustworthiness is shown by his refusal to collect the people's devotion for his own person.



**The Message Requires Moral Courage From the Bearer**

A trustworthy messenger cannot edit the warning to protect his reputation. Hūd must tell ʿĀd that their power, construction, and violence are subject to God's judgment. Such speech risks the anger of chiefs who benefit from the existing order. His trustworthiness includes the courage to say what the people need rather than what will preserve his comfort.

Courage does not mean enjoying confrontation. Hūd speaks because the people need guidance, not because he wants to defeat them. The messenger's firmness serves mercy. If he hid the danger, his politeness would become betrayal of the trust placed in him.


Quran 11:51 records Hūd telling ʿĀd that he asks no reward for the message and that his reward is with the One who created him. That parallel adds a concrete sign of his trustworthiness. He is not seeking money from the people whose beliefs he challenges.

Freedom from payment does not mean the message has no value. It means its value is not sold to the audience. Hūd's reward is with God, so the chiefs cannot buy his silence and the poor cannot be excluded for lacking payment. The mission remains open to the whole people.


**Trustworthiness Protects the Message From Suspicion**

When a community rejects guidance, it often claims that the messenger wants power or wealth. Hūd's declaration forces ʿĀd to test that claim honestly. If he seeks no reward and speaks as one of them, their refusal cannot rest only on an invented motive.

This does not mean that every critic is dishonest. It means that criticism should concern the truth of the message, not a motive assigned without evidence. Hūd offers his character and mission for examination. The people remain free to disagree, but they cannot fairly call the message a private scheme without showing what scheme exists.


Hūd's trustworthiness is not measured by whether his warning makes ʿĀd feel secure. A false guide promises safety while leaving the heart unchanged. Hūd warns because the people are in danger, and he asks them to fear God before their strength becomes their ruin.

The reliable guide is the one who tells the truth about consequence and mercy. Hūd does not flatter the people into believing that their power guarantees survival. He points them to the Lord who can forgive, guide, and judge. Trust in the messenger must lead to trust in God, not to confidence in the messenger's personality.


Hūd's sentence does not force belief, but it removes a reason for dismissing him without thought. The people know their brother and hear his claim of trustworthy service. A fair hearing requires them to consider whether the Lord's message is true rather than whether the message flatters their culture.

The verse therefore establishes a standard for prophetic speech. The messenger is truthful, faithful, independent of payment, and clear about his role. A community that rejects such a messenger is responsible for what it refuses. Hūd has identified the basis on which ʿĀd should listen: not social prestige, but trustworthiness under God.

Trust begins the hearing, but obedience completes it. Hūd's people are not merely asked to admire an honest man; they are asked to follow the Lord whose message he faithfully carries. That is the test of whether they have understood his trustworthiness.
