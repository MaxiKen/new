## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:32

> That is so. And whoever honours the symbols of Allah, it is certainly out of the piety of the heart.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Seal on the Passage**

*Dhālika* — that is how it is. The same demonstrative closed verse 30, and here it closes the second half of the same instruction: the lawfulness of cattle, the impurity of idols, the falsehood of speech, and the parable of the falling man.

The repetition is structural. The sūrah is marking the end of a unit the way a scribe marks a paragraph, and both seals are followed by a statement of principle rather than by further regulation. Verse 30 ended with what to avoid; verse 32 ends with where the observance actually lives.

**Shaʿāʾir: Emblems, Not Merely Rituals**

*Wa-man yuʿaẓẓim shaʿāʾira Allāhi* — and whoever magnifies the emblems of God. The word *shaʿāʾir* is broader than the word rendered "rituals."

Al-Qurṭubī connected it to the root *sh-ʿ-r*, which carries the sense of knowing and of being conscious of something, and on that basis described the *shaʿāʾir* as those things in which God has placed a means by which He makes Himself known or teaches. That is why the term covers not only the rites themselves but the places at which they are performed and the animals with which they are carried out.

Sūrah al-Baqarah gives a concrete instance: *“Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺”* (2:158). Two low hills are called emblems of God, which settles the question of what kind of thing an emblem is. It is not intrinsically impressive. It is a marker that points.

**"It Is From the Reverence of Hearts"**

*Fa-innahā min taqwā al-qulūb* — indeed it is from the reverence of hearts. The sentence is the verse's whole claim, and the sūrah has been building toward it since verse 30's command to shun false speech.

The tradition locates reverence in the same place. Abū Hurayrah reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, "Reverence is here," and pointed to his chest three times; in the same report he said that God does not look at your bodies or your faces but looks at your hearts. It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2564).

The placement of *qulūb*, hearts, at the end of the sentence gives it the emphasis. The question the verse answers is where an action's value is registered, and the answer is not the action. Magnifying an emblem is outward; what makes it count is the state of the person doing it, and that state is invisible.

**The Sūrah's Running Argument With Ritualism**

The sūrah has been circling this point since verse 11. The man on the brink performed the outward acts of the religion and was toppled by the first trial. The man of verse 12 called upon something with the right vocabulary and the wrong object. Verse 9 described a man who turned his shoulder away to lead others astray.

All three are people whose religion was entirely available on the surface, and the sūrah has been dismantling that possibility one figure at a time. Verse 32 states the principle the figures illustrated: what God receives from a rite is the state of the person performing it.

The warning has a particular edge here because the rites in question were inherited. The pilgrimage was not invented by the revelation; it was Abraham's, and the Arabs had been performing a corrupted version of it for centuries. A restored rite performed by habit is the most likely failure mode of any religion, and this verse is the sūrah's guard against its own success.

**What Magnification Looks Like in Practice**

The commentators disagreed usefully over what magnifying the emblems means concretely, and both readings are worth keeping.

Al-Rāzī took it as an attitude: to magnify the rites is to treat the worship of God through the pilgrimage as a weighty matter rather than a formality. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī took it more materially, in the context of the sacrificial animals: that they should be well fed, sound and good-looking, and that a sick or injured animal should not be offered.

The second reading is the more searching, because it tests the first. A person who believes the rite is weighty will bring an animal he is not ashamed of, and the verse that was rejected in Sūrah al-Baqarah sets the standard: *“He replied, ‘Allah says, “It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish”’”* (2:71). What is offered is measured by what it cost.

**The Verse's Warning About Outward Performance**

Read in context, verse 32 is the sūrah's guard against its own legislation. Verses 26 to 31 have given a great deal of outward detail: a site, a call, a circuit, a sacrifice, days, animals, a place of slaughter, and a prohibition on false speech. Every one of those can be performed by somebody whose heart is somewhere else entirely.

The sūrah knows this, which is why it interrupts itself. The rites are real and required, and the thing that makes them worship is not in the rites. A pilgrimage performed without reverence is a journey; a sacrifice performed without it is a slaughter.

The point is not that the outward act is dispensable — the sūrah has just spent six verses insisting on it — but that the inward state is what the outward act is for, and a religion that gets the first without the second has the shell of the thing it was given.

There is a corollary that runs in the other direction, and the sūrah states it by implication. If reverence is what reaches God, then the person who possesses the reverence but cannot perform the rite is not excluded from what the rite was for. The sūrah opens with the Hour and closes with the command to strive; in between it legislates an enormous amount of outward detail, and it interrupts itself here to prevent any reader from concluding that the detail is the substance.
