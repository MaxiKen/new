## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:27

> Call ˹all˺ people to the pilgrimage. They will come to you on foot and on every lean camel from every distant path,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Given to a Man With Nobody to Hear Him**

*Wa-adhdhin fī al-nāsi bi-l-ḥajji* — and proclaim the pilgrimage among the people. The verb is from the root related to calling out publicly, the language of a herald's announcement rather than of private instruction.

The circumstance is the difficulty the commentators dwelt on: Abraham was standing in an uninhabited valley. The reports preserved in the exegetical literature say that he asked how his voice could reach people who were not there, and was told to make the call and that the conveying of it was God's business. [UNVERIFIED: this account is reported by al-Ṭabarī and others without a chain I can verify in this session; it is cited as commentary rather than as established report.]

Whether or not one accepts the miraculous version, the structure of the verse is clear enough on its own. The command is issued, and the response is stated in the same breath as though it had already happened. The sūrah does not wait to see whether anyone comes.

**"They Will Come to You"**

*Yaʾtūka* — they will come to you. The future tense describes an event that, at the time of the command, had no visible cause in existence.

The grammatical confidence is the point of the verse. A pilgrimage is not a gathering that a person organizes; it is a gathering that is summoned into being by a call whose effectiveness does not belong to the caller. Every pilgrim who has arrived in that valley since is, on this verse's account, an answer to a command issued before there was anyone to answer it — which is why the tradition can speak of the pilgrims of every century being present in Abraham's call.

The sūrah also uses the address carefully: they come *to you*, Abraham. The founder is told that the traffic will arrive at him, and the promise is personal before it is institutional.

Abraham's own prayer at the raising of the foundations asks for exactly what verse 27 delivers: *“Show us our rituals, and turn to us in grace”* (2:128). The rites requested before the House is finished, the pilgrims promised before the valley holds anyone — the whole passage runs on commands and prayers that precede their fulfillment. *Yaʾtūka* belongs to that grammar of certainty: the coming is stated as the prayer's answer, and the centuries of arrivals since are the answer still arriving.

**"On Foot"**

*Rijālan* — on foot, walking. The word is the plural of a man on his own feet, and it is placed first in the list of arrivals.

Several commentators drew from it the ruling that walking to the pilgrimage is more excellent than riding, and the inference is a natural one: the word is doing more than describing transport. Walking is the arrival of a person who has spent himself to get there, and the Qur'an's habit is to value the difficulty of an act as part of the act. A pilgrimage that costs nothing is not the thing this verse describes.

The detail also guarantees that the pilgrimage is not an elite observance. Anyone who can walk can come, and the poor arrive in the same manner as everyone else — a point the following verses reinforce by commanding that the sacrificial meat be fed to the destitute.

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the obligation with the ability clause that keeps it open: *“Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people”* (3:97). Whoever is able — the finding of a way, not the owning of a mount. *Rijālan* first in verse 27's list is that clause enacted: the walker has found his way on foot, and the verse counts him before the rider. The obligation ends where inability begins, but inability is never mere poverty of transport.

**"Every Lean Camel"**

*Wa-ʿalā kulli ḍāmirin* — and upon every lean one. The commentators took the animal as the camel, and the leanness as the effect of the journey: a beast arrives thin because it has come a long way.

The image is precise and quietly moving. What is promised to Abraham is not a procession of sleek animals but a procession of exhausted ones, each carrying a person who has already spent weeks getting there. The leanness is evidence, not decoration; it is how the verse says, without saying it, that the call will be answered from farther away than anyone could travel comfortably.

There is a rhetorical effect too. The valley had no water and no pasture. The promise of camels arriving is the promise of a route being opened across country that had none.

The valley's provisioning is named as a standing favour, and the fruits arrive from elsewhere: *“Have We not established for them a safe haven ˹in Mecca˺ to which fruits of all kinds are brought as a provision from Us?”* (28:57). Brought — the barren hollow fed by routes it did not build. The lean camels of verse 27 are those routes in motion: beasts arriving thin across country with no pasture, carrying people to a sanctuary whose food comes the same way they do, from far away, as provision.

**What the Call Was Answering**

It is easy to read the command as though the pilgrimage were summoned into existence for its own sake. Abraham's own prayer, recorded in Sūrah Ibrāhīm, supplies the reason the valley was inhabited at all: *“Our Lord! I have settled some of my offspring in a barren valley, near Your Sacred House, our Lord, so that they may establish prayer. So make the hearts of ˹believing˺ people incline towards them and provide them with fruits, so perhaps they will be thankful”* (14:37).

The call of verse 27 is the answer to that prayer. A man left his family in an unwatered valley for the sake of the prayer, and asked that people's hearts be made to incline toward them. The pilgrimage is the mechanism by which the request was granted: hearts incline, and the valley fills.

That framing changes what the gathering is for. Pilgrims do not come to make a desert town prosperous; they come for the prayer and the rites, and the provision follows. The next verse will name both halves in a single sentence — benefits, and the mention of God's name — which is exactly the order of Abraham's own petition.

**"From Every Distant Pass"**

*Yaʾtīna min kulli fajjin ʿamīq* — they come from every deep ravine, every distant mountain pass. *Fajj* is a gap between mountains, a defile; *ʿamīq* is deep or far.

The geography is the theology here. Makkah sits in a hollow ringed by mountains, and the only way in is through passes. The verse pictures those passes filled, one after another, with people who set out from places the speaker could not have named.

That is what makes this the most universal sentence in the passage. The command in the first clause was *fī al-nāsi* — among humanity, all of it — and the response in the last clause matches it: not from every tribe of Arabia, but from every distant defile. The pilgrimage is described from its founding moment as a gathering of the whole inhabited world at a place chosen by nobody in it.

The House was made for exactly this gathering, and Sūrah al-Baqarah names its purpose at the founding: *“when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people”* (2:125). A centre — *mathābah*, a place of return, visited and revisited — for *the people*, all of them. The distant passes of verse 27 are the roads to that centre, and the depth of the ravines measures the width of the summons: a house made for humanity draws from every defile humanity lives behind.
