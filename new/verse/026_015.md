## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:15

> Allah responded, “Certainly not! So go, both of you, with Our signs. We will be with you, listening.

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Answers Fear Before Giving Further Instruction**

The response begins with a firm denial: certainly not. Moses has feared rejection, speech failing, and death, but God does not allow those fears to become the conclusion of the mission. The answer is brief because the servant needs assurance before he needs more detail. Divine reassurance does not argue that the danger is imaginary. It declares that the danger will not defeat the purpose for which Moses is being sent.

The force of the answer also corrects Moses' estimate of what he can carry alone. He had described several weaknesses, and God answers them with a command that includes Aaron. The reassurance is therefore not empty encouragement. It is attached to a changed situation: both brothers will go, and they will go with signs. Fear is met by companionship, evidence, and divine oversight.

**The Plural of Signs Gives the Mission Breadth**

Moses and Aaron are sent with God's signs, not with personal eloquence alone. The plural points to the signs that will support their claim, including the miracles associated with Moses and the public demonstration before Pharaoh. These signs are not tricks designed to win a contest. They are marks that identify the source of the mission and expose the weakness of Pharaoh's claim to ultimate authority.

A sign does not remove the need for speech. It points to the message and gives the audience reason to take the messenger seriously. Moses will still have to state the command and face objections. The combination of sign and word is significant. Evidence without interpretation can be misread, while words without divine support can be dismissed as a private opinion. The mission receives both.

**Aaron's Presence Is Part of the Divine Answer**

God does not tell Moses to overcome every difficulty by an act of solitary will. The two brothers are ordered to go together. That command confirms that Moses' request for Aaron was not a failure of confidence. It was a means God chose to use. A servant may recognize a need for partnership without doubting the Lord who assigned the work.

The shared mission also gives the audience a visible witness to the truth being proclaimed. Pharaoh will not be dealing with a private complaint from one anxious man. Two messengers will stand before him with one source of authority. Their relationship models cooperation rather than rivalry. Prophetic service is strengthened when gifts are joined under a command larger than either individual.

**Being With Them Means Active Oversight**

God says that He will be with them, listening. The phrase does not suggest physical limitation or that God learns facts only by hearing them. It assures Moses that every word spoken and every response given lies within divine knowledge and care. The brothers will not enter the court of Pharaoh unnoticed or unsupported. Their encounter is heard by the Lord who sent them.

That assurance addresses both fear and loneliness. A person speaking before power may feel that the ruler controls the room and that his own words vanish without effect. Divine accompaniment changes that perception. The messengers' speech has an audience beyond Pharaoh's court, and the court's threats are heard by the One who judges it. Moses and Aaron can therefore speak with care without believing that the outcome depends on the ruler's mood.

**Listening Also Honors the Detail of the Encounter**

The mention of listening gives the mission a personal texture. God is not merely issuing a distant order and leaving the servants to improvise. He knows the exchange as it unfolds. This supports the broader Qur'anic teaching that God knows secret speech, public claims, and the inner motives behind them. Pharaoh may stage the encounter for his chiefs, but the true witness is already present.

For the messengers, that knowledge is comfort. For Pharaoh, it is warning. No performance can make oppression righteous, and no accusation can hide the motive behind it. The brothers need not shout to make God hear them, nor flatter Pharaoh to make their words count. They must speak faithfully because the Lord is attentive to every part of the mission.

**Reassurance Leads Immediately to Obedience**

The verse does not end with comfort alone. After denying Moses' fear, God says go. Reassurance is meant to move the servant, not to create a permanent retreat in prayer. The right response to divine support is action under the command that follows.

This pattern offers a general rule for moral work. Bring fear honestly to God, accept the help He provides, and then take the next required step. Do not wait for every uncertainty to disappear. Moses and Aaron are not promised an agreeable Pharaoh or a painless encounter. They are promised signs and divine attention. That is enough to make obedience possible, and the command to go turns that possibility into duty.

**Divine Company Reorders the Meaning of Risk**

The danger before Moses and Aaron does not become small because God is with them. It becomes properly placed. Pharaoh can threaten bodies, reputations, and public standing, but he cannot decide whether the mission is true or whether the Lord hears it. Divine company moves the center of the encounter away from the ruler's power and back to God's authority.

This is why the answer is stronger than a promise of personal success. Success could be measured by Pharaoh's surrender, while divine companionship remains true even when a ruler resists. The brothers are sent to testify, not to control the response. They can therefore speak with courage, accept the limits of their role, and leave the final judgment to the Lord whose signs accompany them.
