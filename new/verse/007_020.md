## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:20

> Then Satan tempted them in order to expose what was hidden of their nakedness. He said, “Your Lord has forbidden this tree to you only to prevent you from becoming angels or immortals.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whisper**

The verse reports that Satan whispered to the pair, and the Book uses the language of whispering for the enemy's method throughout. The sūrah of the Heights itself states in its later instruction to the children of Adam that Satan is a manifest enemy, and the sūrah of the People uses the image of the whisperer who withdraws, who whispers into the hearts of people (114:4–5). The commentary tradition distinguishes between an audible low speech and a suggestion placed in the heart, and both readings describe an approach that does not announce itself.

The whisper follows the sentence passed on the adversary and the grant of his respite, and it shows the campaign of the previous verses beginning. The sūrah's warnings about the enemy's approach from every direction are given their first illustration here, and the temptation of the pair is presented as the model of what the children of Adam will face.

**The Exposure of What Was Hidden**

The verse states the purpose of the whisper, that the hidden nakedness of the pair might be exposed to them, and the Book repeats the description in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, where their nakedness is disclosed to them after they taste the tree (20:121). The commentary tradition reports that the pair had been clothed in a covering that concealed their bodies, and that the disobedience removed it. The exposure is therefore presented as a consequence of the act rather than as an independent event.

The Book's treatment of nakedness in the sūrah's following passage turns the incident into teaching about clothing and modesty, and the verse that states that God has sent down clothing to cover the children of Adam follows shortly after the account of the fall (7:26). The exposure in front of us is the condition that the gift of clothing addresses, and the sūrah's instruction about dress at worship grows from the same root.

**The False Promise About the Tree**

The verse reports the claim that the tree was forbidden only to keep the pair from becoming angels or from living forever, and the claim is the first lie that the Book records the adversary telling to human beings. The sūrah of Ṭā Hā reports the same approach in the form of a question about the tree of everlastingness and a kingdom that never decays (20:120). In both accounts the prohibition is recast as a deprivation, and the supposed benefit is offered as the reason to disobey.

The Book states elsewhere that the enemy's promises are delusion, and the sūrah of the Night Journey reports the promise he makes and the statement that he promises them nothing but delusion (17:64). The sūrah of the Women states that he makes promises and raises hopes in them and that he promises them nothing but deception (4:120). The verse in front of us records the original instance of the pattern that those verses describe.

**The Two Things Promised**

The promise names angels and immortality, and the two are joined in the narrative because both describe a condition above the human state as the pair then knew it. The commentary tradition notes that the promise of becoming angels works on the desire for a higher rank, and that the promise of abiding forever works on the fear of loss. The two together cover the ambitions that the Book elsewhere warns against: the wish to be other than what one was created to be, and the refusal to accept that the present life ends.

The Book's treatment of immortality as a claim made against God appears in its accounts of the people who denied the resurrection, and the sūrah of the Cave reports the man who entered his garden saying that he did not think the Hour would ever come. The verse in front of us shows the promise of immortality being used at the beginning of the human story, and the sūrah's teaching about the reckoning answers it.

**The Method and Its Repetition**

The temptation in front of us uses the elements that the sūrah's later passages identify in the audience's own practice: a claim about what God intends, a benefit offered for disobedience, and an oath to support it. The chapter goes on to describe the people who attribute to God what He has not commanded (7:28), who forbid what He has permitted (7:32), and who follow their ancestors on the strength of inherited claims (7:28). The first whisper in the Garden is the model of each of them.

The Book's instruction to the children of Adam in the following verses is therefore an instruction about a method as much as about an act. The verse in front of us states the method in its original form, and the reader who recognizes the shape of the argument is prepared for the sūrah's account of the same argument used against the prophets.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands twentieth in the chapter, and it follows the command given to the pair and precedes the oath that the adversary swears to them. The narrative then reports the fall, the exposure, the sewing of the leaves, and the divine question, and the pair's confession follows. The whisper in front of us is the opening move of the sequence.

The reader of the sūrah has been given the boundaries that were set, the enemy who was sentenced, and the first attempt on the boundary. The chapter's teaching about clothing, food, and worship follows the account of the fall, and the verses in front of us supply the narrative on which that teaching rests.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports that the adversary swore to the pair that he was their sincere adviser (7:21). The whisper of the previous verse is supported by an oath, and the narrative's next act is the fall. The verse in front of us has stated the false promise; the verse that follows states the false guarantee attached to it.

The two verses together present the technique of deception in its parts. A claim is made about God's intention, and an oath is sworn to make the claim credible, and the pair accept both. The sūrah then records what followed, and the instruction to their descendants is built on the record.
