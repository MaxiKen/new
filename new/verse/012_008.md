## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:8

> ˹Remember˺ when they said ˹to one another˺, “Surely Joseph and his brother ˹Benjamin˺ are more beloved to our father than we, even though we are a group of so many. Indeed, our father is clearly mistaken.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Envy Begins by Counting**

The brothers' first recorded words are arithmetic. Two against ten, and the smaller number is loved more. That is the whole grievance, and the verse states it in the form of an injustice rather than a feeling. Envy rarely announces itself as envy; it announces itself as a discrepancy that demands explanation. Once the count has been made, the conclusion follows almost mechanically: since the numbers are ours and the affection is not, something must be wrong with the distribution. No sentence in the verse expresses hatred. Hatred arrives only after the calculation has been accepted as evidence, which is why the chapter places the counting first. The reader is shown the moment a family turns into a faction, and the turning point is a subtraction.

**ʿUṣbah: A Word That Measures Strength**

*A group of so many* translates *ʿuṣbah*, a term the lexicographers define as a band of roughly ten to forty people, large enough to act together and to be reckoned with. The brothers choose it deliberately. They are not saying that they are numerous; they are saying that they constitute a force, the kind of group that defends a household, works its land and carries its name. In a tribal setting that was a real argument. Adult sons were the security of an ageing father, and a clan's standing was measured in grown men rather than in children. The word will return in their oath a few verses later, where the same self-image is used to promise that no wolf could take a boy from among them (12:14). Both uses rest on a single assumption: that strength of this kind entitles its holders to a share of love proportionate to their usefulness.

**"His Brother" Means Benjamin, and the Distinction Is Real**

The brother named only as *his brother* is Benjamin, Joseph's full brother — the one son of Jacob by the same mother. The phrase does careful work. It marks the two youngest as a pair inside a larger family, and it records the fact that provoked the resentment: Jacob kept them nearer to him than the rest, and the commentators report that he would not let Joseph out of his company night or day. Their youth explains the closeness and does not excuse the interpretation the older brothers placed on it. Affection for two children who had lost their mother was read as a verdict on ten men who had not. The same logic will drive the second half of the sūrah, when Benjamin becomes the object of a plot and the brothers discover that they have not learned anything from the first one.

**A Domestic Complaint in the Language of Unbelief**

The final clause is the most alarming in the verse. *Indeed, our father is clearly mistaken* renders *fī ḍalāl mubīn*, in manifest error — the phrase the Qur'an uses elsewhere for real deviation, spoken by disbelievers about their prophets and by prophets about idolatry. Here it is turned on a father by his sons over a matter of household affection. The escalation is diagnostic. When a grievance cannot be resolved by argument, it borrows the vocabulary of the largest available accusation, and the accuser begins to experience a preference as a heresy. Jacob is not merely unfair in their account; he has gone astray, which means he cannot be reasoned with and must be worked around. Every plot in this chapter follows from that single substitution of categories.

**Two Logics of Value, and Neither One Is About Joseph**

What the verse exposes is a competition between two ways of measuring a person. The brothers measure by capacity: we are many, we are grown, we can defend and provide. Jacob measures by need and by love, which do not scale with usefulness. Both logics are intelligible, and the tragedy lies in the fact that the brothers apply the first to a question it cannot answer. A father's affection is not a ration to be divided according to contribution. Nor is Joseph the object of their complaint in any strict sense; he is the occasion of it. What they want is their father's attention, and the removal of a rival is only the means. That is why the plan they are about to form aims at separation rather than at gain — no property, no inheritance, no office is mentioned. Only the face of the father turning toward them.

**The Sentence Is Turned Inward**

The verse opens with a small marker easily missed: they said it *to one another*. Not one word here is addressed to Jacob. The complaint is rehearsed in private, confirmed by repetition, and hardened by agreement before any of them meets the man they are complaining about. That is the ordinary mechanism of collective wrongdoing. A grievance stated to the person concerned can be answered; a grievance circulated among the aggrieved becomes a settled fact, and each repetition makes retraction more costly. The Qur'an's decision to report the private conversation gives the reader access to something the father never had. Jacob will hear the polished version — a request to take the boy out to play — and will have to judge it without knowing the deliberations behind it.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

No mention is made of inheritance, land, livestock or office. Ten grown men aggrieved by a father's preference do not ask for anything; they ask to be preferred. That absence narrows the possible remedy. A material grievance can be settled by a division, a payment, a portion allotted in writing. A grievance about love cannot be settled at all, because affection is not a divisible estate and any distribution of it leaves the person who receives less with the same complaint. The brothers have therefore chosen an objective that their own plan cannot reach. Removing Joseph does not produce love for them; it produces an absence, and an absence is felt as loss rather than as gain. Jacob's grief will make that plain within a day, and the chapter will go on for eighty more verses showing that nothing they obtained was what they wanted.
