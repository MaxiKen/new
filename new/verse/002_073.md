## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:73

> So We instructed, “Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow.” This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs so that you may understand.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Smallest Command in the Episode**

After four verses of accumulated specification — age, colour, work-history, soundness — the disclosure arrives as an instruction of almost comic simplicity: *strike him with a piece of it*. The cow, defined to uniqueness across three rounds of questioning, is slaughtered; and the murder's resolution requires from it a fragment, applied as a blow. The part is unspecified. The exegetical reports offer candidates — the tongue, the thigh, a portion of the flank — and the text's silence overrules them all: *bi-baʿḍihā*, with some of it, any piece, the indefiniteness echoing the original command's indefiniteness that the negotiation had destroyed. The irony is the verse's first lesson and it is structural: the specification-mongers who could not act until every property was fixed receive, at the moment of disclosure, a command with no properties at all — and it works. The efficacy was never in the cow's markings; the markings were the questioners' own manufacture, paid for at the market's ceiling. The striking is the smallest possible human act attached to the largest possible divine event, and the disproportion is the point: resurrection does not need instruments, and when it consents to use them, one blow with one fragment is the whole ritual.

**The Brief Return**

The reports converge on what the blow accomplished: the slain man, struck with the piece of the sacrificed cow, returned — briefly, bodily, lucidly — and named his killer, after which the body went back to its death. The tradition preserves the details with variations (the length of the return, the exact question put to the revived man, the identification's aftermath among the accusers), and the Qur'an's own text states none of them: the narration compresses the entire event into its principle, leaving the incident to the reports. The compression is deliberate and theologically exact. What the verse needs from the miracle is not its plot but its demonstration — that Allah gives life to the dead — and the plot's omission keeps the reader on the demonstration. The return's temporariness, which the reports agree on, matters for the demonstration's logic: this is not a resurrection unto life, like the raising of verse 56 that returned a community to its years. It is a resurrection unto testimony — the dead man restored for exactly as long as the truth needs a mouth, then released. The miracle's economy is perfect: the minimum animation that discharges the disclosure, no more. A God who needed to impress would have kept the man alive; the verse's God raises the dead the way the case requires and files the event as a sign rather than a spectacle.

**“Thus Allah Gives Life to the Dead”**

The verse's central clause generalizes the event into doctrine: *kadhālika yuḥyī Allāhu al-mawtā* — thus, in exactly this manner, Allah gives life to the dead. The demonstrative *thus* does the argumentative work: what you have just been told is not one story but one sample, and the sample certifies the rule. The recital had already filed the rule's first instance on this community's own bodies — raised after death at verse 56 — and the Qur'an's wider catalogue stands behind both: the man of the ruined city, killed for a hundred years and brought back with his food unfresh and his donkey's bones reclothed in flesh (2:259); Abraham, shown the mechanism at his own request — *"Then bring four birds, train them to come to you, ˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops. Then call them back, they will fly to you in haste"* (2:260) — life reassembled from scattered parts at a call; ʿĪsā's commissioned sign, *I will heal the blind and the leper and raise the dead to life— by Allah's Will* (3:49); even the calf's animation, a handful of dust from the messenger-angel's hoof-prints cast on moulded clay (20:96). Across every filing the grammar holds: life is Allah's to give, to withdraw, and to give again, and the giving-again is no more difficult than the giving. The cow's fragment is the catalogue's smallest entry and its most forensic: resurrection demonstrated not on a prophet's question or a passerby's wonder but on a murder case, in public, with a legal consequence — the dead made to testify, the living made to understand.

**So That You May Understand**

The purpose clause names the sign's target faculty: *li-ʿallakum taʿqilūn* — so that you may reason, understand, use your ʿaql. The recital's purpose clauses have been graded: gratitude after pardon (52), guidance after the Book (53), mindfulness after the covenant (63) — and understanding after the resurrection-sign. The choice of ʿaql is exact, because the sign is an argument. A miracle aimed at awe would overwhelm; a miracle aimed at understanding reasons — it places a demonstrated case (this dead man lived again, visibly, last week) beside an undemonstrated universal (the dead will live again, finally) and lets the mind close the distance. The sūrah has been conducting this pedagogy since its opening charge that the Book's audience must use its reason, and verse 73 is the recital's clearest instance of a sign functioning as evidence rather than as power-display. The clause also rebukes, silently, the negotiation that preceded it. The questioners wanted certainty by specification — truth defined until it could be held without risk. The disclosure gives certainty by demonstration — truth enacted before witnesses, requiring no specification at all. Understanding, in the verse's usage, is what happens when a person lets a shown thing teach him; the whole cow-episode is what happens when a person tries to make a commanded thing safe before doing it. The sign closes the case and the case closes the argument: Allah shows, the shown is understood, and the understanding is the sign's stated purpose.

**The Valley That Greened**

The tradition preserves the Prophet's ﷺ own answer to the question this verse exists to preempt — *how* does Allah revive the dead? — and the answer keeps the demonstration's register. Asked the question directly, the exegetical record relates, he answered with a scene rather than a metaphysics: have you passed through a barren valley, and passed it again, and found it green? Yes. *Even so is the resurrection.* The analogy is the Qur'an's own standing one — the dead land revived by sent-down water, filed dozens of times across the Book as the visible proof of the invisible promise — and its power is that it asks nothing of the hearer but eyes already used. Barrenness to green is not a metaphor the valley cooperates in; it happens, seasonally, everywhere, and the person who has seen it has seen the form of resurrection in the only scale his world offers. The cow's miracle and the valley's greening are one argument at two magnifications: life withdrawn, life returned, the returning no stranger than spring. The verse's *thus* gathers both: the forensic raising in the wilderness camp is the demonstration, the greening earth is its repetition in every reader's reach, and the understanding the purpose clause asks for is the act of seeing the second in the first. A person who reasons about resurrection has already watched it — on every dead branch, every fallow field, every valley passed twice.

**Two Scriptures, One Heifer**

The cow-murder-disclosure arc has a counterpart in the Torah's legislation, and the comparison fixes this verse's genre precisely. Deuteronomy prescribes, for a body found slain in open country with the killer unknown, a standing rite: an unworked heifer, never yoked, led to a rough valley and killed there; the elders of the nearest town wash their hands over it and swear they did not shed the blood nor see it shed; the guilt of unsolved blood is thereby removed from the community. The two accounts share their load-bearing elements — a cow, an unsolved murder, a community under the blood's unsettled weight, the unworked animal — and diverge at the decisive point: Deuteronomy files a permanent statute for cases that cannot be solved, while the Qur'an files a singular event in which the case *is* solved, the dead man testifying to his own killer. One scripture legislates the disclaiming of unknown guilt; the other narrates the disclosure of hidden guilt. The Qur'an's version is thus not the rite's retelling but its reversal: where the law washes hands over an unanswered question, the recital answers the question — and the answer required a cow specified by the community's own interrogation, slaughtered by its own reluctant hands, applied by the simplest command in the episode. The sūrah's namesake completes its arc here: the command mocked, negotiated, cost, performed, and spent — and the whole negotiation retroactively justified by a truth no negotiation could have produced. The cow was never about the cow. It was the instrument by which a disputing community received what dispute cannot generate: the dead speaking, the concealed disclosed, and the sign understood.
