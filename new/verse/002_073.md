## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:73

> So We instructed, “Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow.” This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs so that you may understand.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Used the Animal**

The verse supplies the instruction for which the whole precedent narrative had been preparing: the slaughtered cow's flesh is to be brought into contact with the dead man, and the Book reports the command in a single clause. The commentators debated whether the phrase describes striking the corpse with a part of the animal or striking the killer, and the majority reading takes the first, which fits the sequence and the demonstration that follows.

The reason for the cow is therefore revealed at the end of the episode rather than at its beginning. The community had demanded a precise description of an animal whose purpose it had not been told, and the answer to its questions turned out to be the instrument of a display of power that settled a murder case. What the delay cost the community was money, and what the command was for was a sign.

**Life Restored as an Answer to a Question**

The Book's account does not describe the dead man speaking; it reports that the demonstration happened and draws a conclusion from it. Elsewhere in the Qur'an the same kind of event is used for the same purpose, and the sūrah of the Cave describes a man who died and was revived after a hundred years as a sign for people to see. In the same sūrah's later verses Abraham asks to be shown how the dead are given life and is answered with a demonstration involving birds.

Those accounts share one feature: the demonstration is given to someone who needed to know something, not to an audience assembled for spectacle. The murder case in this passage required an identification that only the dead man could provide, and the Book presents the restoration as the means by which the case was closed.

**The Comparison the Verse Draws**

The verse states that this is how God brings the dead to life, and it uses the incident as evidence for the general claim of resurrection. The argument is the one the sūrah has already made twice: a power that has produced life once, and that can be shown producing it again in front of witnesses, is not diminished by the scale of what it is asked to do.

The same reasoning appears in the sūrah's earlier account of the community that demanded a sign and was struck down, and in the verse about the lifeless who were given life. The Book's method for establishing the resurrection is not to describe the process but to point at instances, and this verse is one of the instances it offers.

**"His Signs, So That You May Understand"**

The purpose clause names understanding rather than belief, and the word the Book uses for it describes the act of putting things together rather than the act of accepting a report. The sign is offered as something to be reasoned from, and the Book expects its readers to draw the conclusion the verse has already drawn.

That expectation runs through the passage. The community had spent four verses seeking precision and one further action carrying it out, and the result was a piece of evidence that no amount of deliberation could have produced. The Book's invitation is for the reader to consider what kind of power is required to do that, and the verse states it in the plainest terms available.

**The End of the Narrative and the Shape of the Address**

The episode closes here, and the sūrah returns immediately to the themes of the address. The narrative has given the reader a complete case: a crime, a concealment, a dispute, a command, a resistance, an obedience and a sign. The community's history has been recited in this form throughout the passage, and the reader has now seen it in its most detailed telling.

What the Book does next is turn the same historical material into an argument about belief. The verses that follow address the community again, and they use the incidents the reader has just read as comparable cases. The narrative is not left as a story; it becomes the basis for the next stage of the address.

**The Community Left With Its Evidence**

The people who demanded to know the animal's colour were given the answer, and they were also given something they had not asked for: the resolution of a murder they had concealed. The Book reports both without commenting on the connection, and the sequence does the work. A community that had attempted to manage the truth found the truth produced in a way it could not manage.

The verse ends with a purpose clause rather than a threat, which is how the sūrah's narratives about this people consistently conclude. The sign was given for understanding, and the reader who finishes the passage has been given the same material. The address that follows will ask him to do something with it.

**Signs Offered to the Understanding**

The verse closes with a purpose clause that treats the event as material for reasoning rather than as a spectacle to be admired. The Book offers its demonstrations to a reader who is expected to draw a conclusion about the power that produced them, and it attaches the same expectation to its arguments from the created world, from history and from the pattern of the seasons. All of them end by naming the conclusion rather than leaving it implicit.

What the sign in this passage established was the possibility of the general claim: a body that had lost its life was given it back. The sūrah uses the incident as an argument for resurrection, and it does so in a single sentence that moves from the particular to the universal. That movement is the Book's ordinary method, and the passage leaves the reader with the same material the community was given, offered on the same terms.
