## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:67

> For every community We appointed a code of life to follow. So do not let them dispute with you ˹O Prophet˺ in this matter. And invite ˹all˺ to your Lord, for you are truly on the Right Guidance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Statement Is the Same One the Sūrah Made in Verse 34**

*Li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan hum nāsikūhu* — for every community We appointed a rite which they are to perform.

The sūrah has said this before, at verse 34, in almost the same words. There the point was that sacrifice is universal and that the name pronounced over it is what distinguishes a rite from a slaughter. Here the point is procedural: because the rites differ by community, arguing about them is not the task.

The word *mansak* carries the same range it carried there — a ritual act, a body of ritual, or the place where the ritual is performed. Whichever sense is taken, the claim is that these were appointed, and appointed by God rather than chosen by the communities.

The repetition is a frame rather than a slip. Verse 34 stands at the head of the sūrah's longest legislative passage, and everything from there to verse 66 — the sacrificial animals, the pilgrimage, the permission to fight, the ruined towns, the emigrants — sits inside it. Verse 67 takes the same sentence up again at the far side of that material, and this time draws the consequence that was left implicit the first time: since the rites were appointed and not chosen, they are not settled by disputing about them.

**The Inference Drawn From the Plurality**

The verse's logic runs in a direction that readers often miss. The plurality of rites is not presented as a problem to be resolved. It is presented as a fact that settles a question: if God appointed a different rite for each community, then uniformity was not the goal, and disputing about which rite is correct is a category error.

That is the meaning of *fa-lā yunāziʿunnaka fī al-amr* — so let them not dispute with you concerning the matter. The prohibition is not a refusal to give reasons; it is a refusal to accept the terms of the argument. What is being disputed was appointed, and an appointed difference is not a contradiction to be adjudicated by debate.

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the same principle about law and way of life: *“To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life. If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you”* (5:48). The difference is described there as the test rather than as the problem.

**What the Prophet Is Told to Do Instead**

*Wa-dʿu ilā rabbika* — and call to your Lord.

The instruction replaces the dispute with the invitation, and the difference between the two is the whole of the verse.

A dispute takes place on the ground the disputant has chosen: which rite, which community, which practice is correct. An invitation takes place on ground the caller chooses: the Lord. The sūrah's instruction is to decline the first and keep to the second, which is also the strategy of verse 49, where the Prophet was told to announce his office rather than to produce the punishment.

This is a recurring pattern in the Qur'an's guidance to its messenger. Where the opponents want a debate about particulars, the messenger is told to return to the single claim — and to trust that the particulars follow from it if the claim is accepted.

The standing formulation of the same instruction makes clear that the verse is not banning argument as such. *"Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner"* (16:125). Debate conducted well is part of the invitation. What verse 67 withdraws from is a debate conducted on the opponent's terms, about a matter that was appointed rather than argued.

**"You Are Indeed on Straight Guidance"**

*Innaka la-ʿalā hudan mustaqīm* — you are certainly upon a guidance that is straight.

The assurance is absolute, and it is given before the arguments have landed. That is its function: the Prophet is told where he stands so that the disputants' refusal does not become a measure of his position.

The phrase *hudā mustaqīm* is the same pairing the sūrah used at verse 54 for the path to which God guides the believers. The echo is deliberate — the messenger's guidance and the believers' guidance are the same one, and the route is described as straight rather than as easy.

The same assurance is given to him elsewhere in nearly the same words, and the parallel shows what is being protected: *"You ˹O Prophet˺ are truly one of the messengers, upon the Straight Path"* (36:3–4). In both places the statement precedes the outcome. The Prophet is not told that the disputants will be convinced; he is told that his position does not depend on whether they are.

**The Dispute the Sūrah Has in View**

The commentators noted two readings of who is being told not to dispute. One takes it as addressed to the Prophet: do not let them draw you into argument. The other takes it as addressed through him to the opponents: they should stop disputing and submit.

The first is the more natural reading of the grammar, since the verb is a prohibition addressed in the second person with *them* as the object. It is also the reading that fits the sūrah, which has repeatedly told the Prophet what his job is and what it is not (22:49).

The second reading is not to be discarded, however, because it preserves the sense that the opponents are being given a reason as well as an instruction. On that reading the verse tells them what the first reading tells the Prophet: the rite was appointed, so the argument is not available.

Either way, the verse is not a general prohibition of discussion. The Qur'an prescribes argument elsewhere and prescribes its manner: *"Do not argue with the People of the Book unless gracefully, except with those of them who act wrongfully"* (29:46). What is withdrawn in this verse is narrower — a dispute about an appointed difference, with people the sūrah has already described as disputing without knowledge.

**What "Appointed" Excludes**

The verb is *jaʿalnā*, We appointed — the same verb used at verse 34, and the same one used at verse 36 for making the sacrificial camels into emblems.

The subject of the verb is God, and that is the part the argument rests on. If the rites had been appointed by the communities, they would be open to comparison, criticism and revision by anyone with an argument. Appointed by God, they are not comparable in the way the disputants want them to be.

This is the same principle the sūrah applied at verse 34, when it moved straight from the plurality of rites to the oneness of God. The diversity is real and it is ordained; what is not ordained is ranking them by debate.

The verse therefore closes the door that the sūrah opened at verse 17, when it listed six communities and declined to adjudicate between them. Nothing has changed about that deferral. What has been added is the instruction about how to live inside it.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Closing**

Verses 67 to 69 form a small unit on how to conduct disagreement, and they come at a point in the sūrah where the argument has been made, the evidence given and the judgment announced. What remains is instruction for the interval.

That is the right place for it. A community that has been told it is right, and told that the adjudication is deferred to God (22:17, 22:56), needs to know what to do with people who disagree — and the answer the sūrah gives is not argument.

The three verses also close the question that the sūrah has held open since its middle. Verse 69 will state that God will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning what they differed about, which is the same deferral that verse 17 made for the six communities and verse 56 made for the judgment generally. Between the deferral and the day, the instruction is to invite and not to dispute — a rule for the interval rather than a solution to the disagreement.
