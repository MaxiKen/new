## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:12

> ˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the disbelievers, “Soon you will be overpowered and driven to Hell—what an evil place to rest!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Announce an Outcome, Not to Argue for It**

The verse does not invite the disbelievers to consider, compare, or weigh anything. It instructs the Prophet to tell them a result. The form is prophecy delivered as news: an announcement of what is going to happen, spoken before anything about it has happened, in a community where the speaker holds no army of consequence.

That is the whole rhetorical move, and it is worth naming clearly: the Qur'an does not argue that its opponents will lose. It tells them. The claim is the kind that can only be made by someone with knowledge of the future, so the verse stakes its credibility on events that will confirm or destroy it in public. Prophecy of this sort is a wager on history.

**"Soon You Will Be Overpowered" — Certainty in the Future Tense**

*Sa-tughlabūna* uses the Arabic future prefix with the particle of nearness: "you are going to be overpowered, and soon". There is no condition attached and no escape clause offered. Whatever the audience does — fight, negotiate, wait, trade — the outcome is fixed, and the immediacy of "soon" gives it a date without giving a day.

The Qur'an sets this same assurance beside the events it refers to. The community is warned against reading the prosperity of its opponents as evidence of divine approval: *“Do not be deceived by the prosperity of the disbelievers throughout the land. It is only a brief enjoyment. Then Hell will be their home—what an evil place to rest!”* (3:196–197). The phrase that closes verse 12 closes those verses too. A reader who meets the same sentence twice in the same sūrah is meant to hear it as a refrain, one that holds the beginning and the near-end of the argument together.

**Mihād: a Resting Place That Is Not Rest**

The last words — "what an evil place to rest" — render *mihād*, which lexically means a place smoothed and prepared for someone to lie down: a bed, a cradle, a floor spread out. The same word family is used of the earth itself as a resting surface for human beings: *“Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺”* (78:6), and other passages use the related *mahd* for the earth as a cradle (43:10).

The irony is precisely in the lexical comfort. What is promised to the deniers in this verse is a resting place that is prepared, smoothed, and fitted to them — and it is Hell. The Qur'an frequently uses the language of domestic rest for the end of the arrogant, because the punishment it describes is not chaos. It is an accommodation, well made, and the worst possible one.

**What the Verse Does With Its Own Audience**

The command is addressed to the Prophet, but the sentence is to be delivered to the disbelievers, which means its first effect is among the believers standing beside him. They hear the fate of their opponents stated flatly and are asked to conduct themselves accordingly, and the demand that follows from vv. 196–197 is that they not envy men whose end is already known.

For the opponents, the verse offers only the announcement, and the announcement is itself the warning. Nothing further is said about their wealth, their numbers, or their resolve, because none of that is present in the sentence as a variable. The verse reduces the whole conflict to two facts: what they refuse, and where that refusal ends.

The wording of the command is worth a second look for the same reason. The Prophet is told to *tell* them, not to persuade them, and the message is a statement about the future rather than a demand. In the sūrah's economy of argument this is the last resort of a message that has already been argued: after the signs, the warnings, and the invitation, there is an announcement of what is coming. It is short, and it is placed where nobody can claim he was not informed.

**A Report That Some Readers Connected With a Market-Place Warning**

The commentators report that the Prophet delivered a warning of this kind in Madinah after Badr, addressing the Banū Qaynuqāʿ in their market and telling them that a fate like the Quraysh's could be theirs. The account is preserved in the sīrah and tafsīr literature rather than in the sound collections, and it is mentioned here as background for how the community heard such verses, not as established ḥadīth.

**How a Warning Becomes a Mercy**

The verse ends on a place of rest, and the place of rest is Hell. Everything in it is designed to be uncomfortable, including for the reader who has no stake in the outcome, and that discomfort is the only argument it offers. In a sūrah that will shortly tell its audience that the door of return stays open, this announcement serves a purpose that mercy itself requires: it tells a man where the road goes before he has spent his life on the way.

The same pattern appears in the Book's treatment of those who are warned and then spared. Warnings in the Qur'an are always addressed to people still alive, which means the announcement of a defeat that has not yet come is never merely a prediction. It is an invitation with a deadline, and the deadline is what makes the invitation thinkable. A man who believed his choices had no consequence would never change any of them.

A threat made by a truthful speaker is a form of information, and information can be acted on. The verse gives its listeners the one fact that changes the value of everything they own, and it gives it early enough to be used.
