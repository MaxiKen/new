## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**Seven Names for Seven Verses**

No sūrah in the Quran carries as many names, and each name records a use. *Al-Fātiḥah* is "the Opening": it opens the Book, and it opens every cycle (*rakʿah*) of the canonical prayer. *Umm al-Kitāb* and *Umm al-Qurʾān* — "Mother of the Book," "Mother of the Quran" — record the claim that the whole of the revelation is contained in it in principle. The Quran itself calls it *the seven often-repeated verses* (15:87), from which comes *al-Sabʿ al-mathānī*. Tradition adds *al-Shifāʾ*, "the Cure," because it is recited over the sick; *al-Asās*, "the Foundation"; *Sūrat al-Ḥamd*, "the Chapter of Praise," from its second verse; and *Sūrat al-Ṣalāh*, "the Chapter of the Prayer," because no prayer stands without it.

A sūrah acquires a name from what people do with it. Seven names for seven verses means this is the most used passage in the Book, recited several times a day by every praying Muslim and spoken at the thresholds of ordinary life — when someone dies, when a couple marries, when a child is born, when a contract is signed, when a traveller leaves.

**A Makkan Revelation, With One Dissent**

The overwhelming majority of scholars place al-Fātiḥah among the earliest Makkan revelations. Mujāhid (d. 104/722–23), a leading figure of the second generation, is reported to have held that it was revealed in Madinah, and most authorities regard that as impossible, on the ground that the Muslims could not have performed the prescribed prayers without it.

The objection is weaker than it looks, since it assumes the prayer was always performed exactly as it was later legislated. But the majority position has the better evidence regardless: the sūrah's vocabulary, its brevity, and its subject — the nature of God, worship, and the request for guidance rather than legal rulings — match the earliest Makkan stratum. The dissent matters for a different reason. It shows how early the sūrah was tied to the ritual prayer in the community's own memory, to the point that a scholar could argue backwards from the prayer's existence to the sūrah's date.

**Six Verses or Seven: The Count That Divides the Schools**

The sūrah counts as seven verses or as six, and the difference is the opening formula. Those who count *In the Name of Allah* as a verse of the sūrah reach seven, and those who treat it as a separator between sūrahs reach six. The Quran's description of the sūrah as *the seven often-repeated verses* (15:87) is the standard textual argument for the first count.

The disagreement is not academic, because it determines what is recited aloud. The Shāfiʿī school and the Jaʿfarī school recite the basmalah aloud at the head of the sūrah in audible prayers; the rest do not. Both practices rest on reports from the Companions, and the question is argued out at 1:1 below. No school, meanwhile, doubts the sūrah's presence in every cycle. The report of the man who prayed badly, and the many declarations that a prayer without it is deficient, make its recitation the one universally agreed element of the ritual.

**Three Movements and a Hinge**

The sūrah falls into three movements, and the division is not imposed on it from outside. The first three or four verses speak *about* God — His Name, His praise, His mercy, His ownership of the Day of Recompense. The fifth verse speaks *to* Him, and turns from description to address. The last two verses ask Him for something.

A well-known ḥadīth qudsī confirms the structure by narrating it as a conversation. God declares that He has divided the recitation between Himself and His servant into two halves. When the servant praises, God answers that His servant has praised Him, lauded Him, glorified Him. At the verse of worship, the answer changes to *This is between Me and My servant*. At the closing petition, it becomes *This is for My servant, and My servant will receive what he asks for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). The half belonging to God is praise; the half belonging to the creature is request; the verse between them belongs to both.

**Recited in Every Cycle, and Outside the Prayer**

Because the sūrah is recited in every cycle of every prayer, it is the most frequently repeated text in Muslim practice — several times a day for anyone who prays five times. The reports about its status are correspondingly numerous. One describes the Prophet telling a man he would teach him the greatest sūrah, and then naming *the seven often-repeated verses* (15:87). Another states that nothing like it was sent down in the Torah, in the Psalms, in the Gospel, or even in the Quran.

Its use outside the prayer is verified in the same collections. A group of the Companions came upon a tribe whose chief had been stung; one of them recited al-Fātiḥah over him, and the man recovered. When the Prophet heard of it he approved the recitation and asked, *How do you know that it is a ruqyah?* — then told them to keep the flock they had been paid and to give him a share (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5736; also 2276). The title *al-Shifāʾ*, "the Cure," comes from reports of this kind.

**Why the Shortest Complete Sūrah Is Called the Mother of the Book**

The strongest claim made for al-Fātiḥah is that it contains the whole Quran in principle. A saying attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib (d. 40/661) compresses it further: the whole Quran is in the Fātiḥah, the whole Fātiḥah in the basmalah, the whole basmalah in its opening letter, and that letter in the point beneath it. [UNVERIFIED: this saying is widely reported in the commentaries but I could not verify a chain or a source text for it.]

The claim should be read carefully rather than literally. The sūrah does not summarise the Quran's laws, narratives, or rulings; what it states is the framework every one of those presupposes — who God is, what a human being is before Him, what is asked for, and how a person goes wrong. On that reading the title is exact. A reader who understands these seven verses understands what the rest of the Book is doing, and a reader who does not will misunderstand every ruling in it, however well he memorises them.
