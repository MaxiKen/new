## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:74

> They replied, “No! But we found our forefathers doing the same.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idolaters Admit the Evidence Against Their Gods**

Abraham's people answer that the idols cannot hear, benefit, or harm. Their admission is decisive at the level of capacity. They do not defend the idols as active powers. Instead, they give a reason for continuing to worship them: their forefathers did the same. The community has conceded the weakness of its objects and shifted from evidence to ancestry.

This response reveals the structure of inherited religion when it is protected from examination. The practice remains because it belongs to the past, not because the objects have demonstrated any right to worship. Ancestry becomes a substitute for proof. The people may feel that abandoning the idols would mean rejecting their identity, so they defend the custom even after admitting that the idols cannot perform the functions worship assumes.

**Custom Can Preserve a Contradiction**

The answer contains a contradiction. The people know the idols cannot benefit or harm, yet they remain devoted to them. The contradiction survives because custom supplies social meaning that the idols themselves lack. A ritual can feel authoritative when generations have repeated it, even if its object has never shown power.

Abraham's role is to make the contradiction visible without allowing it to hide behind reverence for ancestors. A practice does not become true by aging. If anything, a long-standing error can become harder to see because people confuse familiarity with evidence. The community's answer shows why reasoning must be renewed in every generation rather than outsourced to the dead.

**Forefathers Are Not a Substitute for Guidance**

Respect for parents and ancestors has a rightful place, but the Qur'anic tradition never treats ancestry as infallible. The same ancestors were dependent on God, capable of error, and responsible for their choices. Following them can be right when they followed truth; it cannot make false worship right when they practiced it.

This principle also protects the living from being imprisoned by the dead. A person may honor forefathers more faithfully by correcting an inherited error than by repeating it. True respect seeks the good that ancestors were meant to serve, not every habit they happened to preserve. Abraham's people use ancestry to close the question, but Abraham will reopen it by asking them to consider the practice itself.

**The Admission Makes the Appeal to Authority Weak**

An appeal to forefathers can be strong when the ancestors possess knowledge or reliable testimony. Here the people have already admitted that the idols lack the capacities under discussion. The authority of custom cannot repair that defect. The fact that earlier generations worshipped powerless objects explains how the practice began and continued; it does not prove that the practice deserves continuation.

The distinction between explanation and justification is central. History can explain why a person believes something without showing that the belief is true. Abraham's people offer a history of transmission, not a reason for worship. Their answer describes the path by which the error reached them, while leaving the truth of the error untouched.

**A Community Can Inherit Fear Along With Ritual**

Inherited worship often carries inherited fears. People may worry that abandoning the idols will anger ancestors, disrupt blessings, or bring social punishment. These fears can make the past feel like a living authority. The community's answer may therefore express more than respect. It may express anxiety about what will happen if the chain is broken.

Abraham's questions give them a way to test that anxiety. If the idols cannot harm, then fear of their displeasure cannot be based on the idols' own power. Social consequences may still exist, but social pressure is not divine proof. A person may lose approval for following truth and still gain freedom from the fear that held him to an empty object.

**The Qur'an Repeats This Debate Across Communities**

The same defense appears in other Qur'anic discussions of inherited practice, including 5:104 and 31:21, where people appeal to what their forefathers followed. The repeated pattern shows that Abraham's people are not unique in using ancestry to avoid examination. The argument recurs whenever custom becomes more authoritative than revelation and reason.

The repetition also makes the verse relevant to the Prophet's audience. A community can hear Abraham's story and condemn the ancient idolaters while making the same appeal for its own practices. The point is not to mock the past but to judge every inherited claim by truth. The dead cannot answer Abraham's questions for the living.

**The Answer Hands Abraham the Real Argument**

By admitting that the idols have no power, the people have supplied Abraham with the premise he needs. Their defense now rests on imitation alone. He can show that an error remains an error whether it is new or ancient, common or marginal, practiced by family or stranger.

This is why his next response will ask them to consider what they worship. He will not need to invent a new accusation. Their own answer has separated custom from evidence. The authority of forefathers may explain the idols' hold, but it cannot give them ears, power, or a right to receive worship. The dead can transmit a custom, but they cannot make a powerless object divine. The living must therefore distinguish gratitude for ancestors from obedience to an inherited mistake. Reverence becomes harmful when it prevents the truth from being heard. A community honors its ancestors more wisely when it receives truth even where truth corrects them. An inherited error is not repaired by calling it old; it is repaired by returning to what is true. Truth asks for loyalty greater than the loyalty owed to a custom. Abraham's question therefore protects them from confusing historical continuity with divine approval.
