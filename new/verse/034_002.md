## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:2

> He knows whatever goes into the earth and whatever comes out of it, and whatever descends from the sky and whatever ascends into it. And He is the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Directions That Between Them Leave Nowhere to Hide**

The verse sets out two pairs of opposed movements: down into the earth and up out of it, down from the sky and up into it. Taken together they describe a closed circuit. Anything that exists is either going in, coming out, coming down or going up, and the four verbs exhaust the possibilities. This is a characteristic Qur'anic way of asserting totality — not by saying "everything," which the mind hears as a vague word, but by naming the axes along which motion can occur and then covering all of them. The same four clauses appear once more in the Book, at the centre of a verse in al-Ḥadīd that adds a further clause: *"He knows whatever goes into the earth and whatever comes out of it, and whatever descends from the sky and whatever ascends into it. And He is with you wherever you are"* (57:4). Sabaʾ uses the identical inventory but closes it with mercy rather than with presence. The wording is shared; the conclusion drawn from it is not, and the difference is the commentary each sūrah is making on its own theme.

**What Actually Goes Into the Ground**

The first clause is the broadest, because the earth receives more kinds of thing than any other region. Rain soaks into it and disappears. Seed is buried and rots before it does anything else. Roots push down out of sight. Minerals and metals are already there, formed in darkness. And the dead are lowered into it, which is the meaning that matters most in a sūrah whose opponents will shortly laugh at the idea of being raised from it. Each of these things enters the ground and becomes invisible, and invisibility is exactly what the deniers of resurrection rely on: once a body has scattered into soil, they assume, the record of it is gone because no one can see it any more. The verse answers that assumption before the objection is even voiced. What enters the earth does not enter a filing gap. Al-Anʿām makes the same point at the scale of a single seed: *"not a grain in the darkness of the earth or anything—green or dry—but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record"* (6:59).

**What Comes Back Out, and Why It Is Listed Second**

The return movement is the ordinary miracle of the world — shoots breaking the crust, springs surfacing, ore lifted out of a shaft, and the eventual raising of what was buried. Placing emergence immediately after entry turns the pair into a small argument rather than a list. Something goes down and is lost to sight; something comes up that was not there before; and the person watching the field has no difficulty believing the second even though he cannot explain it. The Qur'an appeals to that familiarity elsewhere in exactly this context, telling those who doubt the resurrection to consider the ground they already trust: *"Then when He calls you out of the earth just once, you will instantly come forth"* (30:25). Naming both directions also blocks a deist reading in which God knows the world as a finished inventory. The knowledge described here is of things in transit, mid-process, half-buried, not yet arrived — the hardest kind of knowledge to have, and the only kind that could support a reckoning of lives still being lived.

**Descent and Ascent: Two Traffics, One Watcher**

The vertical pair widens the scene beyond agriculture. What comes down from the sky includes rain and light, and it includes what the Qur'an describes as sent down by decree — angels, revelation, allotted portions. What goes up includes vapour and the flight of birds, and it includes what the Book explicitly says ascends: *"To Him ˹alone˺ good words ascend, and righteous deeds are raised up by Him"* (35:10). Al-Sajdah describes the return leg of the circuit as a matter of divine administration: *"He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him"* (32:5). So the four clauses read as a single system with two floors and constant traffic in both directions, physical and moral at once. Nothing in the vocabulary forces a choice between the natural and the spiritual reading, and the sūrah gains from holding both. Rain falling and a prayer rising are described by the same verb pair, which is itself a claim: the moral order is not a separate world layered on top of the physical one but the same world, watched by the same knowledge.

**Mercy Named Before Forgiveness, and Only Here**

The verse ends with *al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr* — the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving. Reading this translation across the whole Qur'an, the two names occur in this order at this verse alone; everywhere else the familiar sequence runs the other way, forgiving first and merciful second. The reversal is worth pausing over, because it changes which name governs. Forgiveness answers an offence, so it presupposes a record of wrongdoing. Mercy is prior to any offence and is the reason forgiveness is offered at all. Placing mercy first makes forgiveness its consequence rather than its equal. The placement also does something to the four clauses that precede it. A verse listing exhaustive surveillance could easily close on a name of power — the Overwhelming, the Severe in Punishment — and the sentence would run smoothly. Choosing mercy instead tells the reader what the surveillance is for. The One who misses nothing is not compiling a case; He is the One from whom pardon comes.

**Knowledge Without Mercy Is a Threat; Here It Is an Invitation**

What holds the two halves of this verse together is a single implied claim: complete knowledge in the hands of a merciful owner is good news rather than bad. Human beings fear being fully known because the people who know most about us are rarely the people most inclined to pardon us. The verse breaks that association. Because nothing that goes into the ground is lost, no quiet good deed is wasted and no injustice done in private has escaped; because the One who knows is named merciful and forgiving, the same completeness that would terrify a wrongdoer is the ground of hope for anyone who turns back. Al-Mulk states the underlying logic as a question: *"How could He not know His Own creation? For He ˹alone˺ is the Most Subtle, All-Aware"* (67:14). Sabaʾ has just called Him All-Aware in its opening line (34:1) and now spells out the content of that awareness in four directions. Having established who knows and how completely, the sūrah is ready to report what its opponents say about the Hour.
