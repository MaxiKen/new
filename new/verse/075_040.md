## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:40

> Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Supplies Its Own Answer**

The last verse is a question, and it is the kind of question that supplies its own answer. *A-laysa dhālika bi-qādirin* — is not that One able to give life to the dead? — expects *yes, He is*, and the expectation is part of the grammar rather than an addition to it. The sūrah that opened with an oath by the Day of Resurrection closes by asking whether the Creator can raise the dead, and it leaves the hearer to say the word. Nothing is spelled out and nothing needs to be. After forty verses of argument, the conclusion is being placed in the reader's mouth.

**The Response the Prophet Taught**

The community did not treat that question as rhetorical only; they answered it out loud in prayer. A report in the Sunan collections describes a man reciting this verse on his rooftop and saying, *“Glory be to You, then, why not?”* — and when he was asked about it, he replied that he had heard it from the Messenger of God (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 863, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī). A related narration in the same chapter gives a fuller formula for the reciter of this sūrah: that when he reaches the question about raising the dead, he should say that he is among those who testify to it. The practice turns the closing verse into an act of worship: the reader does not merely concede the conclusion, he confesses it. That is a point about the nature of faith in this sūrah. Belief is not presented anywhere in it as the acceptance of a proposition after weighing the evidence. It is presented as assent given to a Speaker, and the assent is the thing being asked for from the first verse onward. The argument exists to make the assent possible; the assent is not the argument's by-product.

**How the Logic Runs**

The argument constructed over the last five verses is a single syllogism, and it rests entirely on premises the audience granted. The One who made a human being from an emitted drop, took him through the stage of the clinging clot, proportioned him, and brought forth from him the two sexes from whom the earth was populated — is that One unable to bring the dead back? The Qur'an states the comparison of difficulty in the same terms elsewhere: *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”* (30:27). Bringing a person into being from nothing is the greater act; reconstituting him from what remains is the lesser, and the sūrah's closing question is unanswerable on any other premise. The Qur'an adds the guarantee in the plainest terms elsewhere: *“Just as We produced the first creation, ˹so˺ shall We reproduce it. That is a promise binding on Us.”* (21:104). A promise binding on God is not a hope, and the sūrah has built its entire case on a premise the objectors never disputed — that the God who made them is the God who made everything.

**The Circle Closed With Verse 3**

The structure deserves to be stated, because it is the sūrah's design rather than an accident of arrangement. Verse 3 asked whether a man supposes that God cannot gather his bones; verse 40 asks whether that God is unable to give life to the dead. The two questions use different verbs but make the same challenge, and between them the sūrah has supplied the answer to both: the fingertips, the cosmic collapse, the record, the self-witness, the deathbed, and the stages of a single human life. A reader who reaches the last verse has been given everything he needs to answer it, and the answer he gives is the same one the objector refused thirty-seven verses earlier.

**Assent as an Act**

Why does the sūrah end on a question rather than on a declaration? Because the point of the whole argument is to produce something in the hearer, and a declaration would have produced nothing. A question obliges the reader to answer, and the answer — *yes, He is able* — is itself the faith the sūrah has been asking for since v. 1. That is why the Prophet's taught response matters: the confession is not a comment on the argument but its intended conclusion. The sūrah is not finished when it is read. It is finished when the reader says the word. There is a version of this in the practice of the community: the reciter who reaches this verse in prayer does not pass over it in silence but answers it, because a question addressed to a reader is not complete until the reader responds. The sūrah that began by swearing by the Day ends by putting the same Day's possibility into the mouth of whoever is reading, and the pause between the question and the answer is the whole of a person's life.

**The Whole Arc**

The sūrah opened by swearing by the Day of Resurrection and by the self that accuses itself. It named the objection to resurrection and answered it from the body's own extremities. It located unbelief in what a person wants rather than in what he knows. It described the collapse of the sky, the opening of the record, the testimony of the soul against itself, and the two faces. It turned aside to instruct the Prophet about receiving revelation, then returned to rebuke a people who love the hasty world and leave the weighty Day behind. It followed one death from the collarbone to the driving, threatened the swaggerer, and closed by asking whether the God who made him from a drop can raise him from the grave. The question is not rhetorical for the person reading it. It is the reason the sūrah exists.
