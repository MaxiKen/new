## Sūrah an-Naml 27:32

> She said, “O chiefs! Advise me in this matter of mine, for I would never make any decision without you.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**She Asks Before She Decides**

Confronted with a demand from a foreign power, the queen's first act is to request an opinion. She does not announce a position and invite agreement, and she does not ask her council to choose between options she has already drawn up. The question is open: *aftūnī fī amrī* — give me your considered judgment in my affair.

The verb is unusually strong. *Aftā* is the root behind *fatwā*, and it means to deliver a formal, reasoned ruling on a difficult question. She is not asking how her chiefs feel; she is asking them to adjudicate. Coming from a monarch who has just received a letter demanding her submission, that is a significant concession of ground before any deliberation has begun. Al-Rāzī's later comment on the council's balanced reply implies a functioning political culture, and this verse is where its character shows. The court is capable of being asked a real question.

**"I Never Decide Without You" — Custom or Character?**

Her second sentence describes a settled practice rather than a single occasion: *mā kuntu qāṭiʿatan amran ḥattā tashhadūn* — I have never been one to conclude a matter until you are present. The construction in Arabic indicates habit, something she is not in the habit of doing.

Two readings of this are available and they lead in different directions. On one, she is describing a constitutional constraint: the throne of Sheba cannot act without its notables, and she is stating a limit on her own power. On the other, she is describing a personal method, chosen rather than imposed, which she is reaffirming at the moment it would be most convenient to abandon. The text does not settle it, and the difference matters less than it seems, because either way the statement is made publicly at a moment of crisis. Ibn Kathīr suggests she likely understood the situation better than her chiefs did, which would make the consultation more diplomatic than strictly necessary. Even on that reading, a ruler who consults when she does not need to is doing something deliberate.

**A Council Is Not a Formality Here**

What follows in the next verses shows that the consultation was genuine, and the evidence is in the shape of the exchange. Her chiefs answer with a real position — they state their military capacity — and she then argues against the implication of that position, giving her reasons. A rubber-stamp council does not get argued with.

The Qur'an's other courts do not work this way. Pharaoh consults his *mala'* and they tell him what he wants to hear, urging him to act against Mūsā. The chiefs of Nūḥ's people and of Hūd's speak to intimidate rather than to advise. What is described here is deliberation: a question posed, an answer given, and a counter-argument offered before a decision is reached. The Qur'an itself commands consultation of believers in their affairs and praises those whose matters are decided by mutual counsel. That a sun-worshipping kingdom is shown practising it, in a chapter that elsewhere condemns that kingdom's religion, is a distinction the text makes without comment and expects the reader to notice.

**The Question Is Left Genuinely Open**

Notice what she does not tell them. She does not say whether she is inclined to comply or resist, does not characterise Sulaymān as an enemy or a threat, and does not tell them what she thinks the letter means. She called it noble in verse 29 and stops there.

Withholding her inclination is the technical core of good consultation, and the reason is practical rather than moral. A council that knows the ruler's preference will supply reasons for it; advisors reason toward what they expect is wanted. By keeping her position unstated she preserves the possibility of learning something she did not already believe. Whether she was consciously applying a method or simply behaving as she always had, the effect is the same, and the answer she gets is one she is able to disagree with. Her disagreement, when it comes two verses later, is substantive and turns on a reading of history rather than of Sulaymān's character.

**A Model of Rule the Chapter Endorses by Depiction**

Ibn ʿArabī, commenting on this episode in his *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*, argued that the queen's handling of Sulaymān's letter demonstrated her fitness to rule. The judgment is sound on the text as given: she disclosed the letter, judged it fairly, consulted, reasoned from precedent, tested the sender, and revised her position when the evidence required it.

Setting that record beside the chapter's other monarch sharpens it further. Pharaoh's court had certainty and suppressed it. This queen had an error and corrected it. The Qur'an never comments on her being a woman, and the silence is eloquent in a text that comments freely on everything it finds objectionable. What the narrative endorses, by showing rather than stating, is a manner of governing — consultative, evidence-driven, capable of changing its mind — and it locates that manner in a ruler whose religion it condemns and whose eventual submission it records. The answer her chiefs give comes immediately.

**She Calls It "My Matter," Not "Our Danger"**

The phrase she uses is *fī amrī* — in this affair of mine. She does not describe the crisis as the kingdom's or the council's, and she does not distribute the weight of it before asking for help carrying it.

The wording keeps the ownership where it belongs while opening the reasoning to others, which is a harder combination than either extreme. A ruler who says *our problem* before consulting has already begun shifting responsibility; one who says nothing at all is not really asking. Hers is the middle position: the decision is mine and I want your judgment on it. That is also why her council's reply, which hands the matter back to her explicitly, is not an evasion but an acceptance of the terms she set. She named the affair as hers, they agreed, and the deliberation happened inside that frame. Everything that follows in the narrative — the gift, the journey, the declaration — is done by her, and the responsibility was assigned in this sentence.
