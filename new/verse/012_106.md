## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:106

> And most of them do not believe in Allah without associating others with Him ˹in worship˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Belief That Is Not Exclusive**

The verse describes a condition that looks contradictory from the outside. These people believe in God — the fact is granted at the start of the sentence — and their belief is accompanied by association. They are not atheists and they are not indifferent to the divine; they hold a conviction about God that does not exclude other objects of worship alongside Him. The chapter states the combination without explanation, and the statement is more precise than a general charge of unbelief would be. It identifies a particular religious condition rather than a general absence: belief about God's existence and power, held together with practices that treat other beings as sharing in what belongs to Him alone. The verse is therefore not about people who reject God but about people whose acceptance of Him is not exclusive.

**The Occasion Reported for the Verse**

The commentators record that it was revealed concerning the idolaters of Makkah, who acknowledged God as creator and provider while worshipping other deities alongside Him. The identification matters because it explains why the charge is not simply unbelief. The Qur'an repeatedly addresses an audience that accepts God's existence and His creative power, and disputes with it about what follows from that acceptance rather than about the acceptance itself. Verse 106 describes the same situation inside this chapter's closing section: people who already believe something, and whose difficulty lies not in the first premise but in the exclusivity it requires. The verse is thus addressed to a religious audience rather than to a sceptical one, which is why it follows a hundred verses of narrative designed for people who already accept God.

**Association Is Not Limited to Carved Objects**

The commentators extend the category considerably. What is set alongside God is not restricted to physical idols; it includes whatever a person treats as ultimate — desire, power, an idea, one's own judgement. The Qur'an makes the same extension elsewhere, describing the person who takes his own desire as his god and is left astray by God on account of his knowledge. The broadening is significant for how the verse is read after a hundred verses of narrative. Joseph's brothers acted on a preference of their own and described it as fair (12:18); a woman pursued a desire until it governed a household and then a city's gossip (12:30); a prisoner chose his own release over a promise made to a fellow inmate (12:42). None of these is idolatry in the ritual sense, and the commentators' reading of this verse covers all of them.

**The Verse Follows Directly From the Previous One**

Verse 105 described people passing by signs in the heavens and the earth with indifference. Verse 106 states what their belief looks like as a result of that habit. The connection is causal in the chapter's logic: inattention to evidence does not usually produce denial, it produces a mixed conviction in which God is acknowledged and other things are admitted alongside Him because nothing has been sorted out. The sūrah's diagnosis is therefore not that its audience lacks belief but that the belief it has is unsorted, and the sorting requires an attention the audience has not given. Nothing in the verse proposes that additional evidence would resolve this; the signs are already abundant, and the problem identified is what happens to them. That connection explains why no fresh evidence is produced. The previous verse established abundance; this one explains why abundance does not generate belief, and the explanation is association rather than insufficiency. A sign held alongside other objects of devotion loses its power to compel, which is a statement about the arrangement of a person's attention rather than about the sign itself.

**"Most of Them" Continues the Quantifier**

The proportion stated in verse 103 returns here and narrows the claim. Most people will not believe; most of them do not believe without association. The repetition of the quantifier is deliberate. Verse 103 concerned belief in the message; verse 106 concerns the character of the belief that does exist. The chapter is describing an audience in which outright rejection is not the dominant category. The dominant category is partial belief, and the verse treats partial belief as a condition requiring correction rather than as a stage on the way to full belief that will resolve itself. That treatment explains why the following verses turn to security and invitation rather than to further argument about God's existence. The turn is significant. Having established that most people will not believe, the chapter neither withdraws its address nor lowers its claim; it moves to what a person can actually rely on — security from an unexpected punishment, and an invitation stated in the first person by a messenger asking for nothing. The quantifier limits the outcome, not the offer.

**Contrast With the Preaching Inside the Story**

Joseph addressed two prisoners in verses 37 to 40 with an argument aimed precisely at this condition. He asked whether scattered lords are better or God alone, described the objects of their worship as names invented by them and their forefathers without any authority from God, and stated that the decision belongs to God, who has commanded that none be worshipped but He. Verse 106 states the same diagnosis in the narrator's voice to a different audience separated by centuries. The chapter thus contains both the argument against association, delivered by its prophet to two condemned men in a prison, and the statement that most people maintain association anyway. The two are held together without reconciliation. Both are true on the chapter's own account. Joseph called two prisoners to belief in a single verse and one of them was executed without ever responding; the sūrah then reports that most people do not believe even with many signs. The story supplies one successful call and one unsuccessful one, and the general statement covers both without preferring either.

**A Diagnosis Rather Than a Verdict**

Nothing in verse 106 assigns a punishment, closes a door or declares anyone beyond reach. It describes a religious condition in the present tense, and the verses that follow move immediately to questions about security and then to an invitation to a way declared publicly (12:107–108). The chapter's method in its closing section is consistent: state the condition, ask whether the audience feels safe inside it, and then declare what is being offered. Verse 106 belongs to the first of those three movements. It tells the reader what the obstacle is, and it does so in terms that most of its audience would recognise in themselves rather than in their neighbours — which is what makes it a diagnosis rather than an accusation. Locating it in themselves is the verse's purpose. A verdict would identify a group and close the matter; a diagnosis describes a condition the reader can test for in the ordinary business of a day during which signs are constantly present and constantly unfelt. The chapter ends its argument by asking the reader to examine his own attention rather than somebody else's belief.
