## Sūrah an-Naml 27:80

> You certainly cannot make the dead hear ˹the truth˺. Nor can you make the deaf hear the call when they turn their backs and walk away.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Limit Stated Without Softening**

The Prophet is told something blunt about his own effectiveness: there are people he cannot reach. Not people who are difficult, not people who need more time — people in the condition of the dead and the deaf.

This follows directly on the instruction to trust God, and the placement is deliberate. A man who has just been told to rely on God is now told what the reliance is for. He is not being promised that persistence will convert his audience; he is being informed that some of it is beyond his capacity, which is exactly the situation in which reliance becomes the only sane response. The Qur'an says the same thing to him elsewhere in almost identical words, and the repetition suggests it needed saying more than once.

**Two Images, Two Different Failures**

The verse gives two comparisons and they are not interchangeable. The dead cannot hear because they have no capacity left. The deaf described here cannot hear because they are deaf *and* walking away.

That second clause is doing the work. A deaf man standing still might yet be reached by gesture, by writing, by someone catching his eye. The image the verse constructs removes even that: the hearing is gone and the back is turned. What is being described is not a disability but a combination of incapacity and motion, and the motion is the part the person is responsible for. Al-Ṭabarī's reading of similar passages treats the deafness as acquired through refusal, which fits the chapter's account in verse 4 of hearts and eyes turned away after an initial rejection.

**Figurative, and the Figure Is Precise**

Nobody in Mecca was literally deaf in the sense meant, and the Qur'an is not describing a medical condition. It is describing what happens to the faculty of reception when it is repeatedly overruled.

The comparison works because the outcome is genuinely indistinguishable from the outside. A man who cannot hear and a man who will not hear behave identically in the presence of speech, and no amount of increased volume improves either case. That is the practical information the verse is giving the Prophet. His problem is not that he has been insufficiently clear — the chapter has called his message *mubīn* four times — and adjusting his delivery will not help. There is a category of listener for whom clarity is not the operative variable.

**The Speaker Is Not at Fault**

The verse's grammar keeps the responsibility away from the Prophet. He *cannot* make them hear; the incapacity is in them and the emphatic particle *innaka* marks the statement as a correction of something he may have believed.

That correction matters for how he was to understand the preceding years. A messenger watching his own clan refuse him has an obvious temptation to search his own conduct for the failure, and the chapter has already recorded him grieving. What this verse supplies is a diagnosis that locates the problem correctly. It is the same service Ṣāliḥ performed for his people in reverse, when they blamed their misfortunes on his presence and he told them they were being tested. Both cases involve someone attributing a cause wrongly and being corrected.

**Hearing as the Chapter's Central Faculty**

An-Naml has been about listening from its first pages. A prophet was called from a fire and heard a voice. A king was given the language of birds and overheard an ant. A hoopoe reported what he had seen and was heard out. A queen listened to a letter, then to her council, then to a correction about a floor.

Against all of that, this verse names the failure case. Everything the chapter admires is a variety of successful hearing, usually across a gap that should have made it impossible — a human hearing an insect, a king hearing a subordinate contradict him, a foreign monarch hearing a claim about her own error. The people described here are separated from a man speaking their own language in their own city, and the distance is unbridgeable. The chapter's next verse adds a third image to the two given here and then names the one group that can be reached.

**Why the Comparison Is to the Dead First**

The order of the two images is not arbitrary. The dead come first, and the dead are the more absolute case, which is an unusual way to build a comparison — the expected rhetorical move is to escalate.

Starting with the strongest image and then adding the qualified one has a specific effect. It establishes the outer limit immediately, so that the second image is read as a description of how a living person arrives at that condition. The deaf man walking away is the process; the dead man is where the process ends. The Qur'an uses death as a figure for unresponsiveness elsewhere and pairs it with life as a figure for faith, describing the believer as someone who was dead and whom God gave life. This verse names the state before that occurs, and the next verse will name the only people in whom the hearing works.

**A Statement About Audiences, Not About Fate**

One caution belongs here. The verse describes a condition, and nothing in it identifies which individuals are in that condition or declares the state permanent.

The chapter's own narrative forbids the harder reading. Sheba's queen worshipped the sun, led a nation that did so, and changed. The Meccan audience of this verse included men who fought the Prophet for years and then submitted. A verse taken as a roster of the damned would have been falsified within the lifetime of the people who first heard it. What it supplies instead is an explanation the Prophet needed at a particular moment: the absence of response is not always a measure of the speaker. Read as consolation it is accurate; read as prophecy about named persons it is neither what the words say nor what the history bore out.
