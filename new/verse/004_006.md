## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:6

> Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age. Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them. And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺. If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation; but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision. When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Testing Before Returning**

The command *wa abtalū al-yatāmā* — test the orphans — makes return conditional on evidence, not on age alone. A child reaches a marriageable age — *ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāḥ* — which the jurists linked to physical maturity and the capacity to assume family duties, but that alone does not prove financial competence. The Qur’an therefore adds a second threshold: *fa in ānastum minhum rushdan* — if you feel they are capable of sound judgment. *Rushd* in this context was explained as the ability to preserve wealth, to distinguish profitable from wasteful, and to avoid being easily deceived. Some authorities required that *rushd* be shown in both financial handling and general uprightness, while others focused on financial prudence as the immediate concern. The verse does not leave the test abstract. Guardians were expected to observe how the young person handled small amounts, fulfilled commitments, and responded to temptation. That practical testing turns guardianship from passive custody into active preparation. The orphan is not simply waited for; he is trained, supervised, and then trusted with his own property when his conduct shows he can keep it.

**Do Not Eat It Before They Can Ask**

The warning *wa lā taʾkulūhā isrāfan wa bidāran an yakbarū* — do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up to demand it — exposes a temptation that grows as the test nears its end. A guardian who senses the handover approaching may spend quickly, telling himself that small withdrawals will not be noticed or that the orphan will not know what was there. The words *isrāf* — wastefulness — and *bidār* — haste — name two distinct motives: spending beyond need, and spending ahead of time to pre-empt the claim. The Qur’an links both to eating — *akl* — as it did in *“do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables”* (4:2) and *“consume nothing but fire into their bellies”* (4:10), keeping the image of illicit gain as consumption that will later be consumed as fire. The clause *an yakbarū* — before they grow up to demand it — is psychologically precise. The sin depends on the victim’s inability to speak; once he can speak, the opportunity closes. By naming that window, the verse teaches guardians to treat the approaching adulthood not as a deadline to exploit but as a trust to complete. The best guardian is one who, as the account grows, feels more cautious, not more entitled.

**When the Guardian May Take and When He Must Refrain**

The rule *wa man kāna ghaniyyan fa al-yastaʿfif, wa man kāna faqīran fa al-yaʾkul bi al-maʿrūf* — if the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation; but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision — balances two risks. On one side is the well-off guardian who might justify a fee for his effort; the verse asks him to abstain — *yastaʿfif* — to be chaste, to hold back, preserving the trust with no deduction. On the other side is the poor guardian who does real work maintaining property, farming, trading, or managing a share, and who may need support while doing so; he is permitted *bi al-maʿrūf* — according to what is recognized as fair. Early jurists differed on measuring that fair amount — some tied it to the wage for similar work, others to what suffices for need — but all agreed it must be known, moderate, and not a hidden share. The sūrah elsewhere commands *“do not devour one another’s wealth illegally”* (4:29) and permits only what is by mutual consent; here, where consent cannot be fully given by a child, the law substitutes a standard of fairness judged by the community and watched by God. The guardian’s own wealth therefore sets the starting point for his attitude: the richer he is, the more he should give freely; the poorer he is, the more he must keep his taking measured and open.

**Call In Witnesses at Handover**

The instruction *fa idhā dafaʿtum ilayhim amwālahum fa ashhidū ʿalayhim* — when you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses — secures the moment of return. A handover without witnesses leaves both sides exposed: the orphan may later claim he received less, or the guardian may be accused of shortfall. Witnesses close that ambiguity and give both parties a record. The practice also appoints the community as guarantor of transparency. The Qur’an elsewhere requires witnessing for debts and bequests, as part of a larger system where property moves with documentation, and this verse brings orphans inside that system. The command does not suggest distrust of every guardian; it normalizes a procedure that protects the honest as much as it restrains the dishonest. In a society where much property was livestock, coin, and household goods without formal registries, two witnesses at the moment of transfer were the registry. Modern documentation fulfills the same purpose when it achieves the same clarity: the orphan knows what was returned, the guardian can show what he returned, and others can confirm it.

**Sufficient Is Allah as Reckoner**

The verse closes with *wa kafā bi Allāhi ḥasībā* — and sufficient is Allah as vigilant Reckoner. *Ḥasīb* gathers counting, reckoning, and sufficing; He counts precisely and He suffices as judge when human accounting is imperfect. That closing follows every stage that came before: testing, sensing sound judgment, refraining from hasty eating, taking only what is fair, and calling witnesses. After all visible steps are taken, the final audit belongs to God, who saw the small transfers no witness recorded and the intentions behind the timing. The same divine Name returns throughout the sūrah as a refrain for entrusted wealth, matching *“Allah is ever Watchful over you”* (4:1) at the opening and *“Allah is a Witness over all things”* (4:33) later. The repetition creates a frame: what was entrusted by creation is measured by reckoning at return, and the one who kept watch at the beginning is the one who reckons at the end. For the honest guardian, the phrase is reassurance; his care, done for God’s sake without fee, has a witness who misses nothing. For the careless, it is warning that the ledger is already complete.

**From Guardianship to Ownership Without Harm**

Taken together, verses 2 through 6 trace a full arc of trust. Return the wealth (4:2), do not exploit marriage to discount it (4:3–4), do not place it prematurely in weak hands while still meeting daily needs with kindness (4:5), and then, after testing, return it fully, without haste, with witnesses, and with God as the final auditor (4:6). The step *“test until they reach a marriageable age”* ensures that age is not a mechanical trigger and that judgment is not assumed; the phrase *“if you feel they are capable of sound judgment”* makes the guardian answerable for his perception, not only for a date. That balance protects both sides: the orphan from being handed what would ruin him, and from being held indefinitely under tutelage when he is ready. The sūrah therefore teaches a guardianship that ends well. The last image is not a child forever dependent, but a young adult, tested and found capable, receiving his property back in the presence of witnesses, with a clear record and a sufficient Reckoner over all.
