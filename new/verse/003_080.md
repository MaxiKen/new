## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:80

> And he would never ask you to take angels and prophets as lords. Would he ask you to disbelieve after you have submitted?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Prohibition**

The verse continues the description of what a prophet would never ask, and it names the objects: angels and prophets as lords. The previous verse excluded the invitation to worship the speaker; this one excludes the invitation to worship other venerable beings, and it does so in the same construction.

The two sentences together cover the whole range of the error. A false teacher may place himself in the position of a deity, or he may direct his followers to intermediaries whose standing is real but whose status is being inflated. The Book closes both routes with one sentence each, and the sūrah's wider argument has already supplied the principle: whatever has been created, including angels and messengers, stands on the servant side of the line.

**Angels and Prophets Named Together**

The choice of these two categories is not arbitrary. They represent the two classes of being that the Book treats as the closest to God, and the communities the sūrah addresses had strong traditions about each.

The angels are described in the Book as servants who carry out God's commands without disobedience, and the prophets as the most honoured of humanity. Naming them together as the objects the prohibition covers removes the possibility of a religion of elevated intermediaries, and it does so without denigrating the beings themselves. The Book elsewhere records a conversation in the Hereafter in which Jesus is asked whether he told people to take him and his mother as gods apart from God, and his answer is a declaration of his own servitude. The verse under discussion is that answer stated in advance as a rule about every prophet and every angel.

**The Rhetorical Question at the End**

The verse closes with a question: would he ask you to disbelieve after you have submitted? The question presents the proposal being rejected as incoherent on its own terms, since the invitation to worship something other than God is an invitation to unbelief, whatever language it is dressed in.

The Book's use of the question form here is different from its use in the earlier passages addressed to the People of the Book. There the questions asked opponents to explain their conduct; this one works inside the argument by showing that the conduct attributed to a prophet would contradict what a prophet is for. The implication is left for the reader to complete: if a man has brought people to submission to God, he will not afterwards turn around and ask them to place their submission elsewhere, and a report that he did so has misdescribed either him or what he said.

**Where the Verse Places the Prophets**

The sentence puts the messengers of God on the receiving end of the same prohibition that protects God's exclusive claim, which is a striking way of honouring them. They are protected from being made into objects of worship by a rule that is stated as a fact about their own conduct.

The Book's treatment of its prophets is consistent in this regard. Their honours are real and the sūrah has enumerated them for Jesus at length: a word from God, honoured in two worlds, brought near, taught writing and wisdom, given signs. What those gifts never become, in the Book's account, is a basis for a share in divinity, and the verse above supplies the reason in the form of what the prophet himself would refuse. The reader who has followed the sūrah's narrative from the family of ʿImrān to this point has seen the gifts and the refusal together, and the two are not in tension.

**The Two Verses as One Argument**

Read with the verse before it, the passage forms a single statement about the limits of loyalty in religion. A prophet directs his people to God and no further; he does not offer himself and he does not offer others, and any teaching that does either is not from the prophets.

The argument's placement in the sūrah is precise. It follows the passage about the community that attributed false statements to God and comes just before the covenant that God made with the prophets, in which the same messengers would be bound to acknowledge a messenger who came after them. The sūrah is constructing a picture of a single continuous mission whose whole purpose is the worship of God alone, and the two prohibitions in these verses are the boundary markers of that mission.

**What the Reader Is Asked to Weigh**

The verse invites its reader to consider a claim about a prophet's message by asking what the prophet himself would do with the loyalty that message produced. The test is available to anyone with a description of the prophet's teaching, and it does not depend on knowing any of the parties in the sūrah's dispute.

That is one of the ways the Book makes its arguments portable. The immediate controversy concerned communities and their doctrines, and the principle set out here concerns the structure of any teaching that claims to come from God.

There is also a warning in the verse's form that any community reading it can use. The two prohibitions deal with what a prophet would never ask, which means a religious practice can be tested by asking where it directs the loyalty it produces. A teacher who accepts veneration for himself, or who has taught his people to depend on intermediaries rather than on God, has moved outside the pattern the verse describes, and the Book's criterion for the messengers is stated here in their own voice. The following verse takes the same argument and gives it the form of a covenant, which turns the whole history of revelation into a single obligation.
