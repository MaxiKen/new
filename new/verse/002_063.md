## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:63

> And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Covenant Taken Under the Mountain**

The verse files the covenant's most physical scene: Allah takes the pledge from the community and raises the mountain over them in the same breath. The two acts are joined by a single *wa* — took a covenant, raised the mountain — and the joining is the verse's whole subject, because a covenant normally has a signing and this one has a setting. The word for mountain here is *ṭūr*, which the commentators note as a loan from Syriac meaning simply mountain, fully naturalized into Arabic — the same word that names the Sinai of Sūrah al-Ṭūr and the valley where Moses received his mission. The covenant being taken is the covenant of verse 40, the one the sūrah's whole address exists to renew: the Torah's obligations accepted by the community that will carry them. And the acceptance happens under a raised mountain, which the exegetes agree was raised *to frighten them* — the pledge extracted at the moment of maximum seriousness, with the community's standing literally under question. The scene's strangeness is its content: this is what covenant-signing looks like when the covenant is not a contract between merchants but the constitution of a people, received from the Lord of the worlds, by a generation that had just spent its wilderness years testing every alternative.

**Raised Over You: The Exegetes' Two Readings**

How the mountain stood over the camp divides the commentators, and both sides have grounds. The majority reading is literal and total: the mountain — Sinai itself, or a mass torn from Palestine — was uprooted, made to move, and held suspended above the Israelites like a canopy, the fear of its fall the sanction behind the pledge. The alternative reads the phrase as idiomatic: the mountain loomed above them, the way English says a danger *hung over* a people, and the looming was the theophany's terror — the thick cloud, the smoke like a kiln's, the violently shaking mountain of the Exodus account, with its standing order that whoever touches the mountain dies. The Qur'an's own elaboration settles the question toward the literal. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf describes the scene with two details no idiom needs: *"We raised the mountain over them as if it were a cloud and they thought it would fall on them"* (7:171). A mountain *like a cloud* — spread above them as shade is spread — and a community that *thought it would fall*: both details belong to a suspended mass, not to a looming landscape, and the second detail records the camp's own perception, which is exactly what the literal reading claims. The looming-idiom reading remains possible as language but must explain away 7:171's specific imagery; the stronger reading keeps both verses at face value. The mountain was held over them, and they looked up.

**Hold Firmly, With Strength**

The speech under the mountain is the covenant's clause: *hold firmly to that which We have given you, and observe what is in it*. The first verb — *khudhū*, take, seize — is governed by *bi-quwwah*, with strength, which the exegetes read as earnestness, seriousness, and settled resolve: take the Scripture the way a drowning man takes a rope, with the whole body's commitment. The strength is not intellectual assent but grip. The second command — *remember what is in it* in one reading, *observe its teachings* in this translation's rendering of *udh-kurū mā fīh* — makes memory a term of the covenant: the contents are to be held in recall, recited, kept present, because a book unremembered is a book not held. The same command is filed at verse 93 with the mountain scene repeated and the covenant's object named as obedience, and again in al-Aʿrāf with the identical words and identical purpose clause (7:171) — three filings of one signing. The object held is *what We have given you*: the Torah, as the commentators state plainly, the book given at the forty nights' end (verse 53). The verse thereby connects the recital's two mountain scenes — the gift received by Moses above and the covenant gripped by the people below — into one transaction: the Book descended, and the descent's condition was strength of hold and permanence of memory.

**So Perhaps You Will Become Mindful**

The clause of purpose is the covenant's stated end: *laʿallakum tattaqūn* — so that you may become god-fearing. The recital's purpose clauses have been accumulating — pardon for gratitude (52), the Book for guidance (53) — and this one names the deepest target, the virtue the sūrah opened with: guidance is for the mindful (2:2), and the Scripture's holding is for mindfulness. The sequence inside the verse is exact: strength of grip, then memory of contents, then taqwā as the formed disposition. Scripture does not produce god-fearing by being owned but by being held hard and kept in recall until its categories become the holder's reflexes — which is the verse's definition of what the covenant actually is: not a signed document but a training regime with an eschatological objective. The taqwā named here is also the covenant's only adequate sanction, which reorders the mountain scene: the mountain hanging overhead produces fear of falling, but the verse wants fear *of Allah* — and the difference between the two fears is the distance the Scripture must carry the community. The raised mountain gets the pledge spoken; the held Book gets the pledge lived. Verse 63 is thus the sūrah's statement of how a people under terror becomes a people under conscience: the terror is the door, the grip is the road, and mindfulness is the arrival.

**Consent Under the Canopy**

The scene raises the question every reader brings to it: what is a covenant worth if the mountain is hovering? Islamic thought has resources for the tension, and the verse's own text supplies the main one. A contract signed under compulsion is void in the law men make; this covenant is different in kind, because the compulsion is not a party extracting advantage but the Truth making its weight visible once — and the thing consented to is not a burden imposed for the imposer's benefit but the community's own constitution, whose terms the verse states in full: hold the Book, remember it, become mindful. The exegetical reports add the context that sharpens it: the mountain was raised because the community was balking — hesitating at the obligations, inclined to decline — and the raising was the mercy that made the decline impossible to complete. Fear here functions as the arrest that stops a worse choice, the way a parent's shout stops a child at the well's edge. The sūrah's later filings keep the tension honest: verse 93 records the same mountain raised and the same covenant taken, and the community's answer there — *we hear and disobey*, with the calf's love rooted in their hearts (2:93) — proves that the canopy did not manufacture inner assent. The mountain could compel the tongue; only the Scripture, held with strength, could compel the heart. The verse files both facts in one sentence: the most overwhelming signing scene in revelation, and a signing whose real ratification was left to memory and grip.

**The Sūrah's Covenant Register**

Verse 63 is the third filing of the covenant in this sūrah's first hundred verses, and the register is cumulative by design. Verse 40 states the covenant bilaterally — fulfil yours and I will fulfil Mine. Verse 53 files its instrument — the Scripture and Criterion given. Verse 63 stages its signing — the pledge taken under the canopy, with the terms spoken: hold, remember, become mindful. Verse 93 will refile the same scene with the community's answer recorded, and al-Nisāʾ gathers the whole package — the Mount raised for the covenant, the gate entered with humility, the Sabbath kept, the firm pledge taken (4:154) — into a single indictment list. The repetition is the covenant's own method: a bond of this weight is not signed once but re-read at every station, and the Qur'an's habit of refiling Sinai across sūrahs treats the covenant as a living instrument each generation re-enters. That is why the address is second-person — *We took a covenant from you* — spoken to Madinans about a mountain they never stood under: the pledge runs with the Scripture, and every community that receives the Book receives the scene. The verse's last word is its first demand: mindfulness. Everything else — the fright, the grip, the memory — is scaffolding around a single objective, and the scaffolding comes down, in the sūrah's own telling, the moment the community lets go.
