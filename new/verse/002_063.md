## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:63

> And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Covenant Taken Under a Mountain**

The sentence returns to the covenant made with this community and adds a detail that appears in several places in the Book: the mountain was raised above them. Commentators debated whether the mountain was physically lifted out of the ground or whether the language describes it looming over the people, and both readings agree on the effect. The community was made to understand the weight of what it was accepting.

The Book records the same scene in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf, where the mountain is described as a shadow, and it refers to it again when the people are reminded of the pledge they gave. The image is not a threat of annihilation so much as an enforced clarity. A people who had asked to see God and complained about food were now being given a text and shown what would happen if they treated it as casually as their ancestors had treated everything else.

**"Hold Firmly" and "Observe Its Teachings"**

The command has two parts, and they correspond to the two stages of receiving a text. The first concerns grasping it: the Arabic describes taking hold with strength, and the commentators understood it as an earnest commitment to act rather than a merely verbal acceptance. The second concerns what is inside the book: the people are told to remember what it contains, which presumes they will read and study it.

Those two requirements describe a relationship with a scripture rather than an attitude toward one. The Book's later addresses to this community fault them for not acting on what they know and for concealing what they have, and both charges presuppose the situation set out in this command. A community that held the text firmly and kept its contents in view would not be able to commit either offence.

**Why the Word "Perhaps" Appears Again**

The sentence ends with the same hopeful construction that the sūrah has used twice before: so that you may become mindful. The Book attaches this aim to several of the gifts it describes, including the pardon given to the community and the raising of the mountain, and the phrase consistently names the intended outcome rather than a guaranteed result.

The quality being sought is the one the sūrah's opening verse used to describe the people who profit from the Book. Everything the passage has related so far — the rescue, the provisions, the covenant, the punishments, the pardons — is arranged to produce that condition, and the Book states the intention openly. What the readers of the revelation do with the same material is left to them.

**The Mountain as a Witness**

The Book's account places the people in a position where the covenant is not a private understanding. A mountain standing over a crowd is visible to every person present, and it makes the moment a shared one. The Book returns to this scene when it addresses the community later, using it as a fact about their own history that they cannot deny.

That treatment is characteristic of the sūrah's method throughout the address. It recites the community's history and asks the community to recognise itself in the record, and the impressions made on the ancestors are treated as the property of the descendants. Whether a person accepts that the burden of a pledge can continue across generations is the question the address keeps pressing, and the covenant in this verse is the clearest case of it.

**A Text Held in a Frightened Community**

The instruction to hold firmly is given to people at the foot of a mountain, and the Book does not present the setting as incidental. Awe was available to them in the most literal form, and the command attached to the occasion was to keep hold of a book and to remember what was in it. The requirement did not depend on the mountain, but the mountain removed the possibility of misunderstanding the seriousness of the request.

The Book's narratives regularly use such events to establish a baseline for later conduct. Once a people has been addressed in this way, subsequent failure is measured against what they were shown, and the sūrah reports the failure in the very next verse. What the reader learns from the sequence is that the intensity of a moment does not by itself secure a people's obedience.

**The Covenant as the Frame of the Address**

This verse resumes the theme of the covenant that the sūrah introduced at the beginning of the address to Israel, and it does so in the middle of a recitation of their history. The reader who has followed the passage has been told what the community received, what it did with what it received, and how each failure was answered, and he now encounters the terms of the arrangement itself.

What the sūrah says next is that they turned away, and what it says after that is that mercy alone preserved them. The covenant in this verse is therefore the standard against which the rest of the story is measured, and the address continues to hold out the same book to the same people's descendants with the same command attached.

**The Covenant and the Book It Was Made About**

The pledge in this verse concerns a text, and the Book's habit is to describe covenants in terms of what they oblige. The command here has two halves — holding firmly and remembering the contents — and the second half presumes that the community had access to what was inside the book. A book held firmly and read is a book whose requirements can be met; a book held firmly and left closed cannot be violated because it emits no instruction.

The subsequent record of this community is written against both halves. The sūrah will report failures of conduct and, in the course of the same address, failures of teaching, including the concealment of what had been entrusted. The command at the mountain therefore establishes the standard for a community with a scripture, and everything the address says about their scholars and their conduct is measured against the two obligations stated here.
