## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:61

> And there are others who hurt the Prophet by saying, “He listens to anyone.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “He listens to what is best for you. He believes in Allah, has faith in the believers, and is a mercy for those who believe among you.” Those who hurt Allah’s Messenger will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Listening Is Not Gullibility**

The insult calls the Prophet an “ear,” suggesting that he accepts whatever reaches him and can be swayed by any speaker. The accusation confuses a virtue with a weakness. A leader who listens carefully is not necessarily a leader without judgment. The real question is what he listens to, how he tests it, and what he does after hearing it.

The reply gives the answer: he listens to what is best for the community. He receives truth, sincere counsel, and the words that lead to repair. He does not treat every report as equal merely because it has been spoken. The insult is defeated not by claiming that the Prophet hears nothing, but by showing that his hearing is directed toward good. Attentive leadership can be both compassionate and discerning.

**Faith Gives Hearing a Moral Direction**

The verse distinguishes his belief in Allah from his faith in the believers. His inner certainty is rooted in Allah, while his trust in sincere believers concerns their truthful testimony and moral standing. This distinction prevents two errors. A leader should not place human beings in the position of Allah, but neither should he treat every believer as a liar until proven otherwise.

Trust is not credulity. The Prophet's willingness to hear believers creates a community in which people can speak, report harm, and offer counsel. His faith in them also honors the moral agency of those who have earned trust. The hypocrites wanted this openness to appear foolish because it allowed them to hide behind oaths. The verse shows that compassionate trust is strong when it remains anchored in revelation and wisdom.

**Mercy Does Not Mean Surrendering Discernment**

The Prophet is called a mercy for those who believe. Mercy here includes receiving people with patience, protecting their dignity, and seeking what will benefit them. It does not mean allowing falsehood to rule the community. A merciful physician listens to the patient but still diagnoses the disease. A merciful judge hears the parties but still weighs evidence.

This balance matters for families and institutions. Some people use a leader's kindness to demand that every claim be accepted. Others use the possibility of deception as an excuse never to listen. The verse rejects both. The listener asks what is best, tests the report, and chooses a response that preserves truth and people together. Mercy becomes weak only when it refuses to make a judgment after the facts are clear.

**The Accusation Injures More Than a Person**

The hypocrites' words hurt the Prophet because they turn his care into a reason for contempt. A community that mocks its most patient listener teaches others to become hard and suspicious. People then stop bringing sincere concerns, and only the loud or manipulative remain confident enough to speak.

Speech about a religious leader therefore carries moral weight. Criticism can be necessary, and leaders must not be placed above evidence. But criticism should name an actual failure rather than inventing a character defect from a virtue. Calling patience gullibility, mercy weakness, or careful consultation indecision can make a community ashamed of the qualities it needs most.

**Hearing the Best Requires Rejecting Harm**

The phrase “what is best for you” also sets a standard for the listener. People do not merely need to hear more; they need to hear what benefits them. Some speech informs, some heals, and some only inflames. The believer cannot claim that every statement deserves equal attention when repeated exposure to mockery, rumor, and malice damages judgment.

This does not justify silencing uncomfortable truth. Beneficial speech can be painful when it corrects a beloved person. The test is its relation to truth, justice, and repair. A believer listens to the critic who wants the wrong corrected and resists the speaker who wants an audience divided. Good hearing is an ethical act, not passive consumption.

**Hurting the Messenger Has a Serious Consequence**

The warning at the end makes the issue larger than personal offense. To injure Allah's Messenger is to attack the trust he carries and the truth he conveys. The painful punishment corresponds to deliberate contempt for that office, not to every confused question or honest request for understanding.

The believer responds to attacks with dignity and truthful defense. He does not imitate the abuser's cruelty, because defending the Messenger requires honoring the mercy he embodied. He also examines his own speech: careless jokes, cynical insinuations, and contempt for prophetic guidance can injure the mission even when no audience sees the heart. The safest hearing is a hearing that receives truth with respect and lets mercy govern the answer.

A listener who seeks what is best for people may still have to reject a report, discipline a speaker, or expose a danger. That does not cancel mercy. Mercy is not the removal of every consequence; it is the effort to choose the consequence that leads toward truth and repair. The Prophet's example teaches leaders to keep the door of honest speech open while refusing to let slander determine policy. It teaches ordinary believers to speak carefully about those who listen with kindness. Patience should make truth easier to hear, not make falsehood impossible to challenge. When listening is joined to faith in Allah, compassion becomes a form of discernment rather than a surrender of judgment.

Compassionate hearing also protects the believer from the opposite mistake of distrusting everyone. A community in which every listener is called gullible will become a community in which only the manipulative speak with confidence. The Prophet's way gives sincere people a hearing while keeping revelation as the measure. A report is welcomed for its truth, not because it flatters the speaker. A correction is welcomed for its benefit, not because it humiliates an opponent. When this balance is practiced, listening becomes a form of service: it gathers what is useful, rejects what is harmful, and lets mercy guide the final decision.
