## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29

> Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Charges in a Single Question**

Lot's rebuke now names three practices, and the list widens from a sexual offence to the conduct of a whole town. The first charge states the desire; the second concerns travellers; the third concerns what the people do in their own assemblies. Read as a sequence, the three describe a community that has damaged its members, its visitors, and its public life, and the Book presents them as parts of one condition rather than as unrelated vices. This is the chapter's most detailed portrait of a corrupt town before the narrative moves on to the fates of the earlier peoples, and the specificity is deliberate: the warning given to Sodom was about identifiable behaviour, not about a general atmosphere of sin that nobody could have been expected to recognize.

The question reduces the town's practice to three complaints, and each of them concerns conduct a visitor would notice within a day. Nothing in the charge depends on private belief or hidden intention, which is why the verse presents the case as something the audience already knew and had stopped objecting to.

**The Road and the Stranger**

The second charge deserves attention because it concerns an institution older than any of the towns in the Book: the safety of the road. The Book elsewhere reports that the men were accustomed to shameful deeds and describes their aggression toward guests, and the practice of robbing or assaulting travellers is built into the charge here. Hospitality and safe passage were the connective tissue of the ancient world, and a town that made its reputation from the molestation of visitors had declared itself against the terms on which people moved and traded. The Book's interest in this detail is not incidental. It shows what happens when a community's appetite is given priority over its obligations to strangers, and it explains why the judgment that follows is presented as the end of a place rather than the correction of a few individuals.

The road also explains the form the offence took. A settlement on a caravan route grows used to foreigners, and familiarity with strangers is what turns the cutting of a way into a habit needing no planning; the Book's accounts of the city consistently describe the practice as public rather than hidden.

**Immorality in the Open Assembly**

The third charge is the most socially significant: the deeds were performed openly, in gatherings. The Arabic expression suggests assembly and public conversation, and the commentators understand the charge to describe a practice that had come into the open and was discussed and displayed among them. A private vice can coexist with public standards; a public practice means the standards themselves have been rewritten, and the town has decided that what it is doing is normal. This is the point at which a society loses the capacity to correct itself from within, since correction requires a majority that still regards the behaviour as wrong. The Book presents Sodom at that stage: the men do not hide from Lot, they argue with him, and their answer to his warning is a challenge.

**An Answer That Argues Nothing**

The town's reply contains no rebuttal. Instead the people demand that the punishment be brought forward if the warning is true, and the Book marks the tone as mockery. The same challenge is placed in the mouths of earlier communities: the people of ʿĀd tell their prophet to bring down what he threatens if he is truthful (46:22), and the people of Thamūd make the same demand of Ṣāliḥ. The Book treats the formula as a signature of a community that has stopped taking the warning seriously and wants an end to the preaching. It is also a confession of sorts, since the demand concedes that a punishment exists and that only its timing is in question. What the speakers ask for is a delay they believe will never expire.

What the sentence lacks is any attempt at justification. The townspeople do not deny the practices, cite a precedent, or claim the reports were exaggerated; they ask for punishment instead, which is the response of people who have run out of arguments and are relying on the weight of their position in the town.

**The Irony the Sentence Leaves in Place**

The Book does not comment on the mockery; it simply lets it stand in the record, since the reader who continues will see the answer arrive. Communities that issue this challenge always receive what they ask for, and the arrival never resembles their expectation. The demand for immediate proof of the threat is answered with a proof that removes the opportunity to profit from it, and the Book uses this reversal repeatedly as a demonstration that the timing of judgment belongs to God. In the case of Lot's people, the answer comes during the night and in a manner they had not imagined, and the mockery in this verse becomes the last public statement of the town. The challenge was issued by people who assumed they would be alive to see the outcome.

The irony is that the thing being demanded is the very thing the prophet came to warn them about, and the Book lets the contradiction stand without comment. Mockery of the warning turns into a request for its content, and a reader who notices the reversal understands why the following verses do not treat the demand as a serious inquiry.

**The Same Challenge in Sūrah al-Aḥqāf**

Sūrah al-Aḥqāf records the identical demand made to the prophet Hūd: when he warned his people, they challenged him to bring down what he threatened if he were among the truthful (46:22). The Book reports it as a recurring formula in its dialogues, which means the reader should recognize the shape of the sentence rather than treat it as a peculiarity of one town. Communities that have reached the end of their patience with a warning believe they have found a decisive test: if the messenger is right, the punishment will come, and if it never comes, the matter is settled in their favour. The Book's answer to that reasoning is not an argument but a history, and the histories it provides in this chapter are the record of what happened when the challenge was accepted.

**What the Mockery Reveals About the Mockers**

A demand for immediate punishment is not the language of doubt. It is the language of a group that has already settled the question and wants the discussion closed. The people of Sodom had watched Lot live among them, had heard his warning more than once, and had no answer ready for the charges he laid out. What they produced instead was a test they expected to fail, and the expectation is what the Book records for the reader. The sentence also shows how little room the prophet's presence had left them: they could not ignore him and they could not refute him, so they tried to move the matter beyond discussion by inviting the outcome themselves. The Book grants their request at a time of its own choosing, and the interval between the invitation and the answer is the last period of grace the town was given.
