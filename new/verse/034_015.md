## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:15

> Indeed, there was a sign for ˹the tribe of˺ Sheba in their homeland: two orchards—one to the right and the other to the left. ˹They were told:˺ “Eat from the provision of your Lord, and be grateful to Him. ˹Yours is˺ a good land and a forgiving Lord.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A People, Not a Queen**

Sabaʾ names a tribe and a kingdom in the south-west of the Arabian peninsula, in what is now Yemen, and the sūrah takes its title from this passage. The name is familiar from a different Qur'anic episode — the hoopoe's report to Solomon of a woman ruling there with a magnificent throne, whose people prostrated to the sun (27:22–24). Some commentators have joined the two accounts. The better reading separates them, on chronological grounds: the queen's people were contemporaries of Solomon, whose death has just been narrated, while the community destroyed here belongs to a much later phase of the same region's history. The Prophet was asked directly about the word, whether Sabaʾ was a land or a woman, and he answered that it was neither — it was a man who had ten sons among the Arabs, six of whom went south and four north (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3222, narrated by Farwah ibn Musayk, graded *ḥasan gharīb* by al-Tirmidhī; the same report appears in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 3988). So Sabaʾ is first a genealogy, then a people, then a kingdom.

**The Sign Was Where They Lived**

The verse says the sign was *fī maskanihim*, in their dwelling place. This is unusual. Signs in the Qur'an are more often events — a flood, a staff, a she-camel, a night journey — things that interrupt the ordinary. Here the sign is the address itself, the place they woke up in every morning. That relocation is the verse's first argument. A community can be given a standing miracle and, precisely because it stands, stop seeing it; the difficulty with permanent blessings is that permanence reads as ordinariness. The Qur'an makes the same move with Mecca, reminding its inhabitants that their valley was made *"a safe haven"* while people around them were seized (29:67), and that fruits of every kind were brought there as provision (28:57). Both peoples lived inside the evidence. The sūrah has just insisted that the created order is legible to *"every servant who turns ˹to Allah˺"* (34:9), and it now shows what happens when the legible thing is the farm outside the door.

**Two Orchards, Right and Left**

The description is spare and exact: *jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa shimāl*, two gardens, one on the right and one on the left. The dual form in Arabic means precisely two, not several, and the two flank the viewer — the picture is of someone standing between them with cultivation running away on either side. This corresponds closely to what is known of the region's agriculture. Maʾrib, the Sabaean capital, sat where a seasonal watercourse leaves the highlands, and its people built a dam and sluice system that spread the floodwater across two irrigated plains, one on each bank of the wadi. Sabaean inscriptions themselves refer to the site as Maʾrib and its two plains. Estimates of the irrigated area run to several thousand hectares, sustained entirely by catching water that would otherwise have run into the sand. So the two orchards are not a literary figure. They are the two halves of a hydraulic system, and the sign they constituted was engineering that worked.

**A Command in Two Words, Covering Both Hands**

The instruction given to them is as short as a sentence can be: *kulū min rizqi rabbikum wa-shkurū lah* — eat from the provision of your Lord and be grateful to Him. Two verbs, and between them the whole of what was required. *Eat* legitimises the enjoyment outright; nothing here asks them to renounce the harvest, to farm less, or to feel guilty about abundance. *Be grateful* attaches the single condition. Note that the provision is identified by its source before the command arrives — *your Lord's* provision — so gratitude is not being requested as a tax on pleasure but as an accurate acknowledgement of where the water came from. The Qur'an states the mechanism of gratitude as a standing rule: *"If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more"* (14:7). And it defines the opposite not as blasphemy but as forgetting, since *kufr* in Arabic first means to cover something over. A farmer who thinks the orchard is his own achievement has not argued against God. He has covered a fact.

**Good Land and a Forgiving Lord**

The verse closes with a pairing that repays attention: *baldatun ṭayyibatun wa rabbun ghafūr* — a good land and a forgiving Lord. These are two different kinds of thing, and putting them in one clause is the point. The first is the visible gift, the soil and the water; the second is the invisible provision that makes life with the first sustainable. *Ṭayyib* means wholesome, clean, pleasant — used of good food, good speech and good earth. *Ghafūr* is from the root *gh-f-r*, to cover or shield, so forgiveness in Arabic is a covering of faults rather than a pretence they did not occur. The combination anticipates a question their prosperity would eventually raise. A people that has everything will still fail, and the clause tells them in advance that failure was provided for. The land was good and the Lord forgiving, which means the destruction the next verse reports was not caused by the absence of a remedy. It was caused by the refusal to use one.

**What the Sūrah Is Setting Up**

This passage is placed immediately after the collapse of Solomon's kingdom and immediately before the ruin of Sheba's, and the arrangement does the argument's work without stating it. Solomon received extraordinary favour and his house was told to work gratefully (34:13); Sheba received ordinary favour — good soil, reliable water, a fed population — and was told the same thing in the same terms. The scale of the gift differs by an order of magnitude; the instruction attached to it is identical. That identity is what makes the story usable by the sūrah's Meccan audience, who commanded no winds and no jinn but did control a trading city that fed itself comfortably. The verse leaves them exactly where it leaves Sheba: inside a working arrangement, holding a two-word instruction, with nothing prohibited and one thing required.
