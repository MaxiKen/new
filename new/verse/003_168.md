## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:168

> Those who sat at home, saying about their brothers, “Had they listened to us, they would not have been killed.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Try not to die if what you say is true!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Restated**

The verse reports a group saying of their brothers, while they themselves sat at home, that had those brothers obeyed them they would not have been killed. The claim is the same one the sūrah answered earlier in its account of the people who spoke about those who had travelled or campaigned.

The repetition is deliberate. The community had been told not to resemble the speakers of that sentence, and the verse before the reader now reports the claim a second time and answers it with a challenge. The Book's repetitions of this kind mark the matters that needed the most attention, and the argument about the dead was evidently one of them.

**The Description of the Speakers**

The verse describes the speakers as sitting at home while they spoke about their brothers, and the participle marks their position throughout. They were not among those who had gone out.

The commentators identified them as the hypocrites, and some connected the verse with the leader of that group and his companions. The comment on this passage also notes that the brothers in question could be blood relatives as well as brothers in the faith, and the grief of the community at Uḥud touched many households. The claim the verse reports was therefore being made about people who were close to the speakers as well as about fellow believers.

**Why the Counterfactual Fails**

Al-Rāzī's comment on this verse explains the logical problem with the claim. The speakers asserted what would have happened under circumstances that had not occurred, which in effect claims knowledge of what God would have done had things been otherwise.

The sūrah had already given the answer to such claims when it stated that those who were destined to be killed would have gone out to the places where they were to die even if the community had stayed in its houses. The claim that obedience to the speakers' advice would have preserved the dead assumes that the appointed term can be moved, and the Book denies that assumption on grounds that were explained earlier in the passage.

**The Challenge Issued in Reply**

The verse's answer takes the form of a challenge: then ward off death from yourselves, if what you say is true. The Book uses this form when it wants a claim to be recognised for what it is, and the challenge here is addressed to people who were sitting safely at home.

The demand is impossible, and its impossibility is the argument. The men who claimed that a different decision would have saved their brothers were being told to demonstrate their power over death in their own case first. The Book's method in such sentences is to make the implication of a position visible, and the group's claim collapses as soon as it is stated in the challenge's terms.

**The Persons and the Address**

The challenge is addressed to the speakers as a body, and the Book reports their words before answering them so that both parts of the exchange are preserved. The community that recites the passage hears the claim and the answer together.

The sūrah's practice in the whole passage is to leave its readers with the exchange rather than with a conclusion. The listeners are expected to recognise the form of the argument when they meet it again, whether in their own speech or in the talk of others, and the verse supplies them with the reply in a form short enough to remember.

**The Connection With the Earlier Warning**

The passage that forbade the believers to imitate those who spoke in this way had already stated that God gives life and causes death. The verse before the reader returns to the same subject from the other side, addressing the people whose words the community had been told not to adopt.

The connection gives the passage its unity. The first statement warned the community about the sentiment; this verse shows the sentiment being answered directly; and the verses that follow give the community the truth about the state of those who had died. The three movements follow one another in the sūrah's usual order, from instruction to argument to consolation.

**The Book's Teaching About Death in the Passage**

The sūrah has stated several times that death comes at an appointed term and by the permission of God, and it has denied that human arrangements can change the time. The verse before the reader treats the community's losses as falling under that teaching rather than outside it.

The claim that the group was making would have required the appointed term to depend on a decision about whether to march or stay, and the Book's statements about the term exclude that. The community's dead had died when they died because the term had arrived, and the sūrah's answer to the claim rests entirely on that fact.

**The Verse's Function in the Sequence**

The verse prepares the ground for the declaration about the martyrs that follows it. The community had been hearing arguments about whether the dead had died needlessly, and the answer to those arguments is given first in the form of a challenge and then in the form of a statement about where the dead now are.

The order is effective for people in grief. The Book refutes the claim before it consoles, and the refutation removes the framework in which the community's losses would have looked like a waste. What remains, when the argument has been dealt with, is the sentence about the living, and the community is given it in the next verse.

**The Answer That Comes Two Verses Later**

The challenge in this sentence is not left standing on its own. The group is told to avert death from themselves if they are truthful, and the sūrah then turns, in the verses that follow, to those who have been killed in God's way and states that they are alive with their Lord and provided for. The counterfactual and the reality are placed next to each other, and the answer to the challenge is given in the form of a fact about the dead rather than a debate about probability.

The Book uses the same method elsewhere. It tells people that death will overcome them wherever they are, even in fortified towers, and then takes up their habit of attributing good and evil to the wrong source (4:78). The point in both passages is that the attempt to control an outcome by avoiding a place or a decision is an illusion, since the term is set and the location does not change it. The talk of the group in this verse is answered, therefore, not by denying that men die but by stating what death delivers them into.
