## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:19

> Have they not seen how Allah originates the creation then resurrects it? That is certainly easy for Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Argument Built From What the Deniers Already Concede**

The question does not begin by asking whether God can raise the dead. It begins with the fact of creation, which the audience already affirms, and then moves from that fact to the conclusion it wants them to accept. This is the Book's standard method in disputes about the resurrection: find the premise both sides hold, and show that it already contains what the denier is refusing. The pagans of Makkah did not deny that God made the heavens and the earth; they assigned Him that role and then added partners to Him. Having granted the first creation, they had no ground left for doubting the second, and the sentence presses exactly there. The question in its opening words is not rhetorical decoration; it identifies a piece of evidence that the listeners can verify with their own eyes every day of their lives.

The verse also assumes that the audience has been looking at that evidence without drawing anything from it. The Arabic verb for seeing in this construction carries the sense of considering rather than merely perceiving, and the Book uses it when it wants to distinguish an observation that has been registered from one that has been thought about. A farmer in the Hijaz watched his fields come up after rain every year, and a herdsman watched calves and lambs arrive in season, and both of them could describe the process in detail without ever asking what it implied about their own end. The sentence is addressed to people in that position, and it treats their failure as a failure of inference rather than of observation. What follows from the evidence is not complicated: a maker who produced the first instance of a thing has demonstrated the only capacity the second instance requires. The sūrah presses the point without elaborating the biology, because the argument needs no more data than the audience already possessed.

**One Act, Two Occurrences**

The Arabic of the sentence describes God originating the creation and then bringing it back, and the Book repeats the pairing so often that it functions as a formula. He originates the creation and then resurrects it, which is even easier for Him (30:27), and the same sequence is given as the reason why the return to Him is a promise that cannot fail: He originates the creation, then resurrects it, so that He may justly reward those who believe and act well (10:4). The logic of the comparison is simple and airtight. If an agent can produce a thing from nothing, producing it a second time from existing material cannot be the harder operation. The Book chooses the easier word deliberately, since the objection it is answering is always a claim about difficulty, and it answers that claim by removing every possible ground for it.

**Why the Verse Says "Easy" of God**

The word is used with care, because the Book does not describe God as a craftsman who finishes one task and takes up another. No fatigue, no effort, and no passage of strength is in view. When ease is attributed to God in these passages, the purpose is to deny the analogy the objector is making between divine action and human work. A human builder does not remake a house he has demolished without cost, and the denier smuggles that experience into his reasoning about resurrection. The Book's answer is that the category does not transfer. The creation of a human being from a drop of fluid and the raising of that human being from dust are, for the agent who does them, one kind of act rather than two kinds, and the sentence names that single act with two verbs to keep the sequence in view.

**The Interval That Is Not an Obstacle**

Between the two creations lies death, and the denier treats the interval as a wall rather than a passage. The Book treats the decayed body as material that the Maker owns and can reassemble, and its argument never depends on the preservation of the corpse. Dust and bones are named in the objection the Book records elsewhere, and the answer given is always the same: the One who formed the structure the first time is not limited by the condition of the materials. Commentators point out that this is why the verse says God resurrects, using the intensive form, rather than merely saying that He returns things to life. The word depicts repeated, sustained action, which suits a claim about a general resurrection rather than a single exception to nature.

**Where the Verse Sits in the Sūrah's Case**

The sentence functions as a hinge in the chapter's argument. Immediately before it, Abraham told his people that they worshiped objects which cannot provide, and that everything returns to God. Immediately after it, the listener is sent out to walk the earth and observe the origin of life for himself. This verse supplies the doctrinal middle step: the God who made everything is the God to whom everything returns, and the possibility of a second beginning is contained in the first. The Book's order is instructive. It argues from the visible world, then from a fact about God's capacity, and finally from the testimony of travelers. Nothing in the chain requires the hearer to accept a report he cannot test; the whole case is built from ordinary things that no one in the audience had ever thought to press to their conclusion.

**The Choice the Argument Forces**

Once the premise is granted, only two positions remain. A person may hold that the world had a maker and that the maker will never call it back, in which case he must explain what happened between the first act and the second and how the second became impossible for the agent of the first. Or he may accept that the same capacity governs both events. The Book presses the choice because it wants the denial of the resurrection to be recognized for what it is: not a conclusion drawn from evidence but a preference protected from evidence. The sentence under discussion is short because the argument is short. A creation that exists is already a resurrected thing waiting to happen, and the Book considers that observation sufficient to end the discussion. The objection, when it is finally raised, will be phrased as a question about bones and dust, and the answer given then will be this answer repeated: the first act settles the second, and nobody who has watched a garden emerge from a seed has grounds for surprise at the announcement.
