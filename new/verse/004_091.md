## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:91

> You will find others who wish to be safe from you and their own people. Yet they cannot resist the temptation ˹of disbelief or hostility˺. If they do not keep away, offer you peace, or refrain from attacking you, then seize them and kill them wherever you find them. We have given you full permission over such people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Others Who Wish to Be Safe From Both Sides**

The verse opens *sa tajidūna ākharīna yurīdūna an yaʾmanūkum wa yaʾmanū qawmahum* — you will find others who wish to be safe from you and safe from their own people. The pattern is double desire: safety from the believers and safety from their own tribe, without joining either in commitment. The previous exception had spared two groups — those joined to treaty people and those whose chests are constricted at fighting either side (4:90) — for whom God did not give a way. This verse finds a third face that looks like the second but is not: a people who display peace to both sides while keeping a door open to hostility. *Yurīdūna an yaʾmanūkum* — they wish to be safe from you — was explained as showing Islam before the believers and showing hostility’s religion before their own, so that neither side harms them. The sūrah has shown this oscillation before: the lagger who says “God blessed us for not being there” when you suffer, and “would we had been there” when you gain (4:72–73), the speaker whose *mawaddah* — affection — shifts with gain. The wish for double safety is another *mawaddah* that changes with advantage.

**Whenever Returned to Temptation They Are Plunged Back**

The description *kullamā ruddū ilā l-fitnati urkisū fīhā* — yet they cannot resist the temptation, whenever they are returned to temptation they are plunged, thrown headlong into it — was read with *fitnah* as the trial of hostility and sedition, or of idolatry and disbelief. *Fitnah* in the Book ranges from civil strife — *“fight them until there is no strife”* (2:193) — to trial — *“We made some of you a trial for others”* (25:20) — to the testing of beliefs — *“We tried them as We tried the owners of the garden”* (68:17). Here commentators noted the ease of return: whenever they are called back to *fitnah* they fall in, as if their display of safety was only a surface (Ṭ, Q). The sūrah has already described this slide: the hypocrites who turn away stubbornly when called to God and the Messenger (4:61), the people whose trade was for misguidance and who wish you to deviate (4:44), and the regressed whom God allowed to regress because of misdeeds (4:88). Ibn ʿAjībah compared this group to the wavering aspect of the soul, torn between obedience and disobedience, that must be fought when it urges disobedience. The verb *urkisū* — they were cast, plunged — was explained as being turned upside down into it, as one thrown headlong, showing not a slip but a relapse. The phrase gives the criterion that separates this group from the constricted of 4:90: the constricted are distressed at fighting either side and stay withdrawn; these, when returned to hostility, are plunged back without distress.

**If They Do Not Keep Away or Offer Peace**

The condition *fa in lam yaʿtazilūkum wa yulqū ilaykumu al-salām wa yakuffū aydiyahum* — if they do not keep away, offer you peace, or refrain from attacking you, do not restrain their hands — repeats the three signs of genuine peace from 4:90 — withdrawal, casting peace — *alqā al-salām* — and restraining hands — but as a negative test. To keep away — *iʿtazala* — was explained as withdrawing from the conflict; to offer peace — as casting *salām* with sincerity, the same peace the sūrah commanded to answer with better or same (4:86); to restrain hands — as refraining from fighting. The triple condition was read as sparing any of the three: if they do any one sincerely they are spared, as the previous verse gave “no way” over those who withdraw and offer peace. Early reports identified such people among tribes between Madinah and Makkah who tried to be at peace with both, and among persons who outwardly professed belief in Madinah then returned to idolatry in Makkah, showing that the category is not an ethnicity but a behaviour — the wish for double safety plus the ease of being returned to *fitnah*. The sūrah’s standard — fight for the oppressed who cry for a guardian and helper (4:75) — does not extend to fighting those who genuinely withdraw; this verse says the same negatively: if they do not withdraw or offer peace or restrain, then they are not the constricted and the permission returns.

**Seize and Kill Where You Find Them — With Clear Warrant**

The measure *fa khudhūhum wa aqtulūhum ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum* — then seize them and kill them wherever you find them — was explained as the same wartime measure given for the hostile turners who wish you alike in disbelief (4:89) and as given generally for idolaters who initiate hostilities (2:191; 9:5), limited by the three signs of peace. To seize — *khudh* — and to kill — *uqtilū* — where you find them — *thaqiftumūhum* — to come upon, to gain mastery — was read as upon encounter in the field, not pursuit into the protected. The closing *wa ulāʾikum jaʿalnā lakum ʿalayhim sulṭānan mubīnā* — We have given you full permission, a clear warrant, an evident authority over such people — was explained as *sulṭān* — proof, warrant, authority — openly shown. The phrase means the law has made the cause clear: those who do not withdraw, do not offer peace, and do not restrain hands, and who whenever returned to *fitnah* are plunged back, may be seized. The sūrah has said that God is sufficient as Guardian and Helper (4:45), that the oppressed cry for a helper from grace (4:75), and that good and evil are from His decree while evil from the self as means (4:78–79); the warrant here is the community’s instrument of that guardianship where double-faced hostility would otherwise expose the weak for whom burdens were lightened.

**A Verse That Separates Hesitation From Duplicity**

Placed after the exception for treaty and for the constricted, this verse completes the sūrah’s threefold map: bound by treaty, constricted and withdrawn, and double-seeking. The first two are not to be harmed; the third is to be, unless it meets the same three signs. The distinction matters for the community that was divided into two groups about the hypocrites (4:88) and that was commanded not to take as allies those who wish you alike in disbelief until they emigrate (4:89). To be safe from both sides while ready to be plunged back into hostility is not neutrality; it is duplicity that leaves the oppressed without a guardian. The sūrah’s ethic — *“do not kill yourselves, God is ever Merciful to you”* (4:29), do not consume wealth unjustly, return trusts and judge fairly (4:58), and fight so that God may curb the might of disbelievers (4:84) — is therefore not indiscriminate: where a heart is constricted it is spared, where a treaty holds it is respected, and where double safety hides readiness to be plunged back into *fitnah*, the clear warrant is given unless peace is offered and hands restrained. The next verse will turn from the field to the home, and from permission to prohibition, with the rule that a believer does not kill a believer except by mistake (4:92).
