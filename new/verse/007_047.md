## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:47

> When their eyes will turn towards the residents of Hell, they will pray, “Our Lord! Do not join us with the wrongdoing people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Eyes That Turn Toward the Fire**

The verse describes the moment at which the gaze of the people on the heights moves from one side of the barrier to the other, and the movement produces a prayer. They have greeted the people of the Garden in the previous verse and stated their hope of entering; when their eyes fall on the company of the condemned, they ask their Lord not to place them among them. The scene records the two directions of a single outlook, and the prayer follows the sight that produces it.

The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter often use the direction of the eyes to mark the state of a person, and the sūrah of the Clatterer describes the faces of the saved as radiant and laughing while the faces of the condemned are covered with dust. The verse in front of us uses the same instrument, and the sight of the Fire turns the hope of the people on the heights into a petition.

**The Prayer Against the Company of the Wrongdoers**

The words of the prayer are brief and precise: do not place us among the wrongdoing people. The Book records the same petition from Moses, who asks his Lord not to count him among the wrongdoing people when he fears that he may share their fate (23:94), and the sūrah of the Family of Imran records the prayer of the believing community for forgiveness and for the protection of their Lord. The verse in front of us gives the people on the heights the same petition in the same words.

The prayer asks to be spared a company rather than a punishment, and the choice of words matches the scene. The people on the heights are looking at the inhabitants of the Fire, and what they ask is that they not be numbered with them. The Book's habit of describing the two outcomes as two companies appears throughout its accounts of the last day, and the sūrah's later verses continue the exchange between the two groups.

**Fear and Hope at the Same Station**

The verse places the two poles of religious feeling in one person, since the people on the heights hope to enter the Garden and pray to be spared the company of the Fire. The Book joins the same two dispositions in its instructions to the community, and the sūrah of the Heights commands the audience to call upon God with hope and fear (7:56), while the sūrah of the Prostration praises those who call upon their Lord in fear and hope (32:16). The verse in front of us presents the two dispositions in action.

The commentary tradition treats the balance of fear and hope as the mark of a sound religious condition, since fear without hope produces despair and hope without fear produces carelessness. The people on the heights in front of us illustrate the balance, and their prayer states the resolution that the sūrah recommends to its audience in its commands about supplication.

**The Wrongdoing That the Prayer Avoids**

The term used in the prayer is the same one the sūrah has applied to the communities that were destroyed, to the proud who were excluded from the Garden (7:40), and to those who turned others from the way of God (7:45). The people on the heights ask their Lord not to place them in that company, and the petition therefore acknowledges that the company exists and that it has a cause. The verse states the request without stating the action that produces the outcome.

The Book's treatment of the term wrongdoing as the description of a settled condition rather than of a single act appears throughout the sūrah, and the confession of the first pair uses the same vocabulary when they say that they have wronged themselves (7:23). The prayer in front of us belongs to the same moral vocabulary, and it places the speaker among those who fear the condition rather than among those who have entered it.

**The Company of the Hereafter**

The prayer against being placed with the wrongdoers reflects the Book's teaching about the company a person keeps in the Hereafter. The sūrah of the Ornament states that close friends will be enemies on that day except for the mindful (43:67), and the sūrah of the Confederates records the followers blaming their leaders and asking for a double share of punishment for them (33:67–68). The two passages describe the same separation that the prayer in front of us seeks to avoid.

The Book's descriptions of the saved include their gathering as brothers on couches facing one another, and the sūrah of the Rocky Tract states that God has removed rancour from their hearts (15:47). The petition in front of us asks for a place among that company, since the opposite of being numbered with the wrongdoers is being numbered with the grateful. The verse states the request, and the sūrah's following verses complete the scene.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands forty-seventh in the chapter, and it supplies the second half of the speech of the people on the heights. The greeting of the previous verse and the prayer in front of us belong to the same station, and the address to the proud among the condemned follows in the next verse. The passage therefore presents the people on the heights as speakers in both directions.

The reader who has been given the two companies in the preceding verses now hears the petition of those who stand between them. Their greeting of the first group and their prayer against the second state the choice that the sūrah's opening command has placed before its audience, and the chapter's remaining verses continue the account of the two ends.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports that the people on the heights call out to some of the people of the Fire whom they recognize by their appearance, and they tell them that their numbers and their arrogance have not profited them (7:48). The prayer in front of us is followed by the address to the condemned, and the speech of the heights is completed in both directions.

The two verses together present the intermediate station as a position of knowledge and speech. The people on the heights see both companies, greet the one, pray to be spared the other, and address the proud among the second; and the sūrah then states the mercy that the condemned had denied to the weak among the believers.
