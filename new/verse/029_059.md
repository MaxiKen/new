## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:59

> those who patiently endure, and put their trust in their Lord!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Qualities Identified as the Reason**

The verse is a continuation rather than a new sentence, and its function is to say who the workers of righteousness are. The Book singles out two dispositions: patient endurance and trust in God, and it presents them as the qualities that connect a believer to the reward just described. The pairing is not a list of separate virtues but a description of one posture. Patience faces what has already happened, and trust faces what has not yet happened; a person who lacks either is working with half the equipment, and the sūrah has spent its length describing what happens to people who have neither. The two words also complete the chapter's answer to its opening question about faith and trial.

The two qualities are set out in the order in which a migrant needs them. Patience is what a household requires while it waits for work, and trust is what it needs when the work does not arrive, so the pair covers the whole of the interval rather than a single trial inside it.

**Patience as the Discipline of the Long Interval**

The Arabic term covers more than the endurance of pain. It describes holding oneself in check, and the Book applies it to the waiting that occupies most of a believer's life. The emigrants addressed earlier in the sūrah were waiting before they left and waiting after they arrived; the community under pressure in Makkah was waiting for relief that might be years away. The Book's instruction to seek help through patience and prayer in another chapter is addressed to believers who are being asked to remain steady without a schedule (2:153). The quality named in this verse is what makes that interval survivable, and the Book attaches a promise to its company rather than to its results.

The Book's patience is measured in seasons rather than hours. Years of servitude in Egypt, a mission lasting centuries, a decade of drought, and a long exile all appear in its accounts, and the quality praised in this verse is the one that survives a period long enough for the original reasons for the sacrifice to be forgotten.

**Trust in God as the Ground of the Patience**

The second quality explains how the first is possible. The Arabic term describes entrusting one's affairs to another and relying on him to manage them, and the Book uses it for the believer's relationship to God in the specific context of provision and outcome. Sūrah al-Ṭalāq states the connection in one verse: God provides for a person from sources he could never imagine, and whoever places his trust in God finds Him sufficient, since God achieves His purpose and has set a destiny for everything (65:3). Trust in this understanding is not optimism about circumstances; it is a decision about who is handling them. The sūrah has already told its readers that the earth is spacious and that death is certain, and the verse's two qualities are what a person needs to act on both facts at once.

**Why These Two Define the Migrant**

Commentators observe that the believers described here are set against the disbelievers of the preceding verses, who demanded that the punishment be hastened and mocked the delay. The contrast is exact. One group cannot tolerate the interval and tries to force the outcome forward; the other endures the interval and leaves the outcome with its Lord. Sūrah al-Naḥl describes the same people with the same pair of words, saying of those who emigrated and were persecuted that they endured patiently and placed their trust in their Lord (16:42). The two dispositions therefore mark the community that accepted the cost of leaving home, and the Book presents them as the alternative to impatience rather than as an additional set of obligations.

**Patience That Is Not Passivity**

The Book's usage prevents the misunderstanding that patience means doing nothing. The same chapters that praise endurance describe the emigrants packing up and moving, the fighters standing their ground, and the Prophet consulting his companions before a decision and then placing his trust in God (3:159). What the quality removes is not action but anxiety about the outcome, and it is precisely what allows action to be taken under threat. A person who believes that the result depends entirely on his own arrangements has a reason to avoid risks; the person who has entrusted the result has a reason to accept them. The verse's two qualities therefore support the practical obedience described throughout the sūrah, from keeping the prayer to leaving a city.

**The Company This Pair Joins**

The Book attaches these dispositions to some of its highest descriptions. God is with the patient (2:153), He loves those who trust in Him (3:159), and the promise of assistance follows the command to persevere rather than to prevail (8:46). The verse under discussion places patience and trust at the end of a chapter that has argued about evidence, judgment, and the fate of nations, and it does so without adding any other condition. What the sūrah leaves with its reader is a definition of the person who receives the mansions described in the previous verse: someone who waits without demanding, and who acts without owning the result.

The Book keeps a roll of people whose lives make the same argument. Abraham leaving his father's house, Moses leaving Egypt, the youths leaving a city of idolaters, and the believers leaving Makkah all stand in that line, and the verse places its audience inside a company whose members had already survived the choice it asks of them.

**A Sentence That Summarizes the Chapter's Teaching**

Read against the whole sūrah, this short line gathers its argument into a single portrait. The chapter began by asking whether people would be left untested, surveyed the fates of nations that answered their messengers with mockery, and closed by naming the two qualities that carry a believer through a life of testing. The Book typically ends its chapters with such a summary, and it usually places it after the promises rather than before them, so that the reader knows what the qualities are for. The next verse supplies the practical consequence of trusting God for provision, and it takes the argument out of the Hereafter and returns it to the difficulty of living in the present.

The sentence also gathers motives the preceding verses have distributed one at a time. Migration, the loss of property, hostility from relatives, and the temptation to read hardship as abandonment are all answered here with two reasons, and a reader who has followed the chapter from its opening lines will hear much of it compressed into one clause.

**Remembering God in Every Posture**

The Book asks for remembrance in circumstances that leave no room for a scheduled appointment: believers remember God standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth (3:191), and the instruction given to the community after battle is to remember God in the same three postures, then to establish prayer properly once they are secure (4:103). The two verses together show why the sūrah attaches patience and gratitude to the promise of a home. Neither quality can be practised only in a mosque or only in a moment of quiet. They are the practices that survive a journey, a night in a strange village, and a season of waiting, and the Book's pairing of the two with the reward of the righteous is an instruction about what to do while the reward is still pending.

**What the Two Words Ask of a Person's Day**

The pair of qualities is stated in the abstract, and the sūrah's readers would have measured them in the concrete conditions the chapter has described: confinement, pressure to conform, and the loss of a household's security. Patience in those circumstances meant the refusal to abandon a position under sustained pressure, and trust meant the decision not to settle the outcome by unbelief. Neither quality is measured by feeling, and neither depends on a person's temperament; both are decisions that show in behaviour, which is why the Book attaches them to the reward as qualifications rather than as moods. A reader who wants to test whether he possesses them is directed by the verse to look at his conduct during a difficult interval rather than at the state of his emotions.
