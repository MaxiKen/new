## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:12

> **And ˹He is the One˺ Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs, and made for you ships and animals to ride**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Azwāj Kullahā: Every Kind of Pair**

The verse continues the list of God's works with a claim about the whole of creation: He created al-azwāj kullahā, the pairs, all of them. Zawj in Arabic is one member of a pair, and by extension a kind or class, since kinds come in matched sets. The exegetes offer three readings and each catches something the word can carry. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī take the pairs as the species of living things, so that the verse says God made every kind of creature. Al-Qurṭubī also records the narrower sense that every animal kind has male and female, which the Qurʾān affirms elsewhere for plants and for things unknown to human beings (36:36, 51:49). Al-Rāzī takes the widest view: the pairs are all opposites, day and night, land and sea, sweet and bitter, life and death. The word kullahā, "all of them", supports the widest reading, and the three senses do not conflict. Whatever exists in the created order exists as one term of a pair, whether the pair is sex, species, or opposite.

**Pairs Point to the One Who Has No Pair**

Al-Rāzī draws a theological conclusion from the pairing that goes to the heart of the sūrah. Everything created comes in pairs, and to be one of a pair is to be relative, defined by what one is not, dependent on a counterpart. That which is paired is by nature multiple and contingent. The Creator of all pairs cannot Himself be paired, or He would be one more item in the creation He is said to have made. The verse therefore contains, in a single word, an argument for the absolute oneness of God. This is not an incidental point in al-Zukhruf. The sūrah's central charge against the Quraysh is that they gave God partners and daughters, that is, that they paired Him, and this verse quietly lays the ground for the rebuttal. The One who made the pairs has no consort and no offspring, because consorts and offspring are what pairs produce, and He is outside the system of pairing altogether. The same reasoning appears in 6:101: how could He have a child when He has no consort, and He created everything? Pairs belong to creatures. The Creator is fard, single, in a sense that nothing created can be.

**Ships: A Blessing the Makkans Depended On**

The verse turns from creation in general to two things made "for you": al-fulk, ships, and al-anʿām, livestock. Ships come first, and the Qurʾān names seafaring as a blessing in 14:32, 17:66, 22:65, and 30:46. The Quraysh were inland traders, but their trade touched the sea at Jeddah and their caravans to Yemen and Syria connected to maritime routes; and the Arabs of the coast lived by boats. The verse says God "made" ships for people, jaʿala lakum, though ships are built by human hands. The exegetes explain that God made ships in the sense that He created the wood, taught the craft, and, above all, made the sea capable of bearing them. A ship is useless without water that holds it up and wind that moves it, and neither is a human product. The Qurʾān makes the point directly in 45:12: God subjected the sea so that ships may run on it by His command. To say He made ships is to say He made the whole arrangement in which a ship can exist and do what it does.

**Livestock Ridden, and Livestock as Sign**

The second gift is al-anʿām, the grazing animals: camels, cattle, sheep, and goats, with camels foremost for an Arab audience. The verse mentions only riding, mā tarkabūn, what you ride, because riding is the theme of the next verse's prayer. But the Qurʾān elsewhere lists the full range of what livestock give: transport of goods over distances a person could not manage (16:7), warmth and clothing from their hides and hair (16:5, 16:80), milk from between waste and blood (16:66), meat, and even beauty when they are driven home in the evening and out in the morning (16:6). Ibn Kathīr and the other exegetes read the mention of riding here as standing for all of that. The animal that carries a man across the desert is the clearest case of a creature whose strength serves another creature, and the verse presents that service as arranged by God. A camel is stronger than the man on its back and could not be made to carry him if it had not been made willing. The next verse will put words in the rider's mouth for that fact.

**Ships and Animals Named Together**

The pairing of ships and animals in one clause is itself a small instance of the pairs the verse began with. They are the vehicles of sea and land, the two ways a person moves across the earth God laid out in verse 10. The Qurʾān pairs them in exactly this way in 40:79–80, where livestock are for riding and eating and on them and on ships people are carried, and in 16:8 and 16:14, where riding animals and the sea appear in the same list of blessings. The point of naming both is completeness. Wherever a person goes, on water or on land, the means of going was provided. The verse therefore closes the circuit that began with the earth being laid out and paths set in it. The earth was made passable; here the means of passing it are supplied. What remains is for the traveller to recognise the supply, and the next verse gives him the words to do so.
