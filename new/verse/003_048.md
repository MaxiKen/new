## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:48

> “And Allah will teach him writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Curriculum Announced Before the Birth**

The verse continues the angelic announcement and promises that the child will be taught. The verb is in the future, and it is attached to a person who has not yet been born: writing, wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel are his before anything has happened to him.

The Book's habit of describing prophetic preparation as divine instruction is consistent across its narratives. The Prophet of this sūrah is reminded that God has revealed to him the Book and wisdom and taught him what he never knew (4:113), and the community is told that the messenger recites God's revelations, purifies them, and teaches them the Book and wisdom (2:151; 3:164). The wording used for Jesus is the same as the wording used for Muhammad, which places both men in one category of recipients rather than two categories of being.

**"Writing and Wisdom" — the First Pair and the Question Behind It**

The first pair in the list has been discussed at length because the word rendered "writing" can also mean a book, and the sūrah has already used the same word for the Scripture given to Moses. Some commentators resolved the difficulty by taking the term here in its sense of the ability to write, since the Scripture given to Moses was centuries old and could not be something Jesus was taught as a new text.

Others pointed out that the Arabic conjunction joining the items can denote apposition rather than addition, so that the list need not describe four separate subjects — a construction the Book uses elsewhere when it places two names side by side in a list of angels. On that reading, the mention of writing and wisdom introduces the two scriptures, and the sentence describes a single education rather than a curriculum of four subjects.

**"The Torah and the Gospel"**

The two named scriptures are the revelation given to Moses and the revelation given to Jesus, and the sūrah has already described the second of them: the Gospel was given to Jesus as guidance and light, confirming what was revealed in the Torah and serving as a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing (5:46).

Placing both books in the list of what he was taught is the sūrah's way of fixing his position in the chain of revelation. He is taught the book that came before him, and he is given the book that will be associated with his name. He stands inside the sequence, receiving and then delivering, which is what every prophet in the Book does.

**Teaching as God's Act**

The verse's grammar keeps the initiative on God's side throughout. It does not say that Jesus learned; it says that God will teach him. The distinction is not pedantic, since the Book uses the same construction for the Prophet of this sūrah and for figures such as Luqmān, who was given wisdom by God without any account of how he acquired it (31:12).

The pattern tells the reader how to think about prophetic knowledge. It is not the product of study, though study is not despised in this religion; it is a gift that arrives complete enough to be relied on. The verse thus prepares for the announcement that follows, in which the taught prophet speaks with authority to his people about what God gave him.

**Ḥikmah: the Word Behind "Wisdom"**

The second term, *ḥikmah*, appears frequently in the Book and covers sound judgement, right understanding, and the ability to act well in particular situations. It is granted at God's discretion: He gives wisdom to whoever He wills, and whoever is granted it has been given a great good (2:269).

Placing *ḥikmah* between writing and the two scriptures gives the list its shape. The scriptures are the substance of what is known; wisdom is the capacity to use it. A prophet taught the texts without the judgement to apply them would be a scholar with no mission, and the Book does not present its messengers that way. The announcement here promises a person whose knowledge and judgement arrive together, before his life begins.

**Why the Order of the List Matters in This Sūrah**

The sūrah is speaking to communities that had scriptures and argued about them. It has already recounted how the People of the Book were invited to the Book of God to settle their disputes and turned away, and it will go on to describe the guidance they were given. Into that context it drops a list in which the Torah and the Gospel are simply two items in a prophet's education.

The effect is to reduce the temperature of the dispute. Neither book is treated as a trophy or a private possession in this verse; both are described as content that God conveys to a chosen servant, and the servant is then sent with something to say. The quarrel about who owns a scripture is quietly replaced by the question of what God does with it.

**The Child, the Books, and the Servant**

Read with the verses on either side, the sentence completes a portrait. The child has been announced as a word from God, named, honoured, brought near, promised speech in infancy and adulthood, and now promised an education in writing, wisdom, and the two scriptures.

Everything in the list is given. There is no item in it that the child originates, no teaching he hands on as his own possession, and no knowledge he arrives at by his own effort. The doctrine the sūrah will keep insisting on is already present in the grammar of this verse, three verses before the argument about his nature is joined.

**The Other Verses That Pair the Book With Wisdom**

The pairing named in this verse is one the Book uses when it describes what God gives to a messenger. The community is told that a messenger has been sent among them to recite the revelations, purify them, and teach them the Book and the wisdom (2:151), and the same gift is described as the favour shown to the believers with the addition that they had previously been clearly astray (3:164). The descendants of Abraham are said to have received the Book and the wisdom along with great authority (4:54), which shows how old the pairing is.

The Gospel narrative uses it for Jesus, where God recounts the favour of teaching him writing, wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel (5:110), and the sūrah of the Kneeling mentions the Scripture, the wisdom, and prophethood as what was given to the Children of Israel (45:16). The list in this verse therefore belongs to a pattern the reader meets repeatedly: wisdom is named alongside the text rather than derived from it, and the two are given together by the same act.
