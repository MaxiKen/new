## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:42

> So Today neither of you can benefit or protect each other. And We will say to the wrongdoers, “Taste the torment of the Fire, which you used to deny.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Today" — A Word That Closes an Era**

The verse begins with *fa-l-yawma*, so today, and the adverb carries the weight of the sentence. Everything described before this belonged to a period in which arrangements could be made, alliances formed, favours banked. The word announces that the period has ended and a different set of rules now applies. The Qur'an uses this abrupt temporal marker at the decisive moments of its judgment scenes, and its effect is to make the reckoning present rather than predicted. Note also what the word implies about the day before. The relationships now declared void were functioning, or seemed to be, right up to the previous evening — patrons were petitioned, intermediaries were trusted, and the system returned enough apparent results to sustain belief in it. Nothing in the verse says the arrangements were always obviously worthless. It says they stop working today.

**Neither Benefit Nor Harm: The Two Halves of Any Power**

*Lā yamliku baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin nafʿan wa lā ḍarrā* — none of you holds for another any benefit or any harm. The pairing is the Qur'an's standard formula for the full extent of a power, and both halves are needed. A being that can help but not hurt is a patron; one that can hurt but not help is a threat; one that can do neither is neither, and has no claim on anyone's attention. The verb is *yamliku*, to own or have at one's disposal — the same verb used when the sūrah said the false gods do not own an atom's weight in the heavens or the earth (34:22). The repetition ties the two verses together. What was asserted as a doctrine in the middle of the chapter is now enacted as a fact at the reckoning, and the demonstration requires no argument because both parties are present and neither can produce anything.

**Who Is Being Addressed**

The pronoun is plural and ambiguous in the Arabic, and the ambiguity is productive. Read one way, the address is to the angels and their worshippers: the angels cannot help those who petitioned them, and the worshippers can do nothing for the angels either. Read another way, it is addressed to the idolaters among themselves — the leaders and followers who spent three verses earlier in this same sūrah blaming each other (34:31–33), and who now learn that neither faction can shift anything onto the other. Both readings are supported by the context, since both relationships have been dismantled in the preceding verses. The Qur'an states the general rule behind either reading in al-Baqarah: a Day *"when no soul will be of any help to another"* (2:48), with no intercession accepted and no ransom taken.

**From Third Person to Second: The Sentence Is Handed Down**

The verse's second half changes who is being spoken to. Until now the wrongdoers have been discussed — *these*, pointed at while the angels testified (34:40). Now they are addressed directly: *dhūqū ʿadhāba al-nāri* — taste the torment of the Fire. The shift from discussion to address is how a court moves from evidence to sentence, and the Qur'an marks it with the imperative *dhūqū*, taste. The verb is deliberately physical and somewhat coarse; it belongs to eating and drinking, not to suffering in the abstract. It also carries a note of first acquaintance, since tasting is how one finds out what something actually is. People who had only ever heard the Fire described in words are told to acquire the information directly.

**"Which You Used to Deny"**

The relative clause at the end is the whole verdict compressed into four words: *allatī kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn*, which you used to call a lie. The punishment is identified not by its properties but by its relation to the defendants' own speech. What they are entering is the specific thing they publicly denied — not a surprise, not an item they were never warned about, but the exact object of their mockery. This closes a circuit the sūrah opened in its seventh verse, where they staged a public joke about a man who claimed the scattered dead would be raised (34:7). The verb *kadhdhaba* means to declare something false, and it is the Qur'an's standard word for the response of every community that rejected a messenger. Its use here makes the punishment a consequence of a speech act rather than of an ideology. They said it was a lie; they are shown the thing they said it about.

**Why the Scene Ends Without Reply**

The idolaters are given no answer in this verse and no answer anywhere in the passage. They were silent while the angels testified, silent while the mutual disclaimers were announced, and they are silent when the sentence is delivered. Silence of that kind is not humility and the verse does not present it as such; it is what remains when every available line of defence has been closed off in advance by someone else's testimony. This is unusual for the Qur'an's judgment scenes, which often let the condemned speak — pleading to be sent back (32:12), cursing each other (7:38), or admitting their sins. The silence here is more effective than speech would be, because the preceding three verses have already established that there is nothing available for them to say. Their gods have disowned them, their leaders and followers have exhausted their quarrel, and the record of their own mockery has been read out. The sūrah now steps back from the Day of Judgment and returns to the present, to the words they were using while the argument was still open.
