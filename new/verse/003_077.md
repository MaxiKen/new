## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:77

> Indeed, those who trade Allah’s covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain will have no share in the Hereafter. Allah will neither speak to them, nor look at them, nor purify them on the Day of Judgment. And they will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Trade Described in Three Parts**

The verse names a transaction, the price, and the loss. The merchants are those who sell a covenant with God and their own oaths for a small gain, and the consequence is that they will have no share in the Hereafter. The sentence is short and its proportions are deliberate: the thing traded away is a pledge made to God, and the thing received is described by a word that diminishes it.

The Book uses the language of commerce for this kind of exchange repeatedly, and it applies it to several objects. People are said to sell God's signs for a price, to sell the Book, and to sell their oaths; the same vocabulary appears in the sūrah of the Cow's account of those who exchange guidance for error. The consistency of the metaphor tells the reader how the Book assesses such conduct: it is not merely a failure but a purchase, and the assessment of it turns on the value of what was given up against the value of what was received.

**The Covenant and the Oaths**

The two things sold are named separately, and the distinction is worth marking. A covenant with God is the standing obligation of a community that has received a revelation and pledged itself to it; an oath is a particular promise made by a particular person, often in the course of a dispute.

Putting the two together covers both the general and the particular. A community's failure is not usually a single lie; it is a practice of making promises it does not keep, which erodes the general pledge along with each specific one. The Book elsewhere states the requirement in the positive form when it says that those who honour their trusts and are mindful of God are the ones He loves, and the same sūrah has already placed the conduct of the earlier community under that standard. What this verse adds is the mechanism: the pledges are traded, and they are traded for something, which means the person making the exchange has made a calculation about what his word is worth.

**"A Fleeting Gain"**

The price is described by a word that indicates something small and passing, and the Book uses it for the world's goods when they are set against the Hereafter. The same word appears in warnings about those who traded the guidance they had for a brief advantage, and in the charge against the communities that sold their share for a price.

The description is where the verse's argument does its work. The exchange is not condemned because trading is wrong, or because the parties wanted something; it is condemned because the price was inadequate to the thing sold, and the person accepting it had misjudged the value. The Book's ethics of trust therefore rest on a claim about relative worth rather than on a rule about promises; a person breaks his word when he has decided, in a particular case, that what he is being offered matters more than what he has pledged. The verse simply names that decision and states its cost.

**The Three Withholdings**

The second sentence describes the consequence in three negative clauses: God will not speak to them, will not look at them, and will not purify them on the Day of Judgment. The commentators explained the language carefully, since other passages describe God addressing the wicked on that day.

Their conclusion is that the three clauses describe the withdrawal of favour rather than a literal impossibility. In a culture where being addressed, being looked upon with regard, and being treated as clean were the marks of acceptance, the sentence describes a person standing before his Lord in the position of an outcast. The Book does use the language of looking and of speech for divine approval elsewhere, and the sūrah's reader is meant to hear the three clauses as the complete reversal of the welcome that the previous verse promised to the trustworthy.

**The Punishment Named at the End**

The sentence closes with a painful punishment, which is the sūrah's standard formula for the outcome of the conduct it has been describing. The Book often ends an account of wrongdoing with a statement of its consequence, and the effect is to keep the two in view together.

What the reader is being shown, in the passage as a whole, is a community's conduct traced to its end. The verses about mixing truth and falsehood, about the stratagem of morning belief and evening rejection, and about the claim that obligations stop at the community's boundary have each described how a group behaves when it has decided that its position matters more than its word. This verse names the decision in the clearest terms available and states what the Book says follows it, and it does so in one sentence so that the two cannot be separated in the reader's mind.

**A Warning With the Believers in View**

The Book applies this warning to its own community as well, and it does so in more than one place, using the same language of trading a covenant for a small gain. The passage's immediate target is a group among the People of the Book, and the sūrah is not making a prediction about the future of any community.

What the reader is given is a description that he can use to inspect his own practice. The measure is whether one's word holds when keeping it is costly, and the Book leaves that test where the reader can apply it: not in the dispute about which community has the better claim, but in whether the promise made yesterday is still being honoured today. The following verses turn to a different charge, and they concern speech itself.

**Why the Trade Is Named Before the Punishment**

The order of the two sentences in this verse is deliberate. The Book states what was done and what it cost before it says what follows, and the sequence gives the punishment its intelligibility.

A reader who meets only the sentence about the three withholdings is looking at severity without a cause. Meeting it after the account of a pledge sold for a small price, he is looking at a proportion. The Book's habit of arranging verdict and reasoning in this order runs through the sūrah: the earlier passage described the killing of prophets and the killing of those who defend justice before it announced a painful punishment, and the same arrangement appears here in miniature. What is being taught, verse by verse, is that the Book's severity is always a response to something named in the text, and never a display.
