## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:35

> those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah, who patiently endure whatever may befall them, and who establish prayer and donate from what We have provided for them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Marks, and the First Is a Tremor**

The humble of verse 34 are now described, and the description begins with a physical event: *alladhīna idhā dhukira Allāhu wajilat qulūbuhum* — those who, when God is mentioned, their hearts tremble.

The verb *wajila* is the word for fear that shows. It is not calm awe and it is not terror; it is the involuntary response of a person who has been reminded of something enormous and cannot stay unaffected. The Qur'an puts this at the head of the list, which tells the reader that the religion begins in a reaction rather than in a decision.

The same phenomenon is described elsewhere in the same physical vocabulary: *“which causes the skin ˹and hearts˺ of those who fear their Lord to tremble, then their skin and hearts soften at the mention of ˹the mercy of˺ Allah”* (39:23). Note the sequence in that verse — trembling, then softening. The fear is not the destination; it is the first movement, and it is followed by relief.

**The Body Keeps Score**

The second verse often paired with this one is about a different organ: *“When they listen to what has been revealed to the Messenger, you see their eyes overflowing with tears for recognizing the truth”* (5:83).

The pairing is deliberate in the Qur'an's psychology. Faith is not described as an interior state with occasional outward effects; it is described as a state that registers on the body — hearts that tremble, skin that softens, eyes that fill. The commentators who wrote on this verse, including al-Qushayrī, took the trembling as increasing in proportion to a person's awareness of God's self-disclosure in the world, which makes it a function of attention rather than of temperament.

The significance for reading the list is that none of these four marks is purely private. A person cannot verify his own humility, but he can notice whether the mention of God moves him.

Sūrah al-Raʿd names the second half of the heart's response, after the trembling: *“those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort”* (13:28). Trembling first, comfort after — the same sequence Sūrah al-Zumar traced from fear to softening. The body keeps score in both directions: the mention of God moves the heart, and then it settles it. A humility that stopped at trembling would be alarm; the comfort is what makes it worship.

**The Second Mark: Endurance Without Commentary**

*Wa-l-ṣābirīna ʿalā mā aṣābahum* — and those who are patient over what befalls them. The preposition is *ʿalā*, which carries the sense of staying on top of something, holding one's place under a load.

This is the quality the sūrah's earlier figures conspicuously lacked. The man on the brink of verse 11 was content when good came to him and flipped over onto his face when a trial did; the man of verse 15 was consumed by rage at the delay of help. Patience is being named here as the specific difference between the humble and those the sūrah has already described.

The phrasing *mā aṣābahum* — whatever strikes them — is deliberately wide. No category of suffering is excluded and no cause is assigned. The verse does not say they are patient over what they deserve, or over what they understand.

Sūrah al-Baqarah gives the patient their sentence, and the sentence explains the preposition: *“who, when faced with a disaster, say, ‘Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return’”* (2:156). Belonging stated, return faced — the staying-on-top that *ʿalā* describes is not stoicism but ownership acknowledged. What befalls them befalls what is already His; patience is the recognition, spoken at the moment of impact, that the striking and the struck belong to the same Lord.

**The Third and Fourth Marks: Prayer and Giving**

*Wa-l-muqīmī al-ṣalāti wa-mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūna* — and those who establish the prayer, and spend from what We provided them.

The two are always joined in the Qur'an, and the joining is the point. Prayer is what a person does about God; giving is what he does about other people, and the religion insists that neither is the whole of it.

Two details of the phrasing deserve attention. The prayer is *established* — *muqīmī*, set up and kept up — rather than merely performed, which is the language of maintenance rather than of occasional acts. And the giving is *mimmā razaqnāhum*, from what We provided them: the same provision language used of the sacrificial animals in verses 28 and 34. What is given away was never earned outright, and the sūrah says so in the same breath as it commands the giving.

The Qur'an's opening portrait of the righteous joins the same two acts: *“who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them”* (2:3). Unseen believed, prayer established, provision shared — the third and fourth marks of verse 35 as the definition of the guided from the Book's second page. The provision language is identical in both places, which confirms that the giving was never conceived as generosity with one's own: it is distribution of what arrived as gift.

**Why the Humble Are Described Just Before the Fighting Begins**

The placement of this description is one of the sūrah's most deliberate choices, and it is easy to miss. Three verses from here, permission to fight will be granted (22:39), and the community that receives it is the community described here.

The order is a safeguard. A group that has been expelled from its homes and is about to be authorized to take up arms is first told what kind of people it is supposed to be: moved by the mention of God, patient under what befalls them, constant in prayer, open-handed with what they were given. Power is being granted to people whose defining marks are interior.

Read the other way round, the sequence is a warning. The sūrah is describing the people whose good news the Prophet is told to announce, and the next passage will describe what happens when a community is established in the land (22:41). The risk being guarded against is not defeat. It is the loss of the tremor.

**What the List Is For**

Read as a set, the four marks describe a shape rather than a checklist. The humble person is moved by the mention of God, stays in place under what happens to him, keeps the prayer up, and lets go of what he was given.

The order matters. The list begins inward and ends outward: a reaction, an endurance, an act of worship, an act of distribution. A person cannot start at the end of it — a generosity that does not come from the tremor is expenditure, and a prayer maintained without endurance is a habit that lasts as long as comfort does.

That is why the sūrah places this description immediately after the legislation on sacrifice. The sacrifices and the rites were the outward apparatus; these four are the people the apparatus is supposed to produce. The sūrah will say so in its plainest form in the verse after next.

Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ states what the apparatus exists to produce, on the Day it is all that counts: *“Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺”* (26:89). Wealth and children disqualified in the verse before, the sound heart admitted alone. The four marks of verse 35 are that heart described while it is still in the world — trembling, enduring, praying, giving. The sacrifices of verses 28 to 37 were the outward machinery; this list is the product the machinery was built to make.
