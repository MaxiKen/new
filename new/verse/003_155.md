## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:155

> Indeed, those ˹believers˺ who fled on the day when the two armies met were made to slip by Satan because of their misdeeds. But Allah has pardoned them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day the Two Hosts Met**

The verse refers to the day the two armies met, which the commentators identify as Uḥud, and it addresses those who turned away during the fighting. The reference places the sentence within the same narrative the previous verses have been unfolding.

The retreat described here followed the departure of the archers from the high ground, when the enemy's cavalry was able to work around the community's position. The verse does not repeat the details; it names the participants and then states what the sūrah wants the community to understand about what they did and about how God dealt with them.

**Satan Made Them Slip**

The verse attributes the turning away to a slip caused by Satan, and the Book uses the same image of slipping in its account of the first human failure. In the story of Adam and his wife, Satan caused them to slip from the state they were in, and in another passage the Book warns that if people slip after the clear proofs have come to them, God is mighty and wise.

The image describes a loss of footing rather than an intention to abandon the faith, and the verse's use of it keeps the offence in its proper scale. The act was real and had consequences, and the community had been warned against exactly this. At the same time the sentence identifies the agent that caused the slip, and it does so in a way that leaves room for the pardon stated at the end.

**The Question of What Caused the Slip**

The clause about the cause of the slip has been read in two ways. One reading takes it to mean that Satan caused them to slip in part of what they had committed, meaning that the failure was confined to certain matters rather than being general. The other treats the phrase as stating that they slipped because of what they had previously done, so that the earlier offence opened the way to this one.

Al-Rāzī records both and draws the lesson that one error tends to lead to another. The two readings describe different aspects of the same event, and both agree that the community's own conduct had prepared the ground on which the slip occurred.

**Who Turned Away**

The commentators were not unanimous about the identity of those described. Al-Ṭabarī records several views: that they were believers who ran from the fighting, that they were people who had remained behind in the city, and that the group includes both those who fled toward the town and those who climbed the mountain. Another report names specific individuals among the community, and the reasons given for their flight include fear and the rumour that the Prophet had been killed.

The absence of agreement on the question of identity is itself instructive. The verse addresses a class of conduct rather than a list of names, and it describes what happened in terms that apply to anyone who does the same thing.

**Why the Reports About Individuals Matter**

The reports that name particular people, including men of standing in the community, have been read by the commentators as evidence of the scope of God's forgiveness. The people who turned away on that day were pardoned, and the pardon is stated in the same verse that records their failure.

The commentators connect the case with the Book's account of Adam, whose repentance was accepted after his error (2:37). The comparison supports the sūrah's larger purpose in this passage, which is to teach a community recovering from failure that its mistakes do not have to define it, and that the remedy has been provided.

**The Pardon Stated Without Delay**

The verse states that God pardoned them, and the pardon is mentioned in the same sentence as the sin. The sūrah does not create a period in which the community is left to wonder whether it has been forgiven.

The arrangement matches the pattern of the passage. The verses have described the disobedience, the quarrel, the flight, and the secrecy of the anxious group, and each description has been accompanied by a statement that God pardoned. The effect is to keep the community's attention on the correction that follows rather than on a debt it cannot discharge.

**The Two Names That Close the Verse**

The verse ends with two names of God: the Forgiving and the Clement. The first describes the readiness to forgive, and the second describes the quality of patience that does not rush to punish.

The pairing is used elsewhere in the Book when it describes God's dealings with communities that have failed. Clement also means forbearing and unhurried, and the name describes the way the pardon is granted: not as an immediate reaction to a fault but as the settled disposition of the one who forgives. The community that had fled from a battle is told that it has to do with a Lord whose forbearance gave it time to return.

**What the Community Learned From the Verse**

The verse teaches that a failure after faith can be pardoned, and it teaches the same thing without removing the seriousness of the failure. The community is not told that its flight was trivial; it is told what caused it, and it is told how God responded.

That combination is the heart of the sūrah's treatment of Uḥud. The community had to be able to continue after a day that exposed its weaknesses, and the Book gives it both the diagnosis and the pardon. The verses that follow turn from the battle to the lessons the community was to carry out of it, and the passage keeps the two movements in that order.
