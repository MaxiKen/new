## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:41

> They will say, “Glory be to You! Our loyalty is to You, not them. In fact, they ˹only˺ followed the ˹temptations of evil˺ jinn, in whom most of them had faith.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Word Is Not an Answer**

Asked whether they were the ones being worshipped, the angels do not begin with yes or no. They begin with *subḥānak* — glory be to You, or more literally, You are far above that. The word *subḥān* comes from a root meaning to swim or to move freely, and its sense in this formula is of declaring something utterly removed, transcendent, beyond the suggestion just made. So the first movement of their reply is not testimony about the defendants but a disclaimer about God. Before addressing the facts, they clear away the implication that anything could genuinely occupy the position the worshippers assigned. This is the instinctive response of beings who know exactly what they are: not offence at the insult to themselves, but the immediate refusal of any association placed near God. The Qur'an records the same opening in other scenes of questioned servants, where the response to a charge of receiving worship begins with the same word.

**Loyalty Declared, and the Direction It Runs**

*Anta waliyyunā min dūnihim* — You are our *walī*, not them. The word *walī* is one of the Qur'an's important terms and has no single English equivalent: it covers protector, ally, patron, close friend, and the one whose side you are on. What the angels assert is a relationship of allegiance running upward, and they deny any corresponding relationship with those who petitioned them. The phrase *min dūnihim*, apart from them, is the same construction used throughout the sūrah for the false gods worshipped *besides* God (34:22), and turning it around is pointed. The worshippers had treated the angels as figures standing between themselves and God; the angels describe themselves as standing entirely on one side, with no bond at all to the other. An intermediary who is not allied with the petitioner cannot function as an intermediary, which is the second time in this chapter the intercession doctrine has been dismantled from above.

**What They Do Not Say**

The angels never claim the worship did not happen, and they never say the worshippers were mistaken about the existence of angels. Both would have been available answers and neither is given. What they deny is the relationship, not the act — which is the precise point the question was framed to test (34:40). This restraint is worth noting because it makes the testimony credible in a way a broader denial would not. They concede the facts and reject the interpretation, and they add a correction that nobody asked for: the worshippers were not, in truth, dealing with angels at all. Having refused their own implication in the practice, they explain what the practice actually was.

**The Correction: It Was the Jinn**

*Bal kānū yaʿbudūna al-jinn* — rather, they were worshipping the jinn. This is the verse's substantive claim and it reframes the whole history of Arabian idolatry. The rites were conducted in the names of angels and deities; the beings actually involved, on this account, were of a different order entirely. The jinn appear throughout the Qur'an as a created species with will, capable of belief and disbelief, hidden from ordinary sight — and this sūrah has already shown their limits, when Solomon's conscripted workers failed to notice their master's death (34:14). Two readings of the claim are available and neither excludes the other. It may mean that jinn actually received and encouraged these devotions, or that the worshippers were following what the jinn suggested, in which case their obedience was a form of worship regardless of the name on the shrine. The second reading fits the sūrah's earlier statement that most people followed Iblīs' expectations (34:20).

**"Most of Them Had Faith in Them"**

The closing clause is *aktharuhum bihim muʾminūn*, most of them believed in them. The word is *muʾminūn* — believers — used here of people committed to the jinn, which is a startling application of the Qur'an's most positive religious term. Its use is not ironic but exact: faith is a disposition of trust and reliance, and these people had it, directed at the wrong object. The observation removes a comfortable assumption about idolatry, namely that it is a failure to believe. The sūrah's opponents were not men short of conviction. They were men of conviction, confident, publicly committed, and willing to argue — which is precisely how the chapter has portrayed them from its third verse onward. Misplaced faith is presented as a species of faith, not as its absence, and that is what makes it so hard to dislodge by argument.

**The Collapse of an Entire Religious Economy**

Taken with the question that prompted it, this reply ends the case. Worshipping a being that does not know it is being worshipped is one kind of error; worshipping in a direction where a different and hostile party is receiving the traffic is another, and the second is what the angels describe. The Meccan system rested on a chain: worshipper, then intermediary, then God. The sūrah has now cut the chain at both joints. Earlier it showed the upper joint failing, with even the angels waiting in fear for a decree they had no part in shaping (34:23). Here the lower joint fails too, as the supposed intermediaries state that no relationship of patronage ever existed and that the traffic was running to a different destination altogether. What remains of the practice is a lifetime of devotion delivered to an address its senders never intended. The verse does not gloat over this, and the angels' tone throughout is a servant's rather than a victor's. The verdict falls in the next verse, and it falls on both parties at once.
