## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:17

> **Whenever one of them is given the good news of what they attribute to the Most Compassionate, his face grows gloomy, as he suppresses his rage.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Face as Evidence**

The verse supplies the proof for the previous verse's question by describing a scene the Makkans would recognise. A man is told that a daughter has been born to him. His face darkens and he chokes on anger he cannot express. The Qurʾān does not comment on the scene; it describes it and lets it stand as testimony. The evidence is physical, not doctrinal. Whatever the Quraysh said about the angels, their bodies told the truth about how they valued daughters. The verb is ẓalla, which means to remain in a state through the day, so the darkening is not a passing flicker but a lasting gloom. Muswadd is the participle of iswadda, to become black, and the Qurʾān uses the same word for the faces of the damned on the Day of Judgement (3:106, 39:60). The man's face at the news of a daughter looks like the face of someone hearing his sentence. That comparison is not stated, but a listener who knew the Qurʾān would hear it, and it makes the man's reaction not merely unreasonable but ominous.

**"Good News" as Irony**

The news is called bushrā, glad tidings, and the word is chosen for its bite. In ordinary use bushrā is the announcement of something joyful, and the birth of a child is the paradigm case. The verse calls the birth of a daughter good news because it is good news, and then shows a man receiving it as calamity. The irony cuts in two directions. It condemns the man's reaction as a distortion of what any sane person should feel at a birth, and it condemns the theology he professes. The phrase "what they attribute to the Most Compassionate" identifies the news precisely: he is told he has received the very thing he says God has. If daughters are fit for God, they should be more than fit for him. His face says they are not. The Qurʾān uses the same ironic bushrā in 16:58–59, where the man hides from his people because of the evil of what he was given good news of, and in 3:21 and 9:34, where sinners are given "good news" of a painful punishment. The word turns against those who cannot receive what it announces.

**Kaẓīm: Choking on Suppressed Anger**

The last word of the verse is kaẓīm, and it describes a specific inner state. The root k-ẓ-m means to hold back, to shut in, and it is used for tying the mouth of a water-skin so nothing escapes. A kaẓīm is someone full of grief or anger who is holding it in, so that it cannot come out and cannot be let go. The Qurʾān uses the word for Yaʿqūb, whose eyes whitened with sorrow over Yūsuf while he was kaẓīm, suppressing his grief (12:84), and for the believers who restrain their anger, al-kāẓimīna l-ghayẓ (3:134). In the case of Yaʿqūb and the believers the restraint is praised, because what is held back is a natural feeling that must not become a wrong act. Here the same word describes a man holding back what he should never have felt. He is not restraining an outburst of grief over a loss; he is swallowing rage at a gift. The word's ordinary honour makes its use here more damning. The idolater has the self-control of a prophet directed at the wrong object.

**The Same Man Who Worships Daughters**

The verse's argument depends on identifying the man in the scene with the man who attributes daughters to God. The phrase mā ḍaraba li-l-raḥmāni mathalan, what he has set forth as a likeness for the Compassionate, makes the identification explicit. Ḍaraba mathalan means to strike a likeness, to propose a description or comparison, and what the idolater proposed was that the Compassionate has daughters. He is now given a daughter himself and cannot bear it. The exegetes read this as completing the charge of inconsistency begun in verse 16. The Makkan's theology and his behaviour contradict each other in the most visible way possible, and the contradiction proves that the theology was never believed in any serious sense. If he believed daughters were worthy of God, he would rejoice to have one. Since he does not rejoice, he did not believe it; he assigned daughters to God because he thought them inferior and gave the inferior share away. The verse thus shows that the Quraysh's doctrine of divine daughters was not reverence for the angels but contempt for women, dressed as religion.

**The Name al-Raḥmān in This Verse**

The divine name used here is al-Raḥmān, the Most Compassionate, and the choice is not incidental. Al-Zukhruf uses this name seven times, more than most sūrahs, and always at points where the idolaters' claims about God are being examined. The name emphasises mercy, and the verse sets the merciful God beside a man whose face blackens at his own child. The contrast exposes the idolater's distance from the God he claims to describe. He attributes offspring to the Compassionate and cannot show compassion to his own daughter. There is also an argument buried in the name. Al-Raḥmān is, by the Qurʾān's account, the source of the tenderness parents feel toward children; the name derives from raḥim, the womb. To attribute daughters to al-Raḥmān and then meet a daughter with rage is to invert the very mercy the name announces. The verse leaves the man standing with his darkened face, and the next verse asks, in his own voice, what kind of being he supposes he has assigned to God.
