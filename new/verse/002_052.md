## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:52

> Even then We ˹still˺ forgave you so perhaps you would be grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pardon Stated After Idolatry**

The verse follows a report of worship offered to a manufactured idol, and it begins with a concessive phrase that acknowledges the severity of what it is passing over. The Book does not minimise the offence; it names it and then states that forgiveness was granted anyway. The effect is a sentence in which the mercy is measured against the transgression rather than against a general human frailty.

That combination recurs throughout the Qur'an's account of this community, and it recurs with the same verb. Time after time the narrative runs from a specific breach to a specific pardon, and the Book repeats the pattern enough that the reader cannot mistake it for exceptional treatment. The pardon here is not described as a reward for anything the community did between the two events, because the record gives no such interval.

**The Concessive Word and What It Conveys**

The phrase translated as *even then* sets the sin and the forgiveness side by side and lets the reader supply the surprise. Used in this position, it makes the second half of the sentence an act of restraint rather than a legal outcome, and the Book's language elsewhere confirms that reading: it describes God as slow to punish, forbearing, and willing to accept repentance from a people whose record would justify otherwise.

The sūrah's earlier account of Adam uses the same pattern, where a transgression is narrated and a pardon is stated in the next verse. What differs between the two scenes is the scale of the offence: one prohibition was crossed in a garden by two individuals, and in this case an entire community turned to an idol after watching the sea divide. The Book's willingness to state both pardons in the same tone is itself a teaching about the scope of divine mercy.

**The Verb Behind the Forgiveness**

The Arabic verb used here carries the sense of effacing or wiping away a trace, and the Book uses it for the removal of sins rather than for the cancellation of their natural consequences. That distinction matters for how the passage should be read, because the community in this story went on to face other consequences of its conduct. What is removed by the pardon is the standing charge, not the memory of the event.

The sūrah will keep mentioning the calf in later verses, which shows that the Book does not treat a pardon as erasure from history. The community carries the record, and the reader is told about it in revelation. What the pardon restores is the relationship that the offence had broken, and the Book presents that restoration as the point of the whole arrangement.

**The Purpose Named: Gratitude**

The sentence ends with a stated intention, and the purpose named is not obedience or fear but thankfulness. The Book links gratitude to increase in a well-known verse: *“And ˹remember˺ when your Lord proclaimed, ‘If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe.’”* (14:7). Gratitude in the Qur'an is therefore not merely a sentiment but a response that changes what follows.

Gratitude is also the appropriate answer to a pardon, and the Book treats it as such in this verse. A community that has been spared a punishment it had earned has nothing to offer in return that would match the gift. What the verse asks for instead is recognition — a people that knows what it was given and acts accordingly.

**Grace Without Approval of the Act**

Nothing in this verse suggests that the worship of the calf was acceptable or that the community's conduct was ultimately harmless. The Book's judgment on the act stands in the previous sentence and in the later passages that refer back to it, and the pardon concerns the persons rather than the deed. The sentence therefore holds two facts in balance: an act deserving punishment, and a people not punished for it.

That balance is the Book's regular way of describing divine forgiveness, and the sūrah will state the same arrangement in the prayer of its final verse, where believers ask for pardon, forgiveness and mercy. The whole of al-Baqarah ends with a request for exactly what this verse reports as already granted in the story of Israel, which ties the community of the revelation to the community of the exodus.

**Rescue, Failure and Pardon as a Pattern**

This verse is the third movement of a sequence that the sūrah has repeated several times. A gift is given, the recipients fail, and mercy follows. The sequence is not presented as a cycle without end, and the Book does not suggest that the pardon removes the need for change. Several of the verses that follow will raise further failures from the same community's history, and the tone of the address will not weaken.

What the reader is meant to take from the pattern is the persistence of the offer. The sūrah is addressing a later generation of the same people and asking them to accept a messenger who confirms what they already have. Before it makes that request, it establishes, in its own record of their history, that the door has been repeatedly reopened. The sentence ends with gratitude because gratitude is what the situation calls for.

**Why the Record Is Preserved**

The Book keeps the failures of a favoured people in its text, and this verse is one of the places where it does so without softening the facts. A community's history is recorded complete: the rescue, the crossing, the appointment, the idol, and the pardon. Nothing is edited out on either side, and the reader is invited to draw conclusions from the whole of it.

That preservation serves the argument of the address to the community's descendants. If the record had been limited to the favours, the later reproaches would have rested on nothing the audience had been told. If it had been limited to the failures, the pardon in this verse would have had no counterpart. What the sūrah presents is a full account, and it presents it as the ground on which the request to accept a messenger is being made.
