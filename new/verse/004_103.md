## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:103

> When the prayers are over, remember Allah—whether you are standing, sitting, or lying down. But when you are secure, establish regular prayers. Indeed, performing prayers is a duty on the believers at the appointed times.

**Expanded Commentary**

**When the Prayers Are Over — Remember Standing, Sitting, Lying**

The verse states *fa idhā qaḍaytumu al-ṣalāta fa adhkurū Allāha qiyāman wa quʿūdan wa ʿalā junūbikum* — when you have finished, completed the prayer — the fear prayer just described (4:102) — then remember God standing, sitting, lying on sides. *Qaḍaytum* — completed, fulfilled, finished — was explained as ending the formal prayer with its bows and prostrations, the shortened travel form (4:101) and its guarded ranking. *Fa adhkurū* — then remember, mention, invoke — was read as keeping remembrance after the guarded rank disperses, so that prayer does not end as awareness. *Qiyāman wa quʿūdan wa ʿalā junūbikum* — standing, sitting, upon sides — was explained as all postures, the same triad used where those given understanding remember God in all states and reflect (3:191). The sūrah has shown that even in the fear prayer one group stands guard while the other prostrates (4:102) and that vigilance remains even when weapons are laid aside in rain or illness; remembrance in all postures is the inward counterpart to that outward vigilance. The verse was also linked to the emigrant’s many havens (4:100): whether moving standing, resting sitting, or fallen on sides from wound, the remembrance accompanies.

**But When You Are Secure — Establish Regular Prayers**

The clause *fa idhā aṭmaʾnantum fa aqīmū al-ṣalāta* — but when you are secure, tranquil, established, then establish the prayer — was explained as returning to full prayer when fear lifts and place is secure. *Aṭmaʾnantum* — you are secure, at rest, hearts at rest — matches the security opposite the fear that shortened prayer (4:101) and that required the guarded rank (4:102). To *aqīmū al-ṣalāta* — establish prayer — after having shortened it on travel is to return to its complete, timed form: four rakʿahs restored, boundaries kept, congregation reformed. The sūrah has used *iqāmah* throughout for prayer established at its time, after purification with earth where water lacked (4:43), and as witness before God (4:41). The shift from shortened to established was read as the law’s responsiveness: prayer’s form follows security, not the other way. Where the emigrant found many havens and abundance (4:100), security is the haven for prayer itself, allowing it to stand at its full height. The instruction shows that the traveller’s shortened prayer is not lesser worship but worship fitted to insecurity; when insecurity leaves, the form returns without needing new command beyond *aqīmū*.

**Prayer Is a Duty at Appointed Times**

The seal *inna al-ṣalāta kānat ʿalā al-muʾminīna kitāban mawqūtā* — indeed performing prayers is a duty, a decree, a writ upon the believers at fixed, appointed times — was explained as the sūrah’s anchoring of all else in time. *Kitāban* — writ, decree, obligation inscribed — was read as a firm obligation, as inheritance shares are *farīḍah mina Allāh* (4:11) and as testament is a writ. *Mawqūtā* — timed, appointed — was explained as fixed moments — *mawāqīt* — that do not move with convenience, whose establishment the sūrah has required. The phrase was linked to *ḥāfiẓū ʿalā al-ṣalawāt* — guard the prayers (2:238) — and to the Prophet’s teaching that the prayer has appointed openings and closings. The sūrah has said that the good deed is multiplied (4:40) and that striving is given ranks, forgiveness, and mercy (4:96); here the daily multiplication — five writs at their times — is the steady practice that makes striving and guarded prayer possible. The appointed time also explains why shortening is permission and remembrance is extension: the writ’s time does not depart; its form adapts while its time holds.

**A Verse That Binds Guarded Prayer to Constant Remembrance**

Placed after the choreography of prayer under arms (4:102), this verse binds that choreography to constant remembrance and to regular establishment when secure. The sūrah that warned the enemy wishes you neglect weapons and belongings for one sweeping assault (4:102) counters that neglect with remembrance in every posture, so that no posture is without guard. The sūrah that declared God knows best who enemies are and is sufficient as Guardian (4:45) then said disbelievers are your clear enemies (4:101); remembrance is the inward reliance upon that Guardian while the guard is the outward means. For the weak for whom burdens were lightened (4:28), the sequence is a lightening: prayer shortened on travel, guarded under fear, remembered standing, sitting, lying, and established in security, always at its appointed writ, so that no state — fear, rain, illness, security — leaves the believer without prayer. The next verse will turn from prayer’s guard to pursuit’s steadiness: do not falter in seeking the enemy, if you suffer they suffer, and you hope from God what they never can (4:104).

**The Writ That Holds the Sūrah’s Discipline Together**

The final writ — *kitāban mawqūtā* — was read as the sūrah’s discipline. The chapter has commanded returning trusts and judging fairly (4:58), referring disputes to God and Messenger (4:59), verifying reports (4:83), being sure before fighting (4:94), and not confusing permission over hostility with permission over believers (4:92–93). Prayer at fixed time is the training that makes those commands possible: it punctuates the day with reference, divides travel and fear into measured intervals, and returns the community from the rank to the standing, sitting, lying remembrance. The verse therefore closes the passage on prayer that began with purification where water lacked (4:43) and will later meet the judgment passage where the Book was sent to judge by what He has shown (4:105): both begin with a writ — purity and time — that secures justice.
