## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:71

> They asked, turning back, “What have you lost?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**They Turn Back at Once**

The verse records a movement before it records a question. The caravan has left Egypt, loaded and on the road, and at the herald's cry the brothers turn around. There is no hesitation, no debate and no refusal to stop. A group of foreign traders accused of theft by an official of the granaries has very little room to decline an enquiry, and the brothers exercise none. Their immediate return is also a fact about their position: they need Egypt. The famine is in its third or fourth year, the household in Canaan depends on these loads, and verse 60 has already told them that access to the minister is conditional. Turning back is not a choice they can afford to make otherwise.

**The Question Is Genuinely Innocent**

*What have you lost?* The brothers do not know about the cup. They have watched their provisions being loaded, they have received the same full measure as before, and none of them has taken anything. The question they ask is the question a person asks who has no information and no suspicion: what is missing, so that we can help you look. There is no defensiveness in the wording and no anticipation of an accusation. Nine of the eleven men in the party are in exactly this position. The tenth, Benjamin, knows what is in his bag, and the eleventh — the man they are addressing — put it there. The irony is structured rather than incidental. Of the eleven men present, the one asking has arranged everything, the one carrying the object knows about it, and the remaining nine are answering honestly in complete ignorance. The verse holds all three positions at once and reports only the question, leaving the reader to keep the arithmetic the brothers cannot perform.

**Three Levels of Knowledge in One Question**

The verse is the chapter's clearest instance of dramatic irony, and the irony is graded. The reader knows everything: the cup was placed by Joseph, in a specified bag, for a specified purpose (12:70). Benjamin knows the first part and has been told not to disclose it (12:69). Joseph knows all of it and is waiting for a particular answer. The nine brothers know nothing. A single four-word question is therefore spoken by a man with no information to an official with complete information, about an object whose location is known to three people in the scene and to the reader. The chapter constructs this arrangement deliberately and lets the question stand alone in its own verse.

**The Question Invites the Reward**

By asking what is missing, the brothers open the way for the herald to name the object and attach a reward to its return. Had they stayed silent or continued on the road, the accusation would have hung over them without an opportunity to answer it. Their question converts a public charge into a transaction. What follows — a named object, a camel-load offered, a personal guarantee — depends on the brothers having asked. The chapter shows them cooperating with an enquiry they have every reason to want over quickly, and their cooperation is what produces the exchange of the next four verses. The invitation is genuine, and its genuineness is what disarms them. Nothing in the officials' manner suggests that a particular bag is suspected or that the search was arranged in advance, so the brothers respond as innocent travellers do: with indignation, with a counter-question, and with an offer of their own that exceeds what was asked of them.

**A Whole Verse for Four Words**

The Qur'an frequently allocates a verse to a single short utterance when the utterance carries the narrative forward. Verse 61 did the same with a nine-word promise; verse 95 will do it with a dismissal of an old man's claim. Here four words of dialogue receive a verse of their own, and the allocation signals that the question is a turning point rather than a detail. Everything from verse 72 to verse 82 is generated by the herald's cry and answered by this question. The chapter's pacing gives the reader a moment to register the brothers' ignorance before the mechanism begins to work. Expanding so short a statement into a verse of its own is a deliberate slowing of the scene. The reader has just watched the cup being placed; the brothers have not, and the extra space allows that gap in knowledge to be felt before the search begins. Everything that follows depends on the reader knowing more than the speakers, and the verse makes certain of it.

**The Chapter's Dialogue Verses**

Verses 71 to 75 form a tight exchange: a question, an answer with a reward, a denial under oath, a question about the penalty, and a statement of the penalty. Five verses of dialogue and almost no narration. The sūrah uses this method at several points — the prison conversation of verses 36 to 41, the negotiation with Jacob in verses 63 to 66 — and in each case the absence of narrative comment forces the reader to judge the speakers from their words alone. Here the effect is sharp. The reader hears nine innocent men deny a theft, and hears them state a penalty that will fall on the one innocent member of their party.

**They Have Just Been Fed and Sent On Their Way**

The incongruity of the accusation is worth stating. Joseph loaded their provisions himself, as he had done on the first journey (12:59), and the verse before this one reports the loading in the same terms. The brothers have been served at full measure by the official now accusing them, in a country whose rationing he controls, and they are being called thieves as they travel home with what he gave them. The charge is not merely false; it is false in a way the brothers can point to, and verse 73 shows them doing exactly that — appealing to the officials' own knowledge of their conduct in the land. The appeal is available to them only because of the treatment described here. A caravan that had been cheated, shorted or detained would have had no standing to invoke the officials' own observation; these men can, and they do, precisely because the generosity was real and visible. Joseph's earlier kindness is what supplies his brothers with the confidence to protest.

**The Question Also Protects Them**

By asking what is missing rather than denying a charge they have not yet heard specified, the brothers place themselves in the position of cooperating witnesses rather than suspects. That position will matter in verse 73, when they appeal to what the officials already know about them, and in verse 75, when they are asked to state a penalty. A party that had fled or resisted the herald's cry would have had no standing to do either. Their question, innocent as it is, establishes the footing on which the rest of the exchange proceeds — and on which Benjamin is eventually retained.

The chapter gives them that footing without assistance. Joseph does not soften the accusation, the herald does not explain it, and no official offers the brothers a chance to withdraw before the search. What they receive is a question they asked themselves, answered in a way that obliges them further. Nine men who have done nothing wrong are being led, step by step and entirely by their own words, toward the detention of the tenth. Verse 71 is the first step, and it is taken voluntarily.
