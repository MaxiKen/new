## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:79

> Joseph responded, “Allah forbid that we should take other than the one with whom we found our property. Otherwise, we would surely be unjust.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Formula He Used at the Door**

Joseph's first word is *maʿādha Llāh* — God be my refuge, God forbid. The chapter has heard the formula before, in his mouth, in the most tested moment of his life. When the Chief Minister's wife locked the doors and said come to me, he answered: *“Allah is my refuge! It is ˹not right to betray˺ my master, who has taken good care of me”* (12:23). The women of the city used the same exclamation when clearing him before the king (12:51). Here it introduces a refusal of a different kind — not a refusal of temptation but a refusal of an accommodation — and its return ties the two moments together. In both cases the formula marks a line Joseph will not cross, and in both cases the line is drawn by reference to God rather than to preference.

**The Refusal Is Grounded in the Rule They Supplied**

Joseph does not say he cannot take another because it would defeat his purpose. He says it would be wrong to take anyone other than the person in whose possession the property was found. That is the standard the brothers themselves stated in verse 75: the recompense is the one in whose bag the object is found. He holds them to their own law at the moment when relaxing it would serve them, and he presents the relaxation as injustice rather than as mercy. The move is legally precise. A substitution would mean detaining a person against whom nothing was found, which no rule in either jurisdiction authorises. The grounding is what makes the refusal unanswerable. Joseph asserts no Egyptian authority over foreign travellers and claims no discretionary power; he applies the exact formulation the brothers recited two verses earlier. To object they would have to withdraw their own statement of law, in front of the officials who heard it, at the moment when they most need those officials to believe them.

**"Then We Would Be Wrongdoers"**

The word is *ẓālimūn*, and it is the chapter's term for the injury Joseph has spent his life receiving. The wrongdoers never succeed, he said at the door of the ʿAzīz's house (12:23). The brothers described their own conduct to their father in the same vocabulary — *we were wrongdoers* — in verse 64's context and again in verse 91. Now Joseph applies the term prospectively to himself: if he accepted the substitution, he would join the category of people he has been injured by. The refusal is therefore not only legal but self-defining. He will not do the thing that has been done to him, and the verse states the reason in his own word for it.

**Why He Cannot Relax the Rule Even If He Wanted To**

The chapter has already explained the constraint. Joseph could not have taken his brother under the king's law (12:76); what made the detention possible was the penalty the brothers supplied from their own custom. If he now set that penalty aside and took a different man, the whole arrangement would lose its basis. The detention of Benjamin would appear arbitrary — an official seizing a member of a foreign caravan for reasons he would not state — and the brothers would have grounds to contest it. Joseph's insistence on the rule is what preserves the legality of the outcome he wants, and the verse shows him maintaining it against the people whom it disadvantages. Maintaining the rule is also what preserves it. A rule applied selectively in favour of the powerful stops being a rule, and Joseph has staked his administration on being the man whose procedures can be relied upon. The verse shows him refusing the easy exits — taking another brother instead, or simply releasing this one — because either would undo the legal basis on which the detention stands.

**He Speaks as an Office, Not as a Brother**

The refusal is in the plural: *we* should not take anyone other than the one with whom *we* found our property. Joseph speaks as the administration, with the herald and the guards included in the pronoun. The plural is not merely formal. It records that the search was conducted publicly, that the object was found in the presence of officials, and that the penalty is being applied by a jurisdiction rather than by a private person. Nothing in the verse permits the brothers to suspect that the official in front of them has any personal interest in the outcome, which is exactly what Joseph requires if the detention is to be accepted rather than resisted. The office is also the only position from which he can speak. A brother could not detain Benjamin without revealing himself, and a revelation now would collapse the entire arrangement. So Joseph answers as the minister of Egypt, in the third person, citing a law he did not make, and the disguise that began as concealment becomes the working instrument of the plan.

**The Refusal Is Not Cruelty, and the Chapter Shows Why**

Everything Joseph has done for these men in the preceding verses argues against reading the refusal as severity. He fed them at full measure twice (12:59, 12:70), returned their money into their bags without being asked (12:62), and disclosed himself to the brother they are pleading for (12:69). The verse's refusal concerns one thing only: who is detained. It does not withdraw provision, does not impose a fine, does not punish anyone beyond the retention of a single person, and does not close the possibility of a later resolution. The chapter is careful to keep the refusal narrow, and its narrowness is what allows verse 80 to describe the brothers as despairing of persuading him rather than as fleeing from him.

**The Verse Completes the Legal Mechanism**

Verses 70 to 79 form a closed sequence: an object placed, a loss announced, a reward offered, a denial sworn, a penalty requested from the accused, the penalty stated, a search conducted in a deliberate order, the object produced, a substitution offered and refused. Every step is procedural and every step is recorded. The chapter's interest in the mechanism is not antiquarian; it is what allows verse 76 to say that God devised the plan. A divine arrangement executed through ordinary legal steps is the sūrah's account of how providence works in public life — not by suspending procedure but by arranging the statements people make inside it.

The refusal also preserves the position Joseph will occupy in verse 90. When he finally identifies himself, he does so to men who have watched him apply a rule evenly, refuse an advantage offered to him, feed them twice at full measure and return their money unasked. Nothing in the detention can be read back as an act of personal malice, because verse 79 has already shown him declining the one accommodation that would have served the malice reading. The brothers will admit their fault in verse 91 without ever having been accused by him, and this verse is part of the reason the admission is possible.
