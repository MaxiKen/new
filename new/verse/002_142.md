## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:142

> The foolish among the people will ask, “Why did they turn away from the direction of prayer they used to face?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “The east and west belong ˹only˺ to Allah. He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Predicted Before It Was Asked**

The verse arrives in the future tense: the foolish among the people *will ask* why the believers abandoned their former direction of prayer. Al-Rāzī notes that the future form does not exclude the past — it characterizes the question as a standing trait of the objectors as much as it predicts an event, the way a proverb says "the fool will say" because the fool always says it. The word for the objectors is *sufahāʾ*, the light-witted, those whose judgment lacks ballast, and al-Ṭabarī reports that some commentators identified them specifically as the hypocrites, whose question was not a search for reasons but a cynical probe — asked not to be answered but to unsettle the newly converted. The verse's foresight is itself part of its argument: the question is predicted, classified, and answered before the questioners finish asking it, which places the objectors inside a script they did not write.

**What the Question Assumed**

The question — why did they turn away from the qiblah they used to face — carries a buried premise: that the earlier direction had an intrinsic claim on loyalty, and that leaving it required a justification owed to the askers. The premise treats the qiblah as property of a place. The answer refuses the premise rather than the question. Nothing in the east or in the west obligates God, and no direction possesses sanctity in itself; a direction is a qiblah because God commanded it, and it ceases to be one when He commands otherwise. Had the verse offered a historical reason — the Jews boasted, the Prophet longed for Mecca — the objectors would have gained the ground they wanted: that the direction is negotiable in light of human motives. Instead the reply is theological and closes the negotiation: the points of the compass belong to Allah, and He guides whom He wills to a straight path. The same ownership was declared in this sūrah's earlier statement that the east and the west belong to God (2:115); here it becomes the answer to a challenge.

**Sixteen or Seventeen Months Toward Jerusalem**

The historical frame of the question is well documented in the exegetical sources. After the emigration to Medina the Prophet prayed toward Jerusalem for a period the reports place between thirteen and twenty months, with most specifying sixteen or seventeen. The accounts differ on the Meccan period: some hold that in Mecca the community faced the Kaʿbah, shifted to Jerusalem briefly after the migration, then returned to the Kaʿbah; others maintain that Jerusalem was the direction even in Mecca. What is common to the reports is that the Jerusalem orientation was observed in Medina for a substantial, counted stretch — long enough for it to become "the qiblah they used to face" in the questioners' mouths, and long enough for the change to be a genuine test rather than an administrative adjustment. The variation in the counts is itself honest historiography: the tradition preserved the disagreement rather than smoothing it, which is how the reports of this period usually behave on matters of duration.

**The First Ruling Ever Changed**

Within the science of *naskh* — the abrogation or supersession of a ruling by a later revelation — the change of the qiblah holds a paradigmatic place. It is regularly cited as the first instance of abrogation in the chronological order of revelation, and it carries a technical peculiarity the commentators flag: what was superseded was not a Qur'anic verse but a direction established through the Prophet's practice, making this a case of the Qur'an abrogating the Sunnah. The doctrine that makes such change coherent is stated in this same sūrah: *"If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?"* (2:106). The qiblah case became the working example in the legal literature because it is undeniable, datable, and communal — a whole congregation's practice reversed by revelation, with the theological account already in place to explain it.

**The Mosque of the Two Qiblahs**

The change left a physical mark on Medina that survives. A few kilometers from the Prophet's Mosque stands Masjid al-Qiblatayn — the Mosque of the Two Qiblahs — so named because, as the exegetical sources report, a caller arrived while a congregation was mid-prayer to announce the newly revealed command, and the worshippers turned within the prayer itself to face the new direction. The building has been reconstructed in recent times, but its name and its site preserve the memory of the single most literal turning in Islamic history: a community reorienting its bodies while its prayer was still in progress. The story matters theologically as well as topographically. It shows the change executed at the scale of an ordinary neighborhood congregation, not only at the Prophet's own mosque, and it embodies the verse's lesson in obedience: the direction was not their choice before, and it was not their choice now, and the turn was made without the prayer being broken.

**Qubāʾ Turns at Dawn**

The most firmly attested report of the event is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (403): ʿAbdullah ibn ʿUmar narrated that while the people were offering the dawn prayer at Qubāʾ near Medina, someone came to them and said that revelation had come to the Messenger of God that night ordering him to face the Kaʿbah — so turn your faces toward it. Those people had been facing al-Shām, the direction of Jerusalem, and they turned toward the Kaʿbah. The detail that they were mid-prayer at Fajr, that the news arrived from a single messenger, and that the whole congregation rotated on the strength of his word, gives the event its texture: the qiblah changed not in a council but in a village mosque at dawn, on trust in a report, with the prayer continuing through the turn. Bukhārī filed the report under the chapter on praying mistakenly toward a wrong direction without repeating the prayer — the legal conclusion the event itself generated.

**Pointing the Whole World at One Point**

Once a single building became the direction for a global community, the practical problem of finding it from anywhere on earth produced a scientific literature. An early method treated the worshipper as facing one wall of the Kaʿbah extended indefinitely, dividing the world into sectors each oriented to a different wall; since the Kaʿbah's corners align with the cardinal directions and its faces were associated with the summer sunrise and with Canopus, the navigation star, the heavens themselves were recruited into the calculation. Later methods grew mathematical: the great-circle route — the shortest path over the sphere — which from North America begins northeast and curves steadily toward the southeast, against the older sector method's simple southeasterly bearing; the constant-compass rhumb line as a third candidate; astrolabes and armillary spheres as instruments. All are used somewhere today. And a reported saying of the Prophet — whatever lies between the east and the west is qiblah — was read as granting latitude where exact determination is impossible, so that the obligation is orientation toward the House, not surveyor's precision. The fools' question about one village's turning ended by generating a science that turns every village toward the same point.
