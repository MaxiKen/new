## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:142

> The foolish among the people will ask, “Why did they turn away from the direction of prayer they used to face?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “The east and west belong ˹only˺ to Allah. He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Anticipated**

The verse announces that a question will be raised about the change of the direction of prayer, and it supplies the answer in the same sentence. The Book does not wait for the objection to be made and then respond; it states the objection in advance, which gives the community the answer before the controversy reaches it.

The question concerns a change in practice, and the people asking it had grown used to the previous direction. What the Book does is to treat the objection as a matter of curiosity about a human arrangement, and it answers with a statement about God's ownership of the horizon rather than with an explanation of the community's decision.

**Who the Foolish Were**

Commentators identified the questioners in several ways. Some took them to be the hypocrites, who used the change as an occasion for criticism rather than as a question requiring an answer. Others named the Jewish community, and others the idolaters of Makkah, and the reports show that each group had its own interest in the subject.

The sūrah's use of the term for the foolish indicates how it regards the question. A person who treats the direction of prayer as a matter of communal property, rather than as something God appoints, has misunderstood what the practice is. The question is therefore answered at the level of first principles rather than by a defence of the community's decision.

**The Reports About the Previous Direction**

The commentaries describe the community's prayer toward Jerusalem for a period after the migration to Madinah, and they differ on the length of the period, with most reports mentioning sixteen or seventeen months. Some accounts state that the Prophet ﷺ had prayed toward the Kaʿbah before the migration and then faced Jerusalem for a time, while others report that he faced Jerusalem from the beginning.

The variation in the reports is normal for material of this kind, and the verse does not depend on any particular figure. What the passage establishes is that the direction was a command that was changed by a command, and the sūrah's earlier discussion of the replacement of rulings supplies the framework in which the change is understood.

**The Answer About East and West**

The answer given to the Prophet ﷺ restates what the sūrah said a few verses earlier: the horizons belong to God, and no direction has an intrinsic claim to be the direction of prayer. The merit of the practice lies in the command that establishes it, which is why the change of command is a matter within God's authority.

That principle removes the ground of the objection entirely. If the east and the west are God's, then a community that prays in one direction rather than another is not abandoning anything of its own; it is following an instruction. What the two communities treated as a sign of instability was, on the Book's account, a demonstration of the authority to which the community submits.

**Guidance to the Straight Path**

The sentence closes by stating that God guides whomsoever He wills to the straight path, and the clause shows that the direction of prayer is not the main subject of the verse. What is at stake in the change is guidance, and the community's capacity to follow the command is itself a gift.

The Book uses the phrase about the straight path throughout the sūrah, and it appears in the opening prayer that the community recites in every prayer. The reader who has followed the passage is therefore given an answer that connects the controversy about the direction to the larger question the sūrah keeps raising, which is whether a person will follow what he is shown.

**The Mosque of the Two Directions**

The change is associated with a mosque near Madinah where the congregation is reported to have turned during the prayer itself when a messenger brought the announcement that the direction had been changed. The building stands as a marker of the event, and the report shows how the community received the instruction: not as a matter for discussion but as a command to be obeyed.

That report also illustrates the answer given in this verse. A community that treats the direction as a matter of divine appointment can change it in the middle of an act of worship, and the change does not trouble the practice itself.

The report has been preserved in the community’s memory because it shows how quickly the command was absorbed. There was no period of deliberation, and the congregation did not wait for the prayer to end before turning. The building’s name has served ever since as a reminder that the direction of prayer is a matter of obedience, and it also records the moment at which the community’s practice in Madinah changed.

**Where the Verse Sits in the Passage**

The sentence opens a section on the direction of prayer that continues through several verses, and it is placed immediately after the closing of the account of the patriarchs. The transition connects the two subjects through the person whose House became the direction: the sūrah has just narrated Abraham's building of the sanctuary and his prayers for the community, and the next subject is the direction of the community's prayer toward that House.

The sentence opens a section that runs for several verses, and its position gives the reader the answer before the objection is described in detail. The sūrah has just closed the account of the patriarchs with the principle of individual accountability, and the direction of prayer is the first subject that follows. The placement suggests that the two passages are connected: what the communities claim about their ancestry and what they say about the community’s worship belong to one argument about standing before God.

**The Practical Question Behind the Objection**

The objection that the community had changed its practice was also a question about authority, since a community that alters its rites can be accused of instability by those watching. The verse's answer addresses that concern by locating the authority in God, and the passages that follow describe the community's role as witnesses over humanity. The change was therefore not an embarrassment to be explained but a command that carried its own justification.
