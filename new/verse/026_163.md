## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:163

> So fear Allah, and obey me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning Requires a Response**

Lot has identified himself as a trustworthy messenger. He now gives the response that follows from that trust: fear Allah and obey him. The command is not an invitation to admire Lot's sincerity while retaining the community's chosen practice. Trust becomes meaningful only when it produces obedience.

The order also keeps the messenger in his proper place. Fear belongs to Allah, and obedience to Lot is required because he conveys God's command. Lot is not asking the people to replace divine worship with loyalty to a human leader. He is calling them through a human voice to submit to the Lord.


The people of Lot are being confronted with a form of desire that has become public and defended. Taqwa teaches that desire is real but not sovereign. A person is not morally free merely because he wants something or because others around him approve it. He remains answerable to the One who created him and knows the consequences of his choices.

Fear of Allah does not mean despising the body or pretending that temptation never exists. It means refusing to let temptation dictate action. Taqwa gives the heart a higher loyalty than impulse and gives the community a reason to protect moral boundaries even when doing so is unpopular.

**Obedience Is More Than Private Belief**

A person may affirm that God is Lord while keeping God's command outside intimate conduct, public entertainment, or social policy. Lot's sentence refuses that division. Obedience reaches the areas people most want to reserve for themselves. Faith has consequences for the way desire is disciplined and the way a community treats those who are vulnerable.

This does not authorize intrusive policing of every private struggle. Human beings must act with justice and avoid slander. It does mean that a society cannot demand celebration of what revelation forbids and call the demand neutral. Obedience includes refusing to make sin a public virtue.


When appetite rules without restraint, the powerful may turn other people into objects. The moral issue is not only the desire itself but the way it can erase another person's dignity, consent, safety, or right to live without coercion. Lot calls his people back to a standard in which no appetite is allowed to govern without accountability.

Taqwa therefore protects both the person who must restrain himself and the person who might otherwise be used. It refuses the idea that the strong may define morality by what they can obtain. Fear of God limits desire before desire becomes a social order.

**The Messenger's Obedience Is the Pattern**

Lot does not stand outside the command he gives. He fears Allah and obeys the Lord by delivering a difficult warning. His authority is not based on claiming that he is above human moral responsibility. He is the first servant of the message he brings.

This makes prophetic obedience visible. The messenger's life should direct people toward God rather than toward a private code reserved for others. Lot's call is credible because it is a call to submission, not an opportunity for him to enjoy control over the community.


The same wording has appeared in the calls of Noah, Hūd, and Ṣāliḥ. The repetition shows that the moral center of prophecy does not change when the social problem changes. Worship God, fear Him, and obey the messenger. A community cannot declare that its custom is exempt because its temptation has a different form.

Quran 29:36 records Shuʿayb calling Midian to worship Allah, hope for the Last Day, and avoid corruption. The parallel shows how reverence for God becomes a public ethic. Fear is not an escape from society; it is what keeps society from turning desire and power into corruption.

**Obedience Does Not Mean Human Domination**

Because Lot says “obey me,” the people might imagine that he seeks personal rule. The first part of the sentence prevents that reading. Obedience is directed through him to Allah. A true messenger does not make the people dependent on his personality or claim a right to be worshipped.

The distinction is important for every community. Religious obedience must be measured by the revealed call, not by the unchecked preference of a leader. Lot's mission curbs human domination; it does not establish a new form of it.


Lot could have pronounced the people hopeless, but he calls them to fear and obey. The command is an opportunity to stop defending a harmful way of life and return to God. Warning is severe because the danger is real, yet it remains merciful while repentance is possible.

The reader should hear the sentence as more than a refrain. It asks whether divine guidance is allowed to govern desire, speech, and public pressure. Taqwa is not an abstract feeling. It is the courage to obey God when the surrounding culture insists that obedience is unnecessary.

The phrase also teaches the messenger's community how to resist coercion. If a leader commands people to praise what God forbids, refusing that command is not rebellion against truth. It is loyalty to the higher authority. Such refusal should remain principled rather than abusive, because the servant who fears Allah must not become another source of domination.

Lot's call therefore joins personal restraint with public responsibility. The person disciplines his own desire, and the community refuses to build laws, celebrations, or pressures around desire's unchecked rule. Obedience begins in the heart but becomes visible in the moral shape a people agrees to protect. It asks the community to make reverence stronger than pressure, fashion, and the fear of standing alone. A community cannot call itself obedient while punishing every person who honors God's boundary.
