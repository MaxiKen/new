## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:136

> They responded, “It is all the same to us whether you warn ˹us˺ or not.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Indifference Is Their Answer to Warning**

After Hūd recalls the Provider and warns of a tremendous day, ʿĀd responds that warning and silence are the same to them. Their words present themselves as calm, but they reveal a hardened moral posture. The issue is no longer that they failed to understand the message. They claim that understanding would make no difference.

A warning matters only if the hearer allows it to affect judgment and action. By declaring that all outcomes are equal, the people try to remove the force of consequence before they have examined the truth. They protect their habits by pretending that no voice can require a response from them.


A person may say that two options are the same because he lacks information, but ʿĀd has already heard Hūd's call, his claim of trustworthiness, and his explanation of their blessings. Their statement is not an honest admission of uncertainty. It is a refusal to let the warning disturb the order they prefer.

True indifference would involve no passionate defense of the old way. Yet a people that insists that warning is useless is often protecting something. Pride may appear as boredom, but it still has an object: the desire to remain answerable to no one.

**Warnings Are Meant to Preserve Choice**

Hūd warns before the punishment becomes visible. That timing is a mercy because it leaves the people room to change. They treat the warning as irrelevant, but the very fact that it has arrived shows that their choice still matters. A person who can repent does not stand in the same position as one who has already met the consequence.

The answer of ʿĀd attempts to reverse this mercy. By saying that warning and no warning are equal, they make opportunity sound pointless. The Qur'anic account shows the opposite: the warning is evidence that God has not abandoned them to their chosen path. Quran 2:6 uses a closely related formulation for people who persist in disbelief, showing that the problem lies in the hearer's refusal, not in any weakness of the warning.


Humility listens even when a message is unwelcome. Arrogance decides in advance that no correction deserves attention. The people of ʿĀd have made their own judgment the measure of every warning, so they can dismiss Hūd without responding to his reasons.

This posture is dangerous in public life. Leaders who treat criticism as irrelevant cannot distinguish between malicious insult and necessary correction. They surround themselves with agreement, then call the resulting confidence proof that no change is needed. Hūd's people show where such confidence can lead.

**The Messenger's Duty Does Not Depend on Acceptance**

Hūd cannot force the people to fear God. His duty is to convey the truth clearly and warn them of the consequence. Their statement does not make the message useless, even though it reveals that they are refusing its benefit. The value of a warning is not measured only by the immediate response of those who hear it.

This protects the messenger from despair and the believer from a false measure of success. Faithful counsel may be rejected for a time. The servant remains responsible for honesty and compassion, while God judges the hearer's decision and the eventual result.


The people speak as though Hūd's words add nothing. But silence would leave them without a clear invitation to repent, while warning gives them knowledge of what is at stake. The two situations cannot be equal for a community that still has the capacity to respond.

A society also has a duty to welcome truthful warning. If it punishes every critic or mocks every reminder, it creates the very blindness it later calls inevitable. Hūd's speech is a public service because it keeps accountability audible. Rejecting it does not erase the danger; it only removes a means of preparation.

**The Familiar Can Make Warning Seem Ordinary**

Hūd is their brother, and his words may have become familiar through repetition. Familiarity can make a warning seem less urgent, especially when no immediate punishment follows. The people confuse delay with cancellation and repeated mercy with proof that nothing will happen.

The wise listener does the reverse. Repeated warning increases the need to respond because it shows both the seriousness of the matter and the generosity of the opportunity. A message should not be dismissed merely because it has been heard before.


A reader may condemn ʿĀd's indifference while treating present reminders in the same way. The verse asks whether warning changes anything: does it affect worship, the treatment of others, the use of wealth, and the way power is restrained? If not, the heart may be repeating the people's answer even without saying the words aloud.

Hūd's warning is therefore not merely a historical exchange. It places the hearer before a choice. Take the message seriously and return to God, or declare by conduct that warning and silence are the same. The consequence of indifference is that a mercy offered in speech is refused in practice.

Indifference also changes the way a person remembers the past. He may hear that an earlier people was warned and destroyed, then place the account safely outside his own life. That distance is another form of saying that warning and silence are equal. The wise reader lets the story interrupt complacency before a familiar habit becomes a settled verdict.

Hūd's words remain useful even when a hearer does not feel immediate fear. Moral seriousness is not measured by emotion alone. A person can begin by admitting that the warning deserves obedience, then allow that admission to reshape desire and conduct. What matters is that the message is no longer treated as irrelevant. Even a small act of obedience can prove that the warning has been heard.
