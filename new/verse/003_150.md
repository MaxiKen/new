## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:150

> But no! Allah is your Guardian, and He is the best Helper.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Correction Introduced by the Word of Turning**

The verse begins with the particle that sets aside what has just been said and puts something else in its place. The warning in the previous verse concerned dependence on those who deny; this sentence supplies the alternative.

The construction gives the passage a sharp shape: a danger, and then the remedy stated in one line. The Book often answers a warning with a statement about God in this way, so that the community is left holding a truth rather than only a prohibition.

**The Word for Master**

The noun rendered as master or protector covers several related senses: an owner, a patron, a guardian, and the one who takes charge of a matter on behalf of another. The Book applies it to God in this passage and in others where it describes the relation of the community to its Lord.

One passage states that God is the patron of the believers while those who deny have no patron, and another, in the sūrah of the Pilgrimage, describes God as the guardian of the believers and as an excellent guardian and an excellent helper. The vocabulary of the verse before the reader belongs to that group, and the choice of it says what the community is being offered in place of the counsel of outsiders.

**The Best of Helpers**

The second half of the verse states that God is the best of helpers, and the construction is one the Book uses for the attributes it wants its people to rely on. The comparison is not with other candidates who might help adequately; it states that no helper matches the one named.

The Book states the same in a passage that tells the believers that God knows best who their enemies are and that He is sufficient as a guardian and as a helper. Another passage asks whether the community does not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to God and that it has no guardian or helper besides Him. The sentence in this verse gathers the same teaching into six words.

**Seeking Help From No Other**

Al-Ṭabarī stated the practical contents of the verse: it directs the community to seek refuge with God and teaches it to seek help from no other. The instruction is not limited to the crisis that occasioned it.

The Book's teaching on this point runs through its instructions about prayer, hardship, and fear. The community is told to ask God for what it needs, to rely on Him in what it undertakes, and to expect help from Him. The verse before the reader states the reason: no other source of help has the same claim or the same capacity, and a community that turns to another source for its protection is relying on something that cannot replace what it has left.

**A Short Verse With Weight**

The verse is among the shortest in the passage, and its brevity suits its function. It stands at the end of the section that dealt with the aftermath of Uḥud, and it supplies the conclusion the whole section has been building toward.

The Book uses such sentences as conclusions. After the account of a loss, the warnings about apostasy, and the reminder that death comes at an appointed term, the community is given a sentence it can carry: its master is God, and no helper is better. The sūrah does not add qualifications to the promise, and the brevity is part of what makes it memorable.

**What the Community Needed to Hear**

The believers at Madinah were in a position that invited them to look for support in several directions. They had enemies nearby, allies whose commitment was uncertain, and a group inside the city whose loyalty had been exposed during the fighting.

The verse tells them that the security they were looking for was not going to come from a rearrangement of their alliances. The Book does not forbid the making of treaties, and the sūrah refers elsewhere to covenants the Prophet had with other groups. What the sentence does is establish the order of reliance: the community's protection is from God, and any arrangement it enters into operates within that protection rather than replacing it.

**Guardianship and What It Requires**

The statement that God is the master of the believers carries obligations as well as assurances. A community under a patron is expected to follow the direction that patron gives, and the passage as a whole has been describing what that direction is.

The Book connects its promises of guardianship with the conduct of the people who receive them, and it warns against the assumption that protection continues regardless. The verse before the reader states the relationship at its simplest, and the community that reads it is expected to hear both sides of the sentence: the security offered, and the loyalty it assumes.

**The Passage Closed and the Argument Continued**

The verse closes a movement that began with the reminder that Muhammad was only a messenger and would die. The community was told that its life did not depend on the presence of the Prophet, that death comes at an appointed time, and that those who turn back harm only themselves. The close of the movement places the community under the guardianship of God.

The sūrah does not leave its readers there, and the verses that follow go on to describe the fate of those who oppose the community and the help that God gives. The sentence in this verse supplies the premise for everything that follows, and the sūrah treats it as the ground on which the community's confidence rests in every situation it meets.
