## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:55

> This is how We make Our signs clear, so the way of the wicked may become distinct.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Clarity as a Deliberate Act**

The verse states that the signs have been made clear, and it states it as something God does rather than as something that happens. The Book uses this vocabulary repeatedly, and the sūrah alone names it several times, including where the signs are explained for people who know (6:97) and where the Prophet is told to follow the road that has been made plain (6:126). The same formula appears in the sūrah of Fussilat, which takes its title from the word.

The claim matters because the Makkans had been complaining about ambiguity. They wanted a sign that would settle the matter beyond argument, and the verse answers that the Qur'anic method is to make the truth clear by accumulated explanation, argument, warning, and illustration. What they were asking for was not clarity but the end of the discussion.

**The Purpose Named: Distinguishing a Road**

Then the verse states why the clarity is given: so that the way of the wicked may become distinct. The phrase is spatial, and the Book's imagery of two roads runs through it. There is a path and there is a deviation, and the Book's work of explanation is what makes the difference visible. The sūrah earlier used the same image where it asked whether the blind and the seeing could be equal (6:50), and where it described being guided to a Straight Way in contrast to being left to stray (6:39).

That purpose is easy to misread as hostile. The point is not that God marks out a group of people in order to condemn them but that the clarity of the message makes the course of conduct visible for what it is. A road can only be avoided by a person who can see where it goes.

**The Book's Insistence That the Message Is Not Cryptic**

The Book returns to this point in several places because it was a live complaint. It says that it has set out every kind of example for people in the recitation, though most of them persist in disbelief (17:89); it states the same in the sūrah of the Cave (18:54) and in the sūrah of Ṭā-Hā (20:113); and it describes the revelation as a Book whose verses have been perfected and then explained in detail (11:1). The clearest statement of the principle is that the message is a clarification from God rather than a puzzle.

The Makkan objection, in the sūrah's own account, was not that the message was unclear. It was that the message was unwelcome, and the Book's response is to keep making the same points in fresh forms rather than to make them obscure.

**Why the Word "Wicked" Is Used Here**

The Arabic behind the translation describes a person who steps out of bounds, the same vocabulary the previous verse used for the people who deny the signs and are punished for their rebellion. Its appearance here connects the passage: the clarity of the signs has the effect of showing where the boundary lies, and the person who crosses it does so with knowledge.

The word does not describe people who have never heard. The whole point of the sentence is that the explanation preceded the conduct, and the Book's insistence on the clarity of its own message is what makes the responsibility of the reader what it is.

**The Verse as a Closing Statement of Method**

The verse functions as a pause after a long stretch of argument that has covered creation, the terms of life and death, mercy, the fate of earlier peoples, the treatment of the poor, and the greeting owed to believers. The sentence states what all of that material was doing: making the signs clear.

It also states what the clarity cannot do. It cannot remove the will of the person hearing, and the sūrah has shown in several places what happens when clarity meets a settled refusal. The verse therefore closes one section of the argument and opens the next, which consists of the Prophet's own declarations about what he has been forbidden to worship and what he stands on.

**The Reader Left With the Two Roads**

A person reading the sentence is left with the choice the sentence describes. The clarity has been supplied, and the two paths have been laid out: the one that the believers walk with their Lord morning and evening (6:52), and the one the wicked take by crossing the bounds that the signs have marked. The sūrah does not present the matter as unresolved, and it does not present it as a question the reader is free to leave open.

The verses that follow give the Prophet the words to declare where he stands, and they give the audience the sight of a community that has already chosen its road.

**Clarity for People Who Already Know**

The sūrah attaches the same phrase to a specific audience when it says that the stars have been made as guides through the darkness of land and sea and that the signs have already been made clear for people who know (6:97). The Book's explanations are not delivered into a neutral space. They are given to people who are travelling through their own lives and who can recognise what they have been told when it matches what they see.

That is why the verse here ends with the distinction between the two roads rather than with a celebration of the explanation itself. The clarity serves a purpose, and the purpose is that a person should be able to see the difference between the way of the wicked and the way of the guided while he still has time to choose between them.
