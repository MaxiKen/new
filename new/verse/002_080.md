## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:80

> ˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, “The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Tariff on Hell**

The recital quotes the claim itself now, third-person as before, and the claim is a doctrine: *the Fire will not touch us except for a number of days* (2:80). The formula is precise in its comfort — the Fire is real, its touch is conceded, but its duration is capped, and the cap is national: a fixed term, after which the community's stay ends. The exegetes preserve the figures the claim circulated with. Some report forty nights, set equal to the days spent at the foot of the calf, after which another people would inherit the Fire's remainder — and some add the bitter twist that the successors named were the followers of Muhammad ﷺ. Others report forty years; others seven days, one day for each thousand years of the world's existence. Whatever the arithmetic, the structure is identical: punishment converted into a tariff, eschatology into a schedule, and the schedule set by identity rather than by deeds. The verb *touch* is the claim's own softening — contact, not residence; a brushing of the Fire against a people who have already decided, on their own authority, exactly how long the contact lasts. The recital has been documenting a community's management of revelation; here it documents the management of its consequences — a self-issued amnesty, drafted below stairs and signed in Allah's name.

**The Parallel File in Āl ʿImrān**

The same claim is quoted again in the Qur'an, and the second filing supplies the diagnosis the first leaves for the rebuttal to deliver: *"This is because they say, 'The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.' They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying"* (3:24). Two additions carry weight. The deception is located *in their faith* — not beside it, not in politics or profit, but inside the piety itself, which is where a false assurance does its damage: the doctrine of numbered days did not merely misdescribe the hereafter, it reorganized a community's relationship to its own sins, making repentance optional and reform affordable. And the origin is named: *their wishful lying* — fabrication with a history, a doctrine built rather than received, which connects this claim directly to the economy of verses 78–79: the illiterate holding hearsay as certainty, the hands writing what tongues warrant as divine. The numbered days are the output of that machine — an *amānī*-doctrine in the full sense of verse 78, both a story told and a story wanted, the wish for a cheap exit from the Fire wearing the dress of a revealed schedule.

**The Two-Pronged Rebuttal**

The answer comes as a command — *Say* — and it is a disjunction that leaves the claim nowhere to stand. First prong: have they *taken a pledge from Allah* (2:80)? If the numbered days are a divine promise, produce the covenant — the document, the warrant, the moment Allah committed Himself to the term. And the prong carries its own trap: *Allah never breaks His word* — if such a pledge existed, it would indeed be honored; the rebuttal does not question Allah's fidelity but the pledge's existence. No scripture given to this community contains the term, and the community whose file the sūrah has been building holds a documented history of covenant-breaking from its own side (2:40 and 2:63 among them) — a party in default invoking a contract it never signed. Second prong: or are they *just saying about Allah what you do not know* (2:80)? If there is no pledge, then the claim is not a disputed interpretation but speech about God without knowledge — the epistemological crime the recital has been charging since verse 75, where distortion followed understanding, and verse 78, where conjecture replaced the Book. The disjunction is exhaustive and both exits are convictions: either you hold a covenant you cannot produce, or you are authoring eschatology without a source. The claim's advocates have no third option, and the verse's brevity is the confidence of an argument that knows its ground.

**Where the Number Came From**

The tradition's genealogy of the claim exposes its machinery. Forty nights is not an arbitrary figure — it is the length of Moses' appointed term at the mount, the period at the end of which the community below built the calf; the claim takes a completed historical punishment, the discipline of a generation, and reissues it as a permanent eschatological ceiling: we sinned for forty days, so we burn for forty days, and then we are out. The seven-day variant runs the same operation on a larger frame — one day per millennium of the world's age, the calendar of history converted into the tariff of the Fire. In both cases the method is legible: find a number somewhere in the tradition, detach it from its context, and re-attach it to the afterlife with the force of a promise. This is taḥrīf in its speculative register — the slant applied not to a text's wording but to a number's jurisdiction — and 3:24's verdict — deception *in their faith by their wishful lying* — names the psychology: a community under the weight of its own record wanted a bounded sentence, found materials for one in its scriptures, and built the bound. The recital files the wish-doctrine with the same seriousness it files the forged page, because a purchased assurance is the most consequential counterfeit of all: it is spent at the exact moment it is needed.

**Temporary Fire — the Precise Line Islam Draws**

The verse must be read with a precision the Islamic tradition itself drew, because mainstream Islamic theology does hold that some who enter the Fire eventually leave it. The position rests on Qur'anic exceptions — *"staying there forever, as long as the heavens and the earth will endure, except what your Lord wills"* (11:107) — and on the mass of ḥadīth describing intercession for the Fire's inhabitants, believers whose sentence ends. Al-Rāzī's treatment of this verse is the tradition's sharpest instrument for keeping the two things apart. First: the verse does not deny that a soul might reside in the Fire temporarily — it rebukes this specific claim, its absurdly small number, and the impudent certainty voiced without any explicit promise from Allah. Second: no one may assume such release for themselves or their people as an entitlement, but that does not bar Allah's forgiving an individual after a period. Third — the distinction on which everything turns — there is a difference between saying *Allah will not remove a believer from the Fire* and saying *Allah never promised to remove him*: Allah may act beyond His promises, and the absence of a pledge does not bind His mercy. The sin verse 80 indicts is therefore not the hope of release but the claim of a contract: the numbered-days doctrine is a self-issued covenant, and the rebuttal's first prong — a *pledge from Allah*? — is the permanent test of every cheap assurance in every age: release belongs to Allah's mercy, which He may extend; it cannot belong to a schedule, which no one can sign for Him.

**Saying about Allah What You Do Not Know**

The second prong outlives its occasion and becomes the verse's general law. *Saying about Allah what you do not know* (2:80) is the recital's formal charge against ungrounded theology — speech about God without warrant, the sin that sits beneath the forgery of verse 79 and above the conjecture of verse 78, naming in one clause the whole corruption chain's common root. The verse installs a rule of procedure for religious claims: assertions about Allah's dealings — His promises, His punishments, His terms — require a source from Allah, and where the source is absent, the assertion is not opinion but attributed lie. The pastoral transfer to the recital's audience is immediate and uncomfortable. Every religious community generates its numbered days: assurances of national exemption, calculations that cap the consequences of collective behavior, doctrines that let a people sin on schedule. The forms change — the structure of verse 80's claim is the structure of every "God would not punish us, His people, beyond this" ever preached. The rebuttal does not change either. Have you taken a pledge? If yes, produce it. If no, then know that the mercy you are counting on is real — 39:53 forbids despairing of it — but it is Allah's to give on His terms, not yours to schedule on your own, and the difference between hoping in an unbound mercy and claiming a bound one is the difference the verse's two prongs were revealed to draw.
