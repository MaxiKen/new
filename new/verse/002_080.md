## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:80

> ˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, “The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim and Where It Comes From**

The verse reports a belief held by people who expected their time in the Fire to be brief however they lived, and it answers the belief without insult. The Book's method here is to test the claim's foundation, and the two possibilities it names exhaust the grounds on which such a claim could stand.

The claim fits the portrait the previous two verses assembled: a community whose knowledge of its scripture came through recitation and hope rather than study, and which had reached a conclusion about its own future that no text states plainly. The Book quotes it, identifies its two possible bases, and shows that neither holds.

**The Numbered Days in the Commentaries**

The commentators preserved several versions of the figure this community used. Reports mention forty nights, matching the period Moses spent on the mountain; forty years, matching the duration of the wandering in the desert; and seven days, matching a week of atonement. Each version ties the claim to a respected event in the community's own history, and each produces the same result: a limited sentence treated as a standing guarantee.

The variations are useful to a reader today because they show how the claim arose. A story about a period of punishment hardened, over time, into a doctrine about the duration of punishment for everyone belonging to the group. The next sentence in the sūrah attacks the practice rather than the arithmetic, which is why it never bothers to correct the number.

**Testing the Basis of the Claim**

The answer opens with a question the Prophet ﷺ is instructed to put to them. Have you taken a pledge from God on this point? The Arabic invites them to produce the covenant, and the same breath states that God never breaks His word. If such a covenant existed, the claim would stand; the verse grants the possibility and asks for the documentation.

This construction recurs in the sūrah's argument with the same community, which is repeatedly asked to bring its evidence and repeatedly found to have none. Later in the Book the identical claim appears again in the House of ʿImrān, where it is answered with a rebuke about the fabrications the community had grown used to (3:24). The Book's habit with inherited certainties is to ask on what authority they rest.

**"Or Are You Just Saying About Allah What You Do Not Know?"**

The second alternative is the one that applies, and it is framed as a question rather than an accusation. The sentence returns the discussion to ground already covered: a people given a book who substituted hopes for knowledge, speaking about their own fate with a certainty they had not earned.

The phrase about speaking of God without knowledge recurs in the Book as a grave offence, appearing alongside the worship of other beings among the charges attached to those who are ruined (7:33). The verse therefore does not merely correct a doctrinal error. It identifies the error as a member of a wider category, and the same standard is applied to the believers later in this sūrah when they are warned about assertions they cannot support.

**A Hope That Removes the Motive for Conduct**

The claim is dangerous because of what it does to action. A person convinced that his punishment is fixed and short has removed the urgency from every command and prohibition that follows, and a community that has reached this conclusion about itself has eliminated its own reason to reform. The covenant verses that come next describe a people who ratified an agreement and then broke it, and this verse explains how such a breach becomes thinkable.

The Book does praise hope, and it commands its readers to hold it. The difference the verse draws is between hope grounded in God's mercy, which is accompanied by effort and repentance, and certainty grounded in a story, which replaces effort altogether. What the sentence objects to is the second kind, offered as knowledge.

**The Believers and the Same Error**

The passage is not a licence for one community to look down on another. The Qur'an warns the believers in nearly identical language when they are tempted to assume that their standing is settled, and it tells them to work while they still can rather than to rely on an outcome they have not been promised.

The sūrah's opening already established the point for its own audience when it divided people into those who believe and those who do not, basing the division on conduct rather than on inherited labels. The claim quoted in this verse is therefore presented as a human tendency rather than a community trait, and the correction the verse applies to this group is available to anyone who has ever assumed his future on the strength of his name.

**What the Verse Accomplishes**

By the end of the sentence the community's position has been dismantled without a single insult: the claim has no covenant behind it, and the alternative is speech about God without knowledge. The Book leaves the matter there and moves to a statement of the two destinies, which the next two verses set out in matching form.

That the claim survived its answer is itself instructive. Positions held without evidence do not dissolve when questioned, and the sūrah will return to this community's assumptions repeatedly, replacing speculation with a record of conduct in the covenant narrative that follows and with the language of the final accounting that runs through the rest of the passage.
