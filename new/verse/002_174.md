## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:174

> Indeed, those who hide Allah’s revelations, trading them for a fleeting gain consume nothing but fire into their bellies. Allah will neither speak to them on the Day of Judgment, nor will He purify them. And they will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Concealment Gets Its Price Tag**

The curse-verses on concealment (2:159–162) return here with a new element: the motive. The concealers are not merely hiding; they are hiding *for a price* — trading the Book for a fleeting gain, the same traffic the sūrah condemned in its portrait of the editors of Scripture: woe to those who write the book with their own hands, then say "this is from Allah," to sell it for a little price (2:79). The verse renders the transaction with a physical image of unusual violence: what they swallow is not the gain but fire — they *consume nothing but fire into their bellies*. The parallel in the sūrah of the Women fixes the same image to a neighboring crime: those who devour the property of orphans are, in truth, eating fire into their bellies (4:10). Both verses run the same accounting: the wealth sought by corrupting religion is not lost, not confiscated, not merely forbidden — it is *converted*. The price of the hidden truth arrives in the belly as the Fire that truth would have saved them from, and the conversion happens now, invisibly, at the moment of the profitable sin.

**God Will Not Speak to Them**

The second penalty is the withholding of speech: Allah will neither speak to them on the Day nor purify them. Āl ʿImrān states the identical sentence over those who trade God's covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain: *"Allah will neither speak to them, nor look at them, nor purify them on the Day of Judgment"* (3:77) — the same crime drawing the same threefold withdrawal, with the look added there. But the Qur'an also describes God speaking on the Day — questioning all of them about what they used to do (15:92–93), questioning those to whom the messengers were sent and the messengers themselves (7:6), and telling the damned *"Be despised in there! Do not ˹ever˺ plead with Me ˹again˺!"* (23:108). Al-Rāzī harmonized the texts with precision: the plain sense of this verse and 3:77 is total silence, but in light of the questioning-verses it must describe a punishment within speech — God will not speak to them words of peace and welcome. He compared it to the intimacy of a king, whose displeasure is shown by exile and the cutting off of contact, and suggested the angels will be the ones who speak to people that Day. Al-Ṭabarī read it as God not saying things agreeable to them; al-Qurṭubī added the linguistic observation that Arabs say "he did not speak to him" of a man who is angry. The speech withheld is not communication but favor — and for souls arranged entirely toward God, the withdrawal of His address is itself the fire.

**Nor Will He Purify Them**

The third clause withholds purification, and the verb *zakkā* carries the double sense the exegetes exploited: to purify, and to *declare* purified. On the first reading God will not cleanse them — will not set right their evil deeds, will not accept their deeds as He accepts those of the purified. On the second He will not praise them or call them clean, which answers the sūrah's own command not to elevate yourselves as pure (53:32): the self-declared pure are the ones God will not vouch for. The two readings compose into a single exclusion — from cleansing and from commendation — and al-Rāzī drew from it the verse's deepest claim: the punishment of indignity and humiliation is worse than physical punishment, because distance and separation from God constitute the worst suffering in Hell. The concealers sold God's words for money; the sentence pays them in the same currency inverted — the words of God withheld, forever, which is what their money was always for.

**The Anatomy of the Fleeting Gain**

The phrase *fleeting gain* deserves the weight the verse places on it. The gain is characterized not by its size but by its duration — little and passing, a price that cannot be spent twice, in a market that closes at death. Against it the verse sets what was sold: revelations, clear proofs, guidance — goods whose value the sellers themselves acknowledged, since concealing something proves you know it is worth hiding. The trade is therefore never between equals but between the eternal and the temporary, and the verse's economics are exact: the fire in the bellies is not a punishment added to the bargain but the bargain's true content revealed. This is the same accounting the sūrah applied to the traders of the Hereafter for this world (2:86) and the covenant-sellers of Āl ʿImrān, who will have *no share in the Hereafter* (3:77) — a phrase that reads as the balance sheet: their share was taken early, in cash, and the account is closed. The one who hides revelation for money does not earn money and lose heaven; he exchanges one for the other at a rate the verse exists to publish.

**The Speech-Withheld as Mirror of the Crime**

The correspondence between crime and penalty is tighter than general justice requires, and the tradition read it deliberately. The concealers silenced a revelation — they took words God spoke and removed them from circulation, managing what the community would hear. Their sentence is the same act performed on them, by God, at the one moment when speech is everything: He will not speak to them. They made His words unavailable for a price; He makes His words unavailable without price, without appeal, on the Day when every other voice has been collected. The Qur'an's punishments consistently carry this mirrored structure — the fire eaten now answers the money eaten then; the silence then answers the silencing now — and the mirroring is itself information: it tells the reader that the penalty is not arbitrary severity but the sin's own logic run to its end. The concealer chose a world where God's speech is a commodity he controls; the Day gives him the fixed form of that world, where God's speech is simply absent and he has only what he paid for.

**Placed Between Patience and the Law**

The verse's location in the sūrah is part of its argument. It arrives after the patience-triad with its formula of return and its blessings (2:154–157) and before the great definition of righteousness (2:177) and the legislation of retaliation, bequest, and fasting that follows. Between the patient poor who say *to Allah we belong* and the community about to receive its civil law stands the figure of the religious profiteer — the scholar-class sinner whose crime is the precondition of every other corruption, since a community can only be misled where guidance was withheld. The sūrah has now named this figure from every side: the truth hidden knowingly (2:42), the testimony concealed (2:140), the recognition like a father's knowledge of his child (2:146), the curse and its exception (2:159–160), and here the price, the fire, and the silence. The legislation that follows — blood-money, bequests, the fast — is addressed to a community whose knowledge is assumed intact, and this verse is the guarantee that its keepers were warned: the words you carry are not inventory. They are the speech of the One who can withhold His own.
