## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:181

> Give full measure, and cause no loss ˹to others˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Full Measure Makes Honesty Concrete**

Shuʿayb's call begins with an action that can be checked: give the buyer the quantity promised. Faith is not left as a private feeling. It takes shape in the cup filled to its mark, the weight placed on the scale, and the goods delivered without a hidden reduction. A seller may speak reverently about God, but the measure in his hand reveals whether reverence governs his profit.

The positive command matters. It does not merely forbid stealing a little from another person. It requires the seller to complete what the agreement requires. Quran 7:85 places just measure and weight inside Shuʿayb's call to worship Allah, showing that fair exchange belongs to the same moral order as devotion. The market is not a religiously empty space where a different standard applies.

**Loss Can Be Hidden Inside a Fair-Looking Deal**

The second part widens the command beyond an instrument marked with numbers. Causing loss includes withholding quantity, concealing a defect, changing the quality after agreement, or exploiting a person's lack of knowledge. A transaction can look orderly while one party has been deliberately made poorer than the terms allow.

That does not mean every bargain has equal results or that every disappointment is fraud. Trade involves risk, and a lawful price may rise or fall. The wrong addressed here is intentional deprivation: one person secures an advantage by making another receive less than what was represented. The command protects the truth of the agreement, not a promise that every person will profit equally.

A dishonest measurer sees only the amount that can be taken. Shuʿayb's instruction restores the other party's full humanity. The buyer has needs, dependents, and a claim to what was purchased. Taking a small amount from many people can become a large injustice, even when each individual loss seems too slight to provoke a public complaint.

Fair measure therefore builds trust between strangers. People can trade because they expect the other party to honor the stated terms. Without that expectation, every exchange requires suspicion, inspection, and force. A society that protects full measure protects the ordinary cooperation on which families and communities depend.

**Power Must Not Turn Need Into Permission**

The command is especially serious when one side has more wealth, information, or control over access. A hungry customer may accept a bad measure because there is no alternative. A worker may tolerate a reduced payment because the employer controls the job. In such cases, formal consent does not erase the moral duty to give what is due.

Shuʿayb's wording reaches the powerful before it reaches the clever. The one who can profit from another's need is commanded to restrain that ability. Justice is not proved by treating an equal bargain fairly while taking advantage of people who cannot negotiate. The complete measure belongs to the weak party as much as to the strong.

**Worship Is Tested by What the Hand Delivers**

The Qur'an returns to this same pattern in 83:1–3, where those who demand full measure for themselves but reduce what they give others are condemned. The evil is not ignorance of the proper quantity. They know what they want when they are receiving, then choose a different standard when they are giving.

That double standard exposes the heart of the matter. A person may manipulate a mark, a contract, or a calculation, but he cannot hide the moral direction of the choice from Allah. Full measure trains the soul to give others the standard it wants for itself. It turns belief into a habit of exact fairness, one ordinary exchange at a time.

That habit also sets a limit on clever accounting. A contract may use technical language, but a person cannot rely on a customer's inability to read every term and then call the resulting loss voluntary. Consent is meaningful when the terms are clear and the promised good is actually delivered. Shuʿayb's command therefore protects both the visible quantity and the honesty surrounding it.

The same duty reaches beyond a stall. An employer must not make a worker perform hours that are quietly omitted from the record. A lender must not hide a charge that changes the real burden of repayment. A public office must not promise a service and deliver a fraction while keeping the full payment. These examples differ in form, but each asks one party to accept less than the agreed right.

Giving full measure does not demand that every exchange produce identical results. It demands that the result be reached by truthful terms and a fair process. The seller may earn, the buyer may accept risk, and both may benefit from the trade. What cannot be defended is a gain that depends on the other person's ignorance. The scale of the missing amount does not decide whether the act is wrong; the deliberate betrayal of trust does.

This is why honest measurement matters even when the buyer never discovers the shortage. Justice is not created by the victim's ability to complain. The owner of a shop may know that a customer will not return, and an employer may know that a worker cannot challenge a record. Full measure remains due because Allah sees the party who has been made vulnerable. The command gives moral protection where human bargaining power cannot.

A habit of completion can also restore a person's understanding of blessing. Profit gained by reduction may look efficient, but it carries the cost of distrust and the burden of an account before God. A smaller lawful gain can be better than a larger gain built from many hidden losses. Shuʿayb's instruction is therefore not an obstacle placed against prosperity; it is a guard that keeps prosperity from becoming theft.
