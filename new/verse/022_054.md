## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:54

> ˹This is˺ also so that those gifted with knowledge would know that this ˹revelation˺ is the truth from your Lord, so they have faith in it, and so their hearts would submit humbly to it. And Allah surely guides the believers to the Straight Path.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Purpose of the Same Event**

*Wa-li-yaʿlama alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma annahu al-ḥaqqu min rabbika* — and so that those who have been given knowledge may know that it is the truth from your Lord.

The verse is a second purpose clause attached to the same event as verse 53. What Satan casts is a trial for one group and, simultaneously, a confirmation for another. The identical occurrence moves in two directions at once.

That is the sūrah's theodicy completed. The interference is not merely permitted; it is used. The person with a sick heart finds his doubt confirmed by it, and the person with knowledge finds the truth confirmed by it — and what separates them is not information, since both saw the same thing.

Sūrah al-Isrāʾ states the two-directional effect as a rule about the revelation itself: *“We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss”* (17:82). One sending, two receptions — healing in one direction, increase of loss in the other. Verses 53 and 54 are that sentence applied to the interference rather than the message: the same casting that hardens the sick heart confirms the knowledgeable one, and the difference was never in what arrived.

**Who "Those Given Knowledge" Are**

The Qur'an uses this phrase with a consistent profile, and it is worth assembling.

They are the ones who recognise the message when they hear it: *“Indeed, when it is recited to those who were gifted with knowledge before it ˹was revealed˺, they fall upon their faces in prostration”* (17:107). They are contrasted with people who prefer this life: *“But those gifted with knowledge said, ‘Shame on you! Allah’s reward is far better for those who believe and do good’”* (28:80). The revelation is described as residing in them: *“But this ˹Quran˺ is ˹a set of˺ clear revelations ˹preserved˺ in the hearts of those gifted with knowledge. And none denies Our revelations except the ˹stubborn˺ wrongdoers”* (29:49), and *“Those gifted with knowledge ˹clearly˺ see that what has been revealed to you from your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth”* (34:6).

The knowledge in question is therefore not a credential. It is the capacity to recognise the truth when it arrives, and the Qur'an links it directly to belief in the same breath.

**Knowledge Produces Humility, Not Certainty of a Different Kind**

*Fa-yuʾminū bihi fa-tukhbita lahu qulūbuhum* — and so they believe in it, and their hearts humble themselves to it.

The verb is from the same root as *al-mukhbitīn*, the humble ones described in verse 34, whose hearts tremble at the mention of God. The sūrah is deliberately closing a circle: the people promised good news in verse 34 are the people described here, and the mark of both descriptions is not intellectual assent but a heart that has gone quiet.

The sequence is worth holding on to. Knowledge comes first, then faith, then humility — and the order rules out a familiar error. Knowledge that does not end in humility has not, on this verse's account, arrived at its object. A person who knows the truth and remains bristling about it has stopped at the second step.

Sūrah al-Anfāl defines the believer by the same sequence verse 54 traces: *“The ˹true˺ believers are only those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah, whose faith increases when His revelations are recited to them, and who put their trust in their Lord”* (8:2). Trembling, increase, trust — the heart moved, the faith grown, the self handed over. Knowledge that ends in humility is not a lower grade of knowing; on this definition it is the only kind the Qur'an counts as belief, and the bristling possessor of facts has stopped before the third term.

**Why the Same Event Produces Opposite Results**

The sūrah gives no mechanism, and the silence is instructive. It does not say that the knowledgeable are smarter, or that they were given more evidence, or that they were protected. It says they were given *ʿilm*, and that what they did with it was believe.

The implication is that knowledge functions as a capacity rather than as a content. The same occurrence that confirms a doubter confirms a knower, because each brings to it the thing he has been cultivating. A heart that has practised doubt finds confirmation for doubt; a heart that has attended to evidence finds confirmation for the truth.

That is also why the sūrah can hold the two verses together without tension. Nothing in verse 54 rescinds verse 53. Both are describing what a test does.

Sūrah al-Baqarah states the principle for parables, and it holds for every sign: *“Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious”* (2:26). The same parable strays and guides; the straying falls only on the rebellious, which means the outcome was in the recipient before it was in the sign. That is the mechanism verse 54 withholds and verse 53 implies: nothing is added to either party except the occasion, and the occasion reveals what each had been cultivating all along.

**Knowledge Is Described as Something Given**

The phrasing is passive: *alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma*, those who *were given* knowledge. The Qur'an never uses this expression as a compliment to the person's own achievement.

That is worth holding on to, because it changes what the verse is saying about how people differ. If knowledge were an attainment, the knowledgeable would simply be the diligent and the sick-hearted the lazy, and the difference between verse 53 and verse 54 would be a difference of effort. The grammar rules that out. Both groups received; what they did with what they received is what the two verses record.

The sūrah has insisted on this since verse 16: the signs are clear, and guidance is God's to give. Verse 54 is that principle applied to the specific case of a message that has been interfered with. The knowledgeable recognise the truth not because they worked harder at it but because they were given the capacity and did not refuse it.

**The Verse Answers a Question the Sūrah Has Been Asked Twice**

Twice in this sūrah the Prophet's opponents have demanded something that would settle matters by force: the punishment, hurried up (22:47), and now the interference that they hoped would discredit the message.

Both demands rest on the same assumption — that the truth of the revelation should be demonstrable on the spot, in a form nobody can dispute. The sūrah's answer in both places is to refuse the terms. The message is not established by the removal of every objection; it is established by God, after the objections have had their run.

That is the practical content of this verse. The interference does not prevent the knowledgeable from knowing. It is precisely the circumstance in which their knowledge is exercised, and the recognition that follows from it is worth more than a recognition extracted by a display.

The demand for a settling sign has a history, and Sūrah al-Isrāʾ gives the reason it is refused: *“Nothing keeps Us from sending the ˹demanded˺ signs except that they had ˹already˺ been denied by earlier peoples. And We gave Thamūd the she-camel as a clear sign, but they wrongfully rejected it”* (17:59). The demanded demonstration was already tried — the she-camel, visible and undeniable — and the result was rejection followed by destruction. Verse 54's refusal belongs to that history: the interference is not removed because removal never settled anyone, and the knowledgeable are those who know without it.

**"God Guides the Believers to a Straight Path"**

*Wa-inna Allāha la-hādī alladhīna āmanū ilā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm* — and God is the guide of those who believe, to a straight path.

The closing clause returns to the theme the sūrah stated in verse 16: guidance is God's, and it is given to those who believe. The order in that sentence is the point — they believe, and then they are guided, and the guidance is described as continuing rather than as a single act of direction.

A *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm* is a road without bends in it, and the promise is not that the believer will find it easy but that he will find it straight. For a community that has just been told that its message will be interfered with, that is the relevant assurance.

The guidance is described as increasing, not merely given: *“As for those who are ˹rightly˺ guided, He increases them in guidance and blesses them with righteousness”* (47:17). Guided, then more guided — the participle *la-hādī* in verse 54 carries the same continuity, a guiding that goes on rather than a direction given once. For the community told that its message will be interfered with, the tense matters: the guidance does not run out when the interference arrives, because increase is what it does.
