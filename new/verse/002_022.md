## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:22

> ˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you. So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah ˹in worship˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Earth Prepared as a Place to Live**

The verse describes the ground under its audience as something made ready rather than stumbled upon. The Arabic word rendered settlement compares the earth to a spread-out bed, and the word for the sky compares it to a structure raised overhead. Both terms are domestic: the planet is furnished for a resident, and the roof is built over him, which is a way of describing creation as hospitality rather than as spectacle.

The same picture is drawn with almost the same words later in the sūrah's own book: *“It is Allah Who made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy. He shaped you ˹in the womb˺, perfecting your form.”* (40:64). Elsewhere the earth is described as spread out and fitted with mountains and pathways so that its inhabitants can find their way across it: *“And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way.”* (21:31). The Book consistently presents the world's habitability as a deliberate arrangement, and it names the arranger in the first word of the sentence.

**From Sky to Fruit, in One Clause**

The verse then runs the chain of provision in a single breath: water from above, fruit from the ground, food on the table. Nothing in the chain can be produced by the people who benefit from it, and the Qur'an draws out the dependence with the same imagery elsewhere — *“He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky, from which you drink and by which plants grow for your cattle to graze. With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit”* (16:10–11). Another passage ties the gift to gratitude directly: *“It is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you.”* (14:32).

Read with the previous verse, the argument is continuous. The first sentence asked for worship on the ground of creation; this one supplies the daily evidence of the same care. The point is not that God once made the world, but that food keeps arriving in it by a chain that no human being controls, and the verse presents the ordinary meal as the standing proof of the claim.

**"Do Not Set Up Equals, While You Know"**

The prohibition comes at the end and turns the whole description into a charge. The Arabic word rendered equals, *andād*, names things placed on the same level, and the Book uses it for the idols and for anything else given the loyalty that belongs to God. The final phrase, *while you know*, is what gives the sentence its edge: it addresses people who already accept the premise of the argument.

A report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4477, narrated from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, records that the Prophet ﷺ was asked which sin is greatest before God and answered with this very act, describing it as setting up a rival to God though He alone created you. The chapter of the collection in which that report appears carries this verse of al-Baqarah as its heading, which is why the prohibition is treated in the tradition as the sūrah's answer to the practice of associating partners with God. The phrase *while you know* excludes the excuse of ignorance, not the possibility of repentance.

**What the Equals Were, and What the Argument Said About Them**

The men being addressed in Madinah and Mecca were not claiming that their idols had built the sky. The Book states their position plainly in the previous verse's terms: they already attributed creation to God. What the idols received was the loyalty, the petition and the ritual that the argument of this verse assigns to the provider of the rain. The Book's rejoinder to that arrangement is to invite its audience to follow their own admissions to their conclusion.

The sūrah makes the same move with the images of thunder, rain and provision that the parables have just used, and it presses the point about the worthlessness of the partners in plainer language later in the chapter, where the idols are said to be unable to create even a fly (22:73) and are described as fuel for the fire that awaits those who rely on them (21:98). The logic of this verse is that everything the partners are credited with is being supplied by somebody else.

**Why the Argument Is Addressed to Worshipers**

The sentence presupposes an audience that is already religious. It does not argue that worship is unnecessary or that human beings are self-sufficient; it takes worship as a given and asks who should receive it. The Book tends to argue this way, because the alternative it confronts is not unbelief in a maker but the distribution of loyalty among claimants that the maker never authorised.

The sūrah's method here is the method of the whole opening section: it presents the world's order as evidence, names the one who arranged it, and then states the one thing being asked of the people who live in it. What follows the prohibition is a challenge that tests the audience's confidence in their own position, and it addresses them on the basis of the very knowledge this verse has just reminded them that they have.
