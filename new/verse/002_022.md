## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:22

> ˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you. So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah ˹in worship˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Evidence Is the Furniture of the World**

The verse answers the command of verse 21 not with syllogism but with surroundings. Its argument is a list — earth, sky, rain, fruit — and every item is something the addressee is already using. The rhetorical strategy is worth naming: revelation does not here prove God from abstractions but points at the furnished condition of the listener's life and asks him to read the furniture as evidence. Each piece carries a maker's mark. The earth is *firāsh*, a spread resting place; the sky a *bināʾ*, a built canopy; the rain a descent with a purpose; the fruit an output with a beneficiary — "for you," stated twice. The list moves from structure to supply: first the house, then its provisioning system. By the time the verse reaches its prohibition, the reader has walked through his own daily life as an exhibit, and the prohibition lands not as decree but as the only conclusion the exhibit supports. This is the Qur'an's most characteristic argument form — cosmology as testimony, geography as witness stand.

**A Bed and a Building: Domestic Words for Cosmic Things**

The two nouns chosen for earth and sky collapse the cosmic into the domestic. *Firāsh* is bedding, a place spread for rest — the earth described not as a planet but as a mattress laid out for the addressee, fitted to his body, neither too hard nor too hostile. *Bināʾ* is construction, an edifice — the sky as a raised roof, and the same root the Qur'an uses for buildings humans erect. The pairing produces a house: floor and ceiling, and the inhabitant between them. The register is deliberate and it does theological work. A house implies a householder; bedding implies someone who cared whether the sleeper was comfortable; a built roof implies a builder who calculated loads. The extended furnishing continues elsewhere in the Book — mountains set as stabilizers so the earth does not shake, and broad pathways for finding the way (21:31) — each feature an installed fixture. The verse's argument from design is therefore not Paley's watch found on a heath but something more intimate: you are not examining a mechanism, you are lying in a bed someone made.

**The Provision Chain: Rain Sent, Fruit Brought**

Between the structure and the prohibition the verse inserts the supply line: water sent down from the sky, fruits brought out by it, the whole chain ending in the word *rizq* — provision, sustenance, what is apportioned to a creature by another. The verb forms keep the agency exact: Allah *sends down* the water and *brings forth* the fruit; the earth grows, the tree yields, but the verse assigns both motions to the hand above the sky. *Thamarāt*, fruits, carries its double range into the theology — the commentators read it as natural produce for the body and, by extension, as the spiritual sustenance drawn from the same created order for the soul. The double range makes every meal a two-channel communication: calories and testimony arriving together. Provision in the Qur'an is never neutral matter; it is *rizq* — a gift with a Giver named in the grammar — and the verse positions the listener inside an unbroken delivery system whose sender signs every package.

**“Knowingly”: The Adverb That Turns Prohibition Into Indictment**

The final sentence is short and its last word is the blade: *fa-lā tajʿalū li-llāhi andādan wa-antum taʿlamūn* — do not set up equals to Allah *while you know*. The circumstantial clause does not restrict the prohibition — setting up equals is forbidden to the ignorant as well — but it aggravates the case against the addressee. After the list of verses 21–22, no one can plead ignorance of the Builder; the knowledge is built into the furniture, delivered with the fruit, recited in the very verses being read. The word *andād*, equals or rivals, names the specific idolatry of partnership: not denying Allah but assigning peers beside Him — intermediaries credited with independent power, causes treated as causes-in-themselves, favors thanked to the wrong hand. The verse thus converts its whole preceding argument into a legal instrument: you have been shown the house, the builder, and the deliveries; a rival credited in this house is now credited *knowingly*, and the adverb registers the exact weight of every such credit.

**The Greatest Sin, in the Prophet's Own Ranking**

The prohibition's rank is fixed in a verified report. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd asked the Prophet ﷺ directly: what is the greatest sin in the sight of Allah? The answer: *"That you set up a rival unto Allah though He alone created you"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4477). The ḥadīth's logic is the verse's logic compressed — creation cited as the very ground that makes rivalry monstrous — and Bukhārī filed this report under a chapter headed by this verse of al-Baqarah, joining the two texts officially. The rest of the ranking sharpens the first place: asked what comes next, the Prophet ﷺ named killing one's child for fear of sharing food, then adultery with a neighbor's wife. The triad is coherent: a sin against the Creator, a sin against life, a sin against lineage — the three orders this verse's world rests on. That the greatest of the three is theological rather than bloody tells the reader how the Qur'an prices harm: the misdirection of worship outranks murder because it corrupts the source from which every other right flows.

**Where the Equals Actually Hide**

Idolatry in a furnished house is subtler than statues. The verse's own list suggests the hiding places: provision is the most dangerous rival precisely because it is most real — the hand that feeds is thanked before the Hand that sends the food, the salary eclipses the Provider, the rain is credited to the climate. The classical commentators note that *andād* covered the idols of the Arabs, but the Qur'an's usage extends the term to anything granted independent agency beside Allah — and the knowledge clause applies with full force to the modern reader, who knows the food chain, the water cycle, and the astronomy better than any previous generation and is therefore *more* without excuse, not less. Deeper knowledge of the furniture does not refute the Builder; it multiplies His signatures. The verse's demand is finally one of credit assignment: every comfort received is to be traced, consciously, to its source, and every tracing that stops early — at the market, the state, the self — sets up, knowingly, an equal.
