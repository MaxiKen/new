## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:22

> ˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you. So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah ˹in worship˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Runs From Provision**

Four gifts are named in a single sentence: a settled earth, a sky over it, rain sent down, fruit brought up. Then the conclusion: *So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah* (2:22). The *so* is the argument. Worship is being claimed as the response to provision, and the claim rests on something the sūrah treats as visible to anyone.

The logic is worth stating carefully, because it is not the argument from existence. The verse does not say that God made the world and therefore must be obeyed. It says that He keeps making things the world cannot make for itself, continuously, and that the recipient of a continuing gift owes its giver something. Lordship in the Quran's vocabulary is largely this: *rabb* is the one who sustains what he has brought into being.

**A Place of Settlement**

The word for what the earth has been made is *firāsh* — something spread out, a bed, the thing you lie down on. The image is domestic rather than astronomical. The earth is not described as a planet but as a resting place, the surface on which a body can settle and sleep.

The sūrah is also implying what had to be done to make that possible. A settled surface requires stability, and the Quran names it elsewhere: *And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way* (21:31). The habitability of the ground is not a byproduct of its existence. On this account it is a provision, arranged.

**A Canopy**

The sky is called *bināʾ* — a structure, something built, with the sense of a roof or ceiling over the people underneath. The Quran returns to the image as evidence: *Have they not then looked at the sky above them: how We built it and adorned it ˹with stars˺, leaving it flawless?* (50:6). The argument is not that the sky is large. It is that it is ordered, and that order is the sort of thing a building has and an accident does not.

Between the two — a floor spread out and a roof raised over it — the verse describes the world as a dwelling rather than a location. That framing is what makes the conclusion work. A person living in a house that was built and furnished for him is in a different position from a person who happens to be standing somewhere.

**Rain and Fruit: Dependency That Does Not Stop**

Creation here is not a single past event. The verb for sending the rain is continuous, and what it produces is called *rizq*, provision — the standard Quranic term for sustenance. The point is the maintenance rather than the manufacture. Rain falls, fruit grows, the arrangement repeats, and it repeats without the recipient's involvement or permission.

That is what makes the claim on him total. A one-time gift can be received and forgotten; a provision that arrives every season keeps the giver in front of the recipient by the nature of the arrangement. The verse is pointing at something the person addressed already relies on, which is why the sūrah can move from description to prohibition in the space of one conjunction.

**Equals**

The word for what must not be set up is *andād* — peers, rivals, things treated as counterparts. The prohibition is not primarily about denying God's existence. It is about giving something else the standing that belongs to Him, which is a subtler and more common failure. A person can affirm a Creator and still live as though his security, his standing, or his income is what actually governs his life.

The sūrah will return to this word's territory throughout, because the condition it names is the engine of everything the passage has described. The hypocrites' divided trust was exactly this: something set alongside God as a peer in the management of their safety.

The prohibition also arrives at the right point in the argument. Nothing in the verse's first half could have been proved by observation alone; a reader can look at the sky and decline to infer a builder. The verse is not offering a proof that forces the conclusion. It is naming what a person already knows about the arrangement he is living inside, and then drawing the conclusion he would draw himself if he were honest about it.

**Knowingly**

The prohibition carries a qualifier — *do not knowingly set up equals* (2:22) — and the qualifier has been read as limiting the charge to deliberate association. It also tells a reader what the sūrah considers the relevant failure to be: not confusion, but a person who knows what he is doing and places something beside God anyway.

A report preserved in the commentaries has the Prophet asked what the greatest sin is and answering, *To set up equals to God, though He created you*. [UNVERIFIED: reported in the commentaries; I could not verify a collection or numbering for this wording in this session.] The Quran itself states the same hierarchy plainly enough without the report: the gift is named first, and the refusal to acknowledge the giver is the wrong that answers to it. The verse's own sequence makes the point — provision is described before worship is demanded, and the demand is unintelligible without what precedes it.
