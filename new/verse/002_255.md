## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:255

> Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the EverLiving, All-Sustaining. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission? He ˹fully˺ knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them, but no one can grasp any of His knowledge—except what He wills ˹to reveal˺. His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him. For He is the Most High, the Greatest.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Greatest Verse**

Āyat al-Kursī — the Verse of the Pedestal, sometimes rendered the Throne Verse — is arguably the single most widely known verse of the Qur'an, named for the kursī it mentions and recited by Muslims setting out on journeys, seeking protection spiritual and physical, and memorized among the first passages a child learns. Its standing is prophetic, not merely popular. In Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (810), the Prophet asks Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: O Abū'l-Mundhir, do you know which verse of God's Book is the greatest? Ubayy answers by reciting this verse — *Allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa'l-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* — and the Prophet strikes his chest and says: may knowledge gladden you, O Abū'l-Mundhir. Other narrations call the verse a quarter of the Qur'an, direct the Companions to recite it before sleep, and promise — in a report of the Sunan collections — that whoever recites it after every obligatory prayer is kept from Paradise only by death. The tradition of its protective recitation has a famous story behind it: Abū Hurayrah, set over the Ramadan alms-store, caught a night-thief three times running, and the third time released him in exchange for words of benefit — recite Āyat al-Kursī when you go to bed, and a guardian from God will stay with you and Satan will not approach you until morning. The Prophet's verdict, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (2311): he told you the truth, though he is a liar — the thief was Satan himself.

**The Living, the Self-Subsisting**

The verse opens with the shahādah's Qur'anic form — God, there is no god but He — the phrasing that dominates the text's own declarations of unity (2:163, 3:2, and many more), and then names the two divine titles the tradition counts among the mothers of the Names, ummahāt al-asmāʾ. Al-Ḥayy, the Living, occupies in Islamic theology a place the commentators equate with Being itself: a living thing, al-Rāzī formulates it, is one able to fully actualize all its perfections — and the theological cascade runs from there, God's Power directed by His Will, His Will determined by His Knowledge, all of them depending on His Life. Al-Qayyūm, the All-Sustaining, is an intensive form from the root of standing and establishing: God subsists through Himself, and — the second half of the intensity — all things subsist through Him. The two names together state the verse's whole architecture: a Life that cannot lapse and a Subsistence that cannot be borrowed, which is why the next clause denies the lapse's slightest forms.

**Neither Slumber Nor Sleep**

The negation is graded: sinah, the doze — the light slipping at the edge of wakefulness — and nawm, deep sleep. Neither overtakes Him, and the commentators connect the wakefulness-clause to the verse's later statement that preservation does not tire Him: the same sovereignty described twice, once as unfailing awareness and once as unfailing maintenance. The denial of sleep is not a physiological remark but a custodial one: a guardian who sleeps is not a guardian, and the verse's God keeps the heavens and the earth the way wakefulness keeps a trust — without interruption, without lapse, without relief required. The clause also carries the epistemic side: His awareness of the creation never pauses, which means no moment of any creature's existence falls outside it — the sleeplessness is total surveillance in the exact sense the word's Latin root implies, a watching-over that does not blink.

**Ownership and Permission**

*To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth* — the ownership-clause grounds the intercession-clause that follows, and the intercession-clause is the verse's great reconciliation. The sūrah's earlier Day-verse had denied intercession flatly (2:48); here it is affirmed conditionally: *Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?* The rhetorical question concedes nothing to the intercessor — the permission is God's, wholly — and everything to the institution: intercession exists, and it exists as a function of the leave. The tradition's supreme instance is the Prophet's intercession on the Day of Judgment, for his community and for the world, attached to the praiseworthy station (17:79); theologians like al-Ghazzālī extend permission-granted intercession to the saints and ordinary believers according to rank, and Shīʿī doctrine places the Imams at its center. The commentators' deepest observation is structural: every prayer one person makes for another already assumes intercession — the angels' forgiveness-prayers for the believers (40:7), Jacob's promised prayer for his sons (12:98), the command to the Prophet to seek forgiveness for the faithful — since to pray for another is to believe God answers. Intercession, on this reading, is a special kind of supplication, and the verse's permission-clause is its charter: no one stands before God for anyone except by His leave — and His leave is why anyone stands.

**The Knowledge That Cannot Be Grasped**

*He knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them* — the phrase's referents are read three ways by al-Rāzī and the tradition: the Hereafter ahead and the world behind; what comes after death and what preceded creation; the deeds yet to be done and the deeds already done. The pronoun covers both the would-be intercessors and those interceded for — every party to any plea, past and coming. Then the verse closes the knowledge in the other direction: *no one can grasp any of His knowledge — except what He wills to reveal*. Knowledge here, the commentators note, is used in the sense of the known — the content rather than the faculty — and the exception-clause is the Qur'an's standing epistemology: the unseen is disclosed only where God approves its disclosure, *except messengers of His choice* (72:27); the angels' own confession is the verse's echo from the sūrah's opening — we have no knowledge except what You have taught us (2:32). The intercession-clause and the knowledge-clause are thus two statements of one rule: no access to God's court and no access to God's knowledge except by His leave and His will — and both accesses, the verse affirms, are real.

**The Pedestal**

The kursī is the verse's famous crux. Some read it as the place of the feet — the footstool before the throne — but the commentators find the rendering problematic: in the cosmological reports the kursī is not beneath the ʿarsh but below it in rank and above the cosmos — the Throne encompasses the Pedestal, and the Pedestal encompasses the heavens and the earth — an order that collapses if the kursī is a footstool, which sits in front of a throne rather than under it. The report from Ibn ʿAbbās states the encompassment as scale: the seven heavens and the earth beside the kursī are like a ring thrown into a wilderness. Others read the Pedestal metaphorically — knowledge, sovereignty, authority — the way "the crown" names the monarch. The tradition's discipline about both readings is the verse's own lesson in theological method: neither pure allegory, which lets the reader's desires into the text, nor literalist anthropomorphism, which assigns God location and limbs. The verse ends the question where it ends everything: the Seat — however named — encompasses both worlds, and *the preservation of both does not tire Him*, the keeping of everything that exists being, to the Keeper, without cost.

**The Most High, the Greatest**

The two closing names gather the verse's movement. ʿAlīy, the Most High — exalted above the intercessors who stand only by leave, above the knowers who grasp only what is willed, above the cosmos the Seat encompasses. ʿAẓīm, the Greatest — the magnitude the ring-in-the-wilderness report tries to picture and the tireless-preservation clause proves. The verse's whole structure ascends to them: unity declared, life and subsistence named, sleep denied, ownership stated, permission required, knowledge fenced, the Seat measured against the worlds — and then the two names that say what all the clauses were saying: nothing in the verse's inventory is on His level, and everything in it stands by Him. It is fitting that the tradition's most-recited verse is a verse about keeping: the Living who cannot lapse, the Sustainer on whom all subsistence leans, the preserver whom the preservation of two worlds does not tire — recited by travelers because the road is a place of lapse, by sleepers because sleep is the small death, by the frightened because fear is the doubt that anything is being kept. The verse answers the doubt in one sentence's worth of names: He is the Most High, the Greatest, and neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him.
