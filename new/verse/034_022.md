## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:22

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Call upon those you claim ˹to be divine˺ besides Allah. They do not possess ˹even˺ an atom’s weight either in the heavens or the earth, nor do they have any share in ˹governing˺ them. Nor is any of them a helper to Him.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Challenge Issued Rather Than an Argument Made**

The verse begins the sūrah's long series of commands to the Prophet — *qul*, say — and the first of them is not a proposition but a dare. Call them. Go ahead and petition the beings you appeal to, and see what arrives. This is a characteristic Qur'anic method in the dispute over other gods: instead of arguing that they lack power, it invites a demonstration. The same challenge appears at al-Aʿrāf, Yūnus, Hūd, al-Isrāʾ and al-Aḥqāf, always with the same confidence that the test will produce nothing. Its strength is that it cannot be answered with a counter-argument. A believer in the old deities who accepts the challenge must produce a result, and a believer who declines has conceded the point in front of witnesses. Note also the verb *tazʿumūn*, translated *you claim* — a word used in Arabic for assertions made without evidence, closer to "allege" than to "believe". The divinity of these beings is described as a claim before a single objection has been raised.

**The Smallest Unit, Used Again**

*Lā yamlikūna mithqāla dharratin* — they do not own an atom's weight. The sūrah has used this measure before, when it said that nothing of that size escapes God's knowledge (34:3), and the repetition ties the two verses into a single argument. There the smallest unit marked the lower limit of divine awareness; here it marks the upper limit of the idols' property. One party knows down to the mote; the other party owns not so much as a mote. Placing the same phrase on both sides of the comparison is more effective than any adjective could be, because it makes the two claims commensurable. The verb *malaka* is also worth noting: it means to own, to have at one's disposal, and it is the root of *mālik*, owner, and *mulk*, dominion. The question being settled is not whether these beings exist, and not whether they are impressive. It is whether they hold title to anything.

**Three Doors Closed in Sequence**

What follows is a systematic elimination, and the order is not arbitrary. First, ownership: they possess nothing in either realm. Second, partnership: *wa mā lahum fīhimā min shirk* — they have no share in the two. Third, assistance: *wa mā lahu minhum min ẓahīr* — He has no supporter among them. These are the three ways a lesser being could plausibly hold power in a universe with one supreme God. It could own a province outright; it could hold a stake in the whole as a junior partner; or it could own nothing and hold no stake but still be indispensable as a helper, the way a minister is indispensable to a king who could in principle rule alone. The verse shuts all three in a single sentence. *Ẓahīr*, the word for the third, comes from *ẓahr*, the back — the one who backs you up, who stands behind and supports. Arabic has no more modest word for assistance, and even that is denied.

**Why the Intercession Argument Was the Real Target**

The third denial is the one that mattered most in Mecca, and understanding why explains the whole verse. The Quraysh did not generally claim that their deities created the world or governed it. Their theology was subtler and is quoted exactly in al-Zumar: *"We only worship them so they may bring us closer to Allah"* (39:3). The gods were intermediaries — figures with standing at court who could put in a word. That position survives the first two denials intact, since a courtier owns no province and holds no share of the kingdom. It is destroyed only by the third. A being whom the Sovereign does not need as a backer has no leverage to exercise on anyone's behalf, and an intercessor without leverage is an intercessor in name only. The sūrah has arranged its denials to arrive at precisely this point, which is why the following verse takes up intercession directly.

**What the Book Says the Rival Gods Own**

The Qur'an is consistent in measuring the idols' holdings by comparison with something negligible. Fāṭir puts it at less than the skin of a date stone: *"As for those upon whom you call besides Him, they do not possess even the tiniest bit"* (35:13). Al-Ḥajj asks the hearers to consider that everything they invoke could not create a fly. Al-Qaṣaṣ projects the challenge into the Day of Judgment, where the summons is issued again and the result is public: *"It will be said ˹to them˺, 'Call upon your associate-gods.' So they will call them, but will receive no response"* (28:64). Luqmān states the underlying reason in a sentence: *"That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth, and whatever they invoke besides Him is false"* (31:30). The consistency across chapters is itself part of the argument. A scripture that varied its estimate of these beings — powerful here, weak there — would suggest negotiation. This one gives the same figure every time, and the figure is zero.

**Ownership as the Sūrah's Organising Claim**

This verse returns the chapter to the ground it staked out in its first line, where everything in the heavens and on the earth was said to belong to God (34:1). That opening was a statement of title; this is the corresponding statement of exclusion, and between them they define the sūrah's central dispute. Everything in the intervening verses has been evidence for the title — the four directions of knowledge (34:2), the record that misses nothing (34:3), the kings whose powers were loans (34:10–14), the nation whose orchards were withdrawn (34:16). Having established who owns what, the sūrah can now put the case to the opponents as a challenge rather than a lecture, since the only thing left for them to do is produce a counter-example. The next verse takes away the last avenue they had.
