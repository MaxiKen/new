## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:16

> He said, “For leaving me to stray I will lie in ambush for them on Your Straight Path.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim Against God**

The verse reports the words of Iblīs that he has been caused to err, and the claim attributes his condition to the God who sentenced him. The commentary tradition records two readings of the statement. One holds that the claim describes what happened without excusing it, since the Book elsewhere states that God leads astray whom He wills; the other holds that God's role consisted in the command that exposed what was already in Iblīs, so that the testing brought his hidden pride into the open.

The Book's verses about God's guidance and misguidance appear throughout, and the sūrah of the Cow states that God misleads many by a parable while He guides many by it (2:26); the sūrah of the Troops reports the answer given to the condemned that they were misguided in the same way as those whom God abandoned; and the sūrah of the Cloaked One states that God leaves in error those whom He wills (74:31). The verse in front of us places one such statement in the mouth of Iblīs, and the narrative does not treat it as a valid excuse.

**The Questions the Statement Raised**

The passage produced extended discussion among the theologians, and the sūrah's own words supply the material for it. Those who held that human moral destiny is determined by human choice read the divine guidance and misguidance as rewards and punishments for what a person has already done, and they pointed to the sequence in which Iblīs refused and was then left to his error. Those who held that God's control extends over all outcomes read the verse as evidence of that control and answered that God is not answerable for what He does.

The debate concerns the reading of the whole revelation rather than the meaning of the single clause, and the Book's statements about the matter include both the divine will and the human responsibility. The sūrah of the Heights itself states in its third verse that the community is to follow what has been sent down and not to take protectors besides God, and it will state in its thirtieth verse that some have been guided while others are deserving of error because they took the devils as masters. The two statements stand together in the same chapter.

**The Vow to Lie in Wait**

The verse reports the vow of Iblīs to lie in wait for the descendants of Adam, and the image is that of a hunter or a bandit who takes his position where his quarry must pass. The Book uses the same image in its warnings about the enemy, and the sūrah of the Night Journey reports the vow in similar terms (17:62). The verse in front of us states the intention, and the verse after it describes the directions from which the attack will come.

The vow answers the question of what the granted time will be used for. The delay was requested in the previous verse, the reason is stated in this one, and the plan is described in the next. The narrative supplies the whole sequence in three verses, and the sūrah's warnings about the deception of the enemy are grounded in the declaration that the enemy himself makes.

**On the Straight Path**

The path on which Iblīs takes his position is named as God's straight path, and the Book uses the phrase for the religion that the prophets brought. The opening sūrah of the Book asks God to guide its reciter to the straight path (1:6), and the sūrah of the Cattle describes the covenant of commands as God's straight path and commands the Prophet to follow it (6:153). The Book's use of the image elsewhere identifies the path with the revelation and with the community that follows it.

The commentary tradition notes that the choice of the path as the place of ambush indicates the target of the attack. Iblīs does not wait in the byways but on the road itself, and his effort is directed at those who are travelling on it. The Book's statements about the limits of his power accompany the warning: the sūrah of the Rocky Tract states that Iblīs has no authority over the sincere servants of God (15:42), and the sūrah of the Bee states that he has no authority over those who believe and rely on their Lord (16:99).

**What the Vow Says About the Enemy**

The Book describes the methods of the enemy in many passages, and the commentary tradition gathers them into a list that includes whispering to the heart, commanding indecency, sowing fear, making false promises, inciting enmity between people, and making evil deeds appear fair. The verse in front of us states the general intention from which the methods follow, and the sūrah's later passages name several of them in the context of the audience's own practices.

The enemy's tactics are also described as subtle, and the sūrah's account of Adam's temptation shows the method at work: the false claim about the tree, the oath sworn to the pair, and the suggestion that the fruit would make them angels or immortal. The vow in front of us is the announcement of a campaign of that kind, and the verses that follow describe its reach.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands sixteenth in the chapter, and it follows the grant of respite in the verse before it. The verse that follows reports the approach from every direction and the prediction that most of the descendants of Adam will not be grateful, and the sūrah then reports the second sentence passed on Iblīs. The narrative in front of us is the point at which the adversary moves from argument to strategy.

The reader of the sūrah has been given the story of the first refusal and the terms of the delay. The verse in front of us states what the adversary intends to do with his time, and the chapter's later accounts of the prophets and their peoples show the intention being carried out among the communities that refused the messengers.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that Iblīs will approach the descendants of Adam from in front of them and from behind them and from their right and their left, and that God will find most of them ungrateful (7:17). The vow stated in front of us is expanded into a description of the attack's directions, and the verses that follow report the sentence passed on the one who made it.

The two verses together present the enemy's plan in his own words. The reader is given the position on the path and the directions of the approach, and the sūrah's warnings about the deception of the satans are thereby placed in the context of a declared intention rather than a general caution.
