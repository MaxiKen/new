## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:45

> Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces!

**Expanded Commentary**

**"How Many a Town"**

*Wa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin ahlaknāhā* — and how many a town did We destroy. The construction *ka-ayyin* is the Arabic idiom for an uncounted large number, and it is always used in the Qur'an to introduce a claim that could be documented but will not be enumerated.

The rhetorical effect depends on the reader supplying the number. A text that said "seven towns" would be making a historical claim that could be checked and disputed. A text that says *how many* is making a claim about the character of history itself, and it invites the audience to count for themselves from what they have seen.

**The Condition Attached to the Destruction**

*Wa-hiya ẓālimatun* — while it was doing wrong. The phrase is a circumstantial clause: the destruction happened in a state of wrongdoing, and the implication is that it happened *because* of it.

The word is *ẓulm*, the same root the sūrah used in verse 39 for the wrong done to the believers. Here it is used for the wrong the destroyed communities did. The symmetry is deliberate: the Qur'an's account of destruction does not run on religious affiliation. What brings a settlement down is injustice, and the communities listed in verses 42 to 44 are described in their own sūrahs in terms of what they did to people — cheating in the market, highway robbery, assault — rather than only in terms of what they believed.

The clause is circumstantial in the Arabic, and that is what gives it its force: the town was *in the state of* wrongdoing when the destruction came, not wrongdoing at some earlier point and reformed since. The Qur'an's destructions are never presented as the settlement of an old account. They are presented as the interruption of an ongoing practice, which is why the catalogue can be generalised at all — what was seized was a way of life in operation.

**"Fallen Upon Its Roofs"**

*Fa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā* — so it lies hollow upon its roofs. The image is an inversion, and al-Rāzī explained the sequence: the roofs collapsed first, and then the walls fell on top of them, leaving the structure lying on what used to be its uppermost part.

The detail is the kind of thing only someone who has walked through a ruined town would say. A building destroyed from the outside leaves walls standing and rubble at the base; a building whose roof goes first ends up inside-out. The verse is describing the specific look of a place that has been abandoned for a long time.

The word *khāwiya* — hollow, empty, collapsed inward — carries the desolation. What is being described is not a ruined building but a town that used to be full and is now a shell lying on itself.

The ruined town is used elsewhere as the site of a resurrection argument, and the picture is the same one: *“Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins. He wondered, ‘How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?’”* (2:259). What the ruins provoke in both places is the same question about whether what is over is finally over — and in this sūrah the answer is directed at the living rather than the dead.

**A Well and a Palace**

*Wa-biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa-qaṣrin mashīd* — and an abandoned well, and a lofty palace.

The two objects are chosen to cover the whole of a civilisation's capital. The well is water: the thing that makes settlement in that country possible, the infrastructure no town can exist without. The palace is power: the monument a ruling house builds to outlast it.

Both are described as they now are. The well is *muʿaṭṭalah*, out of use, idle — not dry but unused, which is worse, because it means there is nobody left to draw from it. The palace is *mashīd*, raised high, plastered and made lofty — the description is of its ambition rather than of its ruin, which lets the reader supply the irony himself.

Both nouns are indefinite — *a* well, *a* palace — and the indefiniteness is what turns two objects into a rule. The verse does not point to a particular well known to its audience; it says that wherever this has happened, these two things are what is left, and it lets the reader supply the instances he has ridden past. A definite noun would have made the verse a report about one place. The indefinite makes it a description of what the end of a settlement looks like anywhere.

**The Argument Is Empirical**

The Qur'an's appeal here is to evidence, and it is worth being precise about what kind.

The ruined settlements of the peninsula were not known to the Makkans from books. They were on the trade routes. Caravans passed them, watered at some of the wells, and told stories about them. The verse asks its audience to do the arithmetic: this many towns, all destroyed, all of them in a state of wrong at the time.

Sūrah al-Rūm draws the conclusion the Qur'an draws from this evidence: *“Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might; they cultivated the land and developed it more than these ˹Meccans˺ ever have. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs. Allah would have never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves”* (30:9).

Note the last clause. The evidence from the ruins is not that God is harsh; it is that the destruction was self-inflicted. Nothing in the record suggests that a town was taken while it was behaving well.

**Towns, Not Individuals**

The noun is *qaryah* — a town, a settlement, a collected habitation. The Qur'an's destructions are of settlements rather than of individuals or of nations in the abstract.

That choice carries a claim about how God deals with human beings in history. A *qaryah* is the unit at which a way of life becomes visible: a market with agreed weights, a well shared, a ruling house, a set of tolerated practices. Wrongdoing at that scale is not a private matter, and its correction is likewise public.

The flip side is that the town is also the unit at which repentance would have been visible and effective. The Qur'an's accounts of destruction consistently record that a messenger was sent to the town first (16:36), which means the settlement had a collective chance to change before the collective punishment arrived.

This is why the ruins are usable as evidence. An individual's fate proves nothing; a town reduced to a well and a palace proves that something was done about a shared practice.

**Why the Ruins Did Not Settle the Question**

The next verse will say what follows from all this, and it is the harder claim: the evidence was available and did not produce the conclusion. Travelling through the ruins is not sufficient, because travelling is done with the eyes.

The next verse supplies the diagnosis, and it is the harder claim of the two. The ruins are on the trade routes and everyone has seen them; what is missing is not evidence but the faculty that reads it. Sūrah Muḥammad puts the same question in the same words, and the complaint is that the travelling happened and the learning did not: *“Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those before them? Allah annihilated them, and a similar fate awaits the disbelievers”* (47:10).

That is why the sūrah pairs the ruins with a claim about hearts. Verse 46 will say that it is not the eyes that are blind but the hearts in the breasts, and the sequence matters: the evidence is already in front of the audience, so the remaining problem cannot be evidential. A person who walks through a ruined town and comes back with nothing has not failed to look. He has failed to connect what he saw to what he is doing.

The sūrah is therefore doing something more careful than threatening its audience with destruction. It is pointing out that the most accessible evidence in the peninsula — ruins that every caravan passed — had been available for generations and had produced no change. If that is what evidence alone achieves, the argument of the chapter cannot rest on producing more of it.
