## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:192

> Our Lord! Indeed, those You commit to the Fire will be ˹completely˺ disgraced! And the wrongdoers will have no helpers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Disgrace of the Fire**

The verse continues the prayer of the people of understanding with the statement that whoever is made to enter the Fire has been disgraced, and it adds that the wrongdoers have no helpers. The sentence explains why the petition in the verse before it was made.

The word for disgrace describes the loss of honour before others, and the verse treats the entry into the Fire as the greatest form of it. The people of understanding, who have just reflected on the dignity of the creation, ask to be spared the condition that would undo the standing of a human being.

**The Explanation of the Disgrace**

Al-Quṭūbī and al-Ṭabarī record the view that the disgrace described here belongs specifically to those who enter the Fire and never leave it, rather than to everyone who is punished there for a period. Al-Ṭabarī records a remark of Ḥasan al-Baṣrī on the subject: sinners will be admitted to the fire, yet at some stage they will be brought out of it on account of the belief that remains within them.

The distinction reflects the Book's teaching that punishment and dismissal from God's favour are not the same thing, and the community's discussions of the subject have turned on that difference. The verse's own wording leaves room for the reading, since the disgrace is attached to the entry described in the petition rather than to a general statement about all punishment.

**The Spiritual Suffering of the Hereafter**

Al-Rāzī observes that the disgrace named in this verse is not a material punishment, which shows that the Hereafter includes suffering of a kind that has no physical counterpart. He makes the same observation about the rewards of the garden, where the Book describes conditions such as hearing nothing but peace.

The point widens the reader's understanding of what the Book's descriptions of the Hereafter involve. The Fire is described through its physical images, and the sentence before the reader names a form of loss that has to do with a person's standing, and the community's teachers have used the verse as evidence that both kinds of experience belong to the next life.

**The Debate the Verse Entered**

The verse has been used in the theological discussions about the believer who commits a serious sin. One side argued that the Book states elsewhere that the Prophet and the believers will not be shamed on the Day of Judgment, so no believer can enter the fire, since entering it would involve the disgrace described here.

The discussion belongs to a larger set of questions about the relation between faith and punishment, and the verse's contribution is the weight it gives to the disgrace itself. The community's scholars have treated the sentence as one of the texts that must be held together with the passages about forgiveness and the passages about punishment.

**The Statement About Helpers**

The verse closes by stating that the wrongdoers will have no helpers, and the phrase is used in another sūrah for those who associate others with God in worship. There the Book states that their home will be the Fire and that the wrongdoers will have no helpers.

The sentence in this verse applies the same declaration in the context of the prayer of the people of understanding, and its placement at the close of the petition gives the prayer its final note. What they are asking to be spared is a condition in which no assistance from any quarter can change the outcome, and the verse states that condition in the plainest terms available.

**The Prayer as Instruction**

The sūrah places these petitions in the mouths of the people it has just described, and the community that recites them is being taught how to pray. The Book's practice of recording the prayers of the righteous is a form of instruction in itself, and every clause of this passage can be turned into a lesson on what to ask for.

What the people of understanding ask for in this passage is protection, forgiveness, company, and the fulfilment of what God has promised. Each of those requests is stated in a short clause, and the community is given a set of petitions that covers the whole of what a person needs, in the order in which the needs arise.

**The Order of the Petitions**

The passage moves from the protection of the Fire in this verse to the request for forgiveness in the next, and then to the request not to be disgraced on the Day of Resurrection. The order reflects the sequence a person follows when he considers his own case, and it mirrors the movement of the whole passage from the world above to the fate of the person who contemplates it.

The prayer begins with the greatest danger, proceeds to the faults that make the danger real, and ends with the standing a person hopes to have at the end. The community's manuals of prayer have used the passage in that sequence, and the sūrah's shaping of the petitions gives them their model.

**What the Verse Teaches**

The verse teaches the community to think about the Hereafter in terms of both the fire and the loss of standing, and it teaches it to place both under the petition for protection. The people of understanding are described as people who are aware of what is at stake.

The sūrah is drawing toward its close, and the prayer scene is one of its final movements. The community that follows the passage to its end is given a picture of the state of the righteous in the presence of their Lord, asking for what they need and stating what they know, and the answer to their prayer is given in the verse that follows their last request.
