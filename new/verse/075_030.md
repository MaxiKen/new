## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:30

> On that day they will be driven to your Lord ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Driven, Not Asked**

The noun rendered as driving, *al-masāq*, comes from a verb meaning to urge animals forward, and the choice of verb is deliberate. Nobody in this scene is walking voluntarily. The dying man who became certain in v. 28 did not then get up and go; he is conducted. That is consistent with everything the sūrah has said about the closure of options: no refuge, no escape, no plea, and now no choice about the journey. The verb is not inherently violent — it is the ordinary word for driving a mount — but it does place the person in the position of the one being moved rather than the one deciding.

**The Same Verb for Both Destinies**

What makes the word interesting is that the Qur'an uses it for the saved as well. The disbelievers are *“driven to Hell in ˹successive˺ groups”* (39:71), and the mindful are described in the parallel verse with the same verb: *“And those who were mindful of their Lord will be led to Paradise in ˹successive˺ groups.”* (39:73). The verb is identical in both. It describes the conduct of the soul to its destination and says nothing about which destination that is, which is exactly the point of using it here. The sūrah is not yet saying where this person is being taken. It is saying that he is being taken, and that the taking is not his to arrange. The verb also carries a claim about deserts. The groups in those two passages are not processed anonymously: the disbelievers arrive to opened gates and a question about whether messengers had come to them (39:71), while the mindful arrive to gates already open and a greeting of peace (39:73). The driving is the same; what differs is what the person has made of the interval before it, and the sūrah has spent thirty verses describing how that interval is spent.

**To Your Lord, and Not to a Place**

The preposition is the one the sūrah used in v. 12 — *ilā rabbika*, to your Lord — and the repetition brackets the death scene inside the same claim about the end of the journey. Both times the destination is named as a Person rather than a location, and both times the address is to the Prophet, whose Lord is everyone's Lord. The effect of the repetition is to fold death and resurrection into one movement. What the Hour does to the whole of creation and what death does to one man are the same event in two scales, and both end at the same place: not at a gate or a garden or a fire, but in front of the Lord who then assigns.

**Why the Prophet Is Addressed Again**

The possessive matters for the sūrah's polemic. The people who mocked the message are now being driven to the Lord of the man they mocked, and the Qur'an loses no opportunity to make that identification. It is the same strategy as the mockery question itself: the objectors asked when, and were told before whom. Nothing in the verse softens what it says, and the sūrah does not pause to gloat. The point is simply that the argument the Prophet's opponents were having was never really with him. It was with his Lord, and the driving settles which of the two parties was in a position to set terms.

**On That Day: the Compression Continues**

The phrase *on that day* refers most immediately to the day of the death just described, and it is the same phrase the sūrah used in v. 12 for the Day of Resurrection. The double reference is not carelessness. The Qur'an states plainly that a barrier is set behind the dead until they are raised (23:100), so the interval between a person's death and the general resurrection is real; but it is not an interval the dead person experiences, and the sūrah's grammar reflects that. For the dying man, the shroud and the arrival are separated by nothing at all. The driving also has agents, and the Qur'an names them: *“Each soul will come forth with an angel to drive it and another to testify.”* (50:21). One conducts and one bears witness, which means the journey described in this verse is neither solitary nor unrecorded. The person being driven is accompanied by the record of what he did, and the two angels who were assigned to him in life are the two who present him at the end. Nothing in the scene is improvised, and nothing about it is being decided on the way.

**The Answer to the Cry for Escape**

The sūrah asked in v. 10 where escape might be found, and answered in v. 11 that there is none. This verse completes the thought by saying where the person is going instead. There is no refuge, and there is no alternative destination; the driving is to the Lord, and the only question left is the one the sūrah's final verses will put: whether the Lord admits a person to His mercy or has prepared for him a painful punishment. Everything between the bedside and that decision is conducted by Someone else, which is the last thing a person has to reckon with before the journey begins. One further note on the word: *masāq* is a noun of place and time as well as of action, so the phrase names not only the driving but the place driven to and the day of it. The sūrah has used the same economy of language before — *mustaqarr* in v. 12 named the settling and the place of settling at once — and the effect is to compress the event, its location and its hour into a single word. Nothing about the end is described at length, and nothing about it is uncertain.
