## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:159

> It is out of Allah’s mercy that you ˹O Prophet˺ have been lenient with them. Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you. So pardon them, ask Allah’s forgiveness for them, and consult with them in ˹conducting˺ matters. Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gentleness Attributed to Mercy**

The verse states that it was by a mercy from God that the Prophet was gentle with the community, and the sentence places his treatment of the men who had disobeyed within the working of divine mercy. The Prophet had every ground for harshness after the events of the day, and the verse names the quality that he showed instead.

Al-Ṭabarī identified the people concerned as the Prophet's companions and followers who had fallen short in their conduct during the battle. The sentence is therefore not a general observation about his character, though it is that too; it is a statement about how he dealt with the specific failure that the sūrah has just described.

**What Would Have Happened Otherwise**

The verse states that had the Prophet been severe and hard-hearted, the community would have scattered from around him. The word for severity describes a manner that is harsh in its treatment of people, and the hardness of heart describes a person who is not moved by their condition.

The observation is a piece of practical wisdom about leadership, and it is stated as a counterfactual. If the man who had been disobeyed had responded with contempt, the community would have broken apart at the moment when it needed to hold together. The verse gives the alternative its full weight, and then records what actually happened.

**Three Instructions in One Sentence**

The verse then issues three instructions: pardon them, ask forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter. The sequence moves from the wrongs committed against the Prophet to the wrongs committed against God and then to the business the community shared.

Al-Rāzī explained the division by saying that the first instruction concerns what the people had done to the Prophet himself, and the second concerns what they had done against God. The third instruction reaches beyond the question of fault altogether and returns the community to the work it had been assembled for, which is a mark of how completely the failure was to be left behind.

**Consultation in the Battle's Own History**

The instruction to consult arrives after the community's most conspicuous failure, and the sūrah had described the consultation that preceded the battle. The Prophet had asked the Muslims of Madinah whether to remain in the city or to march out, and the decision to go out had prevailed.

The commentators note that the outcome of the fighting did not lead to the abandonment of the practice. The verse instructs the Prophet to seek the community's judgment in the very kind of matter that had just gone wrong, which teaches the community that a defeat is not a reason to abandon a sound method.

**The Scope of the Consultation**

The commentators restricted the command to matters of war and the ordinary affairs of the community, since revelation governed the rest. Al-Qurṭūbī and others record the view that consultation on the Prophet's part was superfluous because he received revelation, and they record the rejection of that view, noting that he consulted his contemporaries, men and women alike, on many matters.

One report quoted in the tradition has the Prophet saying that the people knew more about the affairs of this world than he did. On that basis the consultation was substantive rather than a formality. Al-Rāzī suggested that the Prophet's practice was intended to establish consultation as a settled practice of the community after him.

**Consultation as a Practice of the Believers**

The Book names consultation among the marks of the believers in another passage, where it describes people who respond to their Lord, establish the prayer, conduct their affairs by mutual consultation, and give from what they have been provided.

The instruction in this verse therefore belongs to a wider teaching. The community is not to be governed by the impulse of a single person, however well placed he may be, and the Book presents the practice as one of the qualities it praises. The sūrah places the command in the middle of the passage about the battle, which gives it a particular weight: the consultative practice survived the test of the day that exposed everything else.

**Resolve and Trust**

The verse's final instruction concerns what happens after the consultation has ended. Once the Prophet has decided, he is told to place his trust in God, and the commentators read the sentence as describing a reliance that does not rest on the quality of the deliberation.

Al-Qurṭūbī explained the sequence as a decision made after consultation followed by trust in God rather than in the process. The Book states elsewhere that whoever places his trust in God will find that God is sufficient for him, and other passages repeat the instruction in the same words. The verse is among the Book's clearest statements of the combination: the human process and the reliance that accompanies it.

**The Closing Declaration**

The verse ends with the statement that God loves those who place their trust in him, and the declaration gives the instruction its motivation. Trust is not merely a practical attitude; it is one of the qualities the Book attaches to divine love.

The sentence follows a series of instructions that reach back to the beginning of the sūrah's account of the battle. The community had been told to obey, to stand firm, to consult, and to rely, and the passage closes with the statement that the last of these qualities brings a person under the love of God. The next verse states the same teaching about help and reliance in the form of a declaration, and the passage ends there.
