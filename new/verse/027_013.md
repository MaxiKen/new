## Sūrah an-Naml 27:13

> But when Our enlightening signs came to them, they said, “This is pure magic.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Whole Mission Compressed Into One Sentence**

Between the previous verse and this one, years pass. Mūsā returns to Egypt, confronts the court, delivers the demands, works the signs, endures the contest with the magicians, and watches plague follow plague. The Qur'an reports all of it in a single clause: the signs came to them. The chapter is not telling the Exodus story; it is extracting one exchange from it.

The compression shows what an-Naml is interested in. Other chapters give the confrontation in detail — the magicians' wages, their staffs, their prostration when they lose. Here everything narrative is stripped away and only the transaction remains: evidence arrived, and here is what was said about it. That is the shape of the chapter's argument. The opening verses described people whose deeds were made to look good to them so that they wander blindly, and this verse supplies the first historical illustration. Pharaoh's court is the case study, and the case is made with one line of their own speech.

**"Enlightening" — the Adjective That Removes the Excuse**

The signs are called *mubṣirah*, from the root *b-ṣ-r*, sight. The form is causative in effect: signs that make things visible, that produce seeing. The same root gave the chapter its opening word for the clear Book, and the two descriptions belong together — a legible text and legible evidence.

Placing this word here is not ornamental. It establishes the quality of the evidence before reporting the response to it, which means the response cannot be blamed on the evidence. Had the signs been obscure, calling them magic would be a reasonable misjudgment. The verse forecloses that defence in advance. What arrived was designed to produce sight, and what it met was people who declined to use it. Al-Ṭabarī reads *mubṣirah* as meaning the signs were themselves clear enough that anyone looking would perceive their source. The word therefore functions as a legal finding: the evidence was adequate. Whatever went wrong went wrong at the receiving end.

**Sorcery Was the Only Available Category**

Their verdict — *siḥrun mubīn*, pure or manifest magic — is not a random insult. It was the one classification that let them handle what they had seen without changing anything else they believed. Egypt had an established professional class of practitioners whose work involved apparent transformation. To file Mūsā's staff under sorcery was to place it in a known bin, staffed by known men, answerable to the palace.

The move is intellectually cheap and psychologically effective, which is why it recurs. The Quraysh used the same word against the Qur'an. Naming something as a known phenomenon makes the question of its source disappear, and the label does the work an argument would otherwise have to do. There is a sharp irony in their choice of adjective. They called it *mubīn* — manifest, obvious, clear — the very word the Qur'an applies to itself in this chapter's opening verse. Both sides agree the thing is unmistakable. They disagree only about what it unmistakably is, and the court settles that question by asserting rather than testing.

**A Charge That Refutes Itself**

Calling the signs magic concedes more than the speakers intended. A denial of the events would have been a stronger position: nothing happened, we saw a trick, the staff never moved. They do not say that. They classify what occurred, which admits that something occurred and that it was extraordinary enough to need classifying.

The admission becomes fatal when the outcome of the magicians' contest is recalled. Egypt's own experts were summoned precisely because the court had filed the matter under their profession. They came, they competed, and they lost, and the Qur'an records them prostrating in belief on the spot. The professionals delivered the only verdict that counted on the question of whether this was their craft, and the verdict went against the palace. Yet the label survived the test that disproved it. That persistence is the real subject of the verse. A classification adopted for convenience does not fall when it is falsified, because it was never held for evidential reasons in the first place.

**The Same Word, Three Peoples, One Mechanism**

This response is not unique to Egypt, and the Qur'an makes that point by repeating the vocabulary across centuries and communities. The people of ʿĀd, the Thamūd, the Meccans who heard Muḥammad — each produced a version of the same dismissal, and in several cases the same Arabic word.

The Qur'an records the Meccan version in almost these words, and the parallel would not have been lost on the first audience. They were listening to a chapter that described an ancient court dismissing evidence as sorcery while they themselves were dismissing the recitation in front of them as the same thing. The narrative is a mirror held up mid-argument, and it works without any explicit application, because the vocabulary does the pointing.

What makes the repetition significant is that these communities shared no culture, no religion and no historical contact. They arrived independently at the same verdict because the verdict solves the same problem for each of them. Accepting the sign means accepting its sender, and accepting the sender means accepting the demand that follows: release the Israelites, abandon the idols, change how the poor are treated. The label is not a conclusion about the evidence but a defence of an arrangement. Ibn Kathīr observes that the resort to *siḥr* appears wherever the alternative would be costly, and the observation holds across every instance. The next verse states what those who used the word actually believed while they were using it.
