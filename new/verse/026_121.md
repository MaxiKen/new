## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:121

> Surely in this is a sign. Yet most of them would not believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Story of Noah Becomes a Sign**

The account of Noah is called a sign. Its sign is not only the flood or the Ark. The entire sequence points beyond itself: a messenger warns his people, the powerful dismiss the believers, the community refuses correction, and God saves those who trust Him while judging persistent rejection. History becomes evidence when its pattern reveals the Lord's rule.

The sign also answers the class objection raised against Noah. The people whom the chiefs considered lowly were the people God saved. Social prestige did not determine truth, and numerical strength did not determine safety. The story exposes a world in which divine judgment reverses the rankings that pride treats as permanent.


The flood is not preserved as a spectacle without a message. It shows that warning creates responsibility. Noah's people were not destroyed because they lacked any chance to hear. They were warned, questioned, and invited to fear God. Their refusal became a moral decision, and the judgment confirmed what that decision meant.

A sign therefore asks more than recognition that an extraordinary event occurred. It asks what the event says about worship, arrogance, and accountability. Someone may admire the Ark as a story of survival while missing the demand to obey the Lord who provided it. The sign is understood only when it changes the hearer's relation to God.

**Most Refuse Even When the Evidence Is Clear**

The verse says that most would not believe. The majority's refusal does not weaken the sign. It shows that evidence alone does not compel a heart determined to protect status or custom. The chiefs had seen Noah's character and heard his warning, yet they judged the message by the social position of its followers.

Belief requires a response of the will as well as an act of understanding. A person may see the force of an argument and still refuse the surrender it demands. The verse does not excuse such refusal as an intellectual disagreement when the deeper cause is pride. Clarity can be resisted precisely because it leaves little room for self-protection.


The Ark shows that reliance on God does not mean abandoning means. Noah built and entered as commanded. The vessel was not worshipped, and its materials did not save independently. It became a sign because obedience joined the means to God's promise.

This balance matters in every age. A believer may use knowledge, planning, medicine, and community protection while knowing that none of these controls the outcome. The Ark corrects both laziness and self-reliance. It was prepared through action and made safe by God. The sign points to the Lord who governs both effort and result.

**A Parallel Account Shows the Same Reversal**

The parallel account in 7:64 describes the rejecters as blind to the signs after God saved Noah and those with him in the Ark. That detail confirms the moral character of the present verse. Blindness does not mean that no evidence appeared. It means that the people refused to let the evidence govern their judgment.

The same pattern can appear in a person who treats every warning as someone else's story. He sees the sign but keeps his habits outside its reach. Noah's account refuses that distance. The hearer must ask whether he is learning from the rescued community or repeating the rejection that made the flood necessary.



The chiefs relied on influence and contempt. They believed that the humble followers discredited Noah. The flood shows that social power cannot protect a people from the Lord's decree. The very group they despised is preserved, while the dominant group becomes the one that is gone.

This reversal does not teach hatred of the wealthy or admiration of poverty as an automatic virtue. It teaches that social rank is not a reliable measure of faith. A poor person can be sincere, and a rich person can be sincere; each is judged by God. The sign condemns pride, not possessions.

**Most Is Not All**

The word most leaves room for believers and prevents the refrain from becoming a claim that every person always rejects the truth. Noah's community includes those who believed. Their minority status does not make their faith less real. A truthful response can be numerically small and still be the response God accepts.

This distinction protects the believer from two errors. He should not follow the crowd merely because it is large, and he should not assume that being few makes every minority correct. The measure remains God's message. Noah's story asks for patient loyalty to truth without turning numerical weakness into a source of vanity.


A person may say that he will respond once the consequence becomes visible. Noah's people demonstrate the danger of that delay. The Ark was a sign while they could still change, but once judgment arrived, the same event became a boundary they could not cross.

The sign is merciful because it comes before the final separation. It allows the living to examine pride, class prejudice, and false worship now. Belief is not merely agreeing that Noah was right after the flood. It is trusting God's warning while obedience still has a future.

**The Refrain Opens the Story of ʿĀd**

This verse closes Noah's story and prepares the reader to see the same pattern in another people. The purpose is not to repeat a formula mechanically. It is to show that the sign of a prophetic story remains relevant when the names and customs change. Every community must decide whether it will treat warning as mercy or as an intrusion.

Noah's flood therefore remains a sign for the present heart. The Lord saves through obedience, judges persistent denial, and is not governed by the rankings of the powerful. Most may refuse, but the sign remains clear for the person willing to believe and act.
