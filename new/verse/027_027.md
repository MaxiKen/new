## Sūrah an-Naml 27:27

> Solomon said, “We will see whether you are telling the truth or lying.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The King Does Not Believe the Bird**

After a report that ended in a declaration of God's oneness, Sulaymān's reply is a single flat sentence: we shall look into whether you are honest. No thanks, no praise for the theology, no immediate action against Sheba. The claim is received and set aside pending verification.

Al-Ṭabarī identifies the addressee plainly — Sulaymān is speaking to the hoopoe — and al-Qurṭubī explains the sharpness by pointing back to the bird's opening line. He had said he grasped what the king had not grasped, which is a categorical claim about a prophet's knowledge made by one of his own troops. A statement that bold invites exactly this response. What the king says is not that the bird is lying; it is that the question is open and will be settled. That is the correct posture toward an unverified report from a single source, however impressive its content.

**Why Good Content Does Not Excuse a Missing Check**

The hoopoe's report was, as it turns out, entirely true, and its theology was sound. It would have been easy for Sulaymān to treat the correctness of the doctrine as evidence for the accuracy of the intelligence. He does not, and the separation is the point.

A report can be pious and false. If a story flatters a listener's convictions, the listener is less likely to test it, which is precisely why such stories travel furthest. The hoopoe told a monotheist king that a distant kingdom had fallen into idolatry and needed correcting — a report calculated, whether the bird intended it or not, to be welcome. Sulaymān applies the test anyway. The Qur'an elsewhere commands this directly, instructing believers to investigate when someone of uncertain reliability brings news, lest they harm people out of ignorance and then regret it. The principle is stated there as law; here it is shown as practice, in a case where the news happened to be true and the verification cost nothing but a flight.

**The Stakes of Getting It Wrong**

Consider what would have followed an unverified acceptance. Sulaymān would have acted against a sovereign state on the word of one witness with a motive to justify his own absence. Whatever form that action took — a letter, a demand, a campaign — it would have been launched on testimony that had not been examined.

This is why the verse belongs in a chapter preoccupied with how people handle evidence. Pharaoh's court saw enlightening signs and refused them. Sheba's people accepted an adorned appearance without asking what lay behind it. Sulaymān, offered a report that confirmed everything he believed, asks whether it is true. Three responses to incoming information, and only one of them is a method. Al-Qurṭubī reads the whole episode as instruction in leadership, and the reading holds: the same king who noticed one absent bird now declines to act on one bird's word. Attention and scepticism are the same discipline pointed in two directions.

**"Truthful or Among the Liars" — an Asymmetric Phrase**

The Arabic sets up an imbalance that translation tends to flatten. The first option is *a-ṣadaqta*, did you speak truth — an act. The second is *kunta min al-kādhibīn*, were you among the liars — a class. One side names something the bird did; the other names something he would be.

Arabic uses this construction elsewhere, and its effect here is to make the two outcomes unequal in weight. Telling the truth once is expected and unremarkable. Being found a liar enrols a person in a category with a standing reputation. For a member of a marshalled host, that is a heavier consequence than any single punishment, and it sits behind the threat of verse 21 rather than replacing it. The phrasing also reminds the reader that the bird's defence and his offence are the same claim. If the report is true he was on legitimate business; if false, he deserted and then compounded it. Nothing intermediate is available.

**Verification Without Suspicion**

There is a tone here that is easy to misread as hostility. It is not. Sulaymān does not accuse, does not withdraw the earlier threat's escape clause, and does not prevent the bird from continuing in his service — in the very next breath he gives him a task requiring trust.

That combination is the substance of the verse. Testing a claim and trusting the claimant are compatible, and treating them as opposites is a common error in both directions: some people verify nothing because verification feels like an insult, and others, having decided to verify, treat the person as guilty until the result comes in. Sulaymān does neither. He announces that the matter will be looked into and then hands the hoopoe a diplomatic errand, which is an odd thing to give a suspected deserter unless the suspicion is genuinely provisional. Al-Qurṭubī's observation that a leader should accept his followers' legitimate excuses belongs here as much as to the following verse. The method of verification is specified next, and it turns out to be ingenious.

**Nothing Is Said About Sheba Yet**

Read the verse again for what it omits. The hoopoe has described a kingdom in idolatry and called for it to be addressed. Sulaymān's reply says nothing whatever about Sheba — not that the situation is grave, not that something must be done, not even that the report is troubling if true.

The silence is disciplined. A ruler who begins discussing responses to a situation has already accepted that the situation exists, and the acceptance happens invisibly, in the framing, before anyone notices a decision was made. Sulaymān refuses that slide. Until the report is confirmed there is nothing to respond to, and he declines to speak as though there were. This is harder than it looks, because the content of the report was urgent and the urgency itself pushes toward action. The next verse shows that he was in fact preparing to act — the letter is already written when he speaks — which makes the restraint here more pointed rather than less. He had the response ready and still put the verification first.
