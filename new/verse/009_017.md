## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:17

> It is not for the polytheists to maintain the mosques of Allah while they openly profess disbelief. Their deeds are void, and they will be in the Fire forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sacred Building Requires a Sacred Purpose**

The verse says that polytheists are not to maintain the mosques of Allah while openly professing disbelief. The verb for maintain can include building, repairing, financing, filling, and administering a place. The issue is therefore not only who may enter a mosque. It is who may claim custodianship over an institution whose defining purpose is worship of the One God.

A mosque is not sacred merely because it has walls, a roof, or a respected history. Its identity comes from the worship for which it exists. If a person publicly denies that foundation while claiming authority over the house, the contradiction becomes institutional. The verse addresses that contradiction. It does not deny that non-Muslims may encounter Muslims, ask questions, or receive protection. Verse 6 has already required an audience and safe escort. It distinguishes access to a place from control over its religious purpose.

**“Bearing Witness Against Themselves”**

The verse describes the polytheists as bearing witness against themselves through disbelief. Their public creed is evidence that they do not affirm the One whose house they seek to maintain. This does not mean that every act of charity or physical repair has no worldly benefit. A person may repair a building and make it safer. The verse concerns the religious status and ultimate meaning of the deed when the foundation of worship is rejected.

The Qur'an often says that deeds can come to nothing when their basis is disbelief or when they are directed to other than Allah. In 39:65, association is described as causing deeds to be nullified. The point is not that Allah fails to see the labor. It is that labor performed as a substitute for faith cannot create the spiritual standing the worker imagines. External service cannot repair an inward denial of the One to whom the service is supposed to be offered.

**The Historical Transition of the Sacred Mosque**

The immediate setting involved the end of pagan pilgrimage practices around the Sacred Mosque after the conquest of Makkah. The old custodial order had connected the sanctuary with tribal prestige, pilgrimage revenue, and inherited offices. Islam did not simply measure the holiness of those offices by the fact that they were old. It asked whether the institution served tawḥīd or protected a religious economy built around idols.

This transition did not require the destruction of every family who had held an office. The Prophet returned the key of the Kaʿbah to ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah, according to the well-known reports, while redefining the purpose and authority of the sanctuary. The example clarifies verse 17. The issue is not personal humiliation of a former custodian. It is the transfer of religious legitimacy from inherited prestige to faithful service of Allah.

**Maintenance Is More Than Money**

The verse's language includes more than construction funds. A person maintains a mosque by shaping its teaching, deciding who is welcomed, preserving prayer, and protecting the institution from being used for another purpose. Someone may donate generously yet turn the mosque into a platform for factional influence. Another may sweep its floor, teach Qur'an, or keep its doors open with no public recognition. The verse asks what kind of maintenance is being performed and for whose sake.

This is why the next verse lists belief, prayer, alms-tax, and fear of Allah as qualifications for true maintenance. Verse 17 establishes the negative boundary; verse 18 explains the positive character. The two verses should be read together. A building cannot be judged only by its donor's name or its architectural beauty. Its maintenance must preserve the worship, justice, and God-consciousness that make it a mosque.

**Deeds and the Hereafter**

The statement that their deeds are void and that they will remain in the Fire is an ultimate judgment tied to open disbelief. Believers should not casually apply this verdict to people whose inward state they do not know. The verse addresses a public condition and leaves the final account to Allah. Its severity warns that religious service is not a substitute for worship, and that public management of sacred things cannot conceal a settled denial of their Lord.

This warning also applies to believers who treat institutional service as a shield from accountability. A Muslim committee member may maintain a building while betraying its trust. A donor may fund a mosque while using the gift to control the imam. The verse's broader logic asks whether the work truly serves Allah. Religious form does not automatically make a deed sound.

**The Mosque as a Measure of Community Identity**

For modern communities, verse 17 should be used as a criterion, not as a weapon against ordinary visitors. A mosque can welcome a seeker, protect a neighbor, and offer the truth without handing over its religious definition to someone who rejects it. Hospitality and custodianship are different responsibilities. The first may be extended widely; the second must preserve the purpose of the institution.

The verse finally turns the believer's attention from buildings to foundations. Who decides what is taught? What values govern the budget? Are the vulnerable protected, and is prayer central? A mosque maintained for reputation, faction, or profit may look impressive while losing its meaning. The house of Allah is maintained properly when its people confess the One to whom the house belongs and let that confession govern the institution's life. Without that confession, maintenance has preserved a shell but not the sanctuary's meaning. The building remains, but its central act of worship has been displaced.
