## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:166

> leaving the wives that your Lord has created for you? In fact, you are a transgressing people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Identifies What Is Being Abandoned**

Lot continues by pointing to the wives God has created for the people. His argument is not a statement that women exist merely to satisfy men. It is a reminder that God created a lawful marital path for sexual intimacy, family life, and mutual responsibility, yet the community is turning away from that path toward what revelation forbids.

The verb leaving exposes deliberate refusal. The people are not being described as lacking any lawful relation or as unable to understand the created order. They are choosing to abandon an authorized channel while pursuing another. Lot asks them to see the moral direction of that choice rather than treating it as a neutral preference.


Speaking of wives as created by the Lord does not reduce women to instruments. A marriage governed by God includes rights, duties, affection, protection, and accountability. The lawful path cannot be used to excuse coercion, betrayal, or abuse. Lot's contrast is about the moral framework of sexuality, not a permission for men to treat women as possessions.

A person who invokes marriage while violating a spouse's dignity has not honored the created order. The same God who establishes lawful intimacy commands justice. Moral teaching is coherent only when it protects the human beings within the institution it praises.


Exegetes have discussed the scope of the two verses. Some read the first line as a direct condemnation of male-male sexual acts and the second as a statement that the men avoid women; others consider whether the wording includes forms of the practice involving both men and women. The immediate contrast with wives gives the passage its central direction: the people have rejected the lawful marital path in favor of a forbidden one.

That contextual reading is stronger for this verse because Lot's question is structured around what the people leave and what they pursue. The point is not to settle every possible detail of their private conduct. It is to expose a community's deliberate reversal of the sexual order revealed by God.

**The People Are Called Transgressors**

Lot does not describe the conduct as an innocent variation that becomes right through social approval. He calls the people transgressors, meaning that they have crossed a boundary. The term places their behavior within a larger pattern of refusing God's limits, not merely within a debate over taste.

Transgression also includes the social harms described in the parallel account. Quran 29:29 associates the people with abuse of travelers and public immorality alongside their sexual wrongdoing. That context shows why the community's condition is not a private matter detached from justice. A people that celebrates desire while violating others has crossed several boundaries at once.


Calling a people transgressors does not erase their human status. The warning leaves room for repentance, and the messenger's concern shows that he wants them rescued rather than humiliated. A believer can say that an act is forbidden while refusing to mock, assault, expose, or dehumanize the person who commits it.

This discipline is especially important when a society is polarized. Moral truth should not be weakened to avoid offense, but the delivery of truth must not become an excuse for cruelty. Lot's sentence is severe because the matter is severe, yet its purpose is to stop the community and return it to God.


The mention of wives can also remind the reader that lawful sexuality is not only about a permitted object. Marriage carries obligations toward another person. It requires consent, faithfulness, care, and responsibility for consequences. A person cannot claim to honor God's design while using a spouse for appetite and neglecting her rights.

That broader responsibility keeps the verse from being reduced to a slogan. The lawful path is morally meaningful because it is ordered, relational, and accountable. Lot challenges the people to leave transgression, not simply to exchange one form of selfishness for another.

**The Community Has Made Reversal a Public Identity**

Lot addresses a people, not merely an isolated offender, because the conduct has been woven into its public life. The community's identity is being built around what it refuses to restrain. When wrongdoing becomes a badge of belonging, the pressure on individuals grows and the need for a prophetic warning becomes more urgent.

A person who recognizes the wrong but fears the crowd may need protection and patient counsel. A leader who celebrates the wrong bears a different responsibility. Divine judgment distinguishes among people, but the collective culture still matters because it can make repentance difficult and transgression attractive.

**The Verse Connects Desire to Accountability**

Desire is not self-justifying. The Lord who created the body also sets its boundaries and knows what will preserve dignity and life. Leaving God's guidance for an appetite does not create freedom; it places the person under the appetite's rule and under the judgment of the One whose command was rejected.

Lot's words therefore call for a return to ordered freedom. The people can repent, restore justice, and refuse to let public approval define good and evil. The warning is not meant to produce despair. It is meant to make transgression visible before the consequence becomes unavoidable.


A reader should not soften the verse into a claim that nothing is forbidden. Nor should he use the verse as a warrant for personal abuse. The text calls the practice transgression and calls the people back to God; it does not transfer divine judgment to every angry individual.

The faithful response is principled restraint: uphold the revealed boundary, protect people from harm, avoid slander, and leave repentance open. Lot's warning is strongest when it is carried with the same moral seriousness it demands from the people—submission to God rather than indulgence of one's own anger.
