## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:36

> When she delivered, she said, “My Lord! I have given birth to a girl,”—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered—“and the male is not like the female. I have named her Mary, and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Words of a Mother Meeting Her Child**

The verse reports the delivery and then the first thing the mother said. Her sentence is not a statement of doctrine or a formula of thanks; it is the observation of a woman holding a child and telling God what she is holding. The Book preserves it in the plainest possible form, without softening the disappointment that runs beneath it.

The honesty of the record is worth pausing over. A lesser narrative would have skipped from the vow to the fulfilment and left out the mother's difficulty. The sūrah keeps the difficulty in the text, and everything that follows in the chapter — a girl raised in the sanctuary, a prophet born of her — is set against the background of a woman who had expected something else.

**"And Allah Fully Knew What She Had Delivered"**

Into the middle of the mother's sentence the Book inserts an aside: God knew perfectly well what she had delivered. The clause is not information for the reader; it prevents a misunderstanding that the mother's words might otherwise create.

A person speaking to God in this way might seem to be telling Him something He does not know. The aside removes that possibility and, at the same time, gives the mother's remark its true character. She is not informing God of her child's sex. She is speaking as a human being who had planned on a son, and the sentence is closer to a sigh than to a report. The Book records the sigh and answers it, which is how it treats the prayers of ordinary people throughout this narrative.

**"The Male Is Not Like the Female"**

The phrase has been read in two ways, and both are defensible. On one reading it belongs to the mother's speech: a daughter cannot serve the sanctuary as a son would have, so she cannot fulfil the vow as it was imagined. On the other, the phrase belongs to God's own comment on her words, and it states a difference of sexes that the Creator placed in the design.

The commentators who took the second view discussed what the difference involves in practice. They mentioned physical strength and endurance, which mattered for the work of the sanctuary, and they mentioned ritual purity, which may have limited the service a woman could perform in the Temple. Al-Qurṭubī noticed a third possibility, that the sentence may hint at the elevation of the child rather than her limitation: the mother had wanted a boy, and God gave her a girl who would bear and manifest His knowledge in a way no boy of that household did. The narrative's own outcome supports the last reading.

**"I Have Named Her Mary"**

Naming a child is an act of claiming, and the mother's sentence does it in the middle of her prayer. The name given, Maryam, is the one the Book uses for her throughout, and it is the name by which the sūrah's readers know the household's story.

Naming also fixes a fact about the future. Everything the child will become is spoken for in advance by a woman who does not yet know it, and the Book's practice of naming key figures early — John, Jesus, and here Mary — connects her to a pattern in which the name arrives before the person's role does. The mother had promised a servant to the temple; she now claims a daughter, by name, as the one she is placing there.

**"I Seek Your Protection for Her and Her Offspring From Satan"**

With the child named, the mother asks for protection, and she extends the request beyond her daughter to her descendants. The Arabic verb is the ordinary one for seeking refuge, and the Book commands the practice elsewhere: when you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge in God from the accursed Satan (16:98), and when a provocation from Satan comes, seek refuge in God (7:200; 41:36).

The adjective applied to Satan, "the accursed", is literally the stoned one. The Book's picture of him is of a being driven away and pelted, and the pilgrimage rite in which pilgrims throw stones at the pillars representing him keeps the image in physical use. The mother's request, then, is for a hedge around her child and her child's descendants against the being who is everywhere expelled and still active. She has no means of building that hedge herself, so she asks the one who can.

**A Prayer the Prophet Confirmed by Reciting It**

The request in this verse is the earliest recorded act of protection given to Mary, and the Prophet's own tradition answers it. Abū Hurayrah reports that the Prophet said: *There is none born among the offspring of Adam but Satan touches it, and a child cries loudly at the time of birth because of the touch of Satan, except Mary and her child* — and then Abū Hurayrah recited this very verse with its prayer for protection (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3431).

The report is placed in the Book of the Prophets and it does a specific thing: it treats the mother's supplication as effective. What a woman asked for at the moment of her daughter's birth is presented as granted, and the grant is memorable enough that the Prophet recited the verse in connection with it. The result is a quiet correction of the mother's own sense that she had brought the wrong child. The request she made in her disappointment was answered in full, and the child is named as the exception to a rule that applies to every human being born.

**What the Prayer Actually Secured**

Read with the rest of the sūrah, the protection asked for here covers more than the years of Mary's childhood. The offspring mentioned in the prayer includes the child she will bear without a father, and the sūrah will narrate the hostility that child's mission meets.

The mother's request therefore reaches to the far end of the story, and the Book lets her ask it while knowing nothing about what is coming. That is the shape of prayer in this passage: a person asks for what she can see, and the answer spans more than she could have imagined, so that the record of a private worry in a household becomes the opening of a story that ends with a prophet of God.
