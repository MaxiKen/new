## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:198

> But those who are mindful of their Lord will be in Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever—as an accommodation from Allah. And what is with Allah is best for the virtuous.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Contrast Stated**

The verse begins with a turn that sets the destiny of the mindful against the destination described in the sentence before it. Those who revere their Lord are promised gardens with rivers flowing beneath them, and the promise is given in the same terms the sūrah has used earlier for the reward of the righteous.

The sūrah has been contrasting the two groups from the beginning of its closing movement, and this sentence completes the comparison. The turn at the start of the verse is the Book's familiar way of placing two outcomes side by side. What the wrongdoers receive is named in the previous verse, and what those who revere God receive is named here.

**The Gardens Described**

The gardens are described through the rivers that run beneath them, which is the Book's most frequent image for the reward. The phrase recalls the promise made earlier in the sūrah to those who are mindful, and it recalls the promise made to the believers and the doers of good in the second sūrah, where the same rivers are named.

The community that hears the description is being reminded of what it has been promised. The image is used consistently across the Book, and the repetition of the same words ties the rewards promised in different passages to the same reality. It conveys both the abundance of what is given and the permanence of the home in which it is enjoyed.

**The Word for Welcome**

The verse describes what is given as a welcome from God, and the Arabic word behind the translation names what a host prepares for a guest who arrives. Al-Rāzī explained it in that sense, and the Book uses the same term for the reward in other passages, including the descriptions of the gardens prepared for those who are mindful.

The image presents the reward as hospitality rather than as payment. What awaits the believers is prepared for them by their Lord, and the word chosen for it places the whole scene within the relation between a generous host and the guests he has invited.


The sentence states that the people described will remain in the gardens forever, and the promise of permanence distinguishes the reward from everything the previous verses said about the present life. What the deniers enjoy is measured in a little time, and what the mindful are given is described as abiding.

The Book makes the same point when it contrasts the passing world with what remains with God. The gardens are described as the home of those who are mindful, and the term for their stay removes the reward from the category of things that end.

**The Statement About What Is With God**

The verse closes with the declaration that what is with God is better for the pious, and the sentence returns to the comparison with which the passage began. The Book has used the same phrase in its descriptions of the reward, and it uses it here to redirect attention from the description of the gardens to their source.

The declaration also functioned in the community's teaching as a reminder that the promise is not exhausted by the images used to convey it. What is with God is described as better, and the sentence leaves the reward open to a reality that the language of gardens and rivers can only point toward.

**The Book's Parallel Descriptions**

The Book describes the reward of the mindful in similar terms in several passages. One promises gardens beneath which rivers flow to those who believe and do good, with the promise that they will remain there forever. Another describes the reward of those who are in awe of their Lord as gardens of eternity with rivers beneath them, and states that God is pleased with them and they with Him.

A third passage, addressed to those who persevered, records the greeting of peace and the exclamation about the excellence of the final home. The verse before the reader belongs to that family of descriptions, and the community's recitation of them together gave the promise its full shape.

**The Meaning of Reverence Here**

The people described in the verse are those who revere their Lord, and the quality named is the one the sūrah has been recommending from its opening lines. It is the disposition that the earlier verses of the sūrah connected with guidance, and it appears again in the passage about patience and forgiveness.

The verse therefore identifies the reward not as the payment of a due but as the gift prepared for a particular condition of heart. The sūrah's closing movement has been describing that condition through the prayers and the conduct of the people who possess it, and the sentence before the reader names their destination. The reward is attached to the disposition rather than to any external mark.

**The Comparison at the Sūrah's Close**

The verse stands near the end of the sūrah, and its placement gives the two destinations a final shape. The message of the sūrah has been carried through the community's history, its failures, its obligations, and its arguments, and the last verses gather the outcome into two sentences.

The community is left with the contrast between a brief enjoyment followed by the Fire and an abiding welcome from God. The last verse of the sūrah turns to the community's own duty, so the comparison here serves as the ground of the instruction that follows. The sentence before the reader states the second half of that contrast, and the verses that remain address the community directly.
