## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:117

> The good they do in this worldly life is like the harvest of an evil people struck by a bitter wind, destroying it ˹completely˺. Allah never wronged them, but they wronged themselves.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable About Outlay Without Return**

The verse compares what such people spend in this life to a crop destroyed by a bitter wind, and it ends by stating that God did not wrong them and that they wronged themselves. The image is agricultural, and its subject is labour that produces nothing.

The sentence is addressed to people whose generosity was part of their standing, and the parable does not deny that the labour occurred. A harvest is the result of work, and the verse describes the destruction of something that had grown. What it denies is the survival of the result, and the denial is stated with an image that any farmer in the audience would have recognised.

**Wind in the Book's Picture of Effort**

The Book uses wind in several passages to describe the fate of deeds, and the variations on the image carry different emphases. One passage describes a garden with palms, vines, and every kind of fruit, whose owner grows old with young dependents while a fire-laden whirlwind strikes and burns the whole of it (2:266). Another compares the deeds of those who disbelieve to ashes blown away by a fierce wind on a stormy day, so that nothing they earned remains available to them (14:18).

The same Book describes wind elsewhere as the bearer of good news ahead of rain and as a means by which provision reaches people. The two uses belong to a single doctrine: the wind does not discriminate, and what it destroys is destroyed because of where it blew, not because wind is cruel.

**Which Good Deeds Are in View**

The spending named in the verse is the kind that people perform for reputation, obligation, or the maintenance of standing, and the commentators understood the image as applying to such outlays.

The description does not make the acts invisible. It says that their fruit does not reach the place where fruit matters, which is a statement about the relation of the deed to its giver rather than about the deed's size. The Book's treatment of sincere and insincere giving elsewhere takes the same line, and the account of the sūrah in its opening verses, where the disbelievers' wealth is denied any benefit at all, is the same doctrine in another form.

**"A People Who Wronged Themselves"**

The crop in the parable belongs to people who wronged themselves, and the destruction is attached to that condition. The image therefore places the cause inside the owners rather than in the weather.

That is the point the explanation in the same verse makes explicit. A person's losses are not arbitrary, and the Book allows no doctrine of a blind fate; the wind that destroys the harvest is the wind that blows on a field whose people are described as wrongdoers. The Qur'an's parables are generally constructed this way, with the moral stated inside the picture rather than added afterwards.

**The Denial of Divine Injustice**

The verse states directly that God did not wrong them, and it is one of several passages in this sūrah that insist on the point. The sentence before the reader is the same in substance as the statement that God desires no injustice for His creation.

The insistence is needed because of the verse's subject. A person who has worked hard and lost everything is the readiest audience for the idea that he was treated unfairly, and the Book's answer is that what was lost had already been forfeited by the state of the one who planted. The denial of injustice is therefore an explanation rather than a defense: it tells the reader where to look for the cause.

**"They Wronged Themselves"**

The phrase that people wrong themselves runs through the Book, and the sūrah uses it again in the passage about those who remember God after wrongdoing and ask forgiveness. In every case the wrong is described as directed at the self, which is the Book's way of saying that the primary victim of a person's misconduct is his own account.

The formula fits the parable exactly. The field was the owner's, the labour was his, and the wind took what he had. Nothing in the picture suggests that anyone else was deprived, and the sentence about the wrong being self-directed is the explanation of why the destruction is described in those terms rather than as a punishment imposed from outside.

**The Limit of the Parable**

The parable concerns the fate of what a person spends in this present life, and the Book's statements about the reward of the Hereafter are not contradicted by it. The verses immediately before this one promised that the good done by the upright will not be denied, and the two statements stand together in the same passage.

The difference between them is the state of the person who acts. The Book's consistent position is that deeds are weighed with the giver, so that an identical act performed from faith and from display has different results. The parable states one side of that position, and the promise about the upright states the other, and the reader is expected to hold both.

**Why the Verse Follows the Previous One**

The verse before this one denied that wealth and children can stand between a person and God. This verse takes the next step and describes what happens to what such people give away.

The order dismantles the securities in sequence. First the possessions that a person keeps, then the resources he parts with, and in each case the outcome is stated without reference to the amount. The reader who follows the two verses is left with the conclusion the passage is building: what saves a person is not the size of what he holds or the extent of what he gives, but the state in which he stands before his Lord. The verses that follow turn from the general case to specific conduct within the community.
