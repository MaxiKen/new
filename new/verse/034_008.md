## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:8

> Has he fabricated a lie against Allah or is he insane?” In fact, those who do not believe in the Hereafter are bound for torment and have strayed farthest ˹from the truth˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Charges Offered as a Closed Choice**

The question is framed as a disjunction with exactly two branches: either he invented this deliberately, or he is out of his mind. Arabic marks the alternatives with *am*, the particle used for a genuine either-or, so the speakers are presenting their audience with what looks like a complete set of options. It is not complete, and the missing third branch is the one the whole sūrah exists to argue for: that he is telling the truth. This is the oldest debating trick there is — narrow the field before anyone notices, then let the audience choose freely between two answers that both convict. The charges themselves are shrewdly picked because they are opposites. Fabrication requires full possession of one's faculties; madness rules it out. A man cannot be both a calculating forger and a helpless lunatic, which means the accusers do not actually hold a position. They hold a demand: any explanation but the obvious one.

**Fabrication: The Charge That Concedes the Most**

*Iftarā* means to cut out and stitch together, hence to invent a story — the same verb behind *iftirāʾ*, a manufactured accusation. Calling the message a fabrication concedes a great deal. It admits the Qur'an is coherent enough to have been composed, forceful enough to be worth composing, and effective enough to be worth opposing. The Book records this accusation often: *"You ˹Muḥammad˺ are just a fabricator"* (16:101), and *"This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others"* (25:4). Sabaʾ itself will hear it again later from the same quarter (34:43). What makes the charge implausible on its own terms is motive. A forger forges for gain, and the sūrah eventually puts that test to the accusers directly by having the Prophet renounce any payment: *"If I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it"* (34:47). Note too what the charge is against: a lie *against Allah*. Even the accusers assume the seriousness of speaking falsely in God's name, which means they share more theology with their opponent than they admit.

**Madness: The Cheaper Charge, and the Older One**

*Jinnah* is the second accusation, and the word comes from the root *j-n-n*, to cover or conceal — the same root that gives *jinn*, the hidden beings, and *janīn*, the covered thing in the womb. To be *majnūn* in Arabic usage is to have one's reason veiled, classically by contact with the jinn. This is not an accusation of illness in the modern clinical sense but of possession: the man is speaking, but something else is doing the speaking. The charge follows the prophets everywhere in the Qur'an. Al-Ḥijr: *"O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane!"* (15:6). Al-Ṣāffāt reports the Meccans asking whether they should abandon their gods *"for a mad poet"* (37:36). Al-Dukhān records it of Pharaoh's court toward Moses: *"A madman, taught by others!"* (44:14). The denial is issued plainly in al-Qalam: *"By the grace of your Lord, you ˹O Prophet˺ are not insane"* (68:2). And this sūrah will not leave it standing either; it answers the charge with a procedure rather than an assertion, telling the accusers to stand apart, singly and in pairs, and think (34:46).

**The Verse Turns and Answers in Its Own Voice**

Halfway through, the quotation stops and the sūrah speaks: *bal* — in fact, on the contrary. This particle is the hinge. It does not engage the disjunction on its own terms, does not defend the Prophet's honesty or his sanity point by point. It redirects the whole question to the accusers and diagnoses them instead. The move is not evasion; it is a claim about where the real disorder lies. Men who describe careful speech as madness and a consistent message as a forgery have a problem of judgment, and judgment is what the rest of the verse addresses. The rhetorical effect of the abrupt switch is considerable. A reader following the taunt is carried along by its confidence, and then the ground moves: the people doing the diagnosing are the ones being diagnosed.

**Disbelief in the Hereafter Named as the Root Cause**

The verse does not identify the accusers as those who rejected the Prophet, or those who defended the idols. It identifies them as *those who do not believe in the Hereafter*. That is a diagnosis, and it explains the behaviour recorded in the previous verse. A man certain there is no reckoning has no reason to examine a warning carefully, because nothing turns on getting it right; ridicule costs him nothing and buys him social standing. The whole architecture of the accusation depends on that certainty. Sabaʾ has been building toward this identification since its opening exchange, where the same people announced that the Hour would never reach them (34:3). Here the sūrah connects that denial to its consequences in character. Disbelief in the Hereafter is not treated as one opinion among several but as the hinge on which a person's seriousness turns.

**"Strayed Farthest" — The Sentence Passed on the Judges**

The closing phrase is *al-ḍalāl al-baʿīd*, error at a far distance, translated here as having *strayed farthest*. Arabic error-words are spatial, and this one keeps its geography: the further from the road, the harder the return. But the verse says two things, not one: they are bound for torment **and** they have strayed far. Are these separate penalties or the same thing twice? The stronger reading takes them as sequential and linked — the punishment to come is the consequence, the straying is the present condition. Yet there is a way of hearing the second clause as already a punishment in itself. To be so far off course that a truthful man looks insane to you is a state worth pitying, whatever waits afterwards. The verse leaves both readings open by simply setting the two clauses side by side with a conjunction. What it does not leave open is the direction of the judgment. The accusers opened with two options about the Prophet; the verse closes with one verdict about them.
