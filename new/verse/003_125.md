## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:125

> Most certainly, if you ˹believers˺ are firm and mindful ˹of Allah˺ and the enemy launches a sudden attack on you, Allah will reinforce you with five thousand angels designated ˹for battle˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise With Its Condition Attached**

The verse states that if the believers hold firm and remain mindful of God, and the enemy attacks them suddenly, God will reinforce them with five thousand angels sent with distinguishing marks. The condition is stated before the promise, and the order is the verse's teaching.

The condition has two parts: patient firmness and awareness of God. Both have already appeared in this passage, and both belong to the community's own conduct rather than to anything that can be known about the enemy. What the verse does is attach the promised help to a state that lies within the believers' control.

**The Increase in the Number**

The previous verse recalled the promise of three thousand angels, and this one speaks of five thousand. The increase answers the added condition, and it answers the situation the verse describes, which is an assault launched without warning.

The commentators discussed the word describing the angels, which is usually rendered as marked or designated. Some explained it as describing horses with distinctive marks; others took it as a reference to the angels themselves being distinguished from others. The point of the word, on any reading, is that the reinforcement is a specific and prepared force rather than a general expression of support.

**Sudden Attack as the Scenario**

The verse specifies that the enemy comes upon the believers suddenly, which is the situation a community most fears. An attack that can be seen coming can be prepared for, and a surprise attack removes the time for preparation and for consultation.

The mention of that scenario makes the promise applicable to the moment when a community has nothing left but its own state. Purity of intention and firmness of purpose cannot be improvised, and they are precisely what the verse requires of the believers at the moment when nothing else is available. The Book's instructions are attached to that moment because it is the moment that reveals what a person carries.

**What the Marked Angels Stand For**

The Book's descriptions of angelic support in this passage are not given as military intelligence. They are given so that a community facing a larger force understands the scale of the power that stands behind the promise made to it.

That is why the numbers grow as the conditions become harder. The message is not that five thousand is a sufficient force for a particular engagement; it is that the resources available to God are shown to be beyond anything the community could estimate. The sūrah states the purpose of the promise explicitly in the verses that follow, where it makes clear that the promise was given for the sake of the believers' hearts.

**The Condition Repeated From the Passage**

Patient firmness and awareness of God are the two qualities this passage has asked of the community more than once. They appear in the command that the believers be mindful of God as He deserves, in the promise that patience and mindfulness prevent harm, and here in the condition attached to the promise of aid.

The repetition is the passage's method of teaching. A quality that a community must possess in a moment of sudden danger has to be habitual beforehand, and the Book states it at every point in the narrative where the need for it appears. By the time the reader reaches the account of the battle itself, he has been told three times what the community needed to bring to it.

**Angels and the Source of the Outcome**

The verse speaks about angels as agents, and the passage does not allow the reader to conclude that they fight in place of the believers. The Book's accounts of such aid consistently place the source of the outcome in God and present the aid as encouragement.

The two statements protect each other. A community told about the aid could begin to expect victory without effort; a community told only about its own duty could forget that the outcome is not its own to determine. The sūrah holds both in place, and the verses that follow state the arrangement in a single sentence.

**The Purpose of the Passage's Promises**

The promises of reinforcement in these verses belong to a narrative whose ending the community already knows. The sūrah is recounting a battle that ended in losses, and it places promises of angelic support inside that story.

The effect is to direct attention away from the arithmetic of the conflict and toward the state of the people involved. Promised help that does not prevent a defeat tells the reader that the help was real and that the conditions for it were unmet, and the sūrah's account of what went wrong on the day is given in the verses that follow. The reader is being prepared to examine the community's conduct rather than the balance of forces.

**What the Word for Marked Angels Carries**

The word describing the angels comes from a root that means to mark or distinguish, and commentators explained it in two connected ways. Some took it to describe angels bearing signs by which they were known; others mentioned horses with distinctive markings, since the word is used of animals marked for identification.

Both explanations agree on the practical sense: the force promised in the verse is described as prepared and identifiable rather than as an indistinct multitude. The Book's account of the earlier promise speaks of angels sent in succession, and the two descriptions together convey a body of aid that arrives in order. The precision of the language is part of the encouragement, since a vague promise of help does very little for someone facing a surprise attack.
