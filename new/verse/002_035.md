## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:35

> We cautioned, “O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Charter of Four Clauses**

The address to Adam is a residence charter, and its proportions are the first thing to notice. Four elements: a dwelling — *live, you and your wife, in Paradise*; a company — the wife named inside the address; an open permission — *eat as freely as you please*, unrestricted as to place and kind; and a single prohibition — *do not approach this tree*. The ratio is deliberate and it is the moral shape of life as the Qur'an will legislate it for Adam's children: a vast field of the permitted crossed by one thin line of the forbidden. Readers of later law recognize the pattern — the ḥalāl comprehensive, the ḥarām minimal — and its origin is this verse. The permission's generosity is also strategic: the prohibition will be tested against a background of abundance, so that the transgression cannot plead necessity. Nothing about the tree is needed; everything else is given. The trial is therefore not of appetite cornered but of will indulged — the hardest kind, and the fairest.

**“Do Not Approach”: A Boundary With a Margin**

The prohibition's verb is *lā taqrabā* — do not *approach* — and the choice of verb over "do not eat" builds a safety margin into the command. The forbidden act lies at the end of a path; the prohibition begins at the path's entrance. Islamic ethics drew the principle directly from this grammar: guard the haram by guarding its approaches — the glance before the look, the meeting before the act, the conversation before the commitment. The margin also sharpens the coming failure. When the tree is eaten, the couple did not merely cross a line but traversed a whole protected distance to reach it, which is why the temptation narrative in Ṭā Hā dwells on the approach — the whisper that walked them toward the tree step by step. A command framed as *do not approach* makes every deliberate nearness a partial transgression, and it teaches the reader of the verse that moral boundaries in this Book are defended in depth, not painted at the edge of the cliff.

**Which Tree? A Name the Qur'an Withholds**

The exegetical literature is full of candidates — wheat, the grapevine, the fig — and the Qur'an itself never names the tree, using only *shajarah*, a word broad enough for any tree, shrub, or woody growth. The commentators are candid about where the candidates come from: Jewish and Christian oral traditions, and perhaps Arabian lore, filtered into the tafsīr collections, much of it weakly attested in ḥadīth and some of it, as al-Ṭabarī's own material shows, no better than folklore. The withholding is a lesson in exegetical method that al-Rāzī states as a rule for the whole story: certain details are neither knowable from the text nor needed by it — *one should cease and refrain from being categorical, and God knows best*. What the narrative requires is not the tree's species but its function: one object, arbitrarily chosen by the Commander, made the visible form of obedience. A named species would invite arguments about fruit; an unnamed tree keeps the test where it belongs — on the command, not the commodity.

**Which Garden? The Dispute and Its Weighing**

A larger question divides the commentators: is this the Garden of Reward promised to believers, or a garden of trial prepared elsewhere? The consensus holds the first — it is *al-Jannah*, the definite, well-known Paradise — and answers the objections: Iblīs may have whispered from outside its bounds, and the promise that its dwellers are never expelled (15:48 — *"nor will they ever be asked to leave"* (15:48)) applies to those admitted as reward after the earthly test. The minority position, al-Rāzī reports and presses, notes real difficulties: Satan's lie inside a garden where *"never to hear any idle talk or lying therein"* is promised (78:35); the tempter's offer of a *"Tree of Immortality and a kingdom that does not fade away"* (20:120), which would tempt no one already immortal; and the descent-verb *ihbiṭ*, used in this sūrah for ordinary travel (2:61) without implying vertical fall. Al-Rāzī's verdict is his method again: the proofs on both sides are weak and contradictory, so certainty is withheld — and the story's meaning survives either answer intact.

**The Wife Addressed Inside the Command**

The Qur'an does not name her in this verse or any other; she is *your wife*, and the traditions supply the name Ḥawwāʾ and the details of her creation. What the verse's grammar does supply is joint address: the command is spoken over both of them, the permission belongs to both, and the prohibition binds both. This is a structural departure from the Genesis account's sequencing, and its consequences run through the Quranic story. The temptation in Ṭā Hā is directed at Adam by name (20:120); the eating is joint; the confession is joint and in the dual and plural — *"Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers"* (7:23). No woman is singled out as the entry-point of sin, no man as its victim; the responsibility is shared in the same sentence that shares the pardon. The unnamed wife of this verse is, in the Qur'an's moral universe, a co-addressed moral agent — present in the command, present in the slip, present in the received words of verse 37.

**The Warning Label Stated Before the Trial**

The charter closes with its consequence announced in advance: *or else you will be wrongdoers* — *fa-takūnā mina al-ẓālimīn*. The warning does three things. It makes the coming failure informed: the couple will eat knowing what the eating classifies them as, which removes entrapment from the story entirely. It supplies the vocabulary of the aftermath — *ẓulm*, wronging, the word their own confession will use in al-Aʿrāf: *we have wronged ourselves*. And it defines the sin's essence before the act: approaching the tree is not primarily rule-breaking but self-wronging, a damage whose first victim is the doer. The verse thus installs the theology the whole Adamic cycle will demonstrate — a broad permission, one marked line, a stated consequence, an enemy on the ground, and two free agents who will shortly discover that the only thing the tree could take from them was themselves. The next verse records how the enemy worked the space this charter left open.
