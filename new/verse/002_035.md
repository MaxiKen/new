## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:35

> We cautioned, “O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Permission With One Exception**

The command given to Adam is framed as an invitation. He is told to live in the Garden with his wife and to eat from it freely, and the Arabic verb for that freedom is the strongest available for unrestricted enjoyment. The one limit is attached afterwards, in a short clause, and the sentence therefore opens with generosity and closes with a boundary.

That structure is how the Book introduces the moral life of humanity. The Garden is not presented as a place of scarcity regulated by anxious rules; it is a place of abundance with a single prohibition, and the prohibition is stated in the plainest terms. The very shape of the sentence — a wide permission followed by a narrow limit — tells the reader something about the relationship being established.

**The Garden as the Book Describes It**

This same Garden is described elsewhere in terms of what is absent from it: *“Indeed, you will neither go hungry nor naked there, / nor will you ˹ever˺ suffer from thirst or ˹the heat of˺ the sun.”* (20:118–119). Another telling of the same scene uses nearly the identical wording for the command that is quoted here: *“O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat from wherever you please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be wrongdoers.”* (7:19).

The Book's descriptions of the place are about sufficiency rather than luxury. Food, clothing, water and shade are the four goods named, and they are the four a desert community would count as the whole of security. Adam is placed in a condition where the basic problem of living has been solved, which is precisely what makes the one prohibition the only thing left to test him.

**"Do Not Approach," Not Merely "Do Not Eat"**

The wording of the prohibition is preventive. Adam and his wife are not told to abstain from the fruit; they are told not to come near the tree, and the same technique appears throughout the Qur'an when the Book wants to close off a road rather than punish a step. Islamic law later made the same move a principle, the blocking of means to a prohibited end.

The effect on the reader is to make the boundary generous rather than restrictive. A limit that forbids only the final act leaves the actor free to approach, look, and deliberate; a limit that forbids proximity removes the entire space in which the act becomes imaginable. The Book records the command at the outer edge so that the test is placed at a distance from the crime, and the failure described in the next verse then reads as a deliberate journey toward it rather than a slip.

**The Tree the Book Never Names**

The Qur'an does not say what kind of tree it was, and the commentators filled the silence with guesses drawn from a wide range of sources: grain, grapevine, fig, and others. Al-Ṭabarī registered a number of these reports, and the material varies enough in quality that modern scholarship treats much of it as folklore rather than as transmitted teaching.

The Book's silence on the point is consistent with how it handles the story's other details. It does not name the tree, does not describe the form Iblīs took, and does not give the conversation that led to the fall much stagecraft. What interests the Qur'an is the structure of the event — a command, a whisper, a choice, a consequence — and the details left out are the ones that would satisfy curiosity without changing the lesson. Readers who supply them are adding to a story the Book deliberately left spare.

**"Or You Will Be Wrongdoers"**

The consequence attached to the prohibition names a category the sūrah has already used several times: those who wrong. The Arabic term describes the person who places something where it does not belong, and the Book applies it to a wide range of acts, from the rejection of revelation to the refusal of a command to the corrupting of a community.

What is notable about its use here is that the wrongdoer would be the one wronged. Adam is not told that he will injure God or damage the Garden; he is told that he will place himself outside the condition he was given. The sūrah will state the principle in a later verse: God does not wrong human beings in the least; they wrong themselves. The prohibition in the Garden is the first instance of that pattern in the Book's account of the human story.

**Freedom and Its Boundaries in the Same Breath**

The verse gives the human being a home, a companion, and an unlimited table, and it sets one boundary across them. Nothing in the sentence suggests that the freedom was reluctant or the limit arbitrary; the two are stated at the same moment, which is how the Book treats them everywhere. Obligation in the Qur'an is not a tax levied on liberty but the shape liberty takes, and the scene in the Garden establishes that relationship before the human being does anything at all.

Read beside the appointment of a deputy in the previous scene, the verse describes what that office looks like in practice. The deputy receives what he is given, acts within the limits set for him, and answers for the difference. The test that follows is small in appearance — one tree out of a whole Garden — and the sūrah treats it as the first exercise of the responsibility that was announced in heaven.
