## Sūrah an-Naml 27:47

> They replied, “You and your followers are a bad omen for us.” He responded, “Your omens are destined by Allah. In fact, you are ˹only˺ a people being tested.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Reply That Changes the Subject**

Ṣāliḥ asked why they were hurrying toward punishment when forgiveness was available. Their answer addresses none of it. They tell him he is bad luck.

The evasion is total and it is the most revealing thing they say. A counter-argument would have engaged the question; an accusation of ill omen relocates the discussion entirely, from what they should do to what he is. It also converts a demand into a grievance. Once he and his followers are classified as a source of misfortune, everything that has gone wrong in the community since he started speaking becomes his doing, and the community becomes the injured party. The Qur'an records other peoples reaching for the same move — Pharaoh's people blamed Mūsā and those with him for their bad fortune, and the townsfolk in Yā Sīn told their messengers the same thing. The tactic recurs because it requires no evidence.

**What Augury Actually Was**

The word they use is *iṭṭayyarnā*, built on the root *ṭ-y-r*, birds. The vocabulary preserves the practice it came from. Pre-Islamic Arabs read the future by releasing or startling a bird and watching which way it flew: to the right, a good sign; to the left, a bad one.

Knowing this makes the exchange sharper in a chapter titled after an insect and largely concerned with a hoopoe. Sheba's fate turned on a bird that carried real information across a real distance, and Thamūd is using birds as a random oracle. One is observation; the other is the appearance of observation. The linguistic fossil also explains why the Qur'an treats augury as a matter of speech rather than of science — the whole apparatus consists of assigning a meaning to something that carries none, and then acting on the meaning. Islam later prohibited the practice explicitly, and the prohibition is usually traced to exactly this reasoning rather than to a rival system of divination.

**"Your Omens Are With God"**

Ṣāliḥ's reply is compressed: *ṭā'irukum ʿinda Allāh* — your bird, your omen, is with God. The commentators unfold it in slightly different ways that add up to one position.

Al-Qurṭubī takes it to mean that the things they predict from omens are with God; al-Ṭabarī, that such matters are known to Him alone; al-Rāzī, that whatever good or evil reaches them originates with God rather than with any augury. The common claim is that causation has been misassigned. Something does determine what befalls them, and it is not the direction a startled bird flies, and it is not the presence of a preacher in their town. The answer is economical because the error is simple. They have taken a real question — why are things going badly — and attached it to a mechanism that cannot answer it.

**The Diagnosis: You Are Being Tested**

The second half of his reply is the substantive correction: *bal antum qawmun tuftanūn* — rather, you are a people being tried. The particle *bal* cancels what preceded it. Not an omen; a test.

Al-Ṭabarī's reading is that the trial consisted precisely in Ṣāliḥ being sent to them. That turns their accusation inside out. They said his arrival was their misfortune; he says his arrival is their examination. Both statements agree that his presence is the decisive new factor in their community's life, and they disagree completely about what that means. The difference is between being a victim of circumstances and being a candidate under assessment, and only one of those descriptions leaves anything to do. A cursed people can only complain. A tested people can pass.

**Superstition as an Escape From Responsibility**

Behind the exchange lies a general claim about why augury appeals. An omen explains misfortune without implicating anyone who holds it. If the cause of your troubles is a bird, or a stranger, or a preacher, then nothing about your own conduct requires examination.

That is the function Ṣāliḥ's reply attacks. His correction does not merely deny their metaphysics; it returns the question to them. A test has a subject, and the subject is the one being tested. This is why the Qur'an's objection to divination is moral before it is theological — the practice is condemned not chiefly because it is inaccurate but because it moves the locus of responsibility out of the person and into the sky. Thamūd's nine leading men are introduced in the next verse, and what they do with their responsibility is the last stage of this story.

**What They Actually Accused Him Of**

Their charge is worth stating precisely, because it is not a claim that he is lying. They do not say his message is false, his God does not exist, or his warnings are invented. They say he brings bad luck — *iṭṭayyarnā bika wa biman maʿak*, we take you and those with you as an evil sign.

That formulation concedes a great deal. It accepts that something has changed in their situation since he began speaking and that the change is real enough to need explaining. What it denies is only the explanation he offers. In place of a test they substitute a curse, which keeps the facts and reverses the responsibility. They also extend the charge to his followers, which is how factional thinking works: the believing party from verse 45 is now a category of person rather than a set of positions, and misfortune is attributed to their presence rather than to anything they have done. Ṣāliḥ's reply refuses both halves in one sentence.
