## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:57

> And as for those who believe and do good, they will be rewarded in full. And Allah does not like the wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Half of the Sentence**

The previous verse stated the end of those who disbelieved; this one states the end of those who believed and did good. The two form one sentence in the original sequence, and the sūrah gives the reward the second position, so that the passage closes on the group it hopes its readers will join.

The construction is familiar from the Book's treatment of the Day of Judgment, where the two outcomes are normally stated together. What deserves attention here is the order and the proportion. The warning occupies a full sentence about punishment in two worlds and the absence of helpers; the promise occupies a shorter sentence about full reward, and a clause about God's disposition that does not contain the word reward at all.

**"Rewarded in Full"**

The verb behind the phrase describes the payment of a wage in full, without deduction, and the Book applies it to the settlement of every soul's deeds on the Day of Judgment in several passages. Used here of believers, it carries an implication the reader should not miss: the reward is presented as a payment owed, an obligation the giver has taken upon himself.

The Book's language about reward goes further in other places, offering multiplication beyond measure, gifts without reckoning, and increases out of God's grace. Setting those verses beside this one shows how the Book holds two things together: what is owed is paid in full, and what is given exceeds what was owed. A servant who has done little and receives much is not being treated unfairly; he is dealing with a giver whose account of the transaction is generous rather than exact.

**Belief and Deeds Named Together**

The verse names two conditions, faith and good conduct, and the book pairs them so consistently that separating them would produce a different religion. Faith without works appears in the Qur'an as a claim that has no consequence for the claimant, and works without faith appear as a structure built on a foundation the builder has refused.

The sūrah has already given the reader both warnings. It described the people whose deeds were wasted in two worlds because their acts were not grounded in the proper relationship to God, and it described the supplication of a community asking to be counted among the witnesses. This verse's pairing of belief and good actions is the positive statement of the same doctrine, and its brevity suggests that the Book regards the combination as obvious rather than subtle. What is promised is not to those who felt something or to those who did something, but to those in whom the two ran together.

**"Allah Does Not Like the Wrongdoers"**

The verse ends with a statement about God's disposition that seems, at first reading, to belong to the previous sentence. The wrongdoers are not mentioned in the clause about belief and good deeds, and the reason for the closing phrase lies in the structure of the whole passage.

The Book's convention is to name a divine attribute that explains the verdict just given. Here the verdict is twofold: disbelievers are punished, and believers who do good are rewarded in full. The attribute that covers both is God's disfavour toward wrongdoers, since the punishment of the deniers and the reward of the upright are both expressions of the same preference. A reader who understands the final clause aright understands the verse's claim about God's character: the distinction He draws among people is not arbitrary favour but a settled opposition to wrongdoing and a settled regard for what is right.

**A Reminder About Who Is Being Described**

The verse is often read as a general statement about the destiny of two classes of people, and it functions that way. In its immediate setting, though, it concerns the parties to a specific dispute: the community that rejected a prophet and the community that accepted him and acted on the acceptance. The word translated "wrongdoers" is not a synonym for "disbelievers" in every Qur'anic passage, and here it describes the members of the rejecting party whose conduct earned the description.

The distinction matters because the sūrah will go on, in the following verses, to make a very particular argument about what belief in Jesus requires. The reward described here is not offered to a category of people defined by their ancestry or their community affiliation. It is promised to those whose faith found expression in acts that the Book considers good, which is a condition that any reader of the verse can measure himself against.

**Why the Reward Is Described So Barely**

The warning in the previous verse carries details; the reward here carries almost none. The Book gives the believer a full payment and nothing else, and the silence is worth weighing against the elaborate descriptions of Paradise in other sūrahs, where gardens, rivers, spouses, and the pleasure of God are named.

The difference is one of rhetorical purpose rather than of importance. A passage arguing against people who had misread the destiny of a prophet does not need a portrait of Paradise; it needs the assurance that the accounting will be fair and that the two parties will be separated. The bareness also keeps the reader's attention where the sūrah has placed it, on the question of belief and conduct, rather than on the inventory of the reward. What the verse guarantees is enough: the payment is not partial, and it is owed.

**The Comfort Inside a Warning**

The passage as a whole is a warning addressed to a community under pressure, and its second half is the part that makes the warning bearable. A person told only that his enemies will be punished gains nothing; a person told that his own belief and conduct will be repaid in full has a reason to continue.

The Book's pastoral pattern, visible in this verse and throughout the sūrah, is to make the consequences of both roads clear and then to leave the reader facing a choice that has an obvious better option. The reward described here is deliberately unelaborated. What is emphasised is not the content of the reward but the fact that it is full, owed, and given by the one who does not like wrongdoing. The next verse turns from the destiny of the parties to the source of the account the reader has just received.
