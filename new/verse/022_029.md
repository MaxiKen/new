## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:29

> Then let them groom themselves, fulfil their vows, and circle the Ancient House.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Then Let Them End Their Untidiness"**

*Thumma l-yaqḍū tafathahum* — then let them do away with their *tafath*. The word is unusual, and al-Rāzī made a point of saying so: *tafath* is not known from any source outside the exegetical literature. He reports that a Bedouin, asked what it meant, equated it with filth.

A word that the Arabs of the desert had to be asked about is a word whose meaning was fixed by the rite rather than by general usage, and the commentators' glosses agree on the substance: it refers to the state of physical neglect that belongs to the pilgrimage — uncut hair, untrimmed nails, the dust of the road — and to its removal at the end.

That reading is confirmed by the parallel legislation in Sūrah al-Baqarah, which describes the same moment in procedural detail: *“And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination”* (2:196). The shaving is the release, and the animal's arrival is what triggers it. The two verses together give the shape of the rite: a period of deliberate untidiness, ended by a specified act, at a specified time.

**What the Release Involves**

The commentators took the removal to cover the cutting or shaving of the hair and the trimming of the nails, along with whatever else belongs to leaving the state of consecration.

The sequence carries a meaning. For the duration of the pilgrimage the pilgrim does not attend to his appearance; he is, in a visible way, not at home in ordinary life. The release reverses that: he cleans himself and returns to the world he came from. The rite therefore has a shape that can be described in one sentence — you leave your ordinary condition, you stand before God in it, and you come back changed but presentable.

The physicality of it is the point. Nothing in this is a feeling. A person knows whether he has shaved his head, and the rite is built so that the markers of the sacred period are on the body rather than in the mind alone.

**"And Let Them Fulfil Their Vows"**

*Wa-l-yūfū nudhūrahum* — and let them fulfil their vows. The pilgrimage in the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods was frequently undertaken in discharge of a vow: a person promised that if a thing were granted, he would go.

The command is general, and the commentators took it to include the vow of the pilgrimage itself as well as any vow made during it. The Qur'an's standing rule about vows is that they bind: a promise made to God is not a figure of speech, and this verse places the discharge of it inside the rite rather than before it.

There is a psychology here that is worth naming. A vow is a promise made under pressure, and the natural history of such promises is that they are forgotten once the pressure lifts. Putting the fulfilment inside the pilgrimage — the thing the person is already doing — and naming it as one of the three closing acts makes it harder to leave undone.

**"And Let Them Circle the Ancient House"**

*Wa-l-yaṭṭawwafū bi-l-bayti al-ʿatīq* — and let them go around the ancient House. The verse ends where the pilgrimage's central rite is: the circumambulation.

The adjective is *ʿatīq*, and it carries three senses at once, all of which the commentators noted. It means very old. It means freed or set free. And it means excellent, noble, choice — the sense used of a fine horse or a choice piece of property.

The first sense is the one the Qur'an states elsewhere: *“Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people”* (3:96). The House is ancient in the specific sense that it is the first, which means the religion practised at it is not a new sect but the restoration of the oldest thing.

**"Freed" — and the Report Behind It**

On the second sense, several commentators preserve a report that the Kaʿbah was named the Freed House because no tyrant ever prevailed against it. [UNVERIFIED: this report is cited by Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī in the material available to me without a chain or number I could verify this session.]

Whether or not that etymology is accepted, the claim it makes is historically striking and largely borne out: the assault most famously repulsed is the one the Qur'an records in Sūrah al-Fīl, the expedition of the elephant from Yemen in the year of the Prophet's birth, which the sūrah says was turned back. The building has survived conquest, siege, fire, flood and the collapse of every dynasty that claimed it.

The theological reading of *ʿatīq* as "freed" therefore makes a claim about ownership that runs through this whole passage: the House is not the property of whoever holds the keys, and no power has ever successfully taken it.

**The Order of the Three Commands**

The three imperatives — end the untidiness, discharge the vows, circle the House — are given in an order that matches the rite's own movement.

First the body is restored: the state of consecration is closed and the pilgrim is clean again. Then the outstanding obligation is settled: what was promised is paid. Then the circuit is made: the pilgrim returns to the House, clean and unindebted, for the act that the sūrah's founder was told to prepare the House for in verse 26.

Nothing in the sequence is arbitrary, and its shape is the shape of the whole religion as this sūrah presents it: purification, obligation discharged, and then worship. The sūrah will restate the first and last of the three in different words before the passage closes.
