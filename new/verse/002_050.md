## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:50

> And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh's people before your very eyes.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Verbs, One Subject**

The verse compresses the Exodus into three first-person plural verbs — *We parted, We rescued you, We drowned* — and keeps a single subject across all three: Allah. The wider recitals name the instruments: the staff, the strike, the inspiration to Moses (26:63); the pursuit, the drowning's timing (10:90). Here the instruments are withheld, and the withholding is theology. The sea did not part because a staff was waved; a staff was waved because the sea had been ordered to part. The Qur'an's habitual grammar of miracle operates at exactly this register — human action fully real, divine agency fully primary — and verse 50 states the primary alone. Note too the structure of the middle verb: *parted the sea* takes no human object in form, but the Arabic carries *bi-kum* — "with you," "through you," "for you" — and the commentators divide between the readings without needing to choose: the sea parted *for your sake*, and it parted *as you entered it*, the miracle completing itself through their walking. A delivered people is not a spectator of its deliverance; the road opened under their feet and closed behind them, and the verse's grammar keeps both facts — Allah's sole doing, their bodily passage — in one clause.

**The Mechanism: A Mountain on Each Side**

The Qur'an's fuller filing of the same event supplies the scene this verse's audience already held: *"So We inspired Moses: 'Strike the sea with your staff,' and the sea was split, each part was like a huge mountain"* (26:63). The simile is the verse's most deliberate stroke — water standing as geology, the fluid made structural, each wall a *ṭawd*, the word for a great mass of mountain. The image accomplishes two things. It fixes the crossing as a passage *between* standing things, not a ford or an ebb-tide: the sea did not recede but rose into architecture, and the road ran through a canyon of held water. And it slows the miracle down to visible duration — walls like mountains do not flash and vanish; they stand long enough for a nation to walk through, and for an army to walk in after. The deliberateness is the point of the whole staging: a fleeing people could have been lifted over the water or hidden from pursuit, but the crossing was arranged at walking pace, in daylight visibility, with both parties on site — a sign engineered for witnesses rather than an escape engineered for survival. Verse 50's summary verb *rescued* rests on that staging; its final verb, *drowned*, completes it in the same corridor.

**Wa-Antum Tanẓurūn: The Clause of Watching**

The verse ends with a circumstantial clause — *wa-antum tanẓurūn*, and you were looking on. The drowning of Pharaoh's people happened in the sight of the delivered: from the far shore, the rescued watched the corridor close on their pursuers. The clause is the recital's recurring instrument of aggravation and honor at once. Aggravation, because a sign witnessed binds harder than a sign reported: the coming failures — the calf above all — will be committed not by people who heard of the sea but by people who stood at its edge and saw an army go into it. Honor, because being made a witness is itself a favor: Allah staged the greatest of the Exodus signs with this community in the front row, and the Qur'an's repeated appeals to sight (*do they not see, do they not look*) treat vision as the highest-bandwidth channel of evidence. The clause also does quiet work on the verse's audience in Madina: their ancestors were eyewitnesses, and the eyewitness generation is the generation the recital will hold most accountable — which converts the honor of descent into the liability of inheritance. A community that claims the witnesses' covenant is answerable at the witnesses' standard.

**The Confession That Arrived Late**

The drowning the verse summarizes is unfolded elsewhere with a detail that belongs to any reading of it: Pharaoh's end was not silent. As the water closed, Sūrah Yūnus records his confession — *"I believe that there is no god except that in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am ˹now˺ one of those submit"* (10:90) — the tyrant adopting, at the last second, the exact formula of the people he had been slaughtering. The Qur'an's answer to the confession is refusal: belief at the sight of punishment, when the test's conditions have collapsed, is no longer faith but recognition — the knowledge compelled by evidence no longer avoidable, worth nothing in the ledger because it costs nothing. And the aftermath is filed as a standing sign: *"Today We will preserve your corpse so that you may become an example for those who come after you. And surely most people are heedless of Our examples!"* (10:92). The body of the drowned king — displayed, preserved, made an *āyah* — is the Exodus's epilogue for later generations: the man whose machine slaughtered sons now a public exhibit of the machine's end. Verse 50's audience watched him go in; the Qur'an's later audiences were told the corpse stayed. Both facts are in the favor: the delivered were shown, with their own eyes, the total and permanent closing of the threat.

**Two Rescues in One Verse**

The rescue of verse 50 is double, and the doubling matters. Extraction alone — a fleeing people across a fordable gap — would have left the threat alive and the pursuit resumable; the deliverance is completed only by the drowning, which ends Pharaoh's people as a power. One instrument performed both: the same water that was a road for the delivered was a grave for the pursuers, and the corridor's two verdicts fell along a single line of division. The structure makes the sea the Qur'an's densest image of divine justice — not justice as balancing but as separating: a people and their tormentors placed in one element, and the element itself sorting them. It also explains why the Exodus functions throughout the Qur'an as the standing promise to the oppressed: the pattern is not escape-with-luck but deliverance-with-completion, in which the mechanism of pursuit becomes the mechanism of the pursuer's end. For the sūrah's first Muslim hearers — a small community newly settled in Madina after an exile of the same shape — verse 50 was not history recited but precedent published: the God who parted the sea before witnesses had already demonstrated, in the fullest public form, how He handles a people cornered at water.

**The Honor and the Liability of the Watching**

Verse 50 closes the first movement of the recital — torment (49), deliverance (50) — and leaves the witnesses standing on the far shore with everything still to be asked of them. The verse's final clause will govern the reading of every entry that follows: a sign seen is a sign owned, and ownership runs in both directions. The generation that watched the sea become mountains received the strongest evidence any community was ever given — and the recital will show what that generation did forty nights later, at the foot of a mountain of a different kind. Nothing in verse 50 excuses them, and nothing in it condemns them yet; the verse simply fixes the epistemic condition under which their whole history will be judged: *you were looking on*. The same clause extends, by the inheritance logic of verse 49's "you," to every reader the recital reaches — the Qur'an puts its audience behind the eyes of the witnesses and holds them to what sight obliges. Between the sea and the audit of verse 48 stands the rest of the recital: favors itemized, breaches itemized, pardons offered — the case file of a people delivered before their very eyes, and of the covenant they carried out of the water.
