## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:243

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who fled their homes in the thousands for fear of death? Allah said to them, "Die!" then He gave them life. Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First of the Stories**

The hinge-verse turns, and the sūrah's narrative sequence opens with a scene of pure paradox: thousands flee their homes *for fear of death*, and death is exactly what meets them — by direct divine command. *Allah said to them, "Die!" then He gave them life.* The two imperatives frame the whole event: the command to die arrives at the fleeing thousands in their thousands, and the giving of life follows — a resurrection inside history, witnessed, reported, and made a sign. The opening formula, *Have you not seen*, is the Qur'an's summons to the mind's eye: what follows is not presented as speculation but as a scene the hearer is to see, and its seeing is the lesson's vehicle. The commentators note the verse's function at this exact position: the legislation has just closed with a call to understanding (2:242), and the first narrative unit delivers the understanding's first object — a demonstration, in thousands of simultaneous deaths, of the proposition every preceding ruling assumed: that life and death are God's property, and that no human arrangement reaches them.

**Who They Were**

The tradition's account, recorded by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī, places the scene among the Children of Israel: a community — a town's population or an army's multitude — that fled either a plague or a war, in circumstances the narrations describe as disobedience, seeking a place of safety. They left in thousands, the numbers themselves part of the sign: flight at that scale is a society's decision, not a panic's. Death overtook them anyway, all of them, and God then raised them — the narrations describe a prophet sent to or passing the scene, at whose intercession or by whose witness the thousands stood up alive again. The exegetes also record the position that treats the particulars as beside the point: who they were, when they lived, and where they fell matter less than the verse's demonstrative core — God kills and God gives life, the fleeing is futile, and the revival is grace. Both readings coexist in the commentaries without tension, since the narrative's historicity and its typology carry the same claim.

**The Plague and Its Protocol**

The plague-identification connects the verse to a body of prophetic legislation the commentators cite at this point. When plague breaks out in a land, the Prophet commanded, do not enter it; and when it breaks out in a land where you are, do not flee it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). The two rulings look like quarantine law and are also theology: the first refuses to carry the disease toward the healthy, the second refuses the flight this verse depicts — the departure *for fear of death* that the fleeing thousands undertook and that did not, in the event, save them. Al-Ṭabarī's commentary sets the command beside the verse precisely because the verse is the tradition's proof-text for the futility the plague-protocol assumes: death is not a geography one can leave, and the believer's posture in an epidemic is neither flight nor fatalism but stay-and-trust, since *Allah said to them, "Die!"* reaches the runner and the rester alike. The revival then supplies the protocol's second half: life, too, is not a geography — it is a word, and the same voice that commanded the dying commanded the rising.

**Ezekiel's Valley**

Some commentators see in this verse an allusion to the vision in Ezekiel 37 — the prophet set down in a valley full of dry bones, commanded to prophesy over them, and watching the bones come together, take flesh, and stand as an exceeding great army. The parallel is real and the differences are instructive, and the exegetes mark both. The biblical account gives no reason for the valley's dead — the bones are the whole house of Israel in its exile-despair, and their revival is a parable of national restoration; the Qur'anic account gives its thousands a specific motive, flight from death, and a specific end, the demonstration of God's command over life. Beyond the revival itself, al-Ṭabarī notes, the two accounts share almost nothing — no valley, no bones, no prophesying-over in the Qur'anic scene. The relationship is best read as the Qur'an's characteristic use of known material: a story circulating among the People of the Book, taken up, stripped of its parabolic machinery, and re-pointed at a single doctrinal fact.

**The Lesson the Verse Is Placed For**

Al-Qurṭubī states the verse's purpose with unusual directness: the theme is that God holds the power of life and death over human beings, that they cannot determine their own life and death, and that the Prophet and the believers are to take the lesson — especially, he adds, given what the verse's context makes plain about the struggles ahead of the community. The placement does the argumentative work. The sūrah has just legislated fighting, patience, spending, and the whole architecture of a community under pressure; the narrative sequence that begins here will run through a requested king, a tested army, and a giant felled by a boy. The fleeing thousands are the sequence's first exhibit and its negative example: a multitude that tried to solve the problem of death by moving, and learned that the solution was never spatial. Every story that follows — the army that drinks at the river, the few who stand against Goliath — is the verse's lesson lived forward: the fear of death managed by flight produces death; the fear of death surrendered to God produces, sometimes, an army that cannot be counted by its enemies.

**Bountiful, Ungrateful**

The closing clause generalizes the scene into a standing description: *Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful*. Dhū'l-faḍl — the Possessor of bounty toward mankind — names the relationship the fleeing thousands had forgotten: the God they ran from was the God whose faḍl they ran inside, since life itself, the thing they were trying to save, was and remained His gift. The revival is the bounty's proof — death commanded and life restored, both acts of the same generosity — and the ingratitude is the scene's moral residue: *most people* neither flee-with-gratitude nor stay-with-trust but live as though life were a possession to be defended by technique. The clause sets up the whole narrative sequence's recurring rhythm, which the sūrah will strike again and again — divine bounty extended, human response divided, the few grateful and the many not — and it ends the verse the way the verse began it: with life and death in one hand, and the human task reduced to the single question of whether the hand is thanked.
