## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:117

> ˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth! When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, “Be!” And it is!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name at the Start of the Sentence**

The verse begins by describing God as the Originator, a term that carries the sense of bringing something into being without a prior model. The word is used in the Book for the creation of the heavens and the earth, and its force is that nothing existed before the act and nothing served as a pattern for it.

The sentence follows directly on the previous verse's rejection of the claim that God has offspring, and the connection is deliberate. The answer to a statement about divine fatherhood is a statement about divine creation: whatever exists came into being by God's command, and none of it shares His nature. The title is used in the Book only of God, and it appears where the argument concerns the uniqueness of His action. The sūrah's placement of it here is part of the answer rather than a decorative epithet, since the verse states what kind of being is under discussion before it says what that being does.

**When He Decrees a Matter**

The Arabic for decreeing carries several related senses, including completing, accomplishing, and judging, and the commentators noted that the verse uses it for the decision that precedes the act of creation. What the sentence describes is not a process but a determination followed immediately by the thing determined.

The Book states the point elsewhere in nearly identical words, and the same construction appears in connection with matters that human beings find difficult to accept, including the creation of Jesus and the resurrection of the dead. The verse therefore belongs to a family of passages that use creation to establish what God can do without effort or preparation. The wording also excludes the idea of effort. The Book's account of creation nowhere describes God as requiring time, materials, or assistance, and the sentence places the whole of the divine action in a single determination. What follows from that is a doctrine of absolute dependence: nothing that exists contributes to its own coming into being.

**The Command "Be!"**

The creative command appears in several chapters, and the commentators discussed what the phrase means. Al-Rāzī argued that it conveys ease and power rather than a sequence of events, since one cannot address a thing that does not exist and a thing that exists has no need of the command. Others held that the command is coterminous with the existence of the thing rather than preceding or following it.

The Book uses the same wording for the creation of Jesus, whose birth without a father is explained by the sentence that God created him from dust and then said to him, "Be!" and he was (3:59). The parallel is important in the argument of this passage, since a claim about sonship is being answered with a statement about how everything comes into existence by command.

**What the Command Establishes About Knowledge**

Commentators drew a further conclusion from the sentence, which is that God knows things before they exist. If a matter is decreed before it is brought into being, the decree concerns something known rather than something discovered, and the Book's account of creation therefore includes knowledge as well as will and power.

Some later writers developed the idea at length and described the objects of divine knowledge as forms or essences that are known but not yet created, explaining the command as addressed to what God knows rather than to nothing. Whatever one makes of those elaborations, the point they were expressing belongs to the verse: the creation of anything depends entirely on God's knowledge, will, and power, and on nothing outside Him.

**A Parallel With the Opening of Genesis**

The phrase brought into being by divine speech corresponds to the opening of the Hebrew scripture, where light comes into existence at God's word. The resemblance has been noted by readers of both scriptures, and it serves the same purpose in each: existence is not self-generated, and the world depends at every moment on the command that called it.

The Book's use of the formula goes beyond the creation of light, since it applies the command to whatever God wills, including events within history and the raising of the dead. The sentence is therefore not a description of the first moment only; it states a standing relation between God and everything that exists.

**What the Verse Answers**

The sentence is positioned as the answer to a claim about God having a son, and it also serves the sūrah's larger argument with the people who demanded signs. A community that asked for a particular display of divine power is told that God's dealings with creation are not constrained by the mechanisms they imagine, and that nothing He wills requires preparation.

The verse therefore leaves the reader with a doctrine rather than a narrative. Its two halves state who God is and how He acts, and the sūrah builds on both in the verses that follow, where the demands of the sceptics are described and answered. The sentence also answers a version of the demand recorded in the following verse, where people asked that God speak to them directly. The Book's reply is that God's speech brought the universe into existence, and that the message delivered by His messenger is the form in which His address reaches humanity now.

**The Use of the Present Tense**

The Book often describes the creative command in the present tense, so that the sentence is not confined to the beginning of the world. What the wording conveys is that the same authority which brought the heavens into being remains in force, and that no created thing sustains itself independently of it.

The reader of the passage is left with a statement about God that the surrounding verses will apply to specific disputes. The sentence does not argue with the claimants directly; it states the position from which the argument will proceed, and the following verse describes the response of those who were asking God to speak to them directly.

**Creation Without a Precedent**

The word rendered as Originator describes bringing something into being without a model to work from, and the Book uses it where the subject is the creation of the heavens and the earth. The term therefore answers the claim of the previous verse in a way that a statement about power alone would not, since it concerns the kind of act that creation is. Nothing preceded the act, nothing was consulted, and the thing produced had no earlier version. The Book applies the same thought to the creation of Jesus, whose coming without a father the Qur'an explains by reference to the first creation, and the argument is that a being brought into existence by command cannot stand in a relation of divine offspring to God. The verse gives the reader a ground for the answer that the next sentences develop, and it places the whole discussion of God's activity on the footing of a command that produces what it names.
