## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:29

> Fight those who do not believe in Allah and the Last Day, nor comply with what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden, nor embrace the religion of truth from among those who were given the Scripture, until they pay the tax, willingly submitting, fully humbled.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Addresses a Political Order**

Verse 29 introduces the command concerning certain People of the Book after the passage on Arabian polytheists. It is a legal and political verse, not a statement that every Jew or Christian is identical. The wording itself says from among those who were given the Book, and the Qur'an elsewhere praises upright People of the Book who believe, worship, and hasten to good (3:113–115). The group in this verse is defined by the political and religious conditions named here and by the campaign connected with the northern frontier.

Classical commentators connected the verse with the expedition to Tabūk and the fear of Byzantine intervention. The Muslim polity had become a regional power, and reports of a northern threat made a defensive march necessary. The verse therefore belongs to a setting of state relations, security, and treaty status. It cannot be lifted from that setting and turned into a command for individuals to attack peaceful neighbors because of their religion.

**The Description Is Not a Blanket Statement About All Scripture People**

The verse lists failure to believe in Allah and the Last Day, failure to forbid what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden, and failure to follow the religion of truth. The Qur'an recognizes that Jews and Christians claim belief in God and the Hereafter, so exegetes debated how the description applies. Some understood it as a legal description of their non-Muslim status and rejection of the Prophet. Others emphasized that the verse targets those whose conduct and political allegiance make them part of the hostile case.

The diversity of the Qur'an's language about the People of the Book should govern interpretation. It praises some, criticizes some, and regulates relations with them. No single label erases the distinctions. The verse establishes a rule for a conflict in which a non-Muslim polity enters a treaty and tax arrangement with the Muslim state. It does not authorize the believer to pronounce on every individual heart.

**Jizyah Is a Covenant Arrangement**

Jizyah became a term for the financial obligation attached to protected non-Muslim communities living under Muslim rule. In return, the protected community was not required to pay zakāh as Muslims did and was generally exempt from military service, while the Muslim polity assumed responsibility for protection. The exact amount, collection method, and categories of people exempted were debated by jurists. Those details matter because the verse created a legal relationship, not a license for arbitrary extraction.

The phrase willingly submitting has also been interpreted in different ways. Some took it to mean direct payment and acceptance of the political arrangement. Others treated it as payment made without coercive humiliation. The historical practice was not uniform, and some later rulers abused the language of humility. Yet the Prophetic and early legal ideal described receipt of the payment as a debt received with gentleness. The verse must not be used to defend cruelty that the covenant was meant to prevent.

**Humility Means Accepting the Legal Status**

The final phrase, fully humbled, is difficult because it can be read as personal humiliation or as recognition that the protected community is not the ruling power. The more responsible legal reading distinguishes political subordination from degrading treatment. A treaty community accepts the authority of the state and its public law; it does not follow that its members must be insulted, beaten, or stripped of ordinary dignity.

This distinction is supported by the purpose of dhimmah, the protection created by the covenant. A protected person is not merely tolerated. The state owes him security of life, property, and worship within the terms of the agreement. If the collection of jizyah becomes an excuse to violate those rights, the state has broken the very protection that gives the payment meaning.

**The Verse Must Be Read With Its Neighbors**

Verse 28 had addressed the Sacred Mosque and the end of pagan pilgrimage. Verse 29 addresses a different population and a different legal setting. Verse 30 will discuss theological claims, while the following verses describe religious authority and wealth. The change of subject shows that the chapter is not giving one undifferentiated command about all non-Muslims. It is moving through distinct communities and distinct problems.

The earlier treaty rules remain relevant. A People of the Book community that has a treaty is not to be attacked merely because the verse exists. The Qur'an commands fulfillment of covenants and protection of those who seek safety. The fighting command belongs to the political case in which the hostile order is confronted until it accepts a protected arrangement or the conflict ends. Reading the verse without the treaty framework produces a rule wider than the chapter's own legal architecture.

**A Warning Against Modern Simplification**

Modern readers may approach this verse with either hostility or apologetic denial. One side may present it as a timeless command to persecute Jews and Christians. The other may pretend that the verse contains no rule about political conflict. Both responses avoid the work of interpretation. The verse is severe, historically situated, and legally bounded. It must be explained honestly without making it a weapon against peaceful people or erasing its original force.

For believers, the enduring lesson includes the duty to govern power by covenant. If a state enters a protected arrangement, it must honor the protection. If it collects a public obligation, it must do so justly. If it fights, it must identify the threat and keep the goal visible. The verse names an order of conflict and settlement. It does not release the powerful from accountability to Allah.
