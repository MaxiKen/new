## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:31

> The disbelievers vow, “We will never believe in this Quran, nor in those ˹Scriptures˺ before it.” If only you could see when the wrongdoers will be detained before their Lord, throwing blame at each other! The lowly ˹followers˺ will say to the arrogant ˹leaders˺, “Had it not been for you, we would certainly have been believers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Refusal Announced in Advance**

The verse opens with a declaration of permanent policy: *lan nuʾmina bi-hādhā al-qurʾāni wa lā bi-lladhī bayna yadayh*. The particle *lan* is Arabic's strongest future negation — not "we do not believe" but "we never will", a door closed before any further evidence can be presented. What makes the statement remarkable is its scope. They reject the Qur'an and also the scriptures that came before it, which is a larger commitment than the argument required. Most of the sūrah's opponents had no quarrel with the Torah or the Gospel; those books were somebody else's business. Extending the refusal backwards makes sense only as a defensive move. If a man rejects one revelation he must explain why the others do not count against him, and the simplest way to avoid the question is to reject the category. The position follows logically from what the preceding verses exposed: men who deny God's unity, prophethood and the resurrection have nothing left to do with books that teach all three.

**A Sentence That Breaks Off Unfinished**

The second part of the verse begins *wa law tarā* — if only you could see — and never supplies its own conclusion. Arabic permits this construction, in which the consequence of a conditional is left out because no words would improve on the reader's own completion. You would see something; what you would then do or feel is not stated. The tradition fills it in variously — you would be astonished, you would see a terrible thing — and the omission is more effective than any of the fillings. The verse is describing a scene that its Meccan audience could not witness, and it declines to tell them how to react to it. Notice also the sudden change of grammatical time. The refusal was declared in the present; the scene is given as a future event described as though it were already visible, which is the Qur'an's usual way of collapsing the distance between a promise and its fulfilment.

**Detained, Then Arguing**

*Mawqūfūna ʿinda rabbihim* — made to stand before their Lord. The word carries the sense of being halted and held, stopped in place pending disposal, which is the posture of prisoners awaiting a hearing rather than guests awaiting an audience. And what they do while held is not plead, confess or fall silent. They argue: *yarjiʿu baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍin al-qawl*, they throw speech back and forth at one another. The Arabic idiom is of statements being returned like objects. This is the detail that gives the scene its psychological accuracy. People caught in a joint failure do not, in the first instance, examine themselves; they establish who is responsible, and they do it loudly. The Qur'an lets this quarrel run for three full verses, which is a long time by its standards, and the length is part of the argument. What is being exhibited is not a punishment but a conversation, and the conversation is doing damage to both parties before any punishment arrives.

**Two Words for Two Classes**

The parties are named with terms drawn from social structure rather than from theology. *Alladhīna istuḍʿifū* — those who were made weak, a passive form, so the description includes the claim that their weakness was done to them. *Alladhīna istakbarū* — those who made themselves great, an active form built on the root of *kibr*, pride, so the description places the second group's status in their own hands. The grammar has already assigned a preliminary verdict before either party speaks. The sūrah has been building toward this exchange since it quoted the sneer of the comfortable at the poor who followed the Prophet, and it will name the type directly in three verses when it describes the luxury-dwellers of every warned town (34:34). What the Qur'an consistently declines to do is treat the division as merely economic. The arrogant are not condemned for being rich but for the use they made of position.

**The Followers' Case: A Real Grievance, Deployed Badly**

*Law lā antum la-kunnā muʾminīn* — had it not been for you, we would have been believers. The claim deserves to be taken seriously before it is dismissed, because it contains something true. Leaders do obstruct; a man born into a household that mocks the message hears it under conditions a wealthy independent man does not; the Qur'an itself recognises genuine oppression as a mitigating circumstance and holds out pardon for those truly unable to act, while refusing the excuse to those who merely claimed inability (4:97–99). So the followers are not inventing a category. What they are doing is inflating their own case, converting real pressure into total causation, and the inflation is exposed by the word they chose: *believers*. They do not say "we would have acted differently" but "we would have believed", and belief is the one thing no external party can install or prevent. A man can be stopped from praying in public. He cannot be stopped from being convinced.

**Why the Excuse Is Recorded Rather Than Refuted**

The verse does not answer the followers. It gives them their sentence and moves on, leaving the reply to the arrogant in the next verse and the counter-reply after that. This is a deliberate method. An excuse stated in the presence of the One who knows the facts refutes itself, and the Qur'an is content to let the transcript do the work. There is also a warning embedded in the timing. The excuse is offered after the moment when it could have made any difference, at a hearing where the evidence is already in, and everyone involved knows it. For the sūrah's original audience, many of them ordinary Meccans who followed their clan chiefs because that is what one did, the scene functions as a preview of an argument they were already preparing. The chapter's answer is not that the pressure was imaginary. It is that the pressure was survivable, and that a group is standing nearby who survived it.
