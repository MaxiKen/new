## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:71

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Should we invoke, other than Allah, those ˹idols˺ which cannot benefit or harm us, and turn back to disbelief after Allah has guided us? ˹If we do so, we will be˺ like those disoriented by devils in the wilderness, while their companions call them to guidance, ˹saying˺, ‘Come to us!’ Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah’s guidance is the ˹only˺ true guidance. And we are commanded to submit to the Lord of all worlds,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Put in the First Person**

The sūrah has been answering the Makkans for seventy verses, and here the Prophet is told to put the argument in his own mouth as a question. Should we invoke, apart from God, something that can neither benefit nor harm us? The construction makes the proposal state itself, since the moment the objects of worship are described in those two words, the absurdity is on the surface and no further argument is needed.

The Book uses the same description repeatedly where it deals with the idols. The sūrah of the Table states that those whom the pagans worship besides God can neither harm nor benefit anyone (5:76); the sūrah of Yūnus describes people who worship beings of that kind and call them their intercessors with God (10:18); the sūrah of the Bee asks whether anyone would take besides God lords who cannot even benefit or protect themselves (13:16); and the sūrah of the Prophets records Abraham putting the same question to his people (21:66). The description is not a debating point invented for this verse but the Book's settled account of what an idol is.

**Turning Back on the Heels**

The second clause of the question is about the cost of the choice: should we turn back to disbelief after God has guided us? The Book states the danger of that reversal in the strongest terms, saying that whoever among the believers renounces his faith and dies a disbeliever has rendered his deeds void in this life and the next (2:217), and telling the community that if they yield to the disbelievers they will be dragged back to disbelief and become losers (3:149).

The imagery is of a traveller who has been put on a road and then walks back down it, and the sūrah will use the same figure of speech for a person who returns to his old ignorance. The question in this verse is therefore not only about what the idols can do. It is about what the believer would lose by going back, and the answer is stated in terms of a guidance he had already received.

**The Man in the Wilderness**

The verse then supplies a simile: like one bewildered, seduced by satans on the earth, though he has companions who call him to guidance, saying, Come to us! The picture is of a man who has lost his way in open country, is being pulled in the wrong direction by whispering companions, and is being called back by others. The Book's word for the seducers is plural and general, and the commentators noted that it can cover both the human and the unseen agents who do Satan's work, since the Book speaks elsewhere of devilish humans and jinn who whisper to one another with elegant words of deception (6:112).

The image earns its place because it captures the specific form of the danger. The man is not being argued out of his faith by better evidence; he is being drawn away, step by step, in a place where he has no landmarks. That is the situation the sūrah has described throughout its account of the Makkan response, in which the argument was settled and the dispute continued anyway.

**The Companions Who Call Him Back**

The detail about the companions is the mercy in the image. The man is not alone, and the people calling him are calling him to guidance rather than to their own advantage. Commentators read the companions as sincere and rightly guided people who try to bring him back, and some connected the verse with reports about the son of Abū Bakr, who was being lured toward idolatry while his father called him to the faith he had left.

A minority of commentators understood the callers to be inviting the man to a guidance that was itself false, since the Book elsewhere uses the word for the calling of those who have strayed. The reading is worth noting because it shows how carefully the commentators attended to the pronouns, but the more natural sense fits the verse's argument: the man has people who want his good, and he is still walking away.

**Allah's Guidance Is the Only Guidance**

The Prophet's answer to the imaginary proposal is short: Allah's guidance is the only true guidance. The statement appears in the same wording in the sūrah of the Cow, where the Prophet is told to say it after being pressed to follow the faith of the People of the Book (2:120), and in the sūrah of the Family of Imran, where the same claim is made against a claim of exclusive ownership of guidance (3:73). The sūrah of Fāṭir puts the same idea in a form that leaves no doubt: what God opens of mercy cannot be withheld, and what He withholds cannot be released (35:2).

The exclusivity is the point of the whole passage. Guidance in the Book is not one beneficial practice among several that might be compared on their merits; it is a specific instruction from the One who made the world, and the alternatives are not paths to the same destination. The commentators read the sentence as a claim that there is no guidance outside God's, and that everything else which calls itself a road leads elsewhere.

**Submission to the Lord of All Worlds**

The verse closes with the alternative to the proposal: we are commanded to submit to the Lord of all worlds. The Book places the same declaration on the lips of the Prophet in the sūrah of Ghāfir, where he announces that he has been forbidden to worship what the people worship besides God, since clear proofs have come to him, and that he has been commanded to submit to the Lord of all worlds (40:66). The two verses are almost parallel in structure and content, which shows that this sūrah's closing declarations belong to a fixed pattern in the Book.

Submission, in the Book's vocabulary, is the act that answers the whole argument of the sūrah. The Makkans had been invited to acknowledge a maker, a provider, a rescuer, a judge, and a recorder of deeds, and the response the Book asks for is the one that accepts all of those claims at once. What the Prophet is told to announce in this verse is not a philosophical position but an allegiance.
