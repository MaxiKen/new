## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:116

> ˹O Prophet!˺ If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah’s Way. They follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning Against Following the Majority**

If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah's Way. The sentence is addressed to the Prophet, and it states a principle that the Book repeats in many places: the majority provides no measure of truth. The warning is not that the Prophet was tempted to follow the crowd but that the crowd's position carries a weight that the community around him felt, and the Book names the danger in the plainest terms.

The sūrah's audience was a society in which the majority held the ancestral religion, and the small community of believers lived inside that majority. The Book tells the Prophet that compliance with the majority would not merely have modified his message; it would have taken him off the way altogether. The verse therefore treats the pressure of numbers as a claim to authority and denies it outright.

**The Assumption That Stands in Place of Knowledge**

They follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie. The two clauses name the ground and the consequence. The Book's word for assumption describes the surmise a person inherits or inherits by habit, and the sūrah of Yūnus states that most people follow nothing but inherited assumption and that assumption cannot replace the truth (10:36); the sūrah of the Star makes the same statement in the context of the claim that the angels are daughters of God (53:28); and the sūrah of the Cow describes the illiterate among the People of the Book as knowing nothing of the Scripture except lies and living on speculation (2:78).

The second clause raises the charge from error to falsehood. What the audience affirmed about God, about the idols' power to intercede, and about the resurrection was not merely an unproven opinion; the Book treats it as a statement about God made without warrant, which is the offence this sūrah has been describing since its claim that the idolaters invent lies against Him.

**The Book's Use of This Argument**

The Book makes the majority's position a subject of remark in several passages, and it never treats numerical support as evidence. The sūrah of Yūsuf says that most people will not believe no matter how keen the messenger is (12:103), and it says that most of them do not believe in God without associating others with Him (12:106). The sūrah of the Cattle itself states at the end of the passage about signs that most of the audience are ignorant (6:111), and the sūrah of the Women and others describe the majority in similar terms.

The argument is not a rejection of consensus in favour of idiosyncrasy. What the Book rejects is the use of numbers as a criterion of truth, and it supports the rejection by pointing to the content of the majority's belief rather than to its size. The Makkan majority was large, established, and confident, and the verse says that its followers were following conjecture.

**What the Warning Meant in Makkah**

The warning had a practical edge for the Prophet and for the community that had joined him. The majority controlled the city's worship, its trade, and its kinship structures, and a person who accepted the message faced the loss of all three. The verse tells the Prophet that the majority's way is not a path to be accommodated, and its statement that the majority's followers do nothing but lie gives the reason.

The Book's accounts of the earlier messengers show the same situation. The sūrah of Hūd describes Noah's people rejecting him on the ground that only the lowest among them had followed him, and the Book records communities that measured a claim by the standing of the people who made it rather than by what was said. The verse in front of us states the Book's position on that assumption without qualification.

**The Verse's Relation to the One Before It**

The verse follows the statement that God's word has been perfected in truth and justice and that none can change His words (6:115). The pair establishes the standard by which claims are measured: the perfected word on one side, and the conjecture of the many on the other. The sūrah has already told the Prophet to seek no judge besides God, and this verse removes the most obvious alternative, which is the opinion of the people around him.

The verse that follows answers the warning by stating that the Prophet's Lord knows best who has strayed from His way and who is rightly guided (6:117). The two verses together leave the question of standing where the sūrah has consistently placed it, in the knowledge of God rather than in the counting of heads.

**What the Verse Teaches the Reader**

The instruction concerns the community that would read the Book after the Prophet. Every generation faces the same pressure, and the sūrah's answer is the same in each case: the way of God is known from the revelation, and the size of the company that travels another road is not an argument. The Book states this principle in its accounts of the communities that were destroyed and in its descriptions of the small groups of believers who were saved.

The sūrah has spent much of its length distinguishing between the two roads, and the closing verses of this section continue the contrast with an account of the dietary laws, the divisions among the earlier communities, and the testimony that the Prophet is a warner rather than a keeper. The verse in front of us supplies the criterion by which the two roads are told apart, and the criterion is the revelation rather than the multitude.
