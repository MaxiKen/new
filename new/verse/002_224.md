## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:224

> Do not use Allah’s Name in your oaths as an excuse for not doing good, not guarding against evil, or not making peace between people. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**God's Name Made a Barrier**

The verse prohibits a specific abuse of the holiest word: do not make God an *ʿurḍah* — a barrier, an obstacle, a thing set in the way — through your oaths, against righteousness, against taqwā, and against making peace between people. The image is architectural: the divine name, invoked in a sworn sentence, becomes a wall built across the road of good. A man swears by God that he will not speak to his brother, or will not give charity to a certain needy person, or will not mediate between two feuding kin — and thereafter the oath itself stands as the excuse: the good is not done, and the not-doing is religiously defended, since breaking a sworn oath feels like a greater sin than omitting the kindness. The verse names the mechanism and condemns it: the oath is not a shield for the omitted good but the sin's disguise, and God's name is not a warrant for withholding what God commands. The three goods named — righteousness, mindfulness, peacemaking — cover the oath-abuse's whole field: duties to God, duties of conscience, and duties between people, all three capable of being barricaded by a sentence sworn in the Divine name.

**The Oaths Condemned**

The exegetes specify the cases the verse targets: swearing never to speak to relatives, never to give charity, never to make peace between two people, never to perform some good deed — oaths against righteous behavior in every direction (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī). The list's first item has a social history: the sworn boycott of kin was an Arabian instrument of pressure, a man binding himself by God to silence against his own blood until some grievance was satisfied. The second and third items turn the same instrument against the community's charitable and reconciling life. In each case the oath performs a double wrong — the good withheld and the Divine name enlisted as guarantor of the withholding — and the verse's prohibition addresses both: the good must be done, and the oath that stood against it must not be honored at the good's expense. The legal tradition drew the practical rule from this verse together with its companions: an oath to disobey or to omit an obligation carries no binding force, and the swearer's duty is the good rather than the oath — with the expiation-legislation supplying the means of release.

**The Oath-Culture Corrected**

The verse legislates inside a culture dense with oaths, and the commentators note the culture's own ambivalence: even in pre-Islamic Arabia, immoderate oath-taking was counted a vice — the man forever swearing was suspected precisely because his sacred words were cheap. The Qur'an's own portraits carry the judgment: the sūrah of the Pen's list of the despicable characters to be obeyed-not includes the vain oath-monger — the habitual swearer, the one whose oaths are so frequent they mean nothing (68:10). And the sūrah of the Table legislates the oath's serious tier alongside its casual one: God does not take you to task for what is frivolous in your oaths, but He takes you to task for the oaths you have pledged in earnest — with the expiation defined: feeding ten poor people from the family's ordinary food, or clothing them, or freeing a bondsperson, or, for the one who cannot, fasting three days (5:89). The two verses together draw the oath's whole map: the casual invocation exempted, the deliberate pledge binding, the pledge against good dissolved with expiation — and over the whole territory, the name of God treated as the weight it is, neither banalized by frequency nor weaponized against the good it should serve.

**Hearing, Knowing**

The verse closes with the two names that preside over all speech-legislation: God is Hearing, Knowing. The pairing audits the oath from both ends. Hearing: the sworn sentence is a sound in the world, and the One whose name it invokes heard it — the words, the tone, the audience, the moment. Knowing: the intention behind the words is known — whether the oath was heat or calculation, whether the good it blocks was ever intended, whether the piety it claims is real. The names together defeat the abuse at its root: the man who swears against charity and then pleads the oath's sanctity is pleading before the Hearer of his swearing and the Knower of his purpose, and the verse leaves the plea in Their hands rather than in the community's. The same names closed the war-legislation's hearing of the cease (2:227's coming pair) and will preside over the divorce-resolutions ahead — the domestic legislation runs, clause by clause, under this surveillance: every word of the household spoken before the Hearing, every motive of the household held before the Knowing.

**The Oath-Block's Opening**

This verse opens a short block in which the sūrah legislates the whole life of the sworn word. Here: the oath misused against good. Next: the oath uttered carelessly, exempted from reckoning, with accountability reserved for what hearts have earned (2:225). Then: the oath sworn against a wife — the īlāʾ — bounded by four months and forced to resolve into return or divorce (2:226–227). The block's architecture is complete jurisprudence in four verses: the abusive oath, the empty oath, the harmful oath, and each one's ruling. Its placement matters: the oath-block stands between the household's intimacy-legislation (2:222–223) and the household's dissolution-legislation (2:228 onward), because the oath is the hinge between them — the speech-act by which a marriage's daily life can be poisoned or a divorce's drama staged. A community being taught to govern its bedrooms and its divorces is first taught to govern its swearing: the tongue that invokes God is the tongue that will pronounce the ṭalāq, and the verse that forbids making God a barrier to good is training the same organ that will soon hold a wife's fate.

**The Good That Must Pass**

The verse's deepest claim is about the relation between the sacred and the good: nothing sworn in God's name may barricade what God loves. The three named goods are not marginal favors but the legislation's own centers — righteousness and taqwā, which the sūrah has made the end of every law it wrote, and peacemaking between people, which the whole domestic legislation about to unfold exists to serve (the honorable retention, the graceful release, the reconciliation the waiting-period preserves). An oath against any of these is an oath against the scripture's own project, and the verse refuses such oaths the dignity of binding force. The believer is thereby taught a hierarchy: the Divine name is holy, the good is holy, and where a man's swearing has set the two against each other, the good passes and the swearing is expiated. The expiation-system exists precisely so that the passage costs nothing but the prescribed ransom — feeding, clothing, freeing, fasting — and the wall comes down. The verse ends, as its subject demands, with the hearing and the knowing: the walls we build with sacred words are heard being built, and known for what they block.
