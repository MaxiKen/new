## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:224

> Do not use Allah’s Name in your oaths as an excuse for not doing good, not guarding against evil, or not making peace between people. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prohibition Stated**

The verse forbids the use of God's name in oaths as a means of avoiding good conduct, and it names three instances: failing to be mindful of God, failing to guard against evil, and failing to make peace between people. The sentence treats the oath as a barrier that a person has placed between himself and his obligations.

The Book's concern is the use of a sacred invocation for a purpose that contradicts it, and the community's teachers read the sentence as a rule about the handling of God's name. Readers are shown that an oath cannot be allowed to cancel a duty, and the sentence names the duties that are most commonly obstructed in that way.

**The Practices Addressed**

The commentators described the habits that the sentence corrects, and they mention oaths never to speak to a relative, never to give charity, and never to reconcile two parties who have quarrelled. The list shows the kind of vow that the verse has in view.

The community's scholars noted that such oaths were taken seriously by those who swore them, so that the obligation of the oath appeared to override the duty it prevented. The sentence removes the conflict by ruling that the oath in such a case is not to be observed, and readers are shown that the Book gives the duties of piety and reconciliation precedence over a vow that opposes them.

**The Two Duties Named**

The sentence names the guarding against evil and the making of peace between people, and the two belong to the community's moral obligations that the sūrah has described elsewhere. The making of peace is mentioned in the Book as a good to be pursued, and the community's teachers connected the sentence with the passages that commend reconciliation.

The pairing of the two duties gives the sentence its range, since the first concerns the person's own conduct and the second concerns his relations with others. The passage shows that the verse's concern is not only the private life of the person who swears but the damage his oath may do within the community, and the sentence's inclusion of peacemaking places the matter among the community's interests.

**The Reports About Oaths**

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere states that the community will not be held accountable for what is frivolous in its oaths but will be held accountable for what it has pledged in earnest, and the community's scholars read the passages together. The reports also preserve the Prophet's ﷺ teaching about the seriousness of the oath and the disapproval of excessive swearing.

The material shows how the community understood the sentence, and the community's teachers used it when they set out the rules about vows. The account describes a practice that the Book regulates rather than abolishes, and the sentence in this place supplies the guidance that governs the making of an oath.

**The Names at the End**

The sentence closes with the description of God as hearing and knowing, and the community's scholars read the pair as the ground of the prohibition. The first name concerns what is said, which is the matter of the oath, and the second concerns what is intended, which is the purpose behind it.

The community's teachers noted that the closing names answer the two aspects of the offence, and they used the sentence when they spoke about the weight of a vow. The passage shows that the Book's legislation on the subject rests on the knowledge of the one before whom the oath is taken, and the sentence leaves them with the statement of that knowledge.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The sentence follows the rules about marriage and precedes the treatment of oaths and divorce that occupies the following verses, and it opens a section on the community's commitments. The Book has been organising the household and now turns to the words by which people bind themselves, so that the reader is carried from the relations of marriage to the obligations of speech.

The passage's concern is the seriousness of what a person says in God's name, and the community's teachers read the section as a whole when they set out the law of oaths. The sentence in this place supplies the principle, and the verses that follow describe the standing of oaths that were not intended and the commitments that dissolve a marriage.

**The Oath and the Community's Peace**

The sentence places the duties of piety and of reconciliation above a vow that opposes them, and the community's teachers read it as a statement about the purpose for which an oath is taken. A person swears in order to bind himself, and the Book does not allow the instrument to close the door of what it commands.

The reports describe the occasions of the revelation, including the household in which a man had sworn not to speak to one of his relatives and the disputes between families that had been fixed by oaths of separation. The remedy the sentence supplies is the breaking of the oath together with the expiation that the Book sets out elsewhere, and the teachers noted that the expiation keeps the person's word from becoming weightless.

The passage's concern is the peace of the community, since the sentence names reconciliation among the duties that no vow may cancel. The Book commends those who make peace between people, and the verse places that work above the satisfaction of a promise that was made in anger or in haste.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes that an oath may not be used to prevent what is good, that the duties of piety and reconciliation take precedence over such a vow, and that God hears what is said and knows what is meant. The community's teachers used the verse when they taught about the handling of oaths and about the priority of the community's peace over a person's determination to keep his word.

The community's teachers drew the principle that an oath taken to prevent a good act does not bind the one who swore it, and they applied it to the disputes of their own families and quarters. The sentence also teaches that the good the Book commands is not suspended by the formalities a person has created, and the verses that follow continue the treatment of the oaths the community took.
