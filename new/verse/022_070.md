## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:70

> Do you not know that Allah ˹fully˺ knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth? Surely it is all ˹written˺ in a Record. That is certainly easy for Allah. Almighty Allah or False Gods?
**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Changes From Seeing to Knowing**

*A-lam taʿlam anna Allāha yaʿlamu mā fī al-samāʾi wa-mā fī al-arḍ* — do you not know that God knows whatever is in the heaven and whatever is on the earth?

The sūrah has asked *a-lam tara*, do you not see, four times in the preceding verses. Here the verb changes to *a-lam taʿlam*, do you not know.

The shift is deliberate, and it marks a movement in the argument. Seeing was the appropriate faculty for the signs — rain, ships, the held sky. Knowing is the appropriate response to a claim that cannot be observed: that everything is in a record. Nobody sees the record. The verse asks for assent to something that has to be taken on the strength of the One who says it.

**The Singular "Heaven"**

The Arabic here has *al-samāʾ* in the singular rather than the plural *al-samāwāt* used in verse 64. This is the only place in the Qur'an where the singular form appears in a statement of this kind, and the variation is worth noting precisely because it is unusual.

The simplest explanation is the one the commentators give: *samāʾ* in the singular can denote the sky above as a single thing rather than the layered heavens. In a verse about a record that omits nothing, the singular is apt — the whole expanse over a person's head, taken as one, is included.

**"Indeed That Is in a Book"**

*Inna dhālika fī kitāb* — that is in a Book. The demonstrative points back to *whatever is in the heavens and the earth*, which is to say: all of it, without exception, is written down.

The claim is the one the sūrah has been building toward since verse 10, where the record was described as something a person will be handed and told to read. It will be restated two verses from the end, where God is said to know what is before people and what is behind them.

The Qur'an's insistence on the record is a claim about the kind of knowledge God has. It is not a general awareness or an intuition; it is an entry. Sūrah Luqmān makes the scale explicit: *“˹Even˺ if a deed were the weight of a mustard seed—hidden in a rock or in the heavens or the earth—Allah will bring it forth”* (31:16). The same claim is made elsewhere in the same vocabulary: *“He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth”* (3:29) and *“He knows whatever is in the heavens and on the earth”* (5:97).

**Why a Record Rather Than a Memory**

The metaphor carries a specific assertion: the record is external, fixed and legible.

That matters for the judgment the sūrah has been describing. A verdict based on recollection could in principle be disputed on the ground of error. A verdict based on a written entry that the accused is invited to read cannot — which is why the Qur'an's judgment scenes show the record being produced rather than the charges being announced (17:14; 18:49; 69:19).

For the reader of this sūrah, the consequence is the one verse 68 already put to use: "God knows best what you do" is not a rhetorical flourish. There is a document.

**"That Is Easy for God"**

*Inna dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīr* — that is easy for God.

The sentence is standard in the Qur'an after a statement of comprehensive knowledge or power, and it is easily skimmed. It should not be. The claim is not that the record is a small task; it is that the category of difficulty does not apply.

The difference matters. If recording everything were difficult for God, it would be impressive; if it is easy, it is simply what He is. The verse is making a statement about the divine nature rather than about the size of the archive.

**The Record and the Sūrah's Own Argument**

The claim of a comprehensive record is not an isolated doctrine here; it is the mechanism that makes the sūrah's whole eschatology work.

Verse 10 put a sentence in the mouths of the keepers: *this is for what your hands sent ahead*. For that sentence to mean anything, the shipments must have been logged. Verse 56's judgment requires a procedure, and verse 57's distinction between the two groups requires that each person's case be complete.

Verse 70 is the guarantee that the procedure exists and that nothing is missing from it. Without it, the sūrah's threats would be rhetoric and its promises would be hopes. With it, both are verdicts waiting for a date.

**What the Record Does Not Imply**

The verse is also, in the way the Qur'an handles this doctrine generally, not deterministic in the sense readers often assume.

A record of what people do is not a script of what they will do. The Qur'an holds both without strain: everything is written, and people are responsible for what they do — and the sūrah's own verse 11, which describes a man who chooses to flip over onto his face, is written in the language of choice throughout. The record is the consequence of the act being knowable, not the cause of the act being done.

That distinction matters for how the doctrine is received. A person who believes his entry is already fixed has no reason to act; a person who believes his acts are being written has every reason to.

**What the Verse Requires**

Three things are asserted in one line: comprehensive knowledge, complete inscription, and the ease of both. Together they establish the premise the sūrah has been operating on since its opening — that a judgment is possible at all.

A court needs a record. Without one, the sūrah's eschatology would be a threat without a procedure, and the promise of verse 56 would be a hope rather than a verdict. Verse 70 is the guarantee that the procedure exists.
