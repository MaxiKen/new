## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:12

> He replied, “My Lord! I fear that they will reject me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses Answers the Command With Honest Fear**

Moses does not pretend that the assignment is easy. His first concern is that the people will deny him. The Arabic language of denial here includes calling a messenger false, refusing his authority, and treating his warning as an invented claim. Moses knows that a person who has built power on a settled story will not welcome the one who exposes that story. His fear is therefore a sober reading of the audience, not an excuse to escape the task.

This honesty makes the prayer part of obedience. Moses does not run from the command or answer with a boast about his own courage. He brings his weakness before the Lord who sent him. A servant can accept a mission while acknowledging the real obstacles within it. Faith is not the denial of fear; it is the refusal to let fear become the final authority. The verse gives language to that middle place between panic and bravado.

**Rejection Threatens the Messenger's Ability to Serve**

Moses' concern is not simply that people will insult him. He fears that their denial will obstruct the work entrusted to him. A messenger must speak so that a hearer can understand and consider. If the audience turns his mission into a lie before listening, the public task is made harder. The fear therefore grows out of responsibility. He wants to carry God's command faithfully and knows that hostile reception can affect the way a message is delivered.

The Qur'an does not rebuke him for recognizing this danger. It records the fear so that the later divine answer can address it. A prophet's dependence on God is shown not by the absence of human concerns but by bringing those concerns to God before acting. The prayer turns the anticipated denial into an occasion for support. Moses will go with a message whose truth does not depend on the audience's willingness to honor it.

**The Word Denial Names a Moral Choice**

To reject a messenger is not the same as failing to understand a difficult sentence. Moses fears that Pharaoh's people will decide in advance that he is false. Such denial can be reinforced by habit, political interest, and fear of what acceptance would require. The audience may hear the words clearly and still protect itself by declaring them impossible. That is why the stories of this sūrah focus on response as well as proof.

Moses' fear also shows that prophetic speech enters history rather than floating above it. A message is heard by people with memories, loyalties, and interests. They can connect it to Moses' past, question his right to speak, or accuse him of threatening the land. The truth remains God's truth, but the messenger must meet the human forms through which resistance is expressed. The Lord's help will not make the encounter unreal; it will make faithfulness possible within it.

**The Prayer Preserves Moses' Humility**

A servant who fears rejection may be tempted to soften the command until it becomes harmless. Moses does not do that. He states the danger without changing the assignment. Nor does he turn the fear into contempt for the people he must address. His prayer keeps him dependent and keeps the audience human. They are wrongdoers who need warning, not monsters beyond the reach of speech.

This balance is important for every caller. Fear can make a person harsh, because harshness feels like protection against being dismissed. It can also make him silent. Moses' prayer points to a third course: name the fear before God, seek the means to carry the truth, and speak without making the audience's hostility an excuse for cruelty. Divine support will answer the weakness, but it will not authorize pride.

**Other Accounts Show the Same Need for Assistance**

In 20:25–28 Moses asks for an expanded heart, ease in his task, and clear speech. Those requests illuminate the fear stated here without replacing it. The mission is inwardly demanding because it requires a servant to stand before a ruler while remaining free of the ruler's frame. Moses needs more than information. He needs steadiness, clarity, and the ability to make the message understood.

The cross-reference also shows that prophetic courage can include asking for practical help. Prayer is not a retreat from responsibility. It is preparation for it. Moses' fear becomes a petition, and the petition becomes part of the record of how God forms a messenger. A person need not wait until fear disappears before asking to be useful. He can bring fear into prayer and let prayer train it.

**Rejection Cannot Decide the Truth**

Moses fears that they will reject him, but the verse does not say that their rejection would make him false. The distinction is essential. A speaker may be rejected because a claim is weak, but he may also be rejected because the claim threatens a preferred order. The audience's response must be examined, not simply obeyed. Truth is tested by its evidence and divine source, not by the approval of those who hear it.

The Lord's answer will soon remove the fear's immediate force, but it will not promise that Pharaoh's people will become willing listeners. The servant is strengthened for a mission that may still be resisted. That is the form of courage the verse teaches: not confidence that opposition will vanish, but confidence that opposition cannot cancel the duty to speak.

**Fear Becomes Useful When It Leads to Prayer**

Moses' first movement is toward his Lord. He does not rehearse the danger before Pharaoh or let the audience see private anxiety as a reason to compromise the message. He places the fear where it can be judged and answered. Prayer turns a vague dread into a specific request for help, and a specific request makes faithful action more possible.

This pattern keeps emotion from becoming an idol. Fear is acknowledged, but it is not consulted as though it possessed final authority. The servant listens to the command again after bringing his concern before God. In that way, fear becomes information about the task rather than a ruler over the task. Moses can proceed with a truthful estimate of the danger and a greater trust in the One who sent him.
