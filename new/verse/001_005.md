## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Object Comes Before the Verb**

Arabic normally puts the verb first and the object after it. The verse inverts that order. The pronoun *iyyāka* is placed ahead of *naʿbudu*, we worship, and a direct object moved forward in Arabic carries restriction: it does not simply say what is worshipped, it says what alone is worshipped. The translation registers the grammar by printing *alone* in brackets, because no English word-for-word order reproduces the effect.

The pronoun itself is noteworthy. *Iyyāka* is a detached pronoun that Arabic uses when the object is fronted, and the whole form is peculiar to a handful of constructions. The sūrah's pivot verse therefore announces its doctrine twice: once in the meaning and once in the word order. Nothing in the religion of the Fātiḥah is divided, and no act of worship is left with a second addressee.

**A Sentence Recited in the Plural**

The reciter of the Fātiḥah is alone, yet the verse says *we*, not *I*. Worship and the seeking of help are both conjugated for a community, and the individual who recites stands inside that community even when nobody else is in the room. Muslim prayer keeps the same grammar: the worshipper speaks of himself in the plural at the very moment he is most alone with his Lord.

That choice has consequences for how the religion is practised. A faith whose central prayer said *I worship you and I ask you for help* could be recited without reference to anyone else; this one cannot. The plural places the reciter's needs inside a shared project, so that the guidance requested later in the sūrah is requested for the group the reciter has just joined. The "we" is not stylistic padding. It is a doctrinal position about what a believer is.

**Worship First, Help Second**

*ʿIbādah* names the whole of obedient submission offered to God with humility, not merely the ritual act. *Istiʿānah* is the request for assistance, and the verse places it after worship rather than before. The order matters, because it fixes the relationship from which requests are made: help is asked by someone already engaged in service, not service being offered in exchange for help.

The Qur'an keeps the same order when it teaches how to endure difficulty: *“And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is a burden except for the humble—”* (2:45). Another verse addresses the community directly: *“O believers! Seek comfort in patience and prayer. Allah is truly with those who are patient”* (2:153). In both, the seeking comes through the practice rather than as a substitute for it. Petition in the Qur'an is not a transaction with a patron; it is the natural speech of a servant who has already accepted the service.

**Reliance Without Despair**

Asking for help is an admission of need, and the Qur'an treats that admission as worship rather than as weakness. *“Your Lord has proclaimed, ‘Call upon Me, I will respond to you. Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled’”* (40:60). Refusing to ask, on this reading, is not self-sufficiency but arrogance.

The Book is careful about what reliance means in practice. Joseph says of himself, *“I do not seek to free myself from blame, for indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord”* (12:53), which is confidence placed in God against one's own impulses rather than confidence in oneself. The name that follows the admission is given at 85:14: *“And He is the All-Forgiving, All-Loving—”*. A servant who says both halves of 1:5 has stated the entire relation at once.

**One Sentence, Two Halves of Religion**

The verse gathers the two movements of the religious life. The first half concerns what God is owed; the second concerns what the servant needs. Islamic teaching traditionally described the religion under the same two headings, worship directed to God and dependence on God, and 1:5 holds them in a single line with a single pronoun in front of each.

Because the halves share the fronted pronoun, neither can be detached from the other. Worship without dependence becomes self-congratulation, a performance offered by someone who thinks he is managing; dependence without worship becomes superstition, help sought from whatever is at hand. The report preserved by Muslim 395 places the verse at the exact middle of the Fātiḥah, where the dialogue changes hands, and the phrase it quotes from God is *this is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks for*. The petition that follows belongs to the servant, but it now comes from a servant who has settled who he is speaking to.

**No Mediator Stands in the Sentence**

The verse contains two pronouns and no intermediary. The worshipper addresses God directly in both halves, and no figure is named to carry the worship upward or the help downward. The Book's teaching on petition matches the grammar: *“Call upon Me, I will respond to you”* (40:60), a sentence spoken by God without a channel, and *“In Him ˹alone˺ the faithful put their trust”* (39:38).

That directness distinguishes the Fātiḥah from religious systems in which access to the divine is administered. Islam keeps its intermediaries elsewhere — teachers, scholars, a community and a tradition all carry knowledge — but the act of worship and the request for help are unmediated by design. A servant may ask a living person for a favour, and the Book does not forbid it; what this verse removes is the notion that help must be routed through a hierarchy to arrive.

The practical consequence appears in the way Muslims pray. The petition in the Fātiḥah is spoken in the first person plural to God, with no saint invoked in the sentence and no authority consulted for permission to speak. The verse that follows determines what gets asked for, and it is asked of the One already addressed.
