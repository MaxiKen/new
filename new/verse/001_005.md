## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Turns and Speaks to Him**

The first four verses talk about God. This one talks to Him. Everything before it was description in the third person — Name, praise, mercy, ownership — and here, without announcement, the worshipper switches to *You*. Arabic rhetoric has a name for the switch (*iltifāt*), and its effect is immediate: a passage that could have been recited as information becomes an encounter.

The report that maps the recitation as a dialogue registers the break. For the three verses of praise, the response is *My servant has praised Me*, *has lauded Me*, *has glorified Me* — God receiving what is His. For this verse the response changes character entirely: *This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asked for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). God does not receive this clause as praise due to Him; He claims it as the one place where the worshipper and He are both parties. The verse is the hinge of the sūrah, the point at which the recitation stops being about God and becomes addressed to Him, and the point from which everything that follows is a request.

**The Object Stands Before the Verb**

Arabic normally puts the verb first. Here the object is pulled to the front: *iyyāka naʿbudu*, literally "You — we worship." The displacement is the standard Arabic device for restriction, and it changes what the sentence asserts. *We worship You* would report an action and leave room for others. *You we worship* excludes them: worship goes to You and to no one else.

The restriction falls on both halves, and that is easy to miss. The verse does not only say that worship belongs to God alone; it says that the request for help does too. The grammar binds the two clauses into one claim, so that asking anyone or anything else for what only God gives is placed in the same category as worshipping someone else. The verse thus legislates the interior life as well as the ritual one: the reflex of reliance — on wealth, on standing, on one's own planning — is what the fronting is there to cut away.

**Why the Worshipper Says "We"**

The verb is plural. A person praying alone in a room still says *we*, and the commentators give several reasons that reinforce rather than compete. The plural is the voice of the congregation, and the prayer is recited as one body even when it is performed in solitude. It is also the voice of the whole community across time, so that no worshipper prays as a self-contained individual.

Two further reasons go deeper. Humility is one: the word *I* belongs properly to God, and to stand before Him saying *we* rather than *I* keeps the creature from pretending to a singularity that is not his. The other is representative. Human beings are described as those God *placed… as successors on earth* (6:165), and on that basis the worshipper is understood to speak on behalf of creation rather than only for himself. The recitation is never private in the sense of being about one person's needs, and the plural makes that a grammatical fact rather than a pious intention.

**Worship Here Means Servitude**

The verb is *naʿbudu*, from the same root as *ʿabd*, a servant or slave. The English word worship carries chiefly the sense of ceremony, but the Arabic carries the sense of belonging to a master, and the two are not interchangeable. Servitude is the larger category: a person can perform ceremonies and remain his own, and a person can be a servant of God in acts that no one would call ritual.

The Quran states the condition as universal and unavoidable. *I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me* (51:56) makes worship the purpose of the creature rather than one activity among others. *There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission* (19:93) makes it the destiny of everything, willing or not. What is left to human choice is therefore not whether to serve but whom, and the Quran defines the commanded form as *to worship Allah ˹alone˺ with sincere devotion to Him in all uprightness* (98:5). Sincere devotion is the variable, and it is precisely what the fronted object in this verse is protecting.

**The Request Is for Help to Do the First Half**

The order of the two clauses is deliberate and the commentaries give the reason: a person does not ask a king for help without first acknowledging the king's standing. Servitude is confessed, then aid is requested. The sequence is also logical rather than merely polite, since asking for help from someone whose power has not been conceded is not really asking.

What is being asked for deserves the closest attention. The natural reading — help me with my affairs, my illness, my debts — is not wrong, but several commentators tighten it: the help requested is help to worship, since the worshipper has just undertaken something he cannot carry out unaided. On that reading the verse is a circle, and a deliberate one. The servant pledges worship, then immediately asks for the strength to do what he has pledged, which means the prayer contains its own answer to the pride that would make the pledge in the first place. The Quran matches the structure elsewhere: *Seek comfort in patience and prayer* (2:153), and *And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is a burden except for the humble* (2:45). The grounding statement is blunt about who needs whom — *Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺* (47:38).

**Three Motives, Ranked but Not Void**

A report attributed to Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, preserved in al-Kulaynī's *al-Kāfī*, divides worship by motive: those who worship from fear worship as slaves, those who worship for reward worship as hirelings, and those who worship out of love worship as free people, which is the most excellent. A parallel division is attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib in *Nahj al-Balāghah*, with gratitude replacing love in the highest rank.

The ranking should not be read as a disqualification, and the Quran prevents that reading. Fear and hope are named repeatedly as legitimate motives for ordinary believers: *They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear* (32:16), and *call upon Him with hope and fear* (7:56). The tradition is therefore ordering excellence, not sorting the saved. What makes the highest rank highest is that it survives the removal of the other two — the person who would worship God were there no Garden and no Fire is not serving for payment, and the verse's fronted object belongs to him without residue.
