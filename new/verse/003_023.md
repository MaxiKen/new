## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:23

> Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures? Yet when they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes, some of them turn away heedlessly.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"A Portion of the Scriptures" — the Precision of the Phrase**

The verse does not say that these people were given the Scripture; it says they were given *a portion* of it, and the difference is the whole portrait. The Book uses the same phrase at 4:44 and 4:51 about people whose relationship with revelation is partial, and the choice of wording marks the gap between possessing texts and being possessed by their purpose.

A portion is enough to be held responsible for, and not enough to live on. The reader is looking at people who have inherited a real sacred book, studied it, built institutions around it, and yet whose use of it is selective enough that the Book describes their share as a fraction. The next verse will describe the doctrine that made the fraction comfortable.

**Invited to the Book of Allah**

The invitation is described as a call to *the Book of Allah* to settle a dispute. Commentators discussed which book is meant here, and the readings differ: some understood the reference to the Torah, since the Torah was the book these people already possessed and the one that could settle a matter within their own community; others read it more broadly as the revelation that had now come to the Prophet.

The ambiguity is not damaging, because the argument works either way. The Book's complaint at 5:43 uses the narrower reading as its premise: *“But why do they come to you for judgment when they ˹already˺ have the Torah containing Allah’s judgment, then they turn away after all? They are not ˹true˺ believers.”* If the disputants are asked to consult their own scripture and refuse, the refusal is a verdict on their attachment to it. If they are asked to accept the judgement of God's latest messenger and refuse, the refusal is a verdict on their willingness to let God decide at all. Both roads end at the same wall.

**The Judicial Setting and the Refusal**

The scene is a courtroom rather than a classroom. The invitation is to have a case decided, and the Book's other account of the same behaviour makes the setting explicit: *“And as soon as they are called to Allah and His Messenger so he may judge between them, a group of them turns away.”* (24:48).

There is a reason the Book frames the issue as judgment instead of instruction. A person can listen to teaching and postpone; a person who accepts a judge has already conceded the authority of the decision before he knows what the decision will be. That concession is what the attendees of this verse refuse, and the sūrah states the standard elsewhere without qualification: *“they will never be ˹true˺ believers until they accept you ˹O Prophet˺ as the judge in their disputes, and find no resistance within themselves against your decision and submit wholeheartedly.”* (4:65). The hesitation being described is not doubt about evidence. It is a refusal in advance.

**"Some of Them" — the Verse Stops Short of Collectivising**

The turn away is attributed to *some of them*, and the qualifier is deliberate. The Book distinguishes carefully within communities, and it does so in verses that would have been much easier to write as general condemnations.

The same restraint appears in the stories of covenant and covenant-breaking told elsewhere: *“Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?”* (2:85) addresses a specific conduct without declaring every member of a community guilty of it. The Qur'an's polemics are aimed at positions and practices, and the reader is expected to keep the distinction that the text itself keeps. A community has custodians, dissenters, and quiet people, and the sentence here reaches only one of those groups.

**Turning Away "Heedlessly" — the Posture of the Refusal**

The last word describes the manner of the turn: heedless, inattentive, walking away. The Arabic participle *muʿriḍūn* comes from the motion of turning one's side to someone, and the Book uses it repeatedly for the physical gesture of refusal — the shoulder presented to a speaker so that his words no longer arrive.

It is a telling detail for a verse about people who own texts, because it suggests the refusal is not the result of a difficult argument. They have not been defeated by reasoning or exhausted by a debate; they have decided not to listen. In the account the Book gives of the same problem elsewhere, the custodians of the scripture knew the answer before the question was put, and the turning away had already happened in the heart.

**What the Invitation Offered**

Nothing in the sentence suggests that the invitation was hostile. The disputants were offered a resolution, at their own level, from a source they claimed to honour. The Book's account of that possibility is worth holding on to, since it explains why the following verses move from description to a warning about the Day of Judgment.

An invitation refused is not the same as an invitation never made. The verse ends with the refusal and leaves the reader to draw the conclusion about what remains.

The verse also records a detail about the community's inner life that is easy to miss. These are people whose disputes were real enough that they sought judgments, and who nevertheless rejected the forum that would have decided them. The Book's interest is not in what the cases were. It is in the fact that a community with a sacred text in its possession could still prefer a settlement it controlled to one it would have to accept. That preference, and not the absence of scripture, is what the sūrah keeps identifying as the disease.
