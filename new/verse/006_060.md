## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:60

> He is the One Who calls back your souls by night and knows what you do by day, then revives you daily to complete your appointed term. To Him is your ˹ultimate˺ return, then He will inform you of what you used to do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sleep Described With the Language of Death**

The verb the verse uses for what happens at night is the same verb the Book uses for death, and the commentators noticed it. Yatawaffā is the word that appears when a person's life is taken at the end of his term, and here it describes the ordinary experience of falling asleep. In sleep, the senses and the limbs stop functioning, and what continues is something inward, which is why the Book can use one vocabulary for both states.

The Book makes the comparison explicit in the sūrah of al-Zumar, where it says that God calls back the souls of people at their death and the souls of the living during their sleep, keeping those for whom death has been decreed and releasing the others until an appointed time (39:42). The sūrah of al-Furqān describes the night as a covering, sleep as rest, and the day as a rising (25:47), which is the same three-part sequence this verse follows.

**Waking as a Daily Resurrection**

Then by day He resurrects you: the verse uses resurrection language for the ordinary morning. The point is not decorative. The Book argues for the reality of the final resurrection by pointing to events within human experience that resemble it, and sleep and waking are the most immediate of those events. A person who accepts that he was conscious yesterday, lost that consciousness, and had it returned to him this morning has already accepted the structure of the argument.

The same method appears with the revived earth. The Book describes God sending the winds and driving clouds to a dead land so that rain falls and fruit comes forth, and then says that in the same way He will bring the dead to life (7:57). It repeats the image in the sūrah of the Bee (16:65), the Pilgrimage (22:5), the Spider (29:63), the Romans (30:24), and Fussilat (41:39), and the audience is told in one of those passages that even the idolaters admit who sends the rain.

**The Appointed Term**

Then the verse says that people are revived each day so that a term appointed may be fulfilled. The Book attaches an appointed term to individuals and communities alike, saying that every community has its term and cannot delay or advance it by a single hour (7:34; 10:49), and describing the sun and moon as running their courses for a set period (13:2; 31:29; 35:13; 39:5). The whole creation is described as having been made with a purpose and an appointed term (46:3).

The phrase places the small cycle of sleep and waking inside the larger cycle of a life. Each day that a person is revived is a day added to the course he is running, and the verse treats the supply of days as provision for a task rather than as an entitlement. The Book states that the span is fixed, which is why the demand to hasten a punishment made no sense to a community that could not extend its own lives by a moment.

**The Return and the Reporting**

To Him is your ultimate return, and then He will inform you of what you used to do. The Book uses this closing formula across many sūrahs, sometimes attached to the warning that the guilty will be told what they did, sometimes to the promise that those who did right will be shown their deeds. The sūrah of the Cave states it in almost the same words in the middle of a comparison between two groups of people, and the same ending appears in the sūrahs of Joseph, the Spider, and Luqmān.

The informing is not a formality of the court. The Book describes the record of each person being handed over on that day, with those who receive it in their right hand reading it with joy (17:71; 69:19), and the Book says that every deed, whether small or large, is written down for exactly this purpose. What the verse establishes is that the knowledge described in the previous verse has an outcome: it is not only that God knows, but that the person will be told.

**The Cycle as Provision and Warning at Once**

Read with the verses around it, this one describes the whole apparatus of human life as a loan. The night returns a person to a state like death, the morning gives him back out of mercy, and the days are counted until the term runs out. The Book states the same about the earth, which is revived by rain so that people and animals might eat from it, and it draws the natural conclusion that all of it belongs to the One who runs it.

For an audience that had just been told that the keys of the unseen belong to God and that nothing green or dry escapes His record, this verse explains the practical shape of that knowledge. Every day the account is open, and every night the ledger is held by the One who will read from it at the end.

**The Comfort in the Ordinary Cycle**

The passage also carries a note of mercy that a reader should not miss. Sleep is described in the Book as a mercy, and the sūrah of the Cave explains that God kept the young men of the cave asleep as protection from the city that would have stoned them. A person reading this verse is being told that the ordinary rhythm of every day he has ever lived was maintained by the One who has bound Himself to mercy, and that the same power that returned him to the world each morning will return him to his Lord at the end of the term.
