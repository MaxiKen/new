## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:23

> ˹Also˺ forbidden to you for marriage are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal and maternal aunts, your brother’s daughters, your sister’s daughters, your foster-mothers, your foster-sisters, your mothers-inlaw, your stepdaughters under your guardianship if you have consummated marriage with their mothers—but if you have not, then you can marry them —nor the wives of your own sons, nor two sisters together at the same time —except what was done previously. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

### Expanded Commentary

**A map of permanent family boundaries**

This verse lists women whom a man may never marry because of blood, nursing, or affinity. The list begins with mothers and daughters, sisters, paternal and maternal aunts, nieces, foster mothers and sisters, mothers-in-law, stepdaughters under the specified marital condition, sons’ wives, and the wives of fathers. It closes by forbidding marriage to two sisters at the same time. The verse turns family relationships into protected boundaries rather than matters left to competing custom.

**Blood and nursing**

The inclusion of nursing relationships shows that kinship in Islamic law is not limited to genetic descent. Breastfeeding in the legally recognized manner creates a family relationship with marriage consequences. This protects the social meaning of nursing and prevents a child’s intimate early dependence from being ignored once adulthood arrives. The juristic schools discuss the number and circumstances of feeds that establish the prohibition; families should therefore record such relationships rather than rely on fading memory.

**Affinity and the condition of consummation**

A mother-in-law becomes permanently prohibited upon the marriage contract, while the stepdaughter mentioned in the verse becomes prohibited when the marriage with her mother has been consummated, according to the dominant legal reading. The distinction reflects the different forms of affinity created by a contract and by an established marital household. A person should not make a consequential decision from a partial list; marriage law requires attention to the details of the relationship.

**No simultaneous marriage to close sisters**

The ban on combining two sisters protects the original kinship from being turned into a field of rivalry. It also shows that a prohibition may concern simultaneity rather than the women individually: each sister could, after a lawful end to the first marriage and completion of the waiting period, marry the man, but both cannot be wives at once. The same principle is discussed concerning a woman and her paternal or maternal aunt.

The verse ends by recognizing past cases and by describing God as forgiving and merciful. Mercy does not preserve the custom; it allows people to stop at the point of guidance and rebuild their households within the new boundary.

**Boundaries make trust possible**

The prohibited degrees of marriage preserve spaces in which family care can remain free from sexual competition. They also help people identify obligations that arise through nursing and affinity. Healthy communities need clear boundaries because affection without boundaries can be manipulated by those who have authority over children and dependants.
