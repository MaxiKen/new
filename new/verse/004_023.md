## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:23

> ˹Also˺ forbidden to you for marriage are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal and maternal aunts, your brother’s daughters, your sister’s daughters, your foster-mothers, your foster-sisters, your mothers-inlaw, your stepdaughters under your guardianship if you have consummated marriage with their mothers—but if you have not, then you can marry them —nor the wives of your own sons, nor two sisters together at the same time —except what was done previously. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A List That Defines the Family**

This verse gathers the core of the prohibited degrees — *al-muḥarramāt* — into one place. *Ḥurrimat ʿalaykum ummahātukum wa banātukum wa akhawātukum wa ʿammātukum wa khālātukum wa banātu al-akhi wa banātu al-ukhti wa ummahātukumu allātī arḍaʿnakum wa akhawātukum min al-raḍāʿah wa ummahātu nisāʾikum wa rabāʾibukum allātī fī ḥujūrikum min nisāʾikum allātī dakhaltum bihinna fa in lam takūnū dakhaltum bihinna fa lā junāḥa ʿalaykum wa ḥalāʾilu abnāʾikumu alladhīna min aṣlābikum wa an tajmaʿū bayna al-ukhtayn illā mā qad salaf* — mothers, daughters, sisters, paternal and maternal aunts, brother’s and sister’s daughters, foster-mothers and foster-sisters, mothers-in-law, stepdaughters under guardianship where the mother’s marriage was consummated, sons’ wives, and two sisters together. The previous verse singled out the father’s former wife as a distinct prohibition with three condemnations (4:22); this verse completes the circle. The list is not random; it moves outward from the closest ties — parents and children — to the extended family, then to ties created by nursing and marriage, then to the temporal bar of combining sisters. By naming each degree, the Qur’an removes ambiguity in a context where lineage and inheritance had been blurred, and it gives the new community a clear map of who is permanently outside the choice for marriage.

**Blood, Nursing, and Marriage as Three Kinds of Tie**

The prohibitions rest on three distinct bonds, each treated as creating a sanctity. Blood ties — mothers, daughters, sisters, aunts, nieces — are inviolable because they share a common lineage that marriage would confuse; the sūrah has already rooted humanity in a single soul and its mate (4:1) and built inheritance on that lineage (4:7, 4:11–12). Nursing ties — foster-mothers and foster-sisters — are treated like blood ties, as Prophetic teaching clarified that nursing creates the same prohibitions as birth, so that a foster relationship is not a metaphor but a legal kinship. Marriage ties — mothers-in-law, stepdaughters, sons’ wives — become prohibitions through the covenant itself. The verse adds a condition for stepdaughters: *allātī fī ḥujūrikum min nisāʾikum allātī dakhaltum bihinna* — those under your guardianship if you have consummated marriage with their mothers. If consummation has not occurred, the bar does not apply. That condition was noted as precise mercy: the prohibition attaches where intimacy has already joined households, not where a contract was made but not completed. The same precision applies to the bar on combining two sisters — *an tajmaʿū bayna al-ukhtayn* — which forbids having both as wives at the same time, while after death or divorce the other sister becomes permissible, protecting the bond between sisters from rivalry in one home.

**Why Each Mention Matters**

Each name in the list answers a real temptation or custom. Marrying a daughter or a sister was already seen as foul, but the explicit naming removes any claim that near kin could be chosen for advantage. Aunts and nieces were sometimes married for alliance; the verse closes that path to keep the extended family as a space of protection rather than competition. Foster ties mattered in a society where nursing by another woman created lasting family-like bonds; treating those bonds as marriage bars honours the nurture that was given. Mothers-in-law and stepdaughters and sons’ wives protect the household that has already been joined. A man who marries a woman does not then marry her mother; a mother who has given her daughter does not become a rival; a son’s wife is not to be claimed by his father, just as a father’s former wife is not to be claimed by his son (4:22). Two sisters together are barred because sisterhood would be turned into co-wife rivalry, which the verse spares. The sūrah has repeatedly told the community to live with women fairly (4:19) and to speak kindly at wealth division (4:8); these bars are the structure that makes that fairness possible, by keeping roles distinct.

**Except What Was Done Previously and All-Forgiving, Most Merciful**

The exception *illā mā qad salaf* — except what was done previously — appears here for the bar on combining two sisters, as it did for the father’s former wife (4:22). It functions as clemency for marriages contracted before revelation, requiring separation where the bar now applies but not imposing retroactive worldly punishment for what was done without the light of the new limit. The closing *inna Allāha kāna ghafūran raḥīmā* — surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful — gives the reason that correction is met with forgiveness when it is accepted. The sūrah uses the same pairing after defining repentance that must be soon (4:17, 4:26) and after the command to lighten burdens (4:28). Here it tells families that have to untangle prior marriages that the path back is mercy, not humiliation. That mercy does not reopen the prohibited degree; it forgives the past while holding the limit firm for the future. The pattern matches the sūrah’s treatment of limits — *ḥudūd* — elsewhere (4:13–14): the limit is clear and crossing it deliberately has consequence, but turning back when the limit becomes clear is met with pardon.

**Marriage Within Limits, Not Without**

The verse does not only forbid; it implies what remains lawful. The next verse will note that beyond the named degrees marriage remains open when sought through proper dowry and chaste intent (4:24), confirming that outside the barred list the field is lawful when approached in the right way. That framing matters because the list could be read as restriction alone, while the Qur’an presents it as clarification that makes the lawful field safe to enter. A community that knows who is permanently outside choice can choose inside the lawful field without confusion about lineage, which the inheritance laws depend on, and without rivalry between mothers and daughters, sisters, or foster kin. The sūrah’s earlier training in handling wealth as a trust (4:2, 4:5–6) finds a parallel here in handling persons as trusts. Just as wealth has fixed shares and guarded handovers, family has fixed bars and guarded entrances. Both guards come from the same source: the One described as All-Knowing, All-Wise (4:11, 4:26), who knows which ties, if crossed by marriage, would injure the household, and who is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful when the community realigns its marriages to the map He has drawn.
