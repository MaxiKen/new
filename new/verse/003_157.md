## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:157

> Should you be martyred or die in the cause of Allah, then His forgiveness and mercy are far better than whatever ˹wealth˺ those ˹who stay behind˺ accumulate.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Preference Stated in Plain Terms**

The verse states that if the believers are killed or die in the way of God, forgiveness and mercy from God are better than anything their opponents amass. The comparison is between two collections, and the sentence asks the community to weigh them.

The Book uses this comparative form when it wants to place two things side by side in the reader's mind. The wealth that the community's enemies were gathering at that time was the visible measure of success in the world, and the verse puts the forgiveness and mercy of God against it and states that the second is greater. The sentence does not deny the existence of the first; it denies its weight.

**The Two Ways of Dying**

The verse names both dying in battle and dying in the ordinary course of events, and the pairing is repeated in the following verse. The community had just watched men die at the hands of an enemy, and it had also lost people in ways that had nothing to do with the fighting.

The Book's phrasing removes the distinction between the two in terms of the promise. What qualifies a death for the mercy described here is not the manner of it but the cause in which the person was engaged, and the community is taught to stop grading its losses by how much violence attended them.

**The Qualifier "In the Way of God"**

The promise attaches to a person who dies in the way of God, and the qualifier is the important element of the sentence. The Book uses the phrase for conduct undertaken for God's sake, and it uses it in the same way in its instructions about wealth and fighting.

The community that had lost people on the field at Uḥud was engaged in precisely that cause, and the verse tells it what the deaths of those men amounted to. The claim is not that death is good; it is that the cause in which a person dies determines what his death has earned, and the reward named here is forgiveness and mercy rather than the spoils that the sermon writers of later ages placed beside it.

**What Their Opponents Amassed**

The phrase about what they amass has been explained as referring to their wealth, and also more broadly to their collecting of the vanities of this world. Ibn Kathīr took it in the second sense, which widens the contrast the verse draws.

The sūrah had earlier described the wealth that the community's opponents were gathering and had warned the believers against the love of it. The comparison in this verse therefore connects with the surrounding passages: what the enemies of the community accumulate is what their lives are aimed at, and the sentence states that the mercy that attends a death in God's cause is worth more than all of it.

**Forgiveness Named Before Mercy**

The two nouns are placed in an order, with forgiveness first and mercy second. The arrangement reflects the movement a person makes from his own record to his final state: the faults are forgiven, and the mercy follows.

The Book pairs the two often, and the pairing appears in the sūrah's earlier passages about the people whose reward is forgiveness from their Lord. The community that reads this verse in the aftermath of a defeat hears the two words attached to the men it had lost, and the sentence gives it a way to speak about them that does not depend on the fortunes of the day.

**The Book's Descriptions of the Martyrs**

The Book expands the promise in the verses that follow this one, where it tells the community not to consider those killed in the way of God as dead, since they are alive with their Lord and well provided for. Another passage states that those who have believed and emigrated and struggled with their wealth and their lives are greater in rank with God, and that these are the ones who will triumph.

A further passage says of a certain kind of person that he would dedicate his life to seeking the pleasure of God. Taken together, these sentences describe a death that is not a subtraction from the person who suffers it, and the verse before the reader is the compact statement of what the others explain at greater length.

**The Verse as a Reply to the Talk About Staying Home**

The verse answers the argument reported in the preceding sentence, in which the death of a person was attributed to his decision to go out. The reply is not that the decision was insignificant, but that the outcome of a life consecrated to God is not on that scale.

The community was being offered a way of thinking in which caution was the highest value, and the verse replaces it with a comparison that has a different unit entirely. What is at stake is not whether a person returns safely but whether what he has done for God is met with forgiveness and mercy, and the sentence states that the second is worth more than everything the other side gathers.

**What the Community Is Asked to Weigh**

The verse asks the believers to compare two things whose value cannot be measured in the same currency. That is the nature of the instruction, and it is why the Book states it as a comparison rather than as a command.

The community that recites the passage is expected to hold the sentence in view when its own losses come. The comparison is not a consolation offered after the fact; it is a judgement about value that the community is asked to adopt in advance, so that the losses it meets do not shake the estimate on which its conduct depends.

**What the Word for Amassing Covers**

The sentence closes with a comparison between what God gives and what people gather, and Ibn Kathīr read the word for what is gathered as covering wealth and, more broadly, the accumulation of the world's vanities. The comparison is stated as a fact rather than as a recommendation: the believer is not told to despise what others collect, only to measure it against what is promised to those who die in God's cause. The verse's own subject is the death of the community's members, and the sentence answers the fear that those deaths were a loss. The fuller account of the state of the slain is given a dozen verses later, where the Book states that they are alive with their Lord and provided for (3:169), and the two passages are read together as a single answer to the same anxiety.
