## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:166

> ˹Consider the Day˺ when those who misled others will disown their followers—when they face the torment—and the bonds that united them will be cut off.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day the Leaders Retract**

The verse stages the first scene of the disowning it announced at the end of the previous verse: the wrongdoers see that power belongs wholly to God — and here is what that sight contains. *When those who were followed disavow those who followed.* The exegetes identified the parties at several levels: the leaders of idolatry and their adherents; the devils and the human beings who obeyed them; and, in al-Ṭabarī's most comprehensive reading, all who led and all who followed in the ways of error across the whole life of the world. The identification matters less than the structure, because the structure is universal: every hierarchy of misguidance — teacher and disciple, chief and clan, opinion-maker and crowd — is shown dissolving at its foundation on the Day. The disavowal is total: the leader does not merely decline responsibility; he repudiates the relationship itself. The followers he cultivated, flattered, and used become, at the moment of torment, strangers he refuses to have known — and the refusal is the verse's first revelation about the nature of false authority: it was never a bond, only a use.

**The Bonds Cut: Asbāb Severed**

The second clause names what is destroyed with the relationship: *the bonds that united them will be cut off*. The word — *asbāb*, plural of *sabab* — is rich, and the exegetes worked through its senses: a rope, a means of obtaining something, a cause. Applied to the scene, the asbāb are everything by which the two groups were joined and helped each other in the world — the bonds of love, friendship, blood, tribal solidarity, common interest. In the world these were ropes: means by which the follower gained protection and the leader gained numbers, by which the clan defended its own and the party rewarded its loyal. On the Day every rope is severed at both ends. Al-Ṭabarī records a second reading with a sharper edge: the asbāb are the groups' *deeds* — which remain sound in the Hereafter if they were righteous, but are cut off when they were evil, so that the means which served them in the world become means that serve nothing at all. Al-Rāzī adds the practical consequence: there is no way left to stave off the punishment or escape its consequences. The verse thus describes not only a social collapse but a logistical one — the infrastructure of mutual help, which the world mistook for strength, removed entire.

**Why the Bonds Fail**

The failure is not an arbitrary punishment bolted onto the scene; it is the bonds' own nature finally displayed. The sūrah of the Spider states the principle from the idols' side: they were taken *"only to keep ˹the bond of˺ harmony among yourselves in this worldly life"* — and the same verse delivers the verdict: *"But on the Day of Judgment you will disown and curse one another"* (29:25). A solidarity built around the exclusion of God is built around nothing, and when the nothing is unveiled, the solidarity has no floor. This is the inverse of the love the previous verse described: the believers' love, directed wholly at God, binds them to the only Being whose power survives the Day — and, as the reports of loving for God's sake describe, outlasts it in company. The associators' bonds, directed at powerless objects and mutual advantage, dissolve with those objects. The verse's theology of friendship is therefore strict and complete: every human tie is a rope whose strength equals the strength of what it is tied to, and the Day of Judgment is simply the occasion on which the anchorage of every rope is tested at once.

**Satan's Speech and the Fire's Conversations**

The scene this verse opens is filled in elsewhere in the Qur'an with dialogues that carry the disowning to its last word. In the sūrah of Abraham, after judgment is passed, Satan addresses his followers directly: *"Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise. I too made you a promise, but I failed you. I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot save you, nor can you save me"* (14:22) — the arch-leader disavowing not only his followers but his own former partnership, confessing the call was persuasion without power. In the sūrah of the Heights, the Fire's inhabitants enter group cursing group until they are all gathered, and the followers ask God to multiply their leaders' torment — only to be told the doubling has already fallen on all (7:38) — whereupon the leaders turn on the followers: *you were no better than us* (7:39). The mutual disowning is thus shown from every angle: Satan disowns the humans, leaders disown followers, followers attack leaders, and the attack is answered with the leveling insult. The verse of this passage is the summary headline under which all those dialogues are filed.

**The Torment Seen Together**

One clause of the verse fixes the setting with precision: the disowning happens *when they face the torment* — both parties seeing it, simultaneously. The timing is not incidental. In the world, the leader's authority rested partly on his apparent confidence about the unseen — his claim to know how things really are, his dismissal of the warning. The shared sight of the torment destroys that residue: the followers see that the man they trusted is as exposed as they are, and the leaders see that their following is now only an audience to their failure. The disavowal is spoken in full view of the thing both parties spent their lives explaining away. This is the verse's deepest cruelty and its deepest justice: the relationship of misguidance was founded on a shared denial of this exact sight, so the sight's arrival dissolves the relationship automatically — no argument needed, no trial of the bond, only its founding condition reversed. They face the torment together as they faced the warning together; but the facing is now alone-in-company, each group watching the other watch the Fire, with every rope between them cut.

**Reading the Scene Back Into the World**

The verse is eschatological, but the tradition reads it as instruction for the living, because the disowning reveals what the bonds already were. Every reader is positioned by it to audit his own ropes: the loyalties he holds that would survive a test of truth, and the loyalties that are really arrangements of advantage wearing love's language. The leaders the verse describes had followers who loved them as equals to God (2:165) — a love the leaders accepted and exploited, and a love the Day converts into the leader's own panic-stricken repudiation. The warning to the follower is sharper than the warning to the leader, because the follower's sin was trust misplaced: he gave to a human authority the reliance owed to God alone, and the verse shows him the receipt. The community that hears this in the world is being trained in the discrimination the Day will enforce anyway — attach yourself to what cannot disown you, tie your ropes to the One whose power the wrongdoers will see belongs wholly to Him, and treat every solidarity that asks you to exclude His commands as a rope already half-cut.
