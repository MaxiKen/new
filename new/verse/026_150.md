## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:150

> So fear Allah, and obey me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Returns After the Display of Wealth**

Ṣāliḥ has named Thamûd's gardens, crops, palms, and mountain homes. He now returns to the central command: fear Allah and obey the messenger. The repetition gives the material description its meaning. The issue is not whether the people may possess these things, but whether their possession has placed them under gratitude or lifted them into arrogance.

The command is therefore a response to the way prosperity has been interpreted. Thamûd has treated abundance as security and skill as proof of independence. Ṣāliḥ brings the people back to the Lord behind both. Reverence must govern what the community builds, harvests, protects, and expects from the future.


Fear of Allah is not a demand to destroy every home or abandon every field. It is a guard against using blessings without limit. A person with taqwa asks whether his work is honest, whether his comfort harms another, and whether his success has made him less willing to hear correction.

This is why the call follows the list of material goods. The people are being taught to read provision morally. Gardens can feed; homes can shelter; skill can serve. The same things can also feed vanity, concentrate power, and make a society forget its dependence. Taqwa directs the gifts toward their proper ends.

**Obedience Is the Practical Form of Reverence**

The command has two parts because inner awareness must produce outward fidelity. Fear of Allah that never changes behavior remains a claim without proof. Obeying Ṣāliḥ means accepting the instruction he brings even when it limits a profitable habit or challenges a custom protected by the powerful.

A community can admire the messenger's sincerity while refusing his command. That split is precisely what the verse prevents. Trust in Ṣāliḥ must be expressed through obedience to God. The people cannot keep the pleasant part of the message and reject the part that disciplines their appetite.


Ṣāliḥ has already made the same appeal, but repetition is not needless. The people have many layers of confidence to surrender. They trust their land, their crops, their houses, and their technical skill. The repeated command gives the warning time to reach each layer rather than allowing the first refusal to end the conversation.

Quran 7:73 presents Ṣāliḥ's call to worship Allah alone alongside the sign granted to Thamûd. That parallel shows why the command returns: the miracle and the material blessings are both meant to direct the people to God. Repetition is patient instruction before it becomes testimony against them.

**Obedience Protects Against Self-Worship**

A prosperous people may begin by admiring its work and end by admiring itself. The community's name, buildings, and harvests become sacred symbols of its superiority. Ṣāliḥ's command breaks that movement. Fear Allah means that the people remain servants even when their achievements are admired.

Obedience also protects individuals within the society. When the collective identity becomes an idol, dissent is punished as betrayal. Submission to God gives a truthful person permission to resist the crowd without becoming contemptuous of it. The highest loyalty is not to a people against God but to God for the good of the people.


Ṣāliḥ asks the people to obey him because he is God's messenger, not because he seeks to replace God. His own authority is subordinate to the Lord's. A faithful messenger makes the path to God clearer, while a false leader makes followers dependent on his moods, wealth, or status.

This distinction remains important whenever a community organizes around a religious teacher. Obedience cannot mean surrendering conscience to a personality. It means receiving guidance that is measured by revelation and that turns the heart away from worshipping created power.

**The Command Includes a Limit on Pride**

Thamûd may think obedience is a humiliation after the people have demonstrated such skill. Ṣāliḥ reverses that thought. The humiliating condition is to be ruled by appetite, fear the loss of status, and remain unable to admit dependence. Obedience to the Creator is dignity because it frees the servant from those lesser masters.

Taqwa lets a person say, “I was wrong,” even when the admission costs honor. It lets a society change course before its habits become a public disaster. The command is strong because pride is strong, but its purpose is mercy.


Thamûd cannot be governed at once by God's warning and by the assumption that its desires are final. One principle must control the other. Ṣāliḥ asks the people to place revelation above the instincts produced by wealth, architecture, and custom.

That choice appears in small decisions before it appears in a crisis. It is present when a leader accepts correction, when a wealthy person shares, and when a community refuses to exploit a weaker neighbor. Obedience is built through such choices, not announced only when the consequences are visible.


The command closes Ṣāliḥ's first appeal and leaves Thamûd with a clear standard before it responds. Whatever sign or demand comes next, the principle has already been stated. Fear Allah and obey the messenger. The community's later conduct will reveal whether it heard the command as guidance or as an obstacle.

For the reader, the verse joins faith to stewardship. Reverence is not escape from the world, and material success is not safety from God. Use what He gives, submit the use to His limits, and let obedience remain stronger than the desire to preserve a self-made image.

A community that accepts this command can enjoy what it has without being owned by it. Its fields, homes, and technical gifts become instruments of service. The same abundance that once fed pride can then teach humility, because every successful use of it points back to the Lord who made the blessing possible.
