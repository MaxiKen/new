## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148

> and ˹various˺ crops, and palm trees ˹loaded˺ with tender fruit;

**Expanded Commentary**

**Cultivation Shows the Work of Time**

Ṣāliḥ continues describing the abundance around Thamûd through crops and palms carrying tender fruit. The image is agricultural, but its meaning reaches beyond food. A harvest depends on a sequence that cannot be rushed completely: planting, growth, care, ripening, and collection. The people benefit from time ordered by God, not only from their own effort.

This matters to a community tempted by instant confidence. Crops remind it that life develops through dependence and patience. A society can prepare the soil and tend the plants, but it cannot command every condition that makes fruit appear. Its skill operates within a gift.


The palm provides familiar benefits and also shapes the appearance of the land. Its fruit can nourish, while its form and shade can please the eye. The verse allows Thamûd's prosperity to be recognized without turning prosperity into a moral approval of the people.

Quran 16:11 includes date palms among the growth produced through rain, alongside crops and other fruits. The parallel confirms that agriculture is presented as a sign for reflection. The question is not whether the people may enjoy these goods, but whether enjoyment leads them to remember the One who makes them possible.

**Ripeness Contains Fragility**

The translation's image of tender or ripe stalks carries a sense of fruit gathered heavily upon them. The Arabic description has been understood in more than one closely related way: it can suggest slenderness and delicacy, or stalks burdened by produce. Both readings preserve the same lesson. Abundance can make a plant appear strong while also making it vulnerable to weight and season.

Thamûd should recognize itself in that image. A society loaded with resources may look secure, but its very abundance can create pressure. More possessions mean more duties, more people affected by decisions, and more opportunity for pride. Weight is not always a sign of strength; sometimes it is a test of whether support is adequate.


A crop is the result of human labor, but labor does not own the rain, seed, soil, or life within the plant. Gratitude acknowledges work without making work a rival to God. The farmer is a steward who participates in provision rather than its ultimate creator.

This perspective also disciplines the wealthy. A large harvest should not become proof that the person who controls it is superior to those with less. It should increase generosity, careful storage, fair exchange, and concern for people who depend on the land. Provision becomes worship when it is returned to its proper source and used for good.


Crops and palms may support an entire people, yet their benefit can be restricted by those who control land and water. Ṣāliḥ's reminder invites a society to ask whether its prosperity is shared justly. A garden that feeds only the powerful cannot be used as evidence that the community has fulfilled its moral duty.

Justice does not require denying the work of cultivators or the rights of owners. It requires that ownership be bounded by obligations. The Provider sees workers, families, travelers, and animals affected by the harvest. A community that fears God cannot celebrate abundance while ignoring hunger created by its own arrangements.

**Tender Fruit Counters the Myth of Self-Sufficiency**

Fruit appears as a gift that must be received at the right time. It cannot be forced to maturity by pride. The people must wait, observe, and respond to conditions beyond their complete command. That dependence is a quiet lesson against the idea that Thamûd can secure every outcome through skill.

The same lesson applies beyond agriculture. Human projects mature through stages, and some conditions remain outside calculation. Wisdom does not deny planning; it combines planning with humility. Ṣāliḥ's mention of the harvest asks the people to notice how much of their confidence rests on processes they did not invent.

**Abundance Can Become a Distraction**

Fruit, fields, and palms may fill attention with immediate pleasure. A society can become so occupied with producing and consuming that it forgets why it was given capacity in the first place. The blessing then becomes a curtain hiding the Provider and the coming account.

Fear of Allah does not require refusing food or beauty. It requires that the enjoyment remain subordinate to worship and justice. Eat with gratitude, trade honestly, preserve the land, and do not let a full storehouse persuade you that no answer will be required for how it was filled.


The crops and palms are alive, productive, and apparently secure, yet their condition depends on time and care. They offer a picture of a people whose prosperity is real but conditional. Thamûd is not asked to deny what it sees. It is asked to interpret what it sees correctly.

The right interpretation is neither “we made this alone” nor “wealth must be rejected.” It is “God has provided this, and we will answer for its use.” That answer would turn cultivation into gratitude. Without it, the heaviest harvest can become another reason for the people to imagine themselves beyond correction.

The burdened palm also offers a picture of mature responsibility. A community that has received much must support more than its own appetite. It carries workers, dependents, neighbors, and future generations within the weight of its wealth. The harvest is sound only when the structure beneath it is strong enough to bear those duties without crushing the vulnerable. Such strength is measured by justice, not by the size of the storehouse. A full harvest cannot excuse an empty conscience, and a society is not prosperous if its weakest members are made to carry the cost of its display.
