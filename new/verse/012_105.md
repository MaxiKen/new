## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:105

> How many signs in the heavens and the earth do they pass by with indifference!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Exclamation About What Is Overlooked**

The verse opens with a quantitative exclamation: how many. The construction does not ask a question and does not expect an answer. It registers an abundance — signs in great number, encountered constantly — and then states what happens to them. The chapter has just declared that most people will not believe however keen the messenger is, and that the message asks for nothing and functions as a reminder. Verse 105 supplies the diagnosis. The problem is not a shortage of evidence. It is that evidence is met with indifference, and the indifference is described physically: they pass by. The verb is ordinary and repeated, which is what makes the diagnosis severe — nothing dramatic happens at the moment of passing by, and that is the point.

**Two Locations Named**

The signs are in the heavens and in the earth, and the pairing covers the whole of a person's ordinary experience. Above: the sun, the moon, the stars, the alternation of night and day, the rain that ends a famine. Below: the crops, the soil, the animals, the ruins of previous peoples, the bodies people inhabit. The chapter's own narrative has depended on both categories. Joseph's forecast turned on rainfall and harvest (12:47–49); his vision concerned eleven stars, a sun and a moon (12:4); his deliverance from famine turned on grain stored in the earth. The verse names the same two domains and states that they are full of signs that are passed by. The pairing covers the whole of ordinary experience. Nothing in the two domains requires travel, education or revelation to encounter; the heavens are above every person and the earth beneath every person, and both are visible daily. The verse describes not rare phenomena missed through ignorance but constant ones missed through inattention, which is a different and more indictable failure.

**Turning Away From What Is Seen**

The commentators explain the verse in terms of reflection: people continuously encounter signs indicating the being of their Creator together with His wisdom, power and oneness, and they do not consider what the signs mean or take a lesson from them. The Qur'an puts the same point as a question elsewhere: *Who then does more wrong than those who deny Allah's revelations and turn away from them?* (6:157). The wrongdoing identified is not ignorance but avoidance — a person who has the evidence in front of him and declines to look at it. Verse 105 describes the avoidance as habitual: passing by is something people do repeatedly, without effort and without noticing. The habituality is what makes the diagnosis serious. A person who rejects a sign after examining it has performed an act; a person who walks past it has performed nothing, and the verse treats that nothing as the commoner and more dangerous condition. Most of the signs in this chapter's own story were missed by the people nearest them, including a shirt and a dream.

**The Historical Reading**

One line of commentary connects the verse specifically to past civilisations and the traces they leave behind. On that reading the signs in the earth include the ruins, the roads, the abandoned settlements and the recorded ends of peoples who were more powerful than those now walking among their remains. The Qur'an makes the same appeal repeatedly: travel in the land and see what became of those before. The reading gives the verse a concrete object and links it to verse 109, which asks whether the deniers have not travelled through the land to see the end of those destroyed before them. Signs in the earth, on this account, are archaeological as well as agricultural. The double sense fits the chapter's own method. The story of Joseph is itself a sign in the earth — an account of a real country, a real famine and a real family, preserved and reported — and verse 109 will ask whether people have travelled through the land and seen how earlier generations ended. The historical reading connects directly to the narrative the reader has just finished.

**Why the Verse Follows the Story**

The placement is deliberate and it changes how the story is to be read. Verse 102 claimed that the account of Joseph is among the reports of the unseen revealed to the Prophet ﷺ. Verses 103 and 104 stated that most people will not believe and that the message is a reminder requiring no payment. Verse 105 says that people pass by signs indifferently. The chapter is therefore presenting itself as a sign and asking whether it will be treated like the others. The narrative that occupied a hundred verses is not an illustration appended to a doctrine; on the chapter's own account it is one of the things being passed by. That placement turns the narrative into an instance of its own teaching. The reader who has just spent a hundred verses attending closely to a shirt, a cup and a dream has demonstrated the opposite of passing by; the verse then asks how most people behave toward the signs in the heavens and the earth. The story stands beside the diagnosis as its counter-example.

**The Chapter Itself Contains the Signs**

Nothing in verse 105 requires the reader to leave the sūrah to find examples. A boy's vision fulfilled decades later in a specific gesture by eleven brothers and two parents. A fifteen-year agricultural cycle predicted in detail, including a year that was not in the dream. A torn shirt that established innocence from its direction of tearing. A false accusation overturned by a public confession. A forgotten message that delayed a release for years and then produced a government. A cup planted in a bag under a law supplied by the accused. The chapter has narrated sign after sign, and verse 105 asks what happens to them. The question is not rhetorical for a reader who has just spent a hundred verses inside the material being described.

**Indifference as the Diagnosis**

The verse does not identify the obstacle to belief as stupidity, malice, insufficient evidence or bad argument. It identifies it as passing by. That diagnosis is more severe than the alternatives, because it locates the failure in attention rather than in capacity. A person who lacks evidence cannot be blamed for lacking it; a person who walks past evidence daily and does not look at it is described in different terms. The chapter's account of its own audience is therefore not that people are unconvinced but that they are inattentive, and the remedy implied is not more evidence but a pause. Verse 111 will name the people for whom the pause has already happened. Naming them is how the chapter closes the gap. Between this verse and the last one the difference is stated as a difference in hearing rather than in evidence: the same signs are available to everybody, and some people turn toward them. The final verse of the sūrah describes that turning as belief, and describes what it produces as guidance and mercy.

**The Verse Explains the Shape of the Whole Sūrah**

If inattention is the obstacle, then a narrative is the appropriate response, and this chapter is a narrative. A list of propositions can be declined without being heard; a story has to be followed in order to be refused. The sūrah's decision to spend a hundred verses on one family, one well, one prison and one granary is therefore a method chosen for a diagnosed condition. The reader is not asked to assent to anything until verse 102, and by then he has already spent time inside the material. Verse 105 explains why that arrangement was necessary: signs are passed by when they are encountered briefly, and the chapter's response is to make the encounter long enough that passing by is no longer available.
