## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:38

> Indeed, Allah defends those who believe. Surely Allah does not like whoever is deceitful, ungrateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Defence Is God's Action, Not Theirs**

*Inna Allāha yudāfiʿu ʿan alladhīna āmanū* — God defends those who believe. The verb is from the root shared with the "repelling" of verse 40, and the Arabic is emphatic: God *repels from* the believers, pushes away on their behalf.

The subject of the sentence is worth holding on to. The believers are not the ones doing the defending in this verse, and the sūrah will grant them permission to fight only in the verse after next. Verse 38 is placed first so that the permission, when it comes, cannot be read as the mechanism of their survival.

That ordering is the verse's main work. A community about to be authorized to use force is told, before it is authorized, that its defender is God. The permission does not transfer the defence to them; it enrolls them in a defence that was already under way.

The verb form carries the continuity: *yudāfiʿu* is the intensive stem, the form for repeated and forceful action, and it is in the present tense — God defends, keeps defending, is defending. The sentence does not describe a single intervention but a standing activity, which is why the *inna* opens it: this is the settled state of affairs, not a promise about one battle. A community told that its defence is continuous has been told that no interval of danger falls outside it.

**The Root Shared With Verse 40**

The word used here reappears two verses later in the famous statement that had God not repelled some people by means of others, monasteries and synagogues and mosques would have been destroyed.

The repetition ties the two verses into a single principle. God's defence of the believers is one instance of a general government of history: He restrains, balances and pushes back, and the preservation of anything — a community, a building, a place of worship — is owed to that restraint rather than to the virtue of the people inside it.

Read together, the two verses also remove any suggestion that the believers' fighting is what protects worship. God repels; the believers, in certain defined circumstances, are permitted to take part.

The principle is stated as general history in Sūrah al-Baqarah, in the aftermath of David and Goliath: *“Had Allah not repelled a group of people by ˹the might of˺ another, corruption would have dominated the earth, but Allah is Gracious to all”* (2:251). The same *dafʿ*, the same unnamed agents, the same counterfactual — what is preserved there is the earth from corruption, what is preserved here is the believers from their persecutors and worship from destruction. The verse's defence is one instance of a government that runs everywhere.

**"God Loves Not Every Treacherous Ingrate"**

*Inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla khawwānin kafūr* — God does not love any treacherous, ungrateful person. Two vices are named in one phrase, and they belong together.

*Khawwān* is the intensive form: a person given to betrayal, for whom treachery is a habit rather than a lapse. *Kafūr* is likewise intensive — not merely ungrateful but a denier of what he has been given. The pairing describes a character rather than an act: the man who betrays trusts and then denies that he was ever trusted with anything.

The Qur'an states the same judgement elsewhere in almost the same words, which confirms that this is a standing principle rather than a remark about one group.

The same verdict is pronounced where treachery is most tempting — in the handling of treaties with a party expected to break them: *“And if you ˹O Prophet˺ see signs of betrayal by a people, respond by openly terminating your treaty with them. Surely Allah does not like those who betray”* (8:58). Even anticipating the other side's betrayal does not license one's own; the instruction is to terminate openly, in daylight, rather than to answer secret breach with secret breach. Verse 38 is that ruling stated as a principle before the permission arrives.

**Why This Verse Precedes the Permission to Fight**

The commentators report an occasion that makes the sequence intelligible. Some believers still in Makkah, suffering persecution, wanted to respond with treacherous methods — assassination, deception — and the revelation answered first with this verse and then, in verse 39, with permission to fight openly.

That sequence is the whole moral structure of the passage, and it is worth stating as clearly as the text does. The permission to use force is granted one verse *after* the prohibition of treachery, which means the permission arrives already bounded. You may fight; you may not betray.

The report also explains why the second vice is ingratitude. A person who has been wronged and concludes that he is therefore free of the rules is denying the good he received — including the good of being forbidden to deceive.

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the boundary in its most demanding form: *“Do not let the hatred of a people lead you to injustice. Be just! That is closer to righteousness”* (5:8). The hatred is not denied — the believers hated their persecutors with reason — but it is disqualified as a ground for action. That is the same structure as verses 38 and 39 taken together: the injury is real, the permission is granted, and the hatred the injury produced is not permitted to write the rules of the response.

**Treachery Has No Good Ends**

The temptation the verse addresses is the most durable one in political ethics: that a just cause licenses unjust means, and that the urgency of the situation suspends the ordinary prohibitions.

The Qur'an's answer is structural rather than consequentialist. It does not argue that treachery fails, though it often does; it states that God does not love the treacherous, which settles the matter regardless of outcome. A victory obtained by betrayal is not reclassified as righteous by its result.

The tradition recorded a corresponding image for the Day of Resurrection, and the report al-Qurṭubī cites is a well-attested one. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: "A flag will be fixed on the Day of Resurrection for every betrayer, and it will be announced, 'This is the betrayal of so-and-so, the son of so-and-so.'" It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (6178, and again at 6966).

The image is exact. Betrayal is a hidden act — that is what makes it betrayal — and the punishment announced for it is that it will be made public, with the betrayer's name and his father's name attached to it. The thing he did in secret becomes the thing he is known by in front of everyone.

**Why Ingratitude Is Named Alongside Treachery**

The pairing of the two vices is closer than it looks. *Kafūr* — the habit of denying what one has received — is, on the Qur'an's account, what produces treachery in the first place.

A person who remembers what he was given remembers that he is a trustee, and a trustee who betrays his trust has not merely broken a rule; he has denied the relationship that made the rule. That is why the Qur'an so often couples the two: the man who is ungrateful to God becomes untrustworthy with people, because gratitude is the recognition of having been entrusted.

The application to the occasion of this verse is direct. A believer who had received the faith, the community and the promise of divine defence, and who concluded from his suffering that he was free to deceive, was denying every one of those things at once. The sūrah names the root rather than the branch.

**The Verse's Two Halves Belong Together**

Defence and prohibition are not two unrelated sentences that happen to sit in one verse. They are the same claim from two sides: God is the believer's protector, and God is not on the side of the deceiver.

A community that has been promised divine defence has no need of treachery, and a community that uses it has, by that act, abandoned the protection it was promised. That is the argument of the verse, and the permission that follows in verse 39 is granted to people who have just been told it.

The prohibition's positive form is the keeping of pledges: *“Honour Allah's covenant when you make a pledge, and do not break your oaths after confirming them, having made Allah your guarantor”* (16:91). The oath has Allah as its guarantor, which means breaking it is not merely an offence against the other party but a denial of the guarantee — treachery and ingratitude in a single act, the two vices of this verse named together. A community under divine defence keeps its word because its word is held by its Defender.
