## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:152

> who spread corruption throughout the land, never setting things right.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Corruption Is a Way of Using Power**

Ṣāliḥ explains why the people must not follow the transgressors: they spread corruption through the land and do not set matters right. The charge is not a vague insult. It identifies the effect of their leadership. They use authority, resources, and influence in ways that damage the order God has entrusted to human beings.

Corruption can appear in violence, dishonest exchange, oppression of the weak, abuse of land, and the distortion of truth. Its forms differ, but its direction is the same: it breaks relationships and removes things from the balance in which they should serve life. A prosperous society can be corrupt even when its surface looks impressive.


The verse places corruption beside the failure to set matters right. The opposite of fasād is not mere quietness. It is iṣlāḥ, active repair and restoration. A leader who avoids obvious scandal but leaves injustice untouched has not fulfilled the duty of reform. What matters is whether harm is corrected and order is returned to justice.

This distinction prevents a shallow definition of peace. A society may be calm because the powerless have been silenced. It may look stable because wrongdoing is protected. True repair brings hidden harm into the open, restores rights, and creates conditions in which people can live without fear of arbitrary power.

**Corrupt Leaders Can Call Themselves Reformers**

Those who cause harm rarely describe themselves as corrupt. They may claim to be defending the people, preserving tradition, or bringing progress. Quran 2:11–12 describes a related posture in which people deny that they spread corruption and present themselves as reformers. The parallel shows why self-description cannot be the measure of a leader's work.

Results and methods must be examined. Does the policy protect life or consume it? Does it make truth easier to speak or punish correction? Does it distribute benefit fairly or enrich a narrow circle? A title such as reformer has no value when the actual effect is damage.


The land includes the physical environment, but the phrase also embraces the human order established upon it. Corruption can wound rivers and fields, and it can wound trust, family life, law, and worship. Ṣāliḥ's warning is broad because transgression rarely stays in one area. Disorder in power spreads through the whole community.

Quran 7:56 likewise forbids spreading corruption after the land has been set right and joins the warning to hope and fear of Allah. That parallel connects environmental and social responsibility with worship. A people does not honor God by praying while using its authority to ruin the world around it.


It is easy to identify a corrupt leader and still fail to repair what he damaged. Restoration requires truthful judgment, protection for victims, and patient rebuilding of trust. It may also require changing the systems that rewarded the wrongdoing. Ṣāliḥ's phrase “do not set things right” shows that the failure is active: the transgressors either refuse repair or make it impossible.

A just community must therefore measure leaders by what they restore, not only by what they promise. Apologies without restitution leave the injured carrying the cost. New buildings without honest law leave the underlying corruption intact. Iṣlāḥ joins moral intention to concrete correction.

**Corruption Often Hides Behind Prosperity**

A people can point to gardens, crops, and homes as proof that its leadership works. Material output may distract from the people excluded, exploited, or silenced to produce it. Ṣāliḥ asks Thamûd to look beneath visible success and inspect the relationships that make success possible.

Prosperity is not proof of justice, just as poverty is not proof of guilt. The moral question concerns how resources are gained and used. A community may be rich and well ordered when it protects rights, or rich and corrupt when wealth is built on domination. The land remembers more than the monuments display.


The transgressors lead, but the community is warned not to follow. This means ordinary people are not powerless in the moral sense. They may not control every institution, yet they can refuse praise for injustice, protect one another, and withhold cooperation from harmful commands where they can.

Responsibility should be assigned carefully. A victim is not blamed for the harm done to him, and a powerless person may be unable to resist openly. The verse addresses those who choose to follow and support corruption despite knowing what it does. Their obedience helps a bad order continue.

**Repair Begins With Accurate Perception**

People cannot correct what they insist is already good. The first step of iṣlāḥ is to name harm honestly, even when the harm is committed by respected leaders. Ṣāliḥ gives Thamûd a vocabulary for seeing its condition. The transgressors are not merely energetic; they are crossing limits and leaving the land unrepaired.

That clarity can feel threatening because it removes the comfort of vague language. Yet truthful naming is mercy. It gives the community a chance to change direction before corruption becomes the only arrangement it knows. A land is set right when its people prefer justice to the reputation of being orderly.

**The Warning Protects the Future**

Corruption passes from one generation to another when its beneficiaries call it normal. Repair interrupts that inheritance. It preserves land, law, worship, and human dignity for people who are not yet able to defend themselves. Ṣāliḥ's prohibition therefore has a future-facing concern: do not hand your children a damaged order and call it success.

For the reader, the verse asks whether influence is being used to maintain appearances or to restore what has been harmed. Follow no command that spreads destruction while refusing repair. Fear Allah by making the world around you more truthful and just, not merely more impressive.
