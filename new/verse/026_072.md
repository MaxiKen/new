## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:72

> Abraham asked, “Can they hear you when you call upon them?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham Tests Worship Through a Basic Human Relation**

Abraham asks whether the idols can hear when people call upon them. The question begins with a capacity that any meaningful caller expects from a responsive addressee. Hearing is not the whole of divinity, but its absence exposes a basic problem. The worshippers direct appeals toward objects that do not receive the appeals in the human sense.

The question is careful. Abraham does not begin with an obscure argument about metaphysics. He asks about what happens when the worshipper calls. Does the idol perceive the request, distinguish the voice, and respond to the person standing before it? If not, the ritual has the outward form of communication without an answering subject. The community must explain why it calls to something unable to hear.

**Calling Reveals What the Worshipper Expects**

A person calls upon an idol because he expects some relation to it. He may seek protection, blessing, healing, or help. The call therefore uncovers the worshipper's idea of the object's role. Abraham does not need to list every hope attached to the idol. He asks whether the object can receive the call on which those hopes depend.

This test distinguishes symbolic remembrance from worship. A person can use a symbol to recall a teaching without believing that the symbol hears him. Worship crosses into a different category when the object is addressed as a power capable of response. Abraham's question targets that claim. The issue is not whether an image can stir emotion, but whether it can hear the prayer directed to it.

**Hearing Is a Sign of Relation, Not the Whole Definition of God**

The question should not be mistaken for a claim that God is limited to a created faculty of hearing. Divine hearing is perfect and unlike the hearing of bodies. Abraham uses a humanly understandable capacity to expose the idols' helplessness. He is not defining God by comparison with an image. He is showing that the image lacks even the basic relation its worshippers assume.

This method makes the argument accessible without reducing its subject. A true Lord hears in a manner fitting divine perfection, while a stone or carved figure has no independent perception. The community knows the difference from ordinary experience. The idols remain dependent on the worshippers for their placement, care, and voice.

**The Question Creates Space for Honest Admission**

Abraham's form invites an answer that can be checked. The people do not need to accept his entire theology before considering whether the idols hear. They can look at the objects and ask what they actually do when called. This keeps the debate from being hidden behind the authority of priests or the age of the custom.

A sincere question can loosen the hold of a false practice because it makes the worshipper separate experience from assumption. The idol may be surrounded by ceremony, but ceremony does not create ears. The community must decide whether it will follow the question to its conclusion or protect devotion by refusing to examine its object.

**Silence From the Idol Is Not a Sacred Mystery**

A worshipper may explain the idol's silence by saying that its response is hidden or spiritual. Abraham's question challenges that escape. If the object cannot hear, then the problem is not that the answer is delayed. The object lacks the capacity the worshipper's call presumes. Mystery should not be used to give a created thing powers that observation does not support.

This does not deny that God's response can be delayed or that prayer may involve unseen wisdom. The distinction is between a living Lord who hears and an idol that has no independent life. Abraham's reasoning protects the unseen from being confused with the inert. Faith in the invisible God is not the same as assigning invisible power to a visible object without proof.

**A Call That Cannot Be Heard Cannot Ground Trust**

Trust requires a real relation between the one who calls and the one expected to answer. The idols' inability to hear makes the worshippers' dependence empty at its foundation. They may feel comfort near the shrine, but comfort can arise from habit and group support rather than from any response by the idol.

Abraham's question therefore moves from ritual to reality. He is asking what kind of relationship the people have constructed. If their devotion is directed to objects that cannot hear, then the community has placed its hopes where no answer can come. The next question will test another part of the same claim: whether the idols can affect the worshippers' lives.

**The Question Applies Beyond Carved Images**

Physical idols make the issue easy to see, but the underlying test can be applied to any substitute for God. Wealth cannot hear a prayer, status cannot receive a call, and a political symbol cannot become a lord because people address it with devotion. A created means may help within the limits God gives it, but it cannot be made divine by the language used toward it.

Abraham's question remains a discipline of the heart. Before calling upon something, ask what it is, what it can do, and whether the dependence being placed upon it is rightful. The answer should lead worship back to the One who truly hears.

The ability to hear is therefore a sign of living relation, not a decorative detail. Abraham asks the community to notice whom it is addressing before it asks for help. The question turns a familiar gesture into a moment of accountability: the worshipper must identify the listener, not merely repeat the call. Devotion cannot be justified by the intensity of the caller when the called object is absent from the relation.
