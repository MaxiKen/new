## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:138

> This is an insight to humanity —a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Behind "Exposition"**

The verse describes what has been sent down as an exposition for mankind, and the noun behind the rendering is one of the names the tradition has attached to the Book itself. Its root carries the sense of making a matter clear and separating it from confusion.

The choice of the word at this point in the sūrah is deliberate. The community has just been told to travel and to consider how earlier peoples ended, and it has been given a description of its own conduct in the verses about giving, anger, and forgiveness. The verse now states what the material it has been receiving does: it makes things clear.

**What "This" Points To**

The commentators disagreed in a small and instructive way about the scope of the demonstrative at the beginning of the verse. One view holds that it refers to the whole Book, so that the sentence is a statement about revelation as such. Another holds that it refers to what has just been said in the preceding verses about the battle, the warnings, and the description of the righteous.

Both readings fit the sūrah's movement. The verses around this one have moved from an account of a battle to its lessons, and the sentence states that the whole of the material, wherever its limits are drawn, belongs to the same purpose. The disagreement itself shows how closely the commentators read the connections between consecutive verses.

**Addressed to Mankind**

The verse states that the exposition is for mankind, and the phrase gives the revelation a scope that no community can claim for itself. The Book describes itself as a reminder and a clarification for people generally, and it addresses its arguments to human beings as such.

This universal framing has a practical consequence in the passage. The community that had lost a battle is told that what it carries is not its own private possession but a message with a claim on everyone. That is the same understanding the sūrah states later when it describes the community as one brought forth for the benefit of humanity, and the two statements belong together.

**Guidance and Exhortation as a Pair**

The verse adds that the revelation is a guidance and an exhortation for those who are mindful of God, and the second word refers to a form of speech that moves a person to act rather than merely informing him.

The pairing appears elsewhere in the Book for the earlier scriptures as well, where the Gospel is described in the same terms as a guidance and an exhortation for the reverent. The Book treats its own message and the messages that came before it as one continuous work of clarification, and the vocabulary of this verse is part of that treatment.

**Why the Reverent Are Named**

The verse's second half narrows its audience, and the narrowing is not a restriction of the message's scope but a statement about where its effect appears. The exposition is for all people; the guidance and the exhortation reach those who approach it with the disposition the Book names here.

The sūrah has already introduced this quality in its opening verses, where the Book is described as a guidance for those who are mindful of God. The pattern is consistent across the Book: the same message clarifies for everyone and guides those who receive it with the awareness it asks for. The two audiences are distinguished by their response rather than by anything in the message.

**Exhortation and the Book's Way of Persuading**

The term translated as exhortation covers the kind of speech that warns and encourages at once, and the Book uses it for the preaching of the prophets. It appears in the description of the earlier scriptures and in the commands given to the messengers about how they should address their peoples.

The Book's manner of persuading people therefore goes beyond the statement of facts and commands. It appeals to the consequences of conduct, to the record of history, and to the character of God, and the word in this verse names that appeal as one of the functions of the revelation itself.

**The Verse's Work in the Passage**

The verse stands between the command to consider the ends of earlier peoples and the two commands that follow it, which tell the believers not to weaken and not to grieve. Placed where it is, the sentence explains why the community should be able to absorb the lesson of its own losses.

The losses at Uḥud were severe enough to invite the conclusion that the community had been abandoned. The verse answers that conclusion in advance by stating what the message the community carries actually is: a clarification for everyone, and a guidance and an exhortation for those who take it seriously. The Book's own presence among them was the reason for confidence, and the next verses build on that ground.

**A Statement About the Book Itself**

Sentences of this kind, in which the revelation describes its own purposes, recur throughout the Book and serve a function in the community's life. They teach those who recite it what to expect from it and what to ask of it.

A reader who takes this verse seriously approaches the text expecting clarification, direction, and the kind of warning that moves a person to change his conduct. The sūrah supplies all three in the passage that follows, and the instruction about patience, generosity, and the handling of grief is presented as the working out of exactly what this verse says the Book does.
