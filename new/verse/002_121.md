## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:121

> Those We have given the Book follow it as it should be followed. It is they who ˹truly˺ believe in it. As for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Recipients of the Book**

The verse describes people who were given a scripture and then treats their relation to it as the measure of their belief. Commentators differed on whether the recipients are the Muslims or the learned among the People of the Book, and both readings are supported by the surrounding passage.

The question is answered by the criterion the sentence sets rather than by the identity of the group. What the verse describes is a way of holding a revelation, and it applies to whoever holds one in that way. The sūrah has been addressing communities that received books, and it now states what receiving one requires. The phrase also reminds the reader that no community holds a scripture by right. The Book uses the language of receiving throughout this passage, so that a community's possession of revelation is understood as a gift entrusted to it rather than as an achievement.

**Following the Book as It Should Be Followed**

The Arabic verb rendered as recite also means to follow, and many commentators preferred the second sense here. To follow a scripture properly, on that reading, is to act on what it commands and to abstain from what it forbids, so that the text governs conduct rather than merely informing it.

Where the verb is read as reciting, the commentators described the manner of recitation: with humility, with attention, and with the intention of learning what the text requires. Both readings converge on the same point. The Book is not held properly by being possessed or quoted; it is held properly by being obeyed. The Book's criterion also excludes a middle course in which the text is honoured while its requirements are postponed. What the sentence describes is a settled practice of following, and the contrast it draws is with people who possess the same text and do something else with it.

**The Case of the People of the Book**

Al-Ṭabarī applied the verse to the learned among the People of the Book and explained that following the Torah properly means abstaining from what it forbids and partaking of what it permits. He also drew out the implication the sūrah has been developing throughout the passage, which is that the Torah itself points to the prophet to come, so that following it faithfully leads a person to accept him.

That reading is important for understanding the sūrah's argument. The verse does not set the earlier scripture against the later revelation; it presents the earlier scripture as leading to what confirms it. The community that followed its book as it should be followed would recognise the messenger whose coming the book described.

**The Believers as Recipients of the Book**

The verse also applies to the Muslim community, which had received the same kind of trust in a new form, and the Book addresses the same warning to it elsewhere when it describes people who recite the revelation without acting on it. The criterion in this sentence therefore functions as a standard the new community will be judged by.

The sūrah's later passages instruct the Muslims to hold firmly to what they have been given, and they use the same vocabulary of grasping and following that appears here. What the verse establishes is a relationship rather than a status: the Book is given to be followed, and the community that holds it is answerable for how it does so.

**Belief and Loss**

The sentence pairs belief with following and unbelief with loss, and the Arabic term for loss describes a ruined transaction, the failure of a venture to return what was invested in it. The sūrah has used commercial language throughout this passage for the exchange of guidance for worldly advantage, and the word here belongs to that vocabulary.

The pairing also reflects the Book's standard of judgment, which concerns what a person does with what he has been given. The verse does not say that those who possess a book are saved by possessing it, nor that those who receive it are considered believers regardless of conduct. It states that the result depends on the way the gift is handled.

**What the Verse Says to the Later Community**

The sentence belongs to a passage in which the sūrah is describing the People of the Book and the claims they made about their standing, and the criterion it states applies to the community now receiving the revelation. A Muslim reader who treats the Qur'an as an inheritance to be defended rather than a text to be followed has placed himself in the position the verse does not praise.

The Book states the same principle in other chapters when it describes people who recite the revelation without being shaped by it, and it warns the believers against the fate of communities whose scriptures became a matter of identity rather than of practice. The verse in this place is therefore both a description of the faithful and a warning to those who might take the same gift for granted.

**The Connection With What Follows**

The next verse returns to the children of Israel with the command to remember God's favours, and the transition indicates what the sūrah is doing. Having stated what following a book means, it turns to the community's history and sets out the favours that made such following possible, and then calls Abraham to mind as the person whose faith the Book upholds against the claims of later communities. The sentence also prepares the reader for the reminder that follows, which returns the community to the favours it had received. Having stated what following a book requires, the sūrah calls the community back to the generosity that made its Book possible. The two verses belong together as an instruction and its context.

**Two Ways of Holding a Book**

The sentence sets out a contrast that runs through the sūrah's dealings with the People of the Book. A scripture can be held as a trust, in which case its commands shape the life of the community that received it, or as a possession, in which case it supplies identity and argument while its requirements go unattended. The Book has described the second way in detail: the illiterate who knew nothing but reports, the forgers who wrote with their own hands, the book cast behind the back, and the covenant broken while captives were ransomed. Against all of that the verse states the first way in a single sentence, and it attaches belief to it. The community receiving the final revelation is addressed by the same criterion, since the Book warns it in this sūrah against reciting revelation without acting on it. What the reader is given here is a test that applies to any community that holds a text.
