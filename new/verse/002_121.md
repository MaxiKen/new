## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:121

> Those We have given the Book follow it as it should be followed. It is they who ˹truly˺ believe in it. As for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Following as the True Recitation**

The verse defines the Book's real readers, and the definition turns on a verb with a double root-sense that the exegetes exploit deliberately. *Yatlūnahu* — they recite it — comes from *talā*, which carries both recitation and following: to recite the Book and to follow it are, in the word's own structure, one act seen from two sides. Many of the commentators prefer the following-sense as the verse's operative meaning — those who follow the Book *as it should be followed* — and the preference is doctrinal rather than merely lexical. Recitation without following is the performance the passage behind this verse has been prosecuting: the hearers-and-disobeyers of verse 93, the reciters whose Scripture testified against their conduct. Following gives the verse's standard its content: to follow the Torah properly, al-Ṭabarī specifies, is to abstain from what it forbids and partake of what it allows — the Book's permissions and prohibitions taken as operative law rather than inherited text. Al-Rāzī adds the interior condition: proper recitation is done with humility, the reader's posture matching the text's claim. The verse thereby installs a three-layer standard — read, obey, revere — and makes belief in the Book a function of all three: *it is they who truly believe in it*. Belief is not assent beside the Book but the Book's own operation in a life, and the verse's first sentence is the recital's answer, in positive form, to everything the hundred verses before it filed in negative: the distortion, the rationing, the casting behind backs all failed the same standard this verse states.

**Who Holds the Book Here**

The subject's identification runs in two directions in the exegetical tradition, and both belong to the verse's design. Al-Ṭabarī records the reading that takes *those We have given the Book* as the Muslim believers — the community holding the Qur'an, reciting-and-following it as it should be — and the reading that takes them as the learned among the Jews who follow the Torah faithfully, the remnant the recital keeps sighting at every station. The double reference is not confusion but the verse's method: the standard it states is Book-generic, and every community of a Book is measured by it, so the subject-phrase denotes whoever actually meets the standard — the Muslim follower of the Qur'an and the Jewish follower of the Torah alike, and only them. Sūrah al-Māʾidah supplies the same division inside one community: *had they observed the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord, they would have been overwhelmed with provision from above and below — some among them are upright, yet many do nothing but evil* (5:66). The uprightness-and-evil split is the verse's two classes restated: the following few and the rejecting many, divided not by affiliation but by conduct toward the held text. The reading's consequence for the Madinan moment is precise: the verse is not a blanket transfer of election from one people to another but the installation of a criterion that cuts through every people — and the community hearing it inherits the criterion along with the promise, since *those We have given the Book* includes them from the moment the Book was given.

**The Torah That Leads Onward**

The tradition draws the standard's sharpest consequence for the verse's historical subjects, and the consequence runs through the Torah's own content. To follow the Torah as it should be followed is, on al-Ṭabarī's reading, ultimately to follow the Prophet ﷺ — because the Torah demands it: the unlettered Messenger whose description its holders *find written in their Torah and the Gospel*, whom they are commanded to honor, support, and follow (7:157). The logic is the confirmation-argument the recital has been running since verse 89: a Book truly followed leads its follower to whatever the Book itself testifies to, and a testimony refused is proof that the Book is not being followed but used. The converse completes the circle — to reject the confirmed Messenger is to reject the confirming Torah, since the rejection contradicts the text the rejecter claims to hold. Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the obligation from the Gospel's side in the same grammar: *let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah has revealed in it — and those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are truly the rebellious* (5:47). The verse's two classes thus sort themselves by a single test available to both communities: does your following of your Book carry you to the truth your Book announces? The followers whom verse 121 pronounces believers are, on this reading, the scholars of Israel who followed their Torah into the mosque of Madina — and the recital's audience knew such people by name, the converts whose faith the verse vindicates against both their former community's taunts and any believer's suspicion.

**The Losers**

The verse's second sentence names the other class, and the naming is economic: *as for those who reject it, it is they who are the losers* — *khāsirūn*, the ones whose transaction ends in loss, the sūrah's standing term for the trade of capital for nothing. The word's force depends on what is being lost, and the verse's context makes the loss total in a specific way: the rejecters are not people without capital but people rejecting the capital they hold. The Book given, the description found, the testimony known — all of it is the rejecter's own asset, and rejection is not the absence of a purchase but the disposal of a holding at a price of zero. The recital's economics has filed the same loss at every scale: the signs sold cheap (41), the souls sold for magic (102), the Hereafter traded for the world (86) — and verse 121 states the loss in its purest form, where what is sold is the seller's own title-deed. The two sentences' exhaustive division leaves no third class, and the absence of a middle category is the verse's rigor: there is no standing of nominal holding, no position of respectful non-following, no account of the Book kept open but unused. Recite-and-follow, believe; reject, lose — the verse's arithmetic admits no holding-pattern between the two, which is its permanent discomfort for every community of every Book, this one included.

**The Standard Applied Inward**

The verse's final register is its application to the community that recites it, and the application is immediate because the standard is generic. *Those We have given the Book* — the phrase now denotes the Muslims in the plainest sense: a community given a Book, measured by the same three layers the verse installed — recitation, following, humility. The audit the verse runs on Israel's learned is the audit that runs on every holder: is the Book followed as it should be followed — its forbiddens abstained, its permissions taken, its testimony obeyed even where the testimony costs? The recital has prepared the audit's severity by documenting the failure-modes in a hundred verses, and its mercy by naming the remnant at every station — the few who kept the charter, the upright among them, the believers the verse pronounces. Between the modes and the remnant stands the verse's actual question, which is not historical but present: which class does the current recitation belong to? The question cannot be answered by affiliation, since the verse's own double-reference destroyed affiliation as a criterion; it is answered only by the following, in the three layers, at the current station of the reader's life. The losers are named last because the naming is the verse's warning to its own people: the Book in hand is capital, the following is the investment, and the loss pronounced on the rejecters is available to every holder who recites without following — the same Book, the same standard, the same two classes, with membership determined verse by verse, day by day, in the only ledger the recital has ever recognized.
