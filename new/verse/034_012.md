## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:12

> And to Solomon ˹We subjected˺ the wind: its morning stride was a month’s journey and so was its evening stride. And We caused a stream of molten copper to flow for him, and ˹We subjected˺ some of the jinn to work under him by his Lord’s Will. And whoever of them deviated from Our command, We made them taste the torment of the blaze.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb Left Out, and What Fills the Gap**

The Arabic opens with *wa li-Sulaymāna al-rīḥ* — and to Solomon, the wind — with no verb between them. Classical Arabic permits this ellipsis, and the reader supplies the missing word from context. Two candidates fit. One carries over *We gave* from the account of David two verses earlier, which keeps the whole passage under the heading of gift. The other imports *We subjected* from the parallel passages, as in Ṣād: *"So We subjected to him the wind, blowing gently at his command to wherever he pleased"* (38:36), and al-Anbiyāʾ: *"And to Solomon We subjected the raging winds, blowing by his command"* (21:81). The translation here takes the second, and the choice is sound because subjection is the technical relationship the Qur'an uses for Solomon throughout. The omission itself is worth a moment. By leaving the verb out, the sentence puts Solomon and the wind in direct grammatical contact, with no action word standing between them — a compression that matches the content.

**A Month in the Morning, a Month in the Evening**

The measurement is given in units a caravan trader would understand: *ghudūwuhā shahrun wa rawāḥuhā shahr*. *Ghudūw* is the outward journey at first light; *rawāḥ* is the return leg in the late afternoon. Both are commercial terms from the vocabulary of travel, not poetic words for time of day. So the claim is quantitative: a stage of travel that would take a caravan a month was covered before midday and again before nightfall. Stating a miracle in the units of a trade route rather than in superlatives is characteristic of this sūrah, which spoke of an atom's weight on the merchant's scale a few verses earlier (34:3). The audience being addressed included men who ran caravans north to Syria in summer and south to Yemen in winter and knew precisely what a month of travel cost in animals, water and risk. A measurement they could price is a more effective statement of the gift than any adjective.

**Molten Copper and the Industrial Base of a Kingdom**

*ʿAyn al-qiṭr* is literally the spring of molten metal — *qiṭr* is copper or brass in a liquid state, and *ʿayn* is the ordinary word for a water spring. The image is a wellhead running with molten metal instead of water. The parallel with David is deliberate and the progression is clear: the father was given iron made pliant in his hands, the son is given a source of liquid copper flowing continuously. One is a craftsman's gift, the other a foundry's. The Qur'an elsewhere shows molten copper as a serious engineering material in the hands of another powerful figure, Dhū al-Qarnayn, who has iron blocks heated and then calls for *"molten copper to pour over it"* (18:96) to seal a barrier. Read alongside the workforce named in the same verse and the constructions listed in the next, the copper is not a marvel on display. It is the material supply for a building programme.

**Jinn Conscripted, and the Two Limits on Their Service**

The third grant is labour: *wa min al-jinni man yaʿmalu bayna yadayhi* — some of the jinn who worked before him. The partitive *min* is doing real work in the sentence: **some** of them, not the species. Ṣād specifies the trades — *"every builder and diver of the jinn"* (38:37) — and al-Anbiyāʾ mentions those who dived for him and performed other tasks, adding that it was God who kept them in check (21:82). Two limits are written into this verse. The service runs *bi-idhni rabbih*, by his Lord's permission, so the authority is delegated and revocable rather than owned; and it is enforced, since deviation brings punishment. The Qur'an is careful about this boundary because a claim was made in the other direction. Al-Baqarah explicitly denies that Solomon practised sorcery, laying the charge on the devils instead: *"Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved"* (2:102). A king who commands jinn only by permission, and whose command is backed by a punishment he does not administer, is not a magician.

**The Punishment Clause and Where It Lands**

*Wa man yazigh minhum ʿan amrinā nudhiqhu min ʿadhābi al-saʿīr* — whoever of them deviates from **Our** command tastes the torment of the blaze. The possessive is the key word. A jinn assigned to Solomon who downs tools is not disobeying Solomon; he is disobeying the One who assigned him, which is why the penalty is described as divine rather than royal. *Zāgha* means to swerve off a straight line, and *saʿīr* is a blazing fire. Commentators have divided over when the burning occurs: some take it as punishment in the Fire of the next world, others as an immediate scorching in this one, described in the reports as a strike from an angel. The verse does not say, and the ambiguity is not important to its argument. What matters is that the chain of authority is explicit from top to bottom. The king commands, but the sanction belongs to God, and even the most spectacular sovereignty in the Qur'an's telling operates on a licence.

**Sovereignty That Runs on Permission**

Taken whole, the verse grants Solomon control over an element, a material and a species — air, metal, and an order of hidden beings. It is the largest concentration of power the Qur'an attributes to any human figure, and every clause of it is qualified. The wind is subjected by another; the copper is caused to flow by another; the jinn work by his Lord's will and are punished by his Lord's decree. Ṣād records Solomon himself asking for a kingdom that would not suit anyone after him and framing the request as a petition: *"You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺"* (38:35). This is the frame the sūrah needs. It is arguing against men who read prosperity as entitlement and who will shortly claim that their wealth proves they will not be punished (34:35). Against them it sets a king whose powers dwarfed theirs and who held every one of them on loan. The next verse turns to what his conscripted workforce actually built.
