## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:11

> Surely We created you, then shaped you, then said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused to prostrate with the others.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Account of Adam**

The verse opens the sūrah's account of the creation of Adam, his temptation, and his descent from the Garden, and it is the second of the four extended versions of that story in the Book, following the account in the sūrah of the Cow and preceding the versions in the sūrah of the Night Journey and the sūrah of Ṭā Hā (2:30–39; 17:61–65; 20:115–24). The same narrative is told in shorter form in the sūrah of the Rocky Tract and the sūrah of Ṣād, where the creation of the first human being and the refusal of Iblīs are described without naming Adam in the opening lines (15:28–43; 38:71–85).

The four accounts differ in their details and agree in their structure: the creation, the command to the angels, the prostration, the refusal of Iblīs, his request for respite, and his vow to mislead. The account in front of us is the longest of them, and it continues into the following verses with the temptation of Adam and his wife, their exposure, their repentance, and their descent to the earth.

**Created and Shaped**

The verse addresses all human beings when it says that God created them and then shaped them. The commentary tradition reads the first verb as referring to the creation of humanity through the creation of Adam, and the second as referring to the formation of each individual, whether in the womb or in the loins of Adam. The two verbs together state the double claim that the Book makes on its audience: they were brought into being, and they were given their particular form by the same hand.

The Book's accounts of the creation of the first human being describe the stages by which he was formed, and the sūrah of Ṣād states that God created him from clay with His two hands and asked Iblīs why he had not prostrated before what God had made with His own hands (38:75). The sūrah of the Rocky Tract describes the creation from clay and the breathing of the divine spirit into the form (15:28–29), and the sūrah of the Prostration repeats the breathing of the spirit as the completion of the human being (32:9). The verse in front of us states the fact of the creation before the account of the honour that followed it.

**The Command to the Angels**

The verse reports the command given to the angels to prostrate before Adam, and the command is a key element in every account of the story in the Book. The sūrah of the Cow reports the same command and the same prostration (2:34), and the sūrah of the Night Journey, the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, and the sūrah of Ṣād repeat them. The tradition of commentary understands the prostration as a prostration of honour rather than of worship, since the Book forbids the worship of anything other than God.

The Book provides an illustration of prostration as honour in its account of Joseph's family, where his parents and brothers fall down before him in the fulfilment of his dream (12:100). The command to the angels therefore honours Adam without displacing the worship that belongs to God, and the sūrah's warning about taking protectors besides God is not compromised by the scene.

**The Refusal of Iblīs**

The verse states that all the angels prostrated except Iblīs, who was not among those who prostrated. The Book's phrasing here places Iblīs in the company of the angels, and the sūrah of the Cave, in an identical context, states that he was of the jinn (18:50). The two statements have produced a long discussion among the commentators about his classification, and the discussion is settled by naming him in the same sentence as the angels while also reporting his kind.

What the verse establishes is the fact of the refusal and its position at the head of the sūrah's narrative. The account that follows explains the reason Iblīs gave and the outcome of his pride, and the sūrah's later passages return to his vow to mislead the descendants of Adam. The verse in front of us states the refusal in its plainest form, and the reason is given in the next verse.

**Why the Scene Is Placed Here**

The scene of the prostration and the refusal follows the verses about the destroyed communities and the reckoning, and it supplies the sūrah's deeper explanation of the waywardness it spends the chapter describing. The Book's accounts of the refusal present it as the origin of the enmity between the human being and the tempter, and the sūrah's warnings about the satans as protectors in place of God return to the same event.

The sūrah's reader has been told to follow what has been sent down and not to take protectors besides God. The narrative that begins in front of us supplies the history of the protector who refused the command, and the sūrah's later passages identify him as the enemy whose promises are delusion.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands eleventh in the chapter, and it begins the second movement of the sūrah after the opening section. The verses that follow continue the account through the questioning of Iblīs, his sentence, his request for respite, his vow, and the temptation of the first human pair, and the passage then turns to the instruction given to the children of Adam about clothing, food, and conduct.

The transition from the accounting of the last day to the story of the first human being is the sūrah's method. The warning is stated in the abstract, and then it is shown operating in the history of the beginning of the human race.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the question put to Iblīs about what prevented him from prostrating when he was commanded, and it reports his answer that he was better than Adam because he was created from fire and Adam from clay (7:12). The refusal stated in front of us is given its reason, and the reason is the pride that the sūrah will name in the verse after that.

The two verses together present the first act of disobedience and its justification. The reader who has been told to follow the revelation sees the alternative in its original form: a creature who measured himself against another and set his own reasoning above the command he had received.
