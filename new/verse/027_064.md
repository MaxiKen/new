## Sūrah an-Naml 27:64

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who originates the creation then resurrects it, and gives you provisions from the heavens and the earth? Is it another god besides Allah?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof, if what you say is true.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Splits the Audience**

The preceding questions named things the Meccans largely agreed about. Rain, mountains, guidance through darkness, answering the distressed — a pagan Arab would have attributed all of it to God, with the idols serving as intermediaries. This question contains something they did not accept.

The two halves are *yabda'u al-khalq*, He originates creation, and *thumma yuʿīduh*, then He brings it back. The first was common ground; the Qur'an records elsewhere that if you ask them who created them they will say God. The second was the point of rupture. Resurrection was the specific claim the Meccans found absurd, and the objection they raised repeatedly was that bones, once crumbled, do not return. The verse slides the disputed item in beside the agreed one and lets the grammar do the work.

**Origination Implies Return**

Al-Rāzī states the argument the construction is making: the message of the prophets is not only that God begins everything, which many idolaters accepted, but that all things return to Him — and the origin points toward the return.

The reasoning is an argument from consistency. Whoever brought something into being from nothing faces no greater difficulty in restoring what already existed once. The Qur'an makes this explicit in other places, asking whether the one who created the heavens and the earth is unable to create their like, and answering that He certainly is. Placing origination and return in a single clause performs the same argument compactly. A listener who has conceded the first half has, without noticing, conceded the harder half, because the only ground for accepting one and rejecting the other would be that the second is more difficult, and by the terms of the first it cannot be.

**Provision From Both Directions**

The third item is *yarzuqukum min al-samā'i wa'l-arḍ* — provides for you from the heavens and the earth. Rain from above, growth from below, and the audience lives off the meeting of the two.

Al-Rāzī takes the origination-and-return clause as encompassing every other blessing named in this section, which makes the provision here a summary rather than an addition. There is also a reading, transmitted in the spiritual commentaries, that the provisions from heaven and earth are respectively the spiritual and the material. That reading is an extension and should be marked as one, though it has a foothold in the Qur'an's own habit of calling revelation something sent down. What the plain sense establishes is comprehensiveness: the sources of what keeps a person alive are named in both available directions, leaving nothing outside the account.

**"Show Me Your Proof"**

The refrain is followed by a demand that has not appeared in the previous questions: *qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — bring your proof, if you are truthful. The burden shifts.

This is the section's sharpest move. Five questions have put the case for one position; now the other side is asked to produce an argument of its own. The word *burhān* means a demonstrative proof, evidence that compels rather than persuades, and it is the same register as the *sulṭān mubīn* Sulaymān demanded of the hoopoe in verse 21. The Qur'an uses this demand repeatedly against those who assert partners with God, and the demand is always unanswered in the text, because the position it addresses was never argued for. Meccan paganism was inherited practice, not a set of claims defended with reasons, and asking for proof exposes that in one sentence. The Qur'an records their actual justification in other places, and it is always the same: we found our fathers doing this.

**The Condition Attached to the Demand**

The clause *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — if you are truthful — is doing more than adding emphasis. It ties the request for evidence to the speaker's own honesty rather than to the strength of his case.

That connection has been running through the chapter. Sulaymān told the hoopoe he would see whether he was truthful or among the liars, and the bird answered with sure news from Sheba. Thamūd's nine rehearsed a false statement and certified themselves as truthful. Here the same standard is applied to the audience: a person who asserts something is claiming to know it, and a claim to know carries an obligation to be able to say how. The chapter has consistently treated the willingness to be tested as the mark of an honest position. What cannot be known by anyone at all, including the Prophet, is the subject of the verse that follows.

**The First and the Last**

The pairing of origination and return has a wider home in the Qur'an's language about God. He is named the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward, in a formula that places Him at both ends of everything rather than at one.

That framing explains why this question sits where it does in the sequence. The preceding questions described God acting within the world — sending rain, holding mountains, answering the distressed, guiding through darkness. This one steps outside the world's duration and names the two boundaries. Nothing further can be added after it, which is why the demand for counter-evidence follows here rather than after any of the earlier questions. Having covered the span from origin to return and the provision that fills it, the section has left no domain in which a second god could be doing anything, and the challenge to produce a proof is issued into that emptiness.
