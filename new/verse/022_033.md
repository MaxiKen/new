## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:33

> You may benefit from sacrificial animals for an appointed term, then their place of sacrifice is at the Ancient House.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Animal Has a Life Before It Has a Destination**

*Lakum fīhā manāfiʿu ilā ajalin musamman* — for you in them are benefits, until a stated term. The sentence grants use before it imposes an end.

The commentators took the benefits as the ordinary yield of a living animal: its milk, its wool, its back to ride. The sacrificial animal is not withdrawn from use the moment it is dedicated. It continues to work, feed and carry until the day it is slaughtered, and that interim is described as a benefit belonging to the owner.

The ruling corrects a specific practice. Pre-Islamic custom includedAnimals set aside for idols — the *baḥīrah*, *sāʾibah*, *waṣīlah* and *ḥām* — whose milk and backs were declared forbidden to their owners. Sūrah al-Māʾidah rejects these categories outright: *“Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels. But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah, and most of them lack understanding”* (5:103).

**A Typo in the Custom, and What the Verse Does About It**

The pre-Islamic prohibition was not simple superstition; it was superstition with an economic shape. An animal dedicated to a deity was removed from the human economy while still consuming pasture, and the more a family dedicated, the poorer it became — which is precisely the mechanism the Qur'an dismantles here.

The verse's answer is to reverse the direction of the dedication. The animal is not taken out of use; it stays in use, and the use is called a benefit rather than a theft. What belongs to God in the transaction is the slaughter at the end, not the animal's whole life.

This is consistent with the vocabulary of verse 28, where the sacrificial animal was described as *what He provided them*. Provision is for use. A gift that may not be touched is not provision, and the Qur'an's legislation throughout refuses to create categories of untouchable things.

**"Until a Stated Term"**

*Ilā ajalin musamman* — to a term that is named, appointed, specified. The phrase appears throughout the Qur'an for deaths, debts, pregnancies and nations, and its use here puts the animal's life inside the same framework.

The term is stated, which means it is known: the animal is dedicated to a particular sacrifice on a particular occasion. Dedication in this religion is not a vague aspiration. It creates a dated obligation, and the benefit clause is bounded by it — you may use the animal *until*, and the *until* is what stops use from becoming indefinite postponement.

**"Then Their Place of Sacrifice Is at the Ancient House"**

*Thumma maḥilluhā ilā al-bayti al-ʿatīq* — then its place of release is at the Ancient House. The word *maḥill* is the place where something becomes permissible, where the prohibition lifts and the act is completed.

Two things are being said at once. The first is spatial: the sacrifice happens at the sanctuary and not anywhere else. The second is temporal and legal: the animal, which has been set apart, reaches its moment of release only there. Until then it is, in a qualified way, not entirely ordinary property.

The concentration of sacrifice at one place is unusual and worth reflecting on. A rite performed at a single sanctuary cannot be performed privately, which means it is public, regulated, and shared — a single location makes the act visible to a community rather than to a household.

**What the Benefit Clause Excludes**

The permission is for benefits, and the tradition was careful about which ones. Milk, wool, riding and similar yields were included; sale was not, and neither was substituting another animal for the one dedicated.

The distinction is not arbitrary. A benefit is something the animal produces while remaining the animal that was dedicated. A sale converts it back into ordinary wealth, and a substitution changes what was vowed. Both undo the dedication rather than use the dedicated thing, and the verse's boundary — benefits *until a stated term* — is drawn to permit the first and refuse the second.

There is a principle here that runs through the sūrah's treatment of property generally. Nothing a person owns is his in the absolute sense: cattle are lawful *except what has been recited* (22:30), wealth is *what We provided them* (22:35), and a dedicated animal may be used but not liquidated. Ownership in this sūrah is always real, always limited, and always referred back to the giver.

**A Term Appointed, Like Everything Else Here**

The phrase *ajal musammā* — a stated term — has already appeared in this sūrah, in verse 5, for the developing child in the womb. The repetition is not incidental.

The sūrah's world is built out of appointed terms. Pregnancies run to one, nations run to one (7:34), lives end at one, and now a dedicated animal's usefulness expires at one. Nothing in this sūrah exists in an open-ended present; everything is moving toward a date, and the date is what gives the interval its shape.

That is why the permission to use the animal is framed as it is. The benefit is real and lawful, and it is bounded. A person who milks a sacrificial camel for a season is enjoying something that will stop at a known moment, and the knowledge is part of the point: the animal was never entirely his, and the date on which it stops being usable at all is the date it was always heading for.

**How the Jurists Reasoned From It**

The legal literature derived fine distinctions from this verse, and one of them shows how carefully it was read: riding the sacrificial animal was permitted, but only where necessary, since the permission granted in the verse is for benefits generally while the animal's destination is fixed.

That reasoning captures the verse's balance. Use is permitted; ownership is qualified; the destination is not negotiable. A person who dedicates an animal still milks it and may ride it if he must, and he cannot decide to keep it.

The balance is the same one the sūrah struck in verse 30, when it made cattle lawful while demanding that the invocation be God's, and in verse 32, when it located the rite's value in the heart. Property in this sūrah is never absolute, never worthless, and never free of the purpose it was given for.
