## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:124

> ˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, “I will certainly make you into a role model for the people.” Abraham asked, “What about my offspring?” Allah replied, “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Testing of Abraham**

The verse opens with a test and a response: Abraham was tried by his Lord with commandments, and he fulfilled them. The Book does not name the commandments in this place, and the commentators offered a wide range of identifications, including the purification practices, the rites of pilgrimage, the trials of circumcision and the sacrifice of his son, and the fire into which his people cast him and which God made cool.

The Book praises Abraham elsewhere for having fulfilled his covenant, and it describes his readiness to carry out the sacrifice in detail. The commentators noted that the verse's point is not the content of the test but the completeness of the response, since the sentence attaches the promise that follows to the fulfilment rather than to the trial itself.

**The Range of Interpretations**

Some commentators took the commandments to be the practices of purification and bodily cleanliness that Islamic law associates with the way of Abraham, and others identified them with the rituals of the pilgrimage, including the circling of the House, the walking between the two hills, and the casting of the pebbles.

A third group emphasized the trials of suffering, and they listed the fire, the migration, and the command concerning his son. The variety of interpretations reflects the Book's own reticence: the verse says that the commandments were given and fulfilled, and it leaves the reader to consider the life the Book narrates elsewhere as the content of the promise's condition. The variety of identifications also reflects the passage's focus, which is the response rather than the content of the commands. The Book names the fact of the testing and the completeness of the fulfilment, and it leaves the reader to consider the life narrated elsewhere as the elaboration.

**Made a Role Model for the People**

The promise stated in the verse is that Abraham would be made a leader and a pattern for humanity. The Arabic term covers the leader of a community, the one who leads prayer, and more broadly the example that others follow, and the commentators noted that the promise made him the standard by which the Book later measures the claims of the communities that trace themselves to him.

The sūrah has been dealing with people who treated their descent from Abraham as a guarantee, and the promise here establishes what the descent actually involved. Abraham was made a model because he fulfilled what he was commanded, and the sūrah's later verses draw the consequence by identifying his religion with submission rather than with lineage.

**The Question About His Offspring**

Abraham's question follows the promise immediately, and it concerns his descendants. The Book presents the question as the natural response of a man who has just been told that he will be a pattern for humanity, and it records the answer without describing the exchange as a rebuke.

The commentators noted that the question is answered with a qualification rather than a refusal, so that Abraham's descendants are not excluded from the promise and the principle of the covenant is made clear. The Book elsewhere confirms that blessing was granted to Isaac and to his line while stating that some of their descendants did good and others wronged themselves. The Book also uses the exchange to answer a position the sūrah has been describing, in which a community treated its descent from Abraham as the ground of its standing. The answer distinguishes between the promise made to the man and the claims made by those who came after him.

**"My Covenant Is Not Extended to the Wrongdoers"**

The answer distinguishes between lineage and worthiness. A covenant with God is not inherited as a possession, and the person who does wrong does not enter it, whatever his family. Several readings of the sentence were offered: that an unjust person cannot rightly hold the leadership the promise describes, that such a person receives no promise regarding the Hereafter, or that not all of Abraham's descendants would be virtuous.

The sūrah will apply the principle in the verses that follow, where the community's claims to Abraham's legacy are tested against his actual conduct and belief. The sentence therefore supplies the criterion for the whole passage, and it prepares the reader for the argument about the direction of prayer, the House, and the religion of Abraham that occupies the rest of the section.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Argument**

The passage has just reminded the children of Israel of their favours and warned them about a Day when no substitute will be accepted. It now turns to the figure they considered their father in faith, and it shows that his position rested on obedience rather than on ancestry. What the readers of the Book receive is therefore the answer to a claim they were making about themselves.

The Book returns to Abraham repeatedly in this sūrah and elsewhere, and each time the emphasis falls on the same features: his submission, his uprightness, and his distance from the polytheism of his people. The verse in this place introduces those themes, and the verses that follow narrate his prayers for the House and for the community that would descend from him.

**A Statement About Leadership in General**

The sentence has been read by the community's scholars as a statement about authority in religion. Leadership in the Book's sense is not conferred by descent but by the fulfilment of what has been entrusted, and the qualification attached to the covenant excludes those who do wrong from its promises.

That reading has been used to argue that a person's lineage cannot substitute for the qualities the verse requires, and the argument has been turned to questions of governance as well as to questions of prophecy. Whatever particular application is drawn, the verse's own principle is clear: the promises of God are attached to the conduct the Book praises, and ancestry supplies no exemption from them. The principle also underlies the sūrah's treatment of the community that received the later revelation, since its standing rests on the same condition the verse states. The Book applies the criterion of conduct to the new community as it applies it to Abraham's descendants.

**What Makes a Person a Model**

The promise at the centre of the verse is that Abraham would become a pattern for people to follow, and the sentence records the condition on which it was made: he fulfilled what he was commanded. The Book's language here is worth noting, since the term used for a model covers leadership in prayer, authority in a community, and the example that others imitate. What the Book does not do is present the promise as a reward for status. Abraham was tested and responded, and the leadership was attached to the response. The sūrah will apply the same measure to the community that claims him, and the qualification attached to the covenant in the second half of the verse states the principle in advance. The reader therefore receives both halves of the matter: what makes a person a model, and what disqualifies his descendants from inheriting the position as a possession. The two belong together, and the following verses show the pattern of service that the model performed.
