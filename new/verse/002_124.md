## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:124

> ˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, “I will certainly make you into a role model for the people.” Abraham asked, “What about my offspring?” Allah replied, “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Patriarch Begins With a Test**

The recital turns from the Children of Israel to their covenant's root, and the first fact it files about the patriarch is not his lineage, his calling, or his vision but his examination: *when Abraham was tested by his Lord with commandments, which he fulfilled*. The verb — *ibtalā* — is the trial-verb, the testing of a person against a demand whose weight is real; the object — *kalimāt*, words — the exegetes read as the commands themselves, the divine words that arrived as tasks. The text specifies none of them, and the silence is the verse's first teaching: the itemization matters less than the fulfillment, and the recital records the patriarch's standing not by cataloguing his trials but by certifying their completion. The test-first structure also answers, in advance, the theology the previous section closed against. The honor the Children of Israel claimed as possession (122) is shown here in its actual form: precedence earned through examination, the favored man favored *after* the commands were fulfilled. Sūrah al-Najm states the certification in its own compact line — *and Abraham, who perfectly fulfilled his covenant* (53:37) — and the two verses together give the patriarch's file its header: tested, fulfilled, certified. The office that follows in the verse is granted onto that record, not before it.

**What the Words Were**

The commentators differ widely over the commandments' content, and the spread of their readings maps the patriarch's whole received legacy. One stream takes the words as commands instituting bodily purity — the grooming, cleansing, and ritual disciplines the tradition traces to Abraham, including the practices of natural fitness the Sunnah preserves. A second stream reads them as the rites of the pilgrimage: the circumambulation of the House, the running between Ṣafā and Marwah, the stoning of the pillar — the ḥajj in its full apparatus, which Muslims consider Abrahamic in origin, instituted at this station. A third takes the words as trials of suffering rather than statutes: the circumcision undertaken in old age, the sacrifice of the son (37:102's scene), and the fire his people built for him — the fire answered by the command the sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ preserves: *O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham!* (21:69). The three streams are not rivals so much as strata: purity-commands, pilgrimage-rites, and survival-trials together compose the patriarch's received biography, and each stream's evidence is textual within the larger Qur'an. The weighing the verse itself supplies is structural — it reports the testing and the fulfillment without adjudicating the list, which tells the reader where the emphasis belongs: whatever the words were, they were completed, and the completion is what the office is built on.

**“I Am Making You a Role Model”**

The fulfillment is answered by an appointment: *I will certainly make you into a role model for the people* — an *imām*, the word whose root is the prayer-leader's standing and whose range covers every form of followed leadership, religious and political. The imām is defined by the direction of faces: the one whose pattern others walk in, whose standing others orient by. The appointment's emphatic form — *I will certainly make you* — carries the divine guarantee, and its object — *for the people*, unqualified — carries the scope: not for a tribe, not for a century, imāmah for humanity. The tradition's readings of the office deepen it. In Sunni usage the imām here is the leader in faith whose example is binding by excellence; in Shīʿī doctrine the word carries its technical weight — the bearer of the Muhammadan Light, designated by divine decree — and the fifth and sixth Imams of that tradition cited precisely this verse for the principle that Allah does not establish or permit an unjust imām. Both readings draw from the same clause of the verse, and the verse's own second half arbitrates: whatever the imāmah's metaphysics, its transmission is governed by the answer to Abraham's question — and the answer conditions everything.

**“And of My Offspring?”**

The patriarch's response to his appointment is immediate and generational: *what about my offspring?* — the question of every founder who has received an office: does it continue in the line? The question is not ambition but concern, and its asking is honored by being answered rather than rebuked; the answer, however, redraws the question's map: *My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers*. The exegetes read the reply at three depths. At the first, it is a qualification of eligibility: the wrongdoer — the tyrant, the unjust — does not deserve the covenant and cannot rightly hold the imāmah. At the second, it is a qualification of promise: an imām who does wrong would not receive the covenant's Hereafter-guarantee — the office may be held, the promise is forfeited. At the third, it is simply information: not all of Abraham's descendants will be virtuous — a fact Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt states outright: *We blessed him and Isaac as well. Some of their descendants did good, while others clearly wronged themselves* (37:113). The three readings converge on one doctrine, and the doctrine is the verse's hinge for the whole recital: the covenant runs on righteousness, not blood. The lineage question receives a moral answer — the office is transmissible in principle and conditional in fact, and the condition is personal rather than genealogical. The section just closed had documented the failure of the opposite assumption across a hundred verses; the section now opening states, at its patriarch's first station, the law that assumption violated.

**The Tested Pattern as Inheritance**

The verse's transfer is in the office's nature. An imām is followed, and what Abraham's imāmah makes followable is the tested pattern: commands received, commands fulfilled, appointment granted, concern for the line answered with a moral condition. Sūrah al-Furqān preserves the pattern as every believer's prayer — *our Lord, grant us pious spouses and offspring, and make us models for the righteous* (25:74) — the imāmah-words turned into supplication, the office opened to whoever prays for it on its terms. The terms the verse files are exacting and available: the test comes as commands (revelation, not speculation); the fulfillment is personal (Abraham's own hands, his own fire, his own son); the appointment is divine (no self-declared imām stands in this file); and the transmission is moral (the wrongdoer excluded at the patriarch's own threshold). The recital's audience — a community two generations old, already generating its own claims of precedence — received in this verse the constitution of religious authority as Islam holds it: leadership earned under examination, conferred by Allah, inherited only by the righteous, and measured at every generation against the man who fulfilled. The children of the covenant, hearing their own file closed at verse 123, now hear its root opened at verse 124 — and the opening sentence tells them, and every later claimant, that the covenant's first holder got it by passing a test, and keeps it by the answer given to his question about his children.
