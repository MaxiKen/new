## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:64

> We drew the pursuers to that place,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pursuers Are Brought Into the Scene of Deliverance**

The verse shifts attention from the opened sea to the Egyptian host. God draws the pursuers to that place. Pharaoh's army has followed with confidence, expecting to recover the Israelites. It now arrives at the location where the sea has become a path for the oppressed and a test for the oppressor.

The wording exposes a reversal in movement. The Egyptians believe they are acting freely and closing the distance by their own strength. The narrative reveals that their arrival is also within God's action. They are brought to the place of judgment, not because their pursuit is wise or legitimate, but because their own decision to chase has led them there. Divine sovereignty surrounds the choices of the pursuers without removing their responsibility for those choices.

**A Place of Rescue Can Also Be a Place of Judgment**

The same location has two meanings. For Moses and his companions, it is a route opened by God. For the Egyptians, it is the place toward which the consequences of tyranny are drawing them. A physical site does not carry one fixed moral meaning for everyone who enters it. The sea is a path for those obeying God and a danger for those pursuing oppression.

This contrast helps explain why the verse says that the pursuers were drawn there rather than simply saying they arrived. Their movement is being interpreted from beyond their own intention. They think they are following fugitives. In the larger account, they are entering a place where the distinction between divine deliverance and human arrogance will become visible.

**Pursuit Can Lead a Powerful Person Beyond His Safe Ground**

Pharaoh's strength appeared greatest in the palace, among advisers, wealth, and organized forces. The sea removes him from that secure setting. His army must act in an environment where the ruler's usual resources cannot explain what is happening. The pursuit has carried the host away from the foundations of its confidence and into a path determined by another power.

This is a general pattern in the judgment of tyrants. A ruler may control the conditions in which his authority is praised, but his choices can lead him into circumstances that expose its limits. The Egyptians have left the gardens and residences behind. Now they enter the sea's divided space, where the host's readiness must meet the Lord who opened the way.

**Divine Drawing Does Not Excuse the Pursuers**

The phrase We drew does not make the Egyptians innocent victims of a hidden plan. They chose to pursue a people whom God had commanded to leave. Their anger, military preparation, and claim of ownership are part of the moral background. God's drawing of them to the place brings judgment upon a choice already made; it does not create the choice against their will.

This balance matters. One error denies divine sovereignty and imagines that history lies outside God's control. Another uses sovereignty to erase human accountability. The verse holds both together. God governs the place and outcome, while the pursuers remain responsible for entering the path with the intention of recovering the oppressed.

**The Pronoun Others Keeps the Host in Its Moral Category**

The source description calls the Egyptians the others, distinguishing them from Moses and those with him. This simple contrast is not an ethnic judgment on every Egyptian. It identifies the group in the immediate scene: the people who are pursuing rather than the people being delivered. Their category is defined by action and allegiance in this event.

That is important for reading the story without crude collective blame. The judgment falls on a host that has chosen Pharaoh's pursuit, not on every person who shares an origin or language with the army. The narrative's moral line is drawn by oppression and its defense. People are judged by what they do with the authority and knowledge they possess.

**The Place Is Set Before the Final Act**

The verse ends with the pursuers drawn to the place, not yet describing the water closing over them. That pause preserves the tension. The army has reached the opening and appears to have followed successfully. The path that saved Israel is now occupied by those who seek to reclaim Israel.

The scene is therefore arranged for a final contrast. The Egyptians have entered the space created by God's sign, but they have not become participants in the sign's purpose. They see the route as an opportunity for pursuit; the Lord sees it as the place where pursuit will be judged. A path can be open without being safe for every intention.

**God's Deliverance Does Not Require Hiding the Enemy**

The Israelites are not saved by the Egyptians losing their way or failing to notice the opening. The pursuers are brought close enough for the deliverance to be seen in relation to the threat. This makes the rescue public and complete. The oppressor is not merely kept at a distance; his confidence is brought into direct contact with God's act.

That pattern gives the community a deeper assurance. Their safety does not depend on the enemy's ignorance. It depends on the Lord's command over the situation. The pursuers can see the route, follow it, and still be unable to turn it into possession. The place belongs to God's plan, not to the first army that enters it.

The open path is therefore not a prize that belongs to whoever reaches it first. It is a commanded mercy, and mercy cannot be seized by the force that pursues those to whom it was given. The pursuers arrive at God's opening without gaining authority over it. The same command that creates the passage also determines who may pass through it and who is left behind.
