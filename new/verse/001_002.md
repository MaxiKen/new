## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Praise Stands Before Any Request**

The first sentence the worshipper speaks in the prayer, after the opening formula, is not a request. It is an assessment: *All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds* (1:2). The report that explains why the sūrah is built this way is the well-known ḥadīth qudsī in which God describes the recitation as a conversation. When the servant says, *Praise be to Allah, the Lord of the universe*, the response is *My servant has praised Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). The whole sūrah is then divided: the opening lines belong to God as praise, and the later lines belong to the servant as petition.

That order is the etiquette the Quran teaches for asking. A request that begins with a claim about the one asked is different in kind from a request that begins with a need. Praise establishes that the giver owes nothing and the asker has no claim, which is precisely the recognition a petition is supposed to contain. The sūrah can be read as a single act of address in which the worshipper spends three verses saying who God is before saying, in the fourth and fifth, what he wants. The request for guidance at 1:6 is therefore not a demand placed on a neutral power but an appeal to a Lord already acknowledged as the source of every good.

**Praise Is Not Gratitude**

*Al-ḥamd* is routinely translated as praise, and routinely confused with thanks. The distinction the commentators draw is precise. Gratitude (*shukr*) is a response to something received; it exists only after a favour has been given, and it ends when the benefit is forgotten. Praise is given for a quality the one praised possesses in himself, whether or not anything has been handed over. God is praised for being wise, powerful, and merciful before any gift reaches a creature, and He would be praised if no gift ever did. On that reading, praise is the wider category, and gratitude is one of its occasions.

The definite article carries the same insistence. The verse does not say that some praise belongs to God, or that praise is due to Him in this instance. It places the whole genus — every act of praise, by every praiser, for every reason — with its object. Whatever anyone has ever admired in anything, the verse claims the admiration as ultimately His, since whatever was admired came from Him. This is why the formula is recited on completion of an act rather than at its beginning: the act is over, the outcome is known, and the speaker locates the good in it outside himself.

**The Praise No Creature Can Finish**

The same logic produces a problem, and the tradition states it without softening. If praise is owed for who God is rather than for what He gives, then no created mind can discharge it, because no created mind comprehends who God is. The Prophet is reported to have said in prostration, *I cannot enumerate the praise due to You; You are as You have praised Yourself* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 486; also Sunan al-Nasāʾī 1100). The sentence concedes the limit and then resolves it: the adequate praise of God is the praise God gives Himself.

Several commentators take this as the reason the sūrah was revealed in God's words rather than left to human composition. Human beings were given the vocabulary they could not have invented, so that their praise might be praise in His speech and not merely in their own. The scale of the gap is stated elsewhere in a way that removes any vanity from human worship: *There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification* (17:44). Creation praises without ceasing; the worshipper is joining a chorus, not founding one.

**Lord: Master, Arranger, Cultivator**

*Rabb* is rendered Lord, and the word is denser than the title suggests. It carries three senses at once: a master who is obeyed, one who sets affairs in right order, and one who owns. Applied to God, the three combine into a single claim — that He possesses every creature, arranges every creature's affairs, and is therefore the one to whom the creature's obedience is owed. Lordship here is not a rank; it is a description of what is actually happening.

A fourth sense sits behind the others, since the root is also tied to *tarbiyah*, cultivation or rearing. God is the One who brings things along stage by stage, taking each to the state proper to it. That reading changes the tone of the verse considerably. A lord who cultivates is not a distant owner collecting dues but a caretaker whose work is visible in what the thing becomes. The Quran tests the claim by asking pagans who acknowledges it: *Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah!”* (13:16). The admission is expected; what is missing is its consequence.

**"Worlds," and How Many They Are**

The word is plural, and the plural is where the argument begins. *ʿĀlamīn* may denote the communities of beings within each order of existence — humans, jinn, angels, and satans — or only humans and jinn, on the reasoning that the Prophet is called *a warner to the whole world* (25:1) and only those two communities need a warner. Others take it as every generation of humankind, or every species in creation, or simply all existent things other than God.

The second reading has the better textual evidence for inclusion and the weaker conclusion. That the Prophet warns humans and jinn proves those two are among the worlds; it does not prove nothing else is, since a warner sent to some does not exhaust a ruler's domain. The universal reading — everything other than God — is therefore the stronger one, and it is the reading that makes the verse fit its position. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī pressed it as far as it will go, arguing that nothing prevents God from actualising worlds and orders of being of which human beings have no knowledge, so that the phrase covers what can be seen, what can be imagined, and what neither. Praise is then owed by every order of being, known and unknown.

**The Phrase That Closes Things**

This doxology is not unique to the opening of the Quran; it recurs wherever the Book closes a matter. The angels around the Throne end with *Praise be to Allah—Lord of all worlds* (39:75). The people of Paradise close their prayer with it (10:10). It concludes the account of destroyed peoples (6:45) and stands at the end of the sūrah of the Ranks (37:182), and God instructs the Prophet to call upon Him *˹saying,˺ “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds”* (40:65). The formula appears at verdicts, at endings, at the settling of accounts.

What the recurrence establishes is the sense in which the phrase is an assessment. Praise is due before the outcome is known and after it, in hardship and in ease, at the beginning of the Quran and at the end of every judgement in it. The worshipper who opens the prayer with these words is speaking the same sentence creation will speak when the matter is decided.
