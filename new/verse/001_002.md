## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Praise for What God Is, Not Only for What He Gives**

Praise renders *al-ḥamd*, the act of extolling what is praiseworthy. It differs from thanks, *shukr*, in a way the commentators kept repeating: thanks answers a gift already received, while praise answers the worth of the one praised, whether or not anything has been received yet. Praise is therefore the wider term, and the Qur'an places it at the head of the Book rather than at the end of a list of benefits.

The definite article in *al-ḥamd* does real work. It gathers every form of praise into one statement, so the sentence claims that all praise belongs to God and that no praise offered to anything else is being left out. A famous report preserved by Muslim 395 describes God answering the recitation line by line: when the servant says, *“All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds”* (1:2), God says, *My servant has praised Me*. The prayer is presented as a conversation, and the Fātiḥah is its script.

**Lord: Master, Owner and Cultivator at Once**

*Rabb* is not the bare word for a deity. Arabic uses it for a master who is obeyed, for the one who owns a thing, and for the one who repairs and orders what he owns. The third sense yields the link to *tarbiyah*, the slow work of raising and cultivating. Calling God *rabb* therefore combines authority, possession and care in a single noun, and it explains why the Qur'an can move from God's lordship to God's provision without a change of theme.

The Qur'an gives this dependence as the natural condition of every creature: *“All those in the heavens and the earth are dependent on Him. Day in and day out He has something to bring about”* (55:29). The verse rules out the picture of a creator who withdraws after making the world. Lordship means that the ordering work never stops, and the world's continued existence is an act repeated at every moment rather than a machine left running.

**"Lord of All Worlds" and the Size of the Claim**

*ʿĀlamīn* is a plural of *ʿālam*, and Arabic uses the word for everything by which God is known, not only for the globe and its inhabitants. The commentators disagreed over its contents. One view limits it to human beings and jinn, on the grounds that a warner is sent *“to the whole world”* (25:1) and only these two kinds need warning. A wider view includes angels and every species of creation; the widest takes in all existent things besides God.

Al-Rāzī pressed the last position furthest, arguing that the worlds cannot be counted at all, since God can bring into being whatever is possible and there may be creations of which human beings know nothing. Each reading pushes the sentence in the same direction. The praise offered at the start of the sūrah is not the praise of a tribe or a species; it comes from creatures and is addressed to the Lord of every level of existence. *“There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification”* (17:44).

**The Sentence Muslims Say Most Often**

*Al-ḥamdu lillāh* is one of the most repeated sentences in Muslim life. The basmalah marks the start of an undertaking; this formula closes it, whether the outcome is a meal, a recovery, a safe arrival or a difficult day survived. Islamic teaching treats it as the standard answer to the question of how one is, whatever the answer means, which is why gratitude in this tradition is a discipline rather than a report on circumstances.

The Qur'an ties the formula to reassurance as well as to speech: *“those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort”* (13:28). Praise, on this understanding, is not flattery offered upward. It is a way of describing the world correctly, and the person who describes it correctly is steadier for it. The sūrah puts that description in the mouth of the reciter before it allows the reciter to ask for anything.

**The Greatest Sūrah Opens With Praise**

Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Muʿallā was told by the Prophet ﷺ that he would be taught the greatest sūrah of the Qur'an before leaving the mosque, and the lesson turned out to be this opening: the report identifies it with the phrase *All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds* and calls it *the Seven Oft-Repeated and the Great Quran* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4474). The Qur'an itself names that gift: *“We have certainly granted you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Quran”* (15:87).

That identification fixes the status of this verse. The seven verses of the Fātiḥah are singled out alongside the whole of the revelation given to the Prophet ﷺ, and it is their second line, the line of praise, that gave the report its name. The Book's greatest chapter begins by assigning credit for everything in it before it asks for anything at all.

**Praise Adequate to God Cannot Be Improvised**

The Fātiḥah supplies praise rather than leaving the servant to invent it. The commentators found a reason in that arrangement: human beings cannot measure the worth of God, so the Book states it for them in words God has given. The servant who recites *All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds* (1:2) is praising God with a sentence God composed, which is why the report preserved by Muslim 395 has the divine answer *My servant has praised Me* attached to exactly this line.

The same concern appears in a supplication the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have addressed to God: that there is no way to count the praise owed to Him, and that He is as He has praised Himself. Praise, on that understanding, is not flattery and cannot be exaggerated, because exaggeration requires a limit to exceed and no creature knows the limit. What a person can do is repeat the description God has given of Himself.

The verse's position in the Book follows from the same logic. Praise is the first human speech in the Qur'an, before any request and before any command, and it is a description rather than an offering. The creature's contribution to it is agreement.
