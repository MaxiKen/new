## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Letters Standing Where a Sentence Should Stand**

Three letters, not a word. The reciter names them one after another — *alif*, *lām*, *mīm* — and stops. Arabic does not pronounce the group as a syllable, so the sūrah opens with an utterance that is neither noun nor verb nor sentence, and the pause after the third letter is the point at which revelation proper begins.

Nothing in the Book decodes them. Twenty-nine of the Qur'an's sūrahs open in this way, singly or in clusters: *ṣād*, *qāf*, *nūn*, *ḥā-mīm*, and the pairs and triples built from the same alphabet. This sūrah uses exactly the three that open the sūrah before it — the translation of 2:1 is letter for letter the same line as the one above (2:1). A reader who has just come from al-Baqarah hears the same three notes struck again, and the ear cannot treat them as noise; the repetition turns them into a signature.

Old copies of the Qur'an often set these letters apart, larger or ornamented, as a title-page rather than a sentence. The convention reflects what the text itself does not contradict: whatever else they are, they mark a beginning.

And they are counted. The letters form verse 1 of this sūrah in its standard verse division, not a decorative preamble outside the numbering, which means they are part of the recitation, part of the text that is memorized, and part of what a Muslim means when he says he has read the sūrah. A community that counts its alphabet as scripture has made a decision about what revelation is: speech that can refuse to be useful, and remain revelation anyway.

**What the Reciter Does With Letters Nobody Can Read**

Oral practice fixes the form. Letters of this kind are recited by their alphabet names, kept separate, and never run together into a word; the reciter does not attempt a pronunciation that Arabic phonology forbids. The same discipline governs Sūrah Maryam's five-letter opening and the single letters elsewhere.

Beyond that, the reciter's procedure is silence. There is no commentary in the text, no gloss in the margin, and no tradition of liturgical explanation attached to the letters as letters. Whole religious communities have recited them five times a day for fourteen centuries without agreeing on what they mean, and this has not created a crisis, because the listener is not asked to decode them. The demand placed on the hearer is attention, and attention is exactly what an unexplained opening produces.

Set beside the Qur'an's ordinary speech — commands, narratives, arguments, promises — the letters are the one place where the Book refuses to explain itself at the doorway. The effect is deliberate. A text that explains everything down to its title would be a different kind of book.

**The Alphabet as the Argument**

The letters are the alphabet of the audience, and that fact carries the sūrah's first implicit claim. The Book is not written in a private script or a heavenly vocabulary; it is built from the twenty-eight sounds that a Makkan child learned at his mother's knee. The challenge attached to that claim runs through the Qur'an: *“If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other.”* (17:88), and its shorter forms invite the doubter to bring a single sūrah of the same quality (2:23; 10:38).

Read that way, the opening letters are a dare. Here are your materials — take them and do what this does. The argument has force precisely because the raw material is common property, and the outcome is not: identical bricks, a building nobody else could raise. That is why the letters are followed immediately by a statement about God rather than by a story about the Prophet.

**The Explanations the Commentators Offered**

Reported explanations exist, and their quality varies. In the oldest layers of tafsīr the letters are left to God: *Allāhu aʿlam*, God knows best. Others read them as an abbreviation of a divine sentence, or as three names compressed into three letters — Allāh, Jibrīl, Muḥammad; or as the *alif* of oneness, the *lām* of kindness, and the *mīm* of sovereignty.

The weakest reports attach a decoding key to each letter and use the count of letters to date the community's lifespan; the strongest position remains the plainest, that the Book has not told us and does not require us to guess. Al-Ṭabarī's restraint here is a method, not a gap: where a text withholds, commentary that supplies is invention. This translation makes the same choice by carrying the letters over untranslated, on the sound principle that a meaning nobody can establish should not be quietly manufactured on the page.

**Named Among the Greatest Names**

The weight these three letters carry is shown by where the Prophet places them. Asmāʾ bint Yazīd heard him say that God's greatest name — the name by which, when He is called, He answers — lies in two places: the verse *“Your God is ˹only˺ One God. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.”* (2:163), and the opening of this sūrah (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 3478, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Tirmidhī; Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1496, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī; Sunan Ibn Mājah 3855).

The report attaches the greatest name to 3:1–2 as a unit, which means the letters are recited as part of the most powerful invocation the Prophet knew. Recited beside the sūrah of the Cow, this sūrah is called one of the *zahrāwān*, the two bright ones, which the Prophet said would come on the Day of Resurrection like two clouds or two flocks of birds in ranks, pleading for those who recited them (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 804). The three letters are the doorway into that reading.
