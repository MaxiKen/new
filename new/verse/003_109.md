## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:109

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership and the Return of Matters**

The verse states that everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to God, and that all matters will be returned to Him for judgment. The two clauses are a statement of possession followed by a statement of jurisdiction.

The Book pairs them often. One passage states that whatever is in the heavens and the earth belongs to God, and that God is the Self-Sufficient and Praiseworthy (31:26), which makes the point that the ownership of everything carries no benefit for the owner and no limitation on Him. Another passage states that God will certainly gather humanity on a day about which there is no doubt, and then asks whose word could be more truthful than God's (4:87). The verse before the reader stands in that sequence of declarations.

**Why the Formula Settles Disputes**

The sentence about ownership is the Book's standard reply to arguments about authority. Throughout this sūrah, the disputes have concerned lineage, scripture, and standing, with each party claiming a position that the others contested.

The reply is not to adjudicate the claims one by one but to state who owns the terms. If everything belongs to God, then no community owns a claim on Him, and no lineage carries a right against Him. The sūrah has already used the same reasoning when it stated that God is never unaware of what people do and when it described Him as witness over the conduct of the People of the Book, and the sentence here generalises the point to the whole of creation.

**"All Matters Are Returned"**

The clause about matters being returned appears in several contexts in the Book, and one of them is a passage about the battlefield, where the return of matters to God is given as the reason the outcome of a battle was arranged as it was (8:44).

The phrase describes both the final accounting and the disposal of events in this world. Nothing that occurs falls outside the jurisdiction of the one to whom matters return, and the Book uses the same expression for the settling of disputes that ended badly in this life and for the final reckoning. Its function in the verse before the reader is to add time to ownership: the things that belong to God will also come back to Him, and the reckoning is the moment when the ownership becomes visible.

**What the Verse Means for the Parties of This Sūrah**

The communities addressed in this sūrah had claimed Abraham, held scriptures, and disputed about which of them stood where. The verse places all of that inside a statement that leaves nobody outside God's ownership.

A person reading the passage with those arguments in mind receives a different perspective on them. The dispute about who possessed the truth matters, and the sūrah has pursued it at length; what the sentence about ownership supplies is the fact that the dispute itself is subject to a judge whose authority nobody granted and nobody can withdraw from. The communities may argue as long as they like, and the matter will return to its owner.

**The Comfort the Sentence Contains**

The Book states the return of matters to God as a reassurance as often as it states it as a warning, and both uses are present in this sūrah's surroundings. For people who have been wronged and cannot obtain justice, the clause means that no case is lost by being left unresolved in this world.

The sūrah has just described communities that divide after receiving proofs and groups that work to draw the believers away from their faith. Those are wrongs whose consequences are hard to measure, and the sentence that everything returns to God asserts that the measurement will be carried out. The Book's consolations are frequently of this kind, addressed to people who cannot see the outcome they are owed.

**Ownership and Human Trust in This World**

The Book's statements about divine ownership do not remove human responsibility for the world, and it holds the two together elsewhere, where human beings are described as placed on the earth to act within it and as answerable for what they do with what they have been given.

What belongs to God is entrusted to people for a time, and the return of matters is the accounting for the trust. That is the framework in which the sūrah's commands about wealth, about the community's unity, and about calling others to what is good make sense: each of them concerns the use of something that was never owned outright. The verse states the framework in two sentences and leaves the reader to apply it to the instructions he has just been given.

**The Close of a Section**

The verse ends a section of the sūrah that began with the address to the believers about being drawn away from their faith and continued through commands about unity and correction, a warning about division, and the description of two outcomes.

The sentences that follow begin a new movement, and the reader is carried from the terms of the argument to a statement about the community's identity and its place among the nations. The two verses serve as the hinge between those subjects, and both of them do their work by stating a fact about God rather than by praising or blaming any human party.

**A Sentence Repeated at Points of Decision**

The Book places the statement that matters return to God at the moments where human judgment fails or where a dispute cannot be settled in this world. It appears in the account of a battle whose outcome nobody could have predicted and at the close of passages about communities that refused their messengers.

Its recurrence gives the sentence a practical role. A reader who has exhausted what he can do about a wrong is told where the matter goes next, and a community that has been treated unjustly is told that the case is not closed. The verse above belongs to that family of statements, and its position after a passage about division and opposition is a reminder that the sūrah's arguments are conducted in the presence of a judge.
