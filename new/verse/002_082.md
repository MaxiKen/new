## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:82

> And those who believe and do good will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of a Pair**

The previous verse stated the condition for the Fire and the permanence of that residence. This one states the condition for the Garden and gives the same duration. The two sentences stand directly beside each other so that the reader receives both halves of the Book's standard at once, and neither is left to inference.

The arrangement matters for the passage that follows. The sūrah is about to describe a covenant this community ratified and then broke. A reader needs to know what was offered before the breach can be measured, and this verse supplies the terms: belief, good conduct, and a lasting home for those who hold both.

**Faith and Action Named Together**

The Book pairs belief with righteous conduct in dozens of places, and the pairing is its most frequent description of the people it praises. The phrase occurs so regularly that a reader new to the text soon notices the Book rarely states one without the other, and the reason is that each term is incomplete in the Book's vocabulary without its partner.

The works in question are not confined to ritual. The Qur'an includes prayer, charity, and the freeing of a slave among the deeds of a life completed by faith, and it applies the same formula to men and women alike. The verse therefore describes a way of living rather than a checklist, and it treats conduct as the visible surface of conviction.

**Residents of Paradise**

The word rendered as residents describes permanent dwelling rather than a visit. The Book uses it for the people of the Garden and the people of the Fire in the same passages, and it does so in order to place both outcomes on the same timescale. Whatever a person is called in the Book's final accounting, the description holds without an end date attached.

Later verses of this sūrah describe the reward in sensory terms: gardens beneath which rivers flow, shade and fruit that do not fail, and an abode whose greatest element is the pleasure of God. This verse mentions only residence and duration. Readers have always taken the two descriptions together, since the brief statement here establishes the condition and the later passages fill in what the condition contains.

**The Answer to a Claim About Duration**

The two verses together dismantle the community's claim in three steps. The claim was that the Fire would touch them only for a limited period. The first verse removed the limit for people whose sins surround them. The second assigned permanence to people who believe and act rightly, and left the other category with no such promise at all.

The Book does not offer a ledger of days or a schedule of punishments. It offers a test of character applied to two kinds of life, and it lets the reader work out for himself which of the two descriptions fits him. That is how the passage can correct a specific claim made by a specific community while remaining useful to every reader of the Book, whatever scripture he was raised on.

**What the Verse Does Not Say About Numbers**

The sentence avoids any treatment of proportion between deeds and sins, and it does not present the matter as a balance a person can compute. The next passage sets out the covenant's demands in detail and leaves the accounting to God, and later parts of the Book describe the weighing of deeds on a day when no soul will be wronged in the least.

The omission is deliberate. The Book does not encourage its readers to estimate their own standing, and it gives the two outcomes in their general form so that a person examines the direction of his life rather than the arithmetic of it. The community's claim about numbered days was an attempt to do the opposite, and this answer removes the material such a calculation would need.

**A Promise Framing the Covenant That Follows**

The verse closes a section that began with the hardening of hearts and moved through concealment, speculation, forgery, and a claim about the Fire. Each of those sentences described a departure from a trust. This one describes the people who kept the trust, and it does so immediately before the covenant narrative resumes.

Readers of the sūrah have long noticed that the passage's most demanding commands — the covenant verses about parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, and how people speak to one another (2:83) — arrive right after this promise. The arrangement makes the reward the frame for what comes next, and it puts the community's breach of its own undertaking in the sharpest available light.

**Why the Verse Belongs to Everyone Addressed**

The sentence names no group, and it uses the same two conditions the sūrah applies to the Muslims in its opening definition of the successful. Its function in this passage is to close the argument about inherited guarantees that ran from the claim about numbered days through the description of the forgeries.

What remains for the sūrah is the record. The next verses turn from what people assume about their standing to what they actually agreed to do, and they set out the covenant's terms clause by clause. The promise in this verse is the standard against which that record will be read.

**The Verse in the Chapter's Architecture**

This promise and the warning before it form a hinge in the sūrah. The passage so far has described a community that received a covenant, broke it, and then defended itself with claims about limited punishment and closed hearts. The two destinies are now stated in plain form, and the covenant narrative resumes immediately afterwards with the terms written out in full. The placement means the reader meets the covenant a second time knowing what is at stake, so the breach recorded in the following verses lands against a background of consequences rather than in a vacuum. The Book often arranges its material in this order, promising before commanding and rewarding before warning, and the arrangement teaches its hearers to read the account of an earlier community as instruction rather than as history.
