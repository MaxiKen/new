## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:94

> Then whoever still fabricates lies about Allah, they will be the ˹true˺ wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict on Those Who Invent**

The verse states that whoever fabricates a lie against God after the demonstration just given will be among the wrongdoers. The phrase "after that" ties the sentence to the challenge in the preceding verse, and the commentators read it as the point after which the truth about the dietary laws has been made clear.

The Book's phrase for the conduct it names is making something up and ascribing it to God, which is the most serious form of false speech in its vocabulary. What is at issue is not the inaccuracy of an interpretation but the invention of a divine command. The verse places that invention among the wrongs that damage the person who commits them and the people who accept them from him.

**The Setting: A Claim About Abraham's Practice**

The lie in view is a specific one. The verse before it answered the argument that Abraham had been subject to the food laws of the Jewish tradition, and the evidence offered was the sequence of the Torah's revelation after his lifetime.

A person who continued to assert the earlier position after the timeline had been set out would be claiming, in effect, that God had legislated something He had not legislated. The Book treats the ascription of a law to God as a claim on the highest authority available, and it reserves its severest descriptions for those who make that claim on their own initiative. The verses that follow in this passage address the same behaviour in the same terms.

**The Evidence Still Standing in Their Hands**

The demonstration was not a private revelation that the opponents would have to take on trust. The Prophet was told to ask them to produce the Torah itself, which was the book they possessed and recited, and the argument rested on a document both parties could inspect.

The verse above describes the condition of a person who has such a document in his hands and continues to state the opposite of what it shows. The Book's accounts of the People of the Book repeatedly return to this point, which is that their own scriptures were the source of the case against them. The sūrah will later describe a group among them who twist the scripture with their tongues so that hearers take it for the Book itself, and the conduct described there is the same conduct this verse condemns, in a form that reaches the ear rather than the page.

**"They Will Be the Wrongdoers"**

The description is the Book's general verdict on those whose acts violate what is due. Its use here is instructive because the conduct in question is a corruption of knowledge rather than an act of violence or theft.

The Book places the corruption of religious knowledge among the gravest wrongs, and the reason is visible in the sūrah's own logic. Violence damages bodies, and a false attribution to God damages the way a community relates to its Lord for generations. The Book's anger about the matter is concentrated on those who make the fabrication possible, which is why the verse's descriptive clause sits at the end as a summary of the whole passage rather than as an additional penalty.

**How the Book Treats Fabrication Elsewhere**

The same accusation appears at other points in the Book, and the contexts show what the Book considers the fabrication to involve. It curses those who write a book with their own hands and then say that it is from God, and it names as the worst wrongdoers those who invent a lie about God or deny His verses after they reach them.

The set of passages is consistent about what is at stake. When a person claims that a text or a ruling comes from God, he has given a claim about God that only God can make true, and the Book treats the act as an offence against the source of the message rather than only against the hearers. The verses in this sūrah about the covenant and the turning away from it belong to the same family of warnings, and they carry the same insistence that the message has been received, understood, and then misrepresented.

**The Sentence's Position in the Argument**

The verse closes the short exchange about the food laws. What follows it turns from argument to instruction, and the next sentence summons the community to an allegiance rather than to a proof.

The movement is characteristic of the sūrah. It establishes the facts about the revelation and its history, states what has gone wrong with the people who held the earlier scriptures, and then gives the community the response it should make. The transition from a demonstration to a command is the point at which the reader stops examining the opponents' position and starts examining his own, and the verse above marks the boundary between the two.

**"After That": Why the Timing Matters**

The phrase that opens the sentence's second half is not filler. It marks the point after which the truth has been made plain, and the Book consistently treats responsibility as growing with the clarity a person has received.

The principle appears in several places. The Book asks how many clear signs were given to the Israelites and states that whoever alters the favour of God after it has come to him will answer for it. It also describes those who conceal what was revealed after it has been made clear, and it reports that those who turn back after guidance has been explained have been given a respite rather than an excuse. The verse above is shorter than any of these, and its structure is the same: the act is named, the prior clarity is stated, and the outcome is described in one clause. What the reader is meant to see is that the offence is aggravated by knowledge rather than excused by it.
