## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:170

> When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.” ˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had ˹absolutely˺ no understanding or guidance?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Summons and the Refusal**

The verse is built as a dialogue in two lines. The summons: *follow what Allah has revealed* — the entirety of the religious demand compressed into one verb and one object. The refusal: *No! We only follow what we found our forefathers practicing* — the word "no" stated flatly, then the substitute source named. The exchange is not a debate but a substitution revealed: the question was never what to follow but whom to follow it from, and the answer exposes that for the refusers, the source has already been chosen and is not God. The verse's "them" is wide — al-Rāzī read it as covering the idolaters and possibly the People of the Book, al-Ṭabarī tied it to the food customs of the preceding verses — and the width is justified by the reply's familiarity across all the audiences the Qur'an addresses. Invented prohibitions, ancestral rites, inherited scruples: in every case the same sentence stands behind them, and this verse quotes it in the exact form it is actually spoken.

**The Question the Answer Cannot Survive**

The verse does not argue with the refusal; it asks one question, and the question is lethal: *would they still do so, even if their forefathers had absolutely no understanding or guidance?* The conditional strips the ancestral appeal to its working assumption — that the fathers knew something the sons do not, that custom carries encoded wisdom. The verse invites the refusers to suppose the assumption false: the fathers understood nothing and were not guided. If the supposition changes nothing — and the flat "No!" of the first line suggests nothing would change — then custom was never the real authority; it is a preference wearing authority's clothes. The argument's force is that the refusers cannot answer the question either way. If they admit the fathers might have lacked guidance, the reason for following them dissolves. If they insist the fathers certainly had guidance, they have claimed a knowledge of their ancestors' inner standing that no descendant possesses — which is itself saying about God and the unseen what one does not know, the very sin the previous verse ranked highest.

**A Campaign Across the Whole Qur'an**

This verse is one station in the Qur'an's most persistent polemic. The sūrah of the Table repeats the exchange nearly verbatim — *"When it is said to them, 'Come to Allah's revelations and to the Messenger,' they reply, 'What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us'"* — with the same killing question appended: *"˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had absolutely no knowledge or guidance?"* (5:104). Luqmān's version adds the eschatological stake: the same refusal — *"No! We only follow what we found our forefathers practicing"* — *even if Satan is inviting them to the torment of the Blaze* (31:21). The sūrah of Ornament gives the ancestral creed its full formula — *"We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are following in their footsteps"* (43:22) — and records the warner's question answered by total rejection (43:24). Abraham's people confess the same ground: *"We found our forefathers worshipping them"* — and Abraham's verdict: *"Indeed, you and your forefathers have been clearly astray"* (21:53–54). Pharaoh's court dresses the same refusal in political suspicion (10:78). The repetition across Meccan and Medinan sūrahs shows the Qur'an treating ancestral religion not as one error among many but as the error's default chassis — the structure every false religion installs itself inside.

**Custom as Satan's Slowest Footstep**

The previous two verses supply the connection this verse makes explicit by position: the ancestral refusal is where Satan's footsteps arrive when they are walked for generations. The first step is a scruple — one animal avoided, one practice added; the second is the scruple's hardening into custom; the third is the custom's consecration as religion; and the fourth is the verse's scene, where the summons to revelation is refused because the custom now has ancestors to vouch for it. The sūrah of Luqmān names the walker in this exact context: following the fathers *even if Satan is inviting them to the Blaze* (31:21) — the ancestral road revealed as the enemy's road, its antiquity the measure not of its truth but of how long the footsteps have been followed. This is why the food occasion matters: the tribes' prohibitions were small, local, and pious-sounding — the least alarming form the corruption can take — and the verse's dialogue shows what small pious-sounding corruptions become when time is added: an authority that answers revelation with "No."

**What the Qur'an Does Not Say About Fathers**

The polemic requires one clarification, because the Qur'an itself honors ancestors of a certain kind. The faith of Abraham is held up as the creed to follow (2:135), and the community's confession names Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, and Jacob as recipients of revelation (2:136) — fathers followed, explicitly, across the centuries. The difference between that following and this verse's refusal is precise and instructive: Abraham is followed because what he followed is known — revelation recorded, a faith transmitted in its content, an uprightness documented by God Himself. The ancestral religion of this verse is followed because it is ancestral — its content never examined, its warrant never requested, its fathers' understanding assumed rather than established. The Qur'an's criterion is thus not lineage against lineage but warrant against inheritance. A forefather who received revelation transmits light; a forefather who merely practiced custom transmits habit; and the verse's question separates the two by asking whether the habit was ever lit. The Muslims' own fathers in faith pass the test because God testified for them; the refusers' fathers fail it because no one can testify for them, and their children never asked.

**The Living Edge of the Verse**

The dialogue is quoted in the past, but its structure is permanent, and the tradition reads it as addressed to every generation's refusers. The summons — follow what Allah has revealed — arrives in every age with its scripture complete and its law accessible; the refusal arrives in the same words with new content: not only the fathers' cattle-prohibitions but the community's settled practice against the clear text, the inherited opinion defended after the evidence is shown, the "we have always done it this way" that answers correction with genealogy. The killing question applies unchanged: would you still follow, even if the practice had no understanding or guidance behind it? The verse's pastoral function for believers is therefore a mirror before it is a window — the refusers it describes are outside, but the reflex it exposes is inside everyone, since every person inherits practices he has never examined from people he cannot audit. The command to follow revelation is, on this verse's logic, a command to keep auditing: to hold every inherited act up to the revealed text until it either passes or is dropped, so that no reader ends up quoting the refusal with his own mouth while believing he is quoting his fathers' piety.
