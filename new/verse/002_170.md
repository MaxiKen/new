## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:170

> When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.” ˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had ˹absolutely˺ no understanding or guidance?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Reported**

The verse reports what certain people say when they are invited to follow what God has revealed, and their answer appeals to the practice of their ancestors. The Book places the invitation and the reply side by side in a single sentence, so that the reader sees both at once.

The reply is one the Book reports in several chapters, and the community's teachers noted that the wording is nearly identical wherever it appears. What differs is the group being addressed, and the sūrah here leaves the identity general, which allows the reader to apply the description to any community that answers in the same way.

**The Appeal to Ancestral Practice**

The reasoning in the reply rests on the authority of what has been inherited, and the sentence states the position without defending it. What the speakers treat as decisive is that their ancestors did something, and the Book reports the appeal as sufficient in their own estimation.

The commentators noted that the appeal has a history in the Book's accounts of earlier peoples, since the same answer is given by the people of Noah, by the Egyptians who heard Moses, and by the people of Abraham. The repetition shows that the practice of justifying a position by its antiquity is not confined to one community, and the verse's account of it concerns a habit of the human being rather than the error of a particular group.

**The Question Put to the Claim**

The sentence then asks whether the speakers would follow their ancestors even if those ancestors understood nothing and were not rightly guided, and the question exposes what the appeal leaves out. The two conditions named are the absence of understanding and the absence of guidance, so that what is inherited is a practice with nothing behind it.

The Book's use of the question form leaves the reader to supply the answer, and the community's teachers read the sentence as a demonstration that the appeal proves nothing. What the speakers rely on is the fact that something was done before, and the sentence shows that a fact of that kind cannot establish a rule when the ones who did it had neither knowledge nor direction.

**The Parallel in Another Chapter**

The Book reports the same words in another chapter where it describes those who say that what they found their ancestors practising is sufficient for them, and the Book's answer there is the same question. The community's scholars gathered the passages and noted that the Book treats the appeal as a recurring feature of the human response to revelation.

The repetitions give the community its vocabulary for the subject, and they show how the Book's teaching addresses a habit that every generation is tempted to adopt. The reader who is told that a practice is old is invited by these verses to ask what the people who began it knew, and the question becomes the test the passage supplies.

**Guidance and Its Absence**

The verse names guidance as the second thing the ancestors may have lacked, and the sūrah has used the term from its opening verses onward. The Book describes people either as rightly guided or as lacking direction, and the sentence applies the distinction to the generation whose example is being followed.

The community's teachers drew from the verse a warning about the transmission of practice without understanding. What the Book commends is the following of what God has revealed, and the sentence shows the reader that the age of a custom is not what makes it sound. The standard the verse applies is the guidance that the Book itself provides, and the reader is given the means to test what he has inherited.

The term that the verse uses for direction appears throughout the sūrah, from the description of the Book as a guide for the reverent to the request for the straight path that the community recites in its prayer. The community’s teachers noted that the sentence applies the word to a generation whose example was being followed, and that the Book therefore measures ancestral practice by the direction God gives. What the reader receives is a standard by which the customs of a community can be weighed, and the passage continues with the account of those who cannot use the standard because they do not understand what they hear.

**Who Is Addressed**

The commentators connected the verse with the discussion of food in the preceding sentences, and some read it as a statement about the idolaters and the People of the Book, while others took it as a general description. The two readings agree that the verse concerns the practice of justifying conduct by tradition.

The passage around the sentence names the invitation to follow what God has revealed, and the Book's other reports of the same answer show that the reply comes from several communities. The reader is therefore given a description that applies to whoever makes it, and the sūrah leaves the matter open so that the warning is not confined to the people of a particular time.

**The Verse's Place in the Argument**

The sentence follows the account of what the enemy of humanity commands and precedes the parable of the shepherd and the flock. The Book states the instruction, describes the refusal to accept it, and then supplies a comparison that shows the condition of those who turn away.

The movement gives the passage its force, and the reader is carried from an account of the source of a community's practices to a description of what such a community is like. The verse in between supplies the words that explain why the practices are retained, and the passage continues with the image that the Book uses for those who hear the call without understanding it.

The sentence follows the account of the enemy’s instruction and precedes the comparison of those who hear without understanding. The Book states what the enemy commands, reports what the people answer when they are invited to follow the revelation, and then supplies an image for their condition. The reader who follows the sequence from the prohibition of certain foods through the appeal to ancestral practice arrives at the parable with the words of the refusal still in his ear, and the passage continues with the command to the believers to eat what they have been given.
