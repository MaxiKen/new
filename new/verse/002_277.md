## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:277

> Indeed, those who believe, do good, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax will receive their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Four Named**

The verse states the community's positive economy in four terms, and the terms' order is the verse's architecture: *those who believe, do good, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax*. The first pair is the sūrah's standard opening dyad — believed and worked the righteous — the interior assent and the deeds that verify it. The second pair names the two institutions: the ṣalāt established (āqāmū, the same causing-to-stand the prayer-verse commanded, 2:238) and the zakāt given. The commentators note the dyads' division of labor: belief and righteous work are personal and unlimited, while prayer and alms are the instituted forms the personal takes — the fixed stations of the day and the fixed levy on the year, the two pillars whose performance is measurable and whose neglect is public. The verse's listing is the same four that structure the sūrah's opening portrait of the successful (2:3–5: belief in the unseen, the prayer established, spending from the provision, belief in the revelation) — and the ring it closes is deliberate: the Book that began by defining the guided in these four terms ends its economy-legislation by restating the same four and attaching their reward.

**Alms Against Interest**

The zakāt-clause carries, in this position, a polemical weight the commentators make explicit: the institutional alms are the moral opposite of ribā, and the verse sets the opposition on the record. The two are inverse social machines. The zakāt moves wealth from the possessing to the needing as an obligation of faith — assessed, annual, distributed to the categories the sūrah of Repentance will list — while the ribā moves wealth from the needing to the possessing as an obligation of debt, compounding with time. The one treats the surplus as a trust whose release purifies (the root zakāh: to purify, to grow — both the blight-and-growth verbs of the previous verse gathered in one institution); the other treats the deficit as a revenue-source. The Prophetic saying the exegetes quote here states the opposition's arithmetic: the end of ribā, even where it seems to increase, is diminishment. Placed immediately after the blight-and-growth thesis (2:276), the verse's four terms read as the growth-system's specification — belief, work, prayer, alms are the four channels through which the true rabā flows, and the zakāt is the channel the devourers inverted.

**Their Reward With Their Lord**

The reward-formula is now so established in the section that its recurrence is itself meaningful: *they will receive their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve*. The custody-clause — the reward with, at, from the Lord — has sealed the un-reminding givers (2:262) and the four-quadrant givers (2:274), and its assignment here to the pillar-community extends the custody from the charitable act to the instituted life: the zakāt-payer's reward sits in the same divine keeping as the secret night-gift's. The no-fear-no-grief formula, which the tradition reads as the sūrah's language of final security, has by this point appeared across the revelations' faithful (2:62), the sūrah of the Table's parallel (5:69), the section's complete givers, and now the community's four-term portrait — and each recurrence narrows the distance between the formula's bearers until this verse, where it rests on the instituted believers themselves. The commentators' note that the verse's content parallels 2:62 and 5:69 sharpens the point: the security once stated for the faithful of every revelation is here stated for the community that keeps its two pillars — the same reward, the same custody, the same two negations, now as a description of institutional life rather than of creed alone.

**The Establishment-Verb**

The prayer-clause's verb deserves the attention the tradition gives it: āqāmū — they established, they caused to stand. The sūrah's prayer-guarding verse (2:238) commanded the keeping — ḥāfiẓū — of the prayers and their middle; this verse describes the community's characteristic act as the establishing, and the commentators gloss the difference as the difference between performance and institution: the prayer established is the prayer given standing — in the person's day (its times kept, its devotion honored) and in the community's space (its congregation maintained, its mosques built, its children taught). The same verb has run through the sūrah from its opening — those who establish the prayer (2:3) — and its reappearance in the economy-block is the verse's quiet structural claim: the market's ethics are anchored in the mosque's discipline. The zakāt-clause inherits the anchoring: the alms-tax is not occasional generosity but established giving — assessed, owed, distributed — the charity-section's free quadrants (2:274) crystallized into an institution that survives the moods of the giver.

**The Portrait Between Two Warnings**

The verse's position is the block's hinge: between the ribā-case's closing verdict — the ungrateful evildoer unloved (2:276) — and its opening commands to the believers, fear God and leave the remainder (2:278). The portrait does transitional work in both directions. Backward, it answers the devourers' residency with the community's security: the Fire's permanent residents on one side, the no-fear-no-grief on the other, and the four terms as the road between. Forward, it identifies who is being commanded: the leave-what-remains order, the war-declaration's warning, the respite-and-charity invitation are all addressed to the people this verse has just described — believers whose belief is established practice, and whose practice includes the alms that ribā inverts. The commentators read the sequence as pedagogy: the community is shown what it is (the four terms, the reward, the security) before being told what to do about the outstanding ribā in its ledgers (the commands) — identity before obligation, so that the obligation arrives not as an external imposition but as the four terms' own requirement. The verse is thus the block's mirror held up before its legislation: this is the community the coming commands assume, and the commands exist so that the mirror keeps matching.
