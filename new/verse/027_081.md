## Sūrah an-Naml 27:81

> Nor can you lead the blind out of their misguidance. You can make none hear ˹the truth˺ except those who believe in Our revelations, ˹fully˺ submitting ˹to Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Image Completes the Set**

The previous verse gave two figures for unresponsiveness, the dead and the deaf. This one adds the blind, and with it the series is finished: no life, no hearing, no sight.

The three together cover every channel by which a message could arrive. The Qur'an uses this triad often, sometimes as a list — deaf, dumb, blind — and the repetition indicates a settled way of describing the condition. What makes the version here distinctive is the verb attached to the third term. The Prophet cannot *guide* the blind away from their error, which is a stronger claim than that they cannot see. A blind man can be led. The verse says this one cannot, because the blindness in question is not of the eye.

**Where the Blindness Is Located**

Another chapter states the point directly: it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts within the breasts. That verse is the Qur'an's own gloss on the figure, and it removes any ambiguity about what organ is meant.

Relocating the blindness to the heart explains why guidance fails. A man blind in the eye still wants to reach the destination and will accept a hand; the problem is only in the seeing. A man blind in the heart has no sense that there is a destination, and the offer of a hand is unintelligible to him. This chapter established the mechanism at verse 4, where those who do not believe in the Hereafter have their deeds made attractive to them so that they wander blindly. The blindness there was an outcome of a prior refusal, not a starting condition, and the same is implied here.

**The Positive Clause Is the Point**

After three negations the verse turns: *in tusmiʿu illā man yu'minu bi-āyātinā* — you can make none hear except those who believe in Our signs. The whole passage has been building to this restriction, and it identifies who the Prophet is actually speaking to.

The formulation runs against ordinary expectation. One would think belief follows from hearing, not that hearing is available only to those who already believe. What the verse describes is a receptivity that precedes the argument and determines whether the argument can land at all. The chapter has shown both cases in narrative. Sheba's queen heard a letter from a foreign king, found it worth acting on, and eventually said she had wronged herself; Pharaoh's court saw nine signs, recognised them in their own souls, and called them sorcery. The material presented to each was not the deficient variable.

**"Submitting" as the Second Condition**

Belief is not the only qualifier. Those who hear are described as *muslimūn* — submitting, surrendered. The pairing of belief with submission adds something to the description.

A person can accept that a claim is true and decline to act on it, and the Qur'an is attentive to that gap. Pharaoh's court is the chapter's own example: they were certain in their souls and rejected all the same. Assent without surrender is precisely the position the chapter has been documenting in its opponents. The verse therefore names two things — the inner recognition and the practical yielding that follows — and describes hearing as belonging only to those in whom both are present. The word is also the one Sheba's queen used of herself, twice, when she declared her submission along with Sulaymān.

**Al-Rāzī on What This Does for the Prophet**

Al-Rāzī reads verses 80 and 81 as directed at the Prophet's own condition rather than at the deniers. His argument is that hope of getting something from people weakens one's ability to oppose them, and that absolving the Prophet of responsibility for their belief strengthens his capacity to declare the truth.

The observation is shrewd and it is about the psychology of a person who depends on an audience. A preacher who believes the outcome rests on his performance will begin, without deciding to, adjusting the message toward what his listeners can accept. Removing the outcome from his account removes the pressure that produces the adjustment. On al-Rāzī's reading the passage is not a verdict on Mecca at all but an instruction to the man addressing it, and the instruction to rely on God two verses earlier is what these verses exist to make possible.

**Sealed Hearts and the Question of Responsibility**

Al-Ṭabarī explains the passage in terms of sealing: those whose hearts God has sealed cannot be made to understand, and those God has made deaf cannot then be made to hear. That formulation raises the obvious difficulty about responsibility, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a deflection.

The Qur'an's own sequencing supplies one. Wherever the sealing is described, it follows a refusal rather than preceding it. Al-Baqarah says God has sealed their hearts and then explains, in the surrounding verses, that they had first chosen error over guidance. This chapter's fourth verse works the same way: those who do not believe in the Hereafter have their deeds made attractive to them, with the disbelief stated first and the consequence second. The sealing describes what a settled refusal does to the faculty that refused, which is a familiar enough phenomenon at a human scale. Capacities that go unused close, and they close by a law rather than by an arbitrary decree.

**Why the Chapter Needs This Verse Here**

There is a structural reason this passage sits where it does, between the command to trust God and the description of the final signs. It marks the end of the persuasive section.

An-Naml has argued for eighty verses — with narrative, with questions about rain and rivers and the guidance of travellers, with an instruction to go and inspect the ruins. This verse states the limit of what argument accomplishes and identifies the audience in whom it works. Everything after this point in the chapter is no longer addressed to the possibility of convincing the unwilling. It describes what happens at the end, and the shift in the chapter's mode is announced here.
