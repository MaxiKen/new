## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:6

> Have they not seen how many ˹disbelieving˺ peoples We destroyed before them? We had made them more established in the land than you. We sent down abundant rain for them and made rivers flow at their feet. Then We destroyed them for their sins and replaced them with other peoples.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Put to People Who Had Seen the Ruins**

The sentence begins as an appeal to sight. "Have they not seen" asks the Quraysh to notice what was already lying along their roads: societies destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, "leaving them in total ruin" with abandoned wells and lofty palaces (22:45). The Book repeats the invitation because this audience did travel. Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers (3:137); the earlier peoples were superior in might and cultivated the land more than these Meccans ever have (30:9).

The question, then, is not whether they had been told but whether they had looked. Every caravan headed north passed the wreckage of cultures that had once been stronger. The verse turns a trade route into a library.

**More Established in the Land Than You**

Four things are credited to the destroyed peoples: firm establishment, abundant rain, rivers flowing at their feet, and time. Together they describe the settled agrarian power of the region, the kind of wealth that comes from soil and water rather than from caravans and a sanctuary. Other audiences are told the same thing — that luxurious and splendid generations were erased before them (19:74), and that whole societies far superior in might to the one that drove the Prophet out were destroyed with none to help them (47:13).

The comparison cuts both ways. Those peoples were better equipped than the Quraysh, so their destruction cannot be explained by weakness, and the Quraysh cannot read their own survival as a certificate of favour. What is described here is not merit rewarded with water; it is power held on loan from a lender who had already called in other debts.

**The Loan That Looked Like a Deed**

Rain and rivers feel like permanent property to those who inherit them. The Book treats them as monthly instalments on an account that will be closed. Those who disbelieve should not imagine that a longer life is good for them; they are only given more time, and a punishment follows (3:178). God draws such people on gradually in ways they cannot comprehend, delaying their end while His own planning stays flawless (68:44–45).

Pharaoh read the streams flowing at his feet as proof of his sovereignty (43:51) and was answered by the sea. Water is the last asset a tyrant thinks can be repossessed, and the verse makes the repossessing part of the record. The owners of the irrigation were removed; the irrigation was not.

**The Word Behind "Peoples"**

The Arabic term translated as peoples is *qarn*, a word that names a generation, a stretch of decades, and also a horn — the animal's weapon. That range fits a verse which describes a whole era of strength in the same sentence as its removal. The Book uses the term for the peoples destroyed after Noah (17:17), for the luxurious generations wiped out before this one (19:74), and for the societies of wrongdoers replaced by others (21:11).

A *qarn* is not a tribe on a map and not a nation-state with borders; it is a run of human beings bound together by a shared period, defined by the time they occupied rather than by anything they chose. Measuring them that way is itself the lesson: an age of power is a length of time, and time is the one asset no dynasty controls.

**Then Others Were Raised in Their Place**

Removal is followed by replacement. Another generation was raised after them (23:31), and those who abandon faith are answered with a people whom God will love and who will love Him (5:54). Even the generations who inherited the Scripture and repeated their predecessors' offences were allowed to continue for a while (7:169), which shows that the mechanism in this verse is not a single episode of punishment but a standing order of things.

No clan is necessary to God's project, and no custodianship is permanent by right. That claim struck at the Quraysh where they felt safest, since they believed their service to the sanctuary secured their future. The verse does not argue the point; it describes what has already happened to others who felt the same way.

**Sin Treated as a Public Debt**

The verse says they were destroyed "for their sins," and it says it about a people rather than about individuals. The Book often works this way when it speaks of communities: many are the societies destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, left in total ruin (22:45); whole societies far superior in might to the Prophet's were destroyed with none to help them (47:13). The wrong is corporate because the policy was corporate, made in a council and enforced by a majority that found the practices convenient.

This is not a replacement for personal responsibility, which the sūrah asserts elsewhere in the sharpest terms. It is a statement that a civilisation can be a decision rather than an accident. The audience is told that Allah would never wrong such peoples, but that they wronged themselves (30:9) — the ruin is the return of their own choice, arriving in its own time.

**What the Comparison Leaves With Its Hearers**

Establishment, abundant water, and long years were granted to peoples who fell, and the ones listening to this verse had less of all three. That is the trap inside the comparison. The Quraysh were not the strongest power in the region, and the ruins they camped beside belonged to powers that had been stronger than they were.

If abundance did not preserve its owners, a trading town in a barren valley has even less reason to trust its own continuance. The verse leaves that conclusion unspoken, because every item on its list is already in the past tense, and the audience knew whose rivers had dried up and whose palaces were standing empty.
