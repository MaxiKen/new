## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:98

> Except helpless men, women, and children who cannot afford a way out—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Except the Helpless — Al-Mustaḍʿafīn**

The exception *illā al-mustaḍʿafīna mina al-rijāli wa al-nisāʾi wa al-wildāni* — except helpless men, women, and children — qualifies the verdict that those who remained claiming oppression while earth is wide will have Hell as home (4:97). *Mustaḍʿaf* — deemed weak, reckoned oppressed, held down — was explained not as moral weakness but as lack of means: those judged weak by circumstance, not those who chose weakness where a way existed. The sūrah has used the same root where the oppressed men, women, and children cry for a saviour (4:75); there the cry was a reason to fight for them, here the same class is a reason to excuse them. To name *men, women, and children* was read as comprehensive: not only fighting men are assessed, but women and children whose movement depends on others and whose legal capacity the sūrah has guarded throughout — in dowry (4:4), in inheritance (4:7–11), in marriage limits (4:3), and in the command to return trusts and judge fairly (4:58). Where 4:97 counted those who wronged themselves by staying when they could leave, this verse counts those who are truly *mustaḍʿaf* and therefore did not wrong themselves by staying because staying was not chosen.

**Who Cannot Afford a Way Out**

The definition *lā yastaṭīʿūna ḥīlatan wa lā yahtadūna sabīlā* — who cannot afford a way out, cannot manage stratagem, and cannot find a way — was explained as inability at both ends: no means to move and no knowledge to move. *Ḥīlah* — stratagem, device, means, resource — was read as affordability: provision for journey, mount, guide, refuge at the other end — the same abundance that the emigrant is promised will be found spacious upon His earth (4:100). *Wa lā yahtadūna sabīlā* — nor find a way, nor are guided to a path — was explained as not knowing the road, the pass, the contact among believers, the treaty people to join (4:90). The double negation was stressed: neither resource to scheme a departure nor guidance to a road. The sūrah has said that God wills to lighten burdens because humanity was created weak (4:28) and that burdens were eased for the one who cannot afford to marry a free woman (4:25); here the same lightening appears as exemption from the threat of Hell as refuge. Early reports illustrated it with persons in Makkah — the aged, the nursing, the blind — who could not walk or did not know the desert tracks to Madinah, even while they wished to be with the believers. Their inability was not claimed oppression but real lack of *ḥīlah* and *sabīl*.

**A Verse That Holds Two Judgments Together**

The brevity — *“except helpless men, women, and children who cannot afford a way out”* (4:98) — was read as the sūrah’s balance between two judgments that might otherwise collide. The first judgment is mobility as duty where the earth is wide: *“Was Allah’s earth not spacious enough for you to emigrate?”* (4:97). The second is mercy that does not burden beyond capacity: *“God wishes to lighten”* (4:28), *“do not kill yourselves, God is ever Merciful”* (4:29), *“if you avoid major sins We absolve lesser”* (4:31). To hold both, the sūrah inserts this exception between threat and hope: the threat remains for those who could find *ḥīlah* and *sabīl* and did not, while the hope remains for those who truly could not. The construction also mirrors the earlier distinction between those who stay with valid excuse — *ulū al-ḍarar* — who are promised *ḥusnā* though not equal to strivers (4:95), and those who stay claiming oppression when excuse is absent. Here the women and children are named explicitly because the sūrah earlier quadrupled the weight of infant and female weakness in inheritance and dowry law; their explicit exemption shows that the law that guards the weak also excuses them.

**The Trap That Holds the Sentence Open**

Grammatically the verse is a dependent clause — *except helpless… who cannot…* — whose main clause is the next: *“it is right to hope God will pardon them”* (4:99). The suspension was explained as deliberate: the exception is not left as praise but as the condition for a hoped pardon, keeping both divine threat and divine mercy in place. The sūrah has sealed pardon where error was by mistake with *tawbatan mina Allāhi* (4:92) and strivers’ wages with forgiveness and mercy (4:96); here the pardon for the helpless is left as hope — *ʿasā* — so that neither presumption nor despair settles. Classical commentators noted the inclusion of children — whose accountability is not that of adults — shows that the exception describes a class of persons left behind whose journey depends on guardians; their inclusion underscores that emigration is a household, not only a warrior’s, obligation where household can move. The verse therefore draws a line the divided community (4:88) needed: not every remaining is a hypocrite who wishes you alike in disbelief, and not every claim of *“we were oppressed”* is accepted at soul-taking; only those who cannot afford device and cannot find a way are excepted from the evil destination.

**A Light Verse That Makes the Next Mercy Intelligible**

Though a fragment — five words in Arabic besides the categories — the verse makes the next mercy intelligible. Without it, *“it is right to hope God will pardon them”* (4:99) would have no subject; with it, the hope has precisely these — men, women, children without *ḥīlah* or *sabīl*. The sūrah’s ethic — return trusts, judge fairly, verify reports (4:58, 4:83), be sure before fighting (4:94), and do not confuse permission over hostility with permission over believers (4:92–93) — is here extended to the judgment of the remaining: verify inability, then except. For the weak the sūrah lightens, this is the lightest of sentences: where you truly could not afford device and could not find a way, the evil destination does not attach, and the next verse will place your case under hoped pardon from the Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving . The verse after that will show the opposite face: the one who could find device and way and did emigrate finds many refuges and, if death meets him on the way, his reward secured with the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful (4:100).
