## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:1

> All praise is for Allah, to Whom belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And praise be to Him in the Hereafter. He is the All-Wise, All-Aware.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Five Sūrahs Open With Praise, and No Two Praise the Same Thing**

Five chapters of the Qur'an begin with *al-ḥamdu lillāh*, and each one attaches that praise to a different ground. The Opening praises the Lord of all worlds: *"All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds"* (1:2). Al-Anʿām praises the Maker of light and dark: *"All praise is for Allah Who created the heavens and the earth and made darkness and light"* (6:1). Al-Kahf praises the Giver of a straight Book: *"All praise is for Allah Who has revealed the Book to His servant, allowing no crookedness in it"* (18:1). Fāṭir praises the Originator who staffs the heavens with winged messengers (35:1). Sabaʾ takes the fifth position, and its ground is ownership — everything in both realms already belongs to the One being praised. The pattern rules out the idea that these openings are decoration repeated by habit. A formula that changes its reason every time it appears is an argument, not a refrain. Here the reason chosen is the one the rest of the sūrah will need, because a chapter about to dismantle the claims of rival deities must first establish who owns the property under dispute.

**Ownership Is Stated Before Praise Is Explained**

The clause is *lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Arabic marks possession here with a single prefixed letter, the *lām* of ownership, placed before the pronoun so that the owner is named first and the property second. The relative pronoun is *mā*, the word used for things rather than persons, and its breadth is the point: not some of what is there, not the valuable part of it, but the undifferentiated whole. The same sentence recurs across the Book in slightly varied settings — at the heart of the Throne Verse (2:255), attached to divine exaltation in *"To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And He is the Most High, the Greatest"* (42:4), attached to self-sufficiency in (22:64) and (31:26), and attached to a warning of punishment in (14:2). Placing it here, immediately after the praise, supplies the logic that praise usually leaves unstated. Praise offered to someone who owns nothing is flattery. Praise offered to the owner of everything is simply an accurate description of the situation.

**Praise That Does Not End When the World Does**

The third clause moves the praise past the horizon of this life: *"And praise be to Him in the Hereafter"* (34:1). Most human gratitude is tied to a delivery — a rescue, a harvest, a recovery — and it fades as the memory of the gift fades. This clause detaches praise from that pattern by placing it in the one location where no further gifts are being negotiated and nothing more can be extracted. The Qur'an follows through on the claim in its scenes of the next world. Those admitted to the Garden say, *"Praise be to Allah for guiding us to this"* (7:43); they say, *"Praise be to Allah, Who has kept away from us all ˹causes of˺ sorrow"* (35:34); their final word in the Garden is *"All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds!"* (10:10). Even the judgment itself closes on it: after every case has been decided with fairness, the angels around the Throne are heard glorifying, and the verdict is sealed with *"Praise be to Allah—Lord of all worlds!"* (39:75). Praise, on this account, is not the currency of petition. It is what remains when petition is over.

**Two Names That Answer the Objection Coming Next**

The verse closes with *al-Ḥakīm al-Khabīr*. The first name comes from the root *ḥ-k-m*, which in Arabic carries the sense of restraint as much as of judgment — from the same root comes *ḥakamah*, the bridle-bit that holds a horse to its course. Wisdom in this sense is not cleverness but the ordering of things toward their proper end. The second name, *al-Khabīr*, derives from *kh-b-r*, and *khibrah* in ordinary Arabic is the knowledge a person acquires from handling a thing directly rather than hearing about it: insider knowledge, knowledge of the interior. Searching this translation of the whole Qur'an, the pair "All-Wise, All-Aware" stands at only four places — (6:18), (6:73), (11:1) and here. The choice is exact for what Sabaʾ is about to face. Its opponents will argue that the promised Hour is late, and lateness suggests either bad planning or ignorance. One name closes the first exit, the other closes the second. A wise arrangement can include delay on purpose; an aware one cannot be surprised by what it delays.

**Place and Time Are Both Covered, in Two Other Verses**

Two related statements elsewhere show what this opening is doing by comparison. Al-Rūm spreads praise across locations and hours together: *"all praise is for Him in the heavens and the earth—as well as in the afternoon, and at noon"* (30:18). Al-Qaṣaṣ spreads it across the two ages: *"All praise belongs to Him in this life and the next"* (28:70). Sabaʾ takes the second axis, the temporal one, and states only the far end of it — the Hereafter — because the near end has already been covered by the ownership clause that came before. Reading the three together, no region of space and no stretch of time is left where praise would be misplaced or would lapse. This is also why the verse does not say that people will praise Him then. It says the praise is His, whether or not anyone offers it. The grammar makes praise a property of the One praised rather than an achievement of the one praising.

**A Chapter That Begins From What Its Opponents Cannot Deny**

Sabaʾ opens an argument, and the opening move is deliberately the least contested one available. The Meccan opponents addressed through this sūrah did not deny that God exists, nor that He created; their quarrel was over exclusivity, prophethood and resurrection. Beginning with praise and ownership therefore starts on ground they had already conceded, and everything contested later in the chapter is built on that footing. There is also a link backwards. The previous sūrah ends by describing the trust that the heavens, the earth and the mountains declined to carry while humanity took it up (33:72). Opening the next sūrah with unqualified praise answers the natural worry that follows: if the trust is so heavy, does God gain when it is carried and lose when it is dropped? The answer is that praise is already complete, in this world and the next, owing nothing to the performance of the one who carries it. *"It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy"* (35:15). The verse that follows will press the same independence into a claim about knowledge.
