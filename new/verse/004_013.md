## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:13

> These ˹entitlements˺ are the limits set by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger will be admitted into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. That is the ultimate triumph!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Limits That Protect Rather Than Confine**

The pronoun *tilka* — these — gathers the entire inheritance code that runs across verses 7 through 12 and names it *ḥudūd Allāh* — the limits set by Allah. In Qur’anic usage *ḥadd* marks a boundary that may not be crossed, like the edge of a field or the wall of a sanctuary. To call fractions *limits* teaches that arithmetic has a moral edge. The same term appears elsewhere for fasting, divorce procedure, and penal law, showing that where people are most likely to follow appetite — with food, with family ties, with profit — the law draws a clear line. Here the line runs through property that families have long treated as theirs to divide by custom or by favour. By naming those divisions limits set by God, the verse lifts the shares from negotiation to reverence. A heir who asks for more than his portion is not merely asking family for generosity; he is stepping beyond a boundary God drew. Commentators noted that the sūrah pairs *ḥudūd* with obedience to God and His Messenger precisely because the shares were revealed in a setting where resistance was open.

**Obedience to Allah and His Messenger Together**

The promise *wa man yuṭiʿi Allāha wa rasūlahu yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger will be admitted into Gardens under which rivers flow — joins two obediences in one reward. The shares were recited by the Messenger ﷺ and clarified by his practice — which debts come first, which bequests stand, how the residue goes, how multiple wives share a single portion — so obeying God here includes following the Messenger’s explanation. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah”* (4:80), and this verse makes that union concrete at the moment of estate division, when a person might obey God in prayer yet follow custom in property. The sūrah therefore makes the counting table a place of obedience. Measuring a half, a quarter, an eighth, a sixth, or two-thirds correctly is no longer mere accounting; it is the form obedience takes when wealth changes hands at death. The promise that follows is proportionate: the one who respects limits with wealth is welcomed into a space described as abundant water flowing below — a image of plenty without dispute.

**Gardens Under Which Rivers Flow as the Appropriate Reward**

The description *jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār khālidīna fīhā* — Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever — appears across the Qur’an as the counter to the quarrel over portions. The inheritance code divided fixed pools; the Garden is described as water that does not need to be divided because it flows continuously and suffices all. The sūrah elsewhere promises that avoiding major sins leads to being absolved of lesser ones and admitted to a place of honour (4:31), and this verse shows one concrete major limit whose keeping leads to that honour. The language of permanence — *khālidīna fīhā* — staying forever — answers the impermanence of estates. What a person surrendered in this world — a fraction he thought was his, a bequest he was tempted to write to harm others — returns as a dwelling that does not shrink or expire. The Qur’an elsewhere contrasts the brief enjoyment of this world with the lasting home of the next (4:77), and the same contrast operates here. The limits on wealth today secure wealth that never needs dividing tomorrow.

**That Is the Ultimate Triumph**

The closing *wa dhālika al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm* — that is the ultimate triumph — names the outcome as success on the largest scale. *Fawz* in the Qur’an is not merely entering a pleasant place; it is being delivered from the Fire and admitted to the Garden, as the next verse will make explicit by describing the opposite. The phrase appears elsewhere for those who obey and keep trusts, and it gathers both the inner and outer dimensions of triumph: right relation with God and lasting well-being for the self. By placing that phrase immediately after the fractions, the verse teaches that the triumph is not in securing a larger share through pressure, but in receiving the share God assigned and trusting His promise about what follows. Al-Rāzī observes that the sūrah deliberately links the technical laws of inheritance to the highest theological promise, so that no one treats them as secondary or worldly. What looks like civil law is placed on the same ledger as faith and worship. A person who divides an estate while mindful that *“Allah is ever Watchful”* (4:1) and that *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6) is already living the triumph the verse names.

**Why the Promise Comes Here Before the Threat**

The sūrah gives the Garden before the Fire, the reward before the warning. That order reflects a consistent Qur’anic method of inviting obedience through hope before reinforcing it with fear. The list of shares was demanding in a context where Male relatives had taken full estates; to hear first that keeping the limits leads to perpetual Gardens makes the demand feel like guidance rather than burden. The sūrah will shortly add *“Allah wishes to lighten your burdens, for humankind was created weak”* (4:28), and this promise already lightens the specific burden of parting with wealth by showing what parting correctly secures. The verse also frames the limits as entitlements — *tilka ḥudūd Allāh* can be read as these shares are limits — so that the promise belongs to the rightful recipient as much as to the executor. The widow who receives her quarter or eighth, the daughter who receives her half or two-thirds, the mother who receives her third or sixth — each is not only receiving property but standing inside a limit that, if honoured by all, will be counted as obedience.

**The Limits That Hold the Community Together**

Read with verses 7 through 12, this verse seals the sūrah’s first legal suite with theology. Creation from a single soul taught that all are kin (4:1); trust laws taught that the strong must guard the weak (4:2–10); inheritance laws taught that every close heir has a named portion after debts and bequests (4:11–12). Now the sūrah lifts those portions and says they are *ḥudūd* — limits — whose keeping is obedience and whose breach is disobedience. The following verse will state the other side — humiliating punishment for crossing them — but this verse is allowed to stand first and to linger. It suggests that a community that respects divine limits in its most sensitive moment — when grief and greed meet over a loved one’s goods — builds a habit that steadies every other limit. Gardens under which rivers flow, forever, is the sūrah’s way of saying that what is surrendered at the division table is not lost; it is transformed. The measurer who gave each his due and sought no more has kept faith with the limits, and with the One who set them.
