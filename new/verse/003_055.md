## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:55

> ˹Remember˺ when Allah said, “O Jesus! I will take you and raise you up to Myself. I will deliver you from those who disbelieve, and elevate your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment. Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and I will settle all your disputes.

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Speaking to His Servant**

The verse opens with a reminder of a conversation: when God said, O Jesus. The speaker is God and the addressee is the messenger, and the words that follow are a set of promises spoken at the moment the plot of the previous verse was in motion.

Placing the promises in God's own voice matters for how the passage is read. Everything here is a commitment made by the one who made the plan, and the reader is being shown the content of the countermeasure at the exact point where the sūrah has just told him that a countermeasure was made. The promises are delivered to the man in danger, and the sūrah records them in full.

**Mutawaffī: the Word That Carries the Dispute**

The first promise uses a word from the root that governs taking and completing, and the sūrah's translation renders it as taking to Himself. The commentators spent considerable effort on it, and their disagreement is worth setting out plainly. A large group held that the word ordinarily refers to death, that God takes a person to Himself at the end of his life, and that in this verse, in view of what the Book says about the crucifixion, it means God's removal of Jesus from the world rather than his death at the hands of enemies. Others held that the word does retain its ordinary sense here and that the verse describes God causing him to die. A third reading took it in the sense of sleep, comparing the verse that says God calls back souls by night (6:60) and the verse that says He takes the souls of people at their death and those who do not die during their sleep (39:42).

Each reading has a Qur'anic usage behind it. What is not in dispute is that the word places the whole matter in God's hands: whatever the taking is, the taker is the one being addressed, and no enemy appears in the sentence.

**"And Raise You Up to Myself"**

The second promise names an elevation, and the Book states the same event when it answers the boasts of the plotters: they did not kill him, and God raised him up to Himself (4:158). The two verses describe one act from two angles, one as the removal of a servant from a hostile world and the other as an ascent.

The phrase "to Myself" is the part that has drawn the most attention from the commentators. The Book uses it nowhere else of a human being at the end of a mission, and whatever it means in detail, it states a destination rather than a location. The promise to a man whose enemies were arranging his execution is that he will be brought near to the one who sent him, and the nearness is the answer to everything the plot intended.

**Purification From the Disbelievers**

The third promise is that God will clear him of those who disbelieved. The commentators gave the verb two senses that complement each other: freeing him from their power, and absolving him of the charges they laid against him. Both are needed if the verse is to answer the situation described.

The followers of Jesus had accused him of blasphemy and sought his death; the sūrah's readers knew what the community had said about his mother and about his birth. A promise that he would be freed from their grasp and cleared of their claims is the answer to a slander as well as to a threat, and the Book's later defenses of Mary and of her son belong to the same act. What God promises in this clause is that the record will be straightened out, and the sūrah's narrative is part of how that happens.

**Elevating His Followers Above the Disbelievers**

The fourth promise concerns his followers, and the commentators offered distinctive readings. One takes it as a statement about the historical relation between the Christian community and the community that rejected Jesus, the former prevailing politically and culturally for centuries. A second takes the followers to be those who believed in him correctly rather than all who later claimed his name. A third reads the elevation as a matter of proof and argument: the case of the believers will stand above the case of the deniers.

The three readings are not mutually exclusive, and they converge on the Book's usual way of describing success. The Book does not promise its people victory in numbers; it promises that truth will not be extinguished, and it allows that promise to be fulfilled in the arguments people make, in the survival of the community, and in the verdict of the Hereafter, often in combinations that no single generation can observe.

**"Then to Me You Will All Return"**

The promises end with a return, and the return is addressed to everyone involved rather than only to the messenger: the plotters, the believers, the deniers, and the men who made promises of support in the earlier verses. The disputes are to be settled there.

The sūrah has already used this closing device, and it will use it again. A statement about God's handling of history acquires its finality from the return, since a world without it would leave the schemes of the powerful as the last word on how matters are arranged. What the verse gives its readers is a limit on every controversy: no dispute in this Book is left open at the end, and none of them is settled by the party that happens to hold power while it is running.

**The Return of Jesus in the Tradition**

The commentaries also record the belief, held across the classical authorities, that Jesus will return before the end of time, that he will fight the false messiah, and that he will rule according to the law of the final Prophet. The reports about this are numerous and their like is not found for other prophets, but the verse above and the passages that parallel it do not state the doctrine, and the reader should be careful to attribute it to the tradition that carries it rather than to this sentence.

What the verse does state is complete on its own. A messenger was taken, raised, cleared, and given followers who would prevail, and he returns to the one who sent him. The next verses describe what happens on that day to two groups.
