## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:101

> ˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and earth. How could He have children when He has no mate? He created all things and has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Originator of the Heavens and the Earth**

He is the Originator of the heavens and earth. The title is one the sūrah has used before, both in the command given to the Prophet to declare that he will take no guardian other than the Originator of the heavens and the earth (6:14) and in Abraham's declaration when he turned his face toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth (6:79). The Book uses the title consistently in the phrase Originator of the heavens and the earth, and the commentators noted that its root carries the sense of splitting open, which connects it to the image of the seed that splits at the beginning of this passage about signs.

The term describes a creator who brings things into being without a prior model, and the sūrah uses it here to answer the claim about offspring made in the previous verse. A being who originates the heavens and the earth has no need of a consort, and the sentence that follows states the reasoning plainly.

**How Could He Have Children?**

How could He have children when He has no mate? The question reduces the claim to an impossibility in a single clause, and the Book makes the same argument elsewhere in its own voice. The sūrah of the Cow answers the claim that God has offspring by saying that everything in the heavens and the earth is subject to Him (2:116), and the sūrah of Maryam states that it is not for God to take a son, since when He decrees a matter He simply says to it, Be, and it is (19:35).

The Book's argument about offspring rests on two observations: children are produced through the union of mates, and God has no mate; and everything other than God is created, whereas a child would have to share in the divine nature. The sūrah of Sincerity gathers the conclusion into a single declaration that God has never had offspring and was never born (112:3), and the sūrah of the Women addresses the same claim in its discussion of Jesus, saying that the Messiah was no more than a messenger of God and that God is far above having a son (4:171).

**The Claim About Jesus in the Sūrah's Setting**

The Makkans' claim about daughters and the Christians' claim about a son were both present in the region the sūrah addresses, and the Book treats them together as instances of a single error. The verse's question applies to both, since the reasoning does not depend on the identity of the offspring. What the sūrah denies is the dependence of God on anything at all, and the next clause states the positive basis of the denial.

The Book's statements about Jesus elsewhere uniformly preserve his honour while denying the claim about his nature, and the sūrah of the Table records his own declaration that he is a servant of God. The verse in front of us belongs to that pattern, and its argument applies to any creature that the audience might have placed on the divine side of the line.

**He Created All Things**

He created all things. The clause states the fact on which the argument turns. If everything besides God is created, then nothing besides God is ultimate, and the relations that belong to created things cannot be predicated of Him. The sūrah has used this reasoning in every form of the argument against idolatry it has presented: the idols are created like their worshippers (7:194), the jinn are created and therefore cannot be partners (6:100), and the angels are servants of the Most Compassionate (43:19).

The Book states the same principle in the sūrah of the Thunder, where it declares that God is the Creator of all things and the One, the Supreme (13:16). The sentence in this verse is therefore more than a statement of fact; it is the premise from which the sūrah's denials proceed, and the audience is expected to recognise it as the basis of everything the Book has been saying about worship.

**Knowledge of Everything**

And has perfect knowledge of everything. The verse closes with the attribute the sūrah has attached to God at several points in the chapter. It said earlier that God knows what is on land and sea and that not a leaf falls without His knowledge (6:59), and it described His knowledge as encompassing everything in the argument attributed to Abraham (6:80). The closing phrase here connects the account of creation with the account of the record, since the One who made all things is the One who knows all things.

The pairing of creation and knowledge appears in the Book's descriptions of God wherever it describes His relation to what He has made, and it appears in the sūrahs' closing formulas. The knowledge is not a general awareness but the knowledge of particulars, as the sūrah's earlier verses on the leaf and the grain made clear, and the phrase in this verse carries the same force.

**What the Verse Completes**

The verse completes the sūrah's answer to the claims made in the previous one, and it does so by supplying the positive account of God that the denials presuppose. The audience had associated created beings with God and attributed offspring to Him; the sūrah answers that God originates everything, needs no mate, creates all things, and knows everything. Each clause of the answer is a statement the sūrah has already made elsewhere in the chapter.

The next verse moves to a new subject and opens one of the sūrah's most striking passages, in which the Book describes the limitations of human sight and the reality of God's perception. The argument about God's nature has been stated in terms the audience could follow; the sūrah now turns to what can and cannot be seen.
