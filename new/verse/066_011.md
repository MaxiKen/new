## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:11

> And Allah sets forth an example for the believers: the wife of Pharaoh, who prayed, “My Lord! Build me a house in Paradise near You, deliver me from Pharaoh and his ˹evil˺ doing, and save me from the wrongdoing people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exact Reverse of the Previous Case**

The formula is repeated deliberately: God sets forth an example, as in the verse before, but now *“for the believers”* (66:11). Two women married to righteous men were ruined; one woman married to the most notorious tyrant in the Qur'an is held up as the model. The symmetry is precise and the lesson is the completion of the one just given. If a prophet's household could not save a woman who betrayed the mission, a tyrant's household could not destroy a woman who did not.

Her name is not in the Qur'an, though the reports give it as Āsiya bint Muzāḥim. What the text supplies instead of biography is her prayer, quoted in full, which is an unusual distinction. The Qur'an names few women and quotes fewer. Preserving her words rather than her story suggests what the example is for: the reader is not being told about a historical figure so much as given something to say.

**A House Requested Before a Rescue**

The order of her three requests repays attention. She asks first for a house in Paradise near her Lord, and only then for deliverance from Pharaoh and from the wrongdoing people. A woman in immediate danger puts the eternal request first and the urgent one second.

The phrasing of the first request is also precise: *“Build me a house in Paradise near You”* (66:11). The Arabic places the nearness before the location — near You, in the Garden — so that proximity to God is the specification and the Garden is where it happens. She was, by any material measure, already housed better than almost anyone alive. Palaces were the one thing Pharaoh's wife did not lack, and the Qur'an elsewhere records Pharaoh boasting of his kingdom and its rivers. The prayer asks for the one kind of dwelling that his wealth could not commission, and it asks for it in the vocabulary of building, as if to set the two construction projects side by side.

**Deliverance From a Man and From His Deeds**

The second request separates two things that could have been said as one: *“deliver me from Pharaoh and his ˹evil˺ doing”* (66:11). Escaping the man would not have been enough. What she asks to be free of is also what he did — the system of oppression she lived inside, benefited from, and could not have wholly avoided as the wife of the person running it.

That distinction is the most searching part of the prayer, and it is what makes her a model rather than a victim. A person embedded in an unjust arrangement is implicated by it even when they did not design it, and the honest prayer is not merely to be rescued but to be cleared of the whole business. She then widens it once more, asking to be saved from *“the wrongdoing people”* (66:11) — not just the tyrant but the court, the officials, the whole apparatus that made his rule possible. Tyranny in the Qur'an is rarely the work of one man; it requires a society of people willing to carry out what he wants.

**What the Reports Add About Her End**

The commentators report that Āsiya was tortured by Pharaoh for believing in Moses, and that she spoke these words during that torture, after which she was taken to Paradise. The detail explains the shape of the prayer as nothing else does. A woman under torture asking first for a house near her Lord is describing what she can see; the deliverance she asks for second arrives in the same moment as the house.

Her standing in the tradition is high. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have named the best of the women of Paradise as Khadīja bint Khuwaylid, Fāṭima bint Muḥammad, Maryam bint ʿImrān and Āsiya bint Muzāḥim, the wife of Pharaoh (transmitted in Musnad Aḥmad and cited by Ibn Kathīr in his commentary on this verse). Two of the four named are in this passage, and the chapter closes with the second of them. The pairing is not accidental: the sūrah selects, from all the believing women available, the two whose fidelity to God was worked out entirely without the support of a believing household.

**A Prayer Given to People Who Are Not in Palaces**

The example is set forth *for the believers*, plural and general, which means it is meant to be used by readers whose situations bear no resemblance to hers. That is possible because the prayer is built around a structure that transfers. Ask for nearness to God as the primary thing; ask to be extracted from the specific wrong you are inside; ask to be kept clear of the people who sustain it.

Read against the chapter's earlier verses, the placement becomes sharper still. Believers were told to guard themselves *and* their families from the Fire (66:6), and the obvious objection is the case where the family is the problem. Āsiya answers it. A person whose household is hostile to faith is not thereby excused from faith and not thereby doomed by association; the duty contracts to what remains possible, which is the state of one's own soul and the direction of one's own prayer. Nothing in her prayer asks for her husband to be changed, and nothing asks for the court around him to be reformed. She asks to be taken out. That is a narrower request than the one a believer in a healthy household might make, and the Qur'an records it as sufficient — indeed as exemplary — which tells a reader in a comparable position what is actually being required of them. The chapter has one more example to give, and it concerns a woman with no household at all.
