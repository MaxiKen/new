## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:72

> Whenever Our clear revelations are recited to them, you ˹O Prophet˺ recognize rage on the faces of the disbelievers, as if they are going to snap at those who recite Our revelations to them. Say, “Shall I inform you of something far more enraging than that? ˹It is˺ the Fire with which Allah has threatened those who disbelieve. What an evil destination!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Face Betrays What the Argument Conceals**

*Wa-idhā tutlā ʿalayhim āyātunā bayyinātin taʿrifu fī wujūhi alladhīna kafarū al-munkara* — and when Our signs are recited to them as clear proofs, you recognise the denial in the faces of those who disbelieve.

The sūrah has been interested in faces before. Verse 11 described a man turned over onto his; verse 9 described a shoulder turned away. Here the face is where the state of the heart becomes visible, and the verb *taʿrifu*, you recognise, implies something that can be read without being told.

That is the verse's first claim: the reaction is involuntary. Whatever these people say in argument, their faces have already answered, and the answer is not doubt or boredom. It is rejection that shows physically.

The Qur'an treats the face as a readable surface elsewhere, and always at moments of exposure. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān makes it the index of the verdict: *"On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy. To the gloomy-faced it will be said, 'Did you disbelieve after having believed?'"* (3:106). The same contrast opens Sūrah al-Qiyāmah and Sūrah ʿAbasa (75:22; 80:38–40). What happens here is the same disclosure in miniature: an interior state becoming legible on a face, in public, before anyone has been told anything.

**"They Almost Pounce"**

*Yakādūna yasṭūna bi-alladhīna yatlūna ʿalayhim āyātinā* — they are on the point of assaulting those who recite Our signs to them.

The verb *saṭā* means to seize violently, to spring on something and grip it. The commentators glossed it in exactly that sense. What is described is not debate and not even interruption; it is the edge of physical violence against the reciters.

The detail is worth holding on to, because it tells us what was actually happening. The recitation of the Qur'an in Makkah was not being met with counter-argument. It was being met with something closer to an attack, and the verse names the impulse precisely.

The verb's prefix matters just as much as the verb. *Yakādūna* — they are on the point of, they *almost* — records an impulse that stopped short of the act. The reciters in Makkah were unarmed and unclan-protected, and the sūrah is describing a hostility that could have been carried out and was not. Read as history, that is a statement about the community's exposure; read as revelation, it is a statement about the restraint that kept the recitation going.

**Why Recitation Produces Rage Rather Than Indifference**

The question the verse invites is why. If the message were merely false, the natural responses would be dismissal or boredom. Rage requires that something has been touched.

The sūrah has given the answer in its own vocabulary. The signs are *bayyināt*, clear proofs; the audience's position was described at verse 71 as resting on nothing sent down and nothing known. A person whose practice has no warrant, and who is told so plainly, does not experience the telling as information. He experiences it as an assault on the arrangement his life is built on.

That is also why the rage is directed at the reciters rather than at the argument. The reciter is the one who keeps bringing the thing into the room.

The noun for what shows on their faces carries the same charge. *Al-munkar* is the Qur'an's word for what is to be forbidden — the same word the believers are commanded to prohibit: *"enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong"* (3:104). What the Prophet recognises on these faces is therefore not a neutral expression of displeasure. It is the thing the community is commanded to stand against, appearing on the face of the person who objects to being told.

**"Shall I Tell You of Something Worse Than That?"**

*Qul a-fa-unabbiʾukum bi-sharrin min dhālikum* — Say: shall I inform you of something worse than that?

The rhetorical move is worth noting. The Prophet is not told to answer the rage with rage, or to answer it with a warning delivered in anger. He is told to ask a question, and the question is calibrated to the emotion in front of him.

Worse than what? The commentators took the comparison as worse than the anger itself — worse than the feeling that is contorting their faces. The question therefore meets these people where they actually are, in the middle of the emotion, rather than where they claim to be, in an argument about proofs.

The Qur'an uses the same interrogative frame when it wants to expose a ranking the audience has got backwards: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Shall I inform you of those who deserve a worse punishment from Allah ˹than the rebellious˺?'"* (5:60). The device is a revaluation rather than a threat: the hearer is invited to agree that something is bad, and then shown that he has been worried about the wrong thing.

**"The Fire Which God Has Promised the Disbelievers"**

*Al-nāru waʿadahā Allāhu alladhīna kafarū* — the Fire, which God has promised to those who disbelieve.

The answer reframes the comparison. They are enraged by the recitation; there is something more enraging than the recitation, and it is the destination the recitation was warning them about.

The phrasing is precise. The Fire is described as *promised* to them, which is the language of covenant and appointment rather than of threat. And it is described as what God promised, which means the promise will be kept — the sūrah said at verse 47 that God does not fail His promise.

The symmetry is the part that deserves attention, because the same verb is used for what the believers are given: *"Allah has promised the believers, both men and women, Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever"* (9:72). One verb, two destinations, and the certainty is identical on both sides. The verse is not saying that God has issued a threat which may or may not be carried out. It is saying that these people have an appointment, in the same sense and from the same source as the believers have theirs.

**"What an Evil Destination"**

*Wa-biʾsa al-maṣīr* — and what an evil place of arrival.

The closing formula is the same one the Qur'an uses for the worst outcomes, and the noun *maṣīr* is the word for where a journey ends. The sūrah used the same root at verse 48: *to Me is the destination*.

The echo is deliberate. What these people are heading toward has already been named in this sūrah, and the verse's closing phrase is a summary rather than a new threat.

The formula is not peculiar to this context; Sūrah al-Taghābun closes the fate of the rejecters with it word for word: *"As for those who disbelieve and reject Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Fire, staying there forever. What an evil destination!"* (64:10). Identical wording, identical position after a description of rejection — which tells the reader how to hear it. It is the settled term for an arrival that cannot be reversed, and the sūrah is using a fixed formula rather than inventing an insult.

**The Reaction Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The scene in this verse is not unique, and the repetitions are worth collecting because together they describe a single recognisable pattern.

Sometimes the response is mockery: *“When Our clear revelations are recited to them, the disbelievers ask the believers ˹mockingly˺, ‘Which of the two of us is better in status and superior in assembly?’”* (19:73). Sometimes it is a charge of sorcery: *“Whenever Our clear revelations are recited to them, the disbelievers say of the truth when it has come to them, ‘This is pure magic’”* (46:7). Sometimes it is dismissal: *“whenever Our revelations are recited to him, he says, ‘Ancient fables!’”* (68:15).

What none of them is, is a counter-argument. Every recorded response is a reclassification or a taunt, and this verse adds the one response that is not even verbal: the faces, and the impulse to strike.

That is why the Prophet is told to answer with a question rather than a proof. There is no argument in front of him to answer.

**What the Messenger Is Given to Say**

One last observation about the verse's construction. Everything up to *Say* is addressed to the Prophet as diagnosis — you will see this, you will recognise it, they will nearly attack. Then the Prophet is given exactly one sentence to say in response.

That sentence contains no insult, no threat of his own and no prediction about them. It asks a question and names a destination. The sūrah has been consistent about this since verse 49: the messenger's office is the warning, and the warning is not his to enforce.

What he is conspicuously not told is as important as what he is told. He is not told to stop reciting, to recite privately, to soften the passages that provoke this, or to answer the rage with a display of strength. The diagnosis is given to him in detail — the faces, the near-assault — and the instruction that follows it is to keep reciting and to say one sentence. The sūrah's confidence is that the recitation is doing something even where it produces nothing but fury, and that the fury itself is part of the evidence.
