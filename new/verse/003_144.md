## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:144

> Muḥammad is no more than a messenger; other messengers have gone before him. If he were to die or to be killed, would you regress into disbelief? Those who do so will not harm Allah whatsoever. And Allah will reward those who are grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name Used in the Sentence**

The verse names the Prophet directly and states that he is nothing more than a messenger, and other messengers had passed away before him. The sūrah uses his name only a few times, and the use of it here is part of the sentence's teaching.

The Book elsewhere insists on the same distinction between the messenger and the message. One passage states that Muhammad is not the father of any of the men among his people but is the messenger of God and the seal of the prophets, and the sentence in this verse works in the same direction by placing him among a line of messengers who came and went.

**Messengers Who Have Passed Away**

The statement that other messengers have gone before him places the Prophet within a sequence, and the Book states the mortality of the messengers in several places. One passage asks whether, if the Prophet were to die, his people would live forever, and another declares that everything is bound to perish except God Himself.

The verses quoted in the comment on this sentence include the declaration that every soul will taste death, and the comment tradition placed it alongside the present verse as a single teaching. The Book prepares its community for the death of the one it loves most, and this verse is the clearest of those preparations, since it asks the question in the community's own voice and answers it in the same breath.

**The Question About Turning Back**

The verse asks whether the community would turn back on its heels if the Prophet died or was killed, and the image describes apostasy in the physical terms of a retreat. Turning around is what a person does when he flees, and the verse uses the picture for the abandonment of the religion.

The question is not rhetorical in the sense of being unanswerable. The sūrah has just described a day when parts of the community did fall back, and the possibility the verse raises had already been demonstrated in a lesser form. The word for turning describes the movement of a person who reverses his direction, and the Book uses it for the abandonment of a commitment rather than for a momentary hesitation. What is being given here is the principle that the day's events made visible.

**To Whom the Harm Would Fall**

The verse states that whoever turns back does not harm God in the least, and the sentence relocates the consequences of apostasy. The loss is entirely on the side of the person who turns, and the cause is unaffected.

The Book repeats this teaching in other passages where it considers the possibility of the community's failure. It states that God has no need of anyone's obedience and that the worlds are not improved by the conduct of those who abandon the truth. The statement in this verse is the same idea applied to a specific crisis, and it removes the last excuse a person might use to justify leaving when the leader is gone.


The verse's final clause states that God will reward the thankful, and the thanksgiving it names is the opposite of turning back. A person who acknowledges what he has been given will not abandon the giver when circumstances change.

The Book connects gratitude with steadfastness throughout, and the ending of the previous verse about the fear of death uses a similar close. The arrangement of the sentence is effective: instead of threatening those who might turn, it directs attention to the people whose response is right, and the reward is stated for them.

**The Rumour at Uḥud**

The comments on this verse record the crisis that occasioned it. During the fighting the Prophet was badly wounded, and word spread that he had been killed, which unsettled parts of the community and raised the morale of its opponents.

The reports describe a companion calling out that if Muhammad had died, the Lord of Muhammad does not die, and urging the people to live and die as he did. They also describe hypocrites among the community taunting the believers with the argument that a true prophet would not have been killed, and pressing them to abandon the religion. The verse answered all of it in a few lines, and the reports that describe the day note how quickly the community's composure was restored once the Prophet was seen alive and the line was held again.

**The Day the Prophet Died**

Years later, when the Prophet did die, the community's response showed what the verse had taught. The reports describe ʿUmar refusing to accept the news and speaking to the assembled people as though the Prophet would not die, and Abū Bakr mounting the pulpit to address them.

Abū Bakr recited this verse, beginning with the statement that whoever had worshipped Muhammad should know that Muhammad had died, and that whoever worshipped God should know that God is alive and does not die (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1241, 1242). ʿUmar later said that it was as though he had never read the verse before that day. The tradition preserved the incident because it shows the verse carrying the community through the moment it had been preparing them for.

**What the Verse Teaches About the Message and the Messenger**

The verse separates the permanence of the message from the mortality of the person who conveys it, and it prepares the community to continue without confusing the two. The Prophet is honored as the messenger, and the mission is what the community is responsible for.

The teaching mattered immediately at Uḥud and again at the Prophet's death, and it has continued to matter in every generation since. A community that identifies its cause with the presence of a particular individual will collapse when that individual is gone. The sūrah provides the correction in advance, and the closing clause about the thankful describes the people who take it.
