## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:76

> Joseph began searching their bags before that of his brother ˹Benjamin˺, then brought it out of Benjamin’s bag. This is how We inspired Joseph to plan. He could not have taken his brother under the King’s law, but Allah had so willed. We elevate in rank whoever We will. But above those ranking in knowledge is the One All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Search Starts With the Other Bags**

The verse specifies an order. Joseph begins with the brothers' baggage and leaves his brother's bag until last. The order is the whole of the device's credibility. Had he gone straight to Benjamin's saddlebag, the search would have looked like a retrieval rather than an enquiry, and the brothers — who have just sworn to their innocence and stated a penalty — would have had grounds to object that the official knew where the object was. By working through ten bags first, Joseph conducts the kind of search that a charge of theft actually requires, and the object emerges at the end of a procedure rather than at its beginning. Beginning elsewhere is what gives the search its evidential value. A bag opened first would have implied a target; ten bags opened in sequence, with the object appearing in the last, produce a result that looks like the outcome of a procedure rather than its premise. The procedure was conducted properly and it found what had been planted, and both facts are true at once.

**What the Order Produces in the Brothers**

Ten bags are opened and found empty. Each one narrows the field, and each narrowing makes the eventual discovery harder to contest. By the time the last bag is reached the brothers have watched the accusation fail nine times over, which is precisely why they are unprepared for the tenth. The chapter is describing how certainty is built in a group of observers: repeated confirmation of a negative result produces confidence that the pattern will hold, and the confidence is what makes the exception devastating. Verse 77 records the brothers' response — an attempt to shift the implication away from themselves — which is the response of people who have just watched their certainty collapse. The collapse is total and immediate. Two verses earlier these men swore by God that they had come to do no harm and offered a penalty drawn from their own law; now they are reaching for a dead brother as an explanation. The chapter measures the distance between those positions without comment, and the distance is the price of a certainty built on ignorance.

**"Then He Brought It Out of His Brother's Bag"**

The discovery is reported in a single clause with no elaboration. There is no description of the object being held up, no reaction from the crowd, no statement from the herald. The cup is produced, and the verse moves immediately to the narrator's comment. The chapter's restraint here is consistent with its treatment of the whole Egyptian section: the significant events are stated and not dramatised, and the significance is supplied by interpretation rather than by description. A reader who has followed verse 70 already knows what is in the bag, so the verse's economy costs nothing. The economy also preserves the dignity of the procedure. The verse describes no face, no reaction and no astonishment; it records a search conducted in order and an object produced at the end of it. What the chapter withholds in drama it returns in procedural exactness, and the exactness is what every verse that follows will rely upon.

**"Thus Did We Devise a Plan for Joseph"**

The narrator's comment uses the chapter's central word. *Kidnā li-Yūsufa kaydan* — We devised a plan for Joseph. The noun is *kayd*, the same term the sūrah applied to the brothers' plot against a child (12:5), to the women's pressure on Joseph (12:28, 12:33), and to the treachery that God does not guide (12:52). Here the word is attached to God and directed in Joseph's favour. The chapter has spent fifty verses showing *kayd* as a human instrument used against the innocent; it now uses the same word for a divine arrangement that protects him. Verse 52 said God does not give success to the stratagem of the treacherous. Verse 76 shows whose stratagem succeeds.

**"He Could Not Have Taken His Brother Under the King's Law"**

The constraint is stated explicitly, and the commentators explain it. Egyptian procedure for theft, on one account, required the offender to be beaten and to pay double the value of the stolen item, after which he went free. Applied to Benjamin, that rule would have returned him to Canaan with his brothers. Joseph was the ruler's minister and was bound by the ruler's law; he could not substitute a penalty of his own. What made the outcome possible was that the accused party supplied the applicable rule from their own tradition (12:75), and that the rule they supplied retained the offender in the custody of the person stolen from. The verse records the jurisdictional difficulty and its resolution in a single sentence.

**"Unless God Had So Willed"**

The exception is attached to the constraint. Joseph could not have taken his brother under the king's law — but God willed otherwise, and the willing is what produced the brothers' own statement of the penalty. The chapter attributes the mechanism to divine initiative rather than to Joseph's cleverness, and the attribution is the same one made about his knowledge elsewhere: God taught him the interpretation of events (12:21), gave him wisdom and knowledge at maturity (12:22), and here devises the plan through him. A reader who takes the verse at its word sees a prophet executing a revelation rather than a minister manipulating a legal system. That is the reading the verse demands, and it changes the moral register of the episode. If the plan is Joseph's cunning, the detention of Benjamin is a stratagem; if it is willed by God, the same detention is a stage in a family's restoration that neither brother can yet see. The chapter offers both descriptions and subordinates the first to the second.

**"We Raise in Degrees Whom We Will"**

The statement generalises beyond the scene. The same assurance appears in the account of Abraham's argument with his people: *“We elevate in rank whoever We please”* (6:83), and the Qur'an describes God elsewhere as exalted in rank and Lord of the Throne (40:15). In this chapter the raising of degrees has already been demonstrated — a boy sold for counted coins now controls a country's grain — and the clause attaches the demonstration to a principle. Elevation is not the reward of a merit that compels it; it is given according to a will, and the chapter's own hero is its instance rather than its exception. The generalisation is set at the summit of Joseph's career for a reason. He has been raised from a well to a slave household to a prison to the granaries of Egypt, and no step in that sequence was earned by ordinary reckoning. The verse names the mechanism behind it and refuses to make it a system: elevation is willed, not calculated, and nobody can claim it as an entitlement.

**"Above Every Possessor of Knowledge Is One More Knowing"**

The closing maxim is one of the most quoted sentences in the Qur'an. The commentators read it on two levels. In context, it notes that Joseph's brothers were themselves men of knowledge and standing, and Joseph knew more than they did; and that all knowledge terminates in God, who is named throughout the Qur'an as the Knower. In general use the sentence expresses a hierarchy: however much a person learns, there is a higher degree of knowledge above him. The maxim is placed at the end of a verse about a plan nobody in the scene understood, which is the chapter's way of saying that the knowledge operating here exceeded every participant's — including, on the tradition's account, the brother who had been told the truth in private.
