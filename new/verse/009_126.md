## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:126

> Do they not see that they are tried once or twice every year? Yet they neither repent nor do they learn a lesson.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Trials Are Opportunities to Wake Up**

The verse points to recurring trials and asks why the people do not repent or take heed. Hardship is not automatically proof that a person is being punished, but it is always an opportunity to examine direction. Illness, hunger, conflict, exposure of secret dealings, and other disruptions can reveal what comfort allowed the heart to ignore.

The hypocrites experience the trial and then return to their old habits. Their problem is not a lack of signs. It is a refusal to let signs change them. The believer asks what a hardship is teaching, even while seeking relief from it and refusing to romanticize pain.

The trials occur once or twice every year. Repeated warning creates repeated chances. A person who ignores one lesson may claim confusion, but a person who ignores the same lesson again and again is choosing a pattern. He sees the consequences and still protects the behavior that produced them.

This applies to private life and public institutions. A family may experience the same conflict after every dishonest conversation. An organization may face the same crisis after every failure of oversight. The wise response is not merely to survive the latest event. It is to identify the habit that keeps recreating it.

**Repentance Is a Change in Direction**

Repentance is more than feeling bad during a trial. It means returning to Allah through confession, abandonment of the wrong, and repair. If greed caused a dispute, the repentant person changes the financial practice. If arrogance caused refusal of advice, he invites honest counsel. If neglect of worship caused spiritual emptiness, he protects the neglected obligation.

The verse joins repentance with taking heed because emotion without learning fades. A person may cry in difficulty and repeat the same behavior when ease returns. Real repentance carries the lesson forward into the next decision.

**Trials Can Expose Hidden Alliances**

A crisis reveals what a person truly trusts. Some trust Allah and use lawful means; others trust reputation, wealth, or a political connection. When those supports fail, the heart's dependence becomes visible. The believer treats this exposure as useful knowledge, even when it is painful.

He asks whether he has made comfort a condition for obedience. Does he pray only when life is orderly? Does he speak truth only when it costs nothing? Does he care for others only when praised? Trials can answer these questions. The answer is not meant to produce despair but to show where reliance must be rebuilt.

Taking heed means preparing differently after the trial. A community that suffered for lack of supplies plans better. A person who was deceived learns to verify without becoming paranoid. A leader whose secrecy caused harm creates transparent procedures. Learning is practical because the Quranic warning is intended to protect future choices.

The believer also prepares spiritually. He stores habits of prayer and generosity before the next hardship comes. A heart trained in ease is more able to remain steady in difficulty. Preparation is not a denial of reliance on Allah. It is one of the means through which reliance becomes responsible action.

**Mercy Appears in the Chance to Notice**

Repeated trials can be understood as mercy because Allah gives the person more than one opportunity to wake up. The warning arrives before the final outcome. A person who sees the pattern can still turn, and a community can still repair its institutions.

This mercy should not be abused. More chances do not guarantee an endless supply of time. The servant does not ask for another crisis before obeying. He takes the present sign seriously, thanks Allah for exposing the weakness, and begins the correction while the heart can still respond.



“Do they not see?” asks whether the problem is vision rather than evidence. The trials are visible, but the hypocrites refuse to interpret them. The believer trains himself to notice moral meaning without pretending to know the exact divine reason for every event.

He says, “What can I learn?” rather than, “Whom can I blame?” That question turns disruption into guidance. It leads to repentance, wiser preparation, and greater compassion for others who are being tested. A trial becomes a sign of mercy when it returns the servant to Allah.

The believer also learns to distinguish a trial from a convenient excuse. A hardship may explain why an obligation was difficult, but it does not automatically erase every responsibility. He asks what was within his capacity and what correction is now possible. This honest accounting avoids both self-condemnation and self-deception. The trial becomes beneficial when it produces a clearer plan, a softer heart toward others, and a stronger habit of turning to Allah before the next difficulty arrives.

Taking heed also includes compassion for people whose trials differ from one's own. The believer does not use another person's illness or loss to announce a simple verdict about his faith. He asks how to help, what justice requires, and what lesson applies to himself. Allah may be teaching the whole community through one person's difficulty. When people respond with mercy and honest reflection, the trial produces more than survival. It produces a community less proud, better prepared, and more attentive to the Lord.

The lesson is received when the servant turns the next choice into evidence that the previous warning was not wasted.

A trial that produces this kind of attention has become a teacher. The servant does not claim to know every hidden reason for the event, but he knows enough to repent, prepare, and become more compassionate. He has allowed the warning to serve its purpose.
