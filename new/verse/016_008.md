## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:8

> **˹He also created˺ horses, mules, and donkeys for your transportation and adornment. And He creates what you do not know.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Different Kind Of Animal For A Different Kind Of Need**

Verse 5-7 spoke of anʿām, grazing livestock that provide warmth, food, beauty, and burden-bearing. Verse 8 turns to khayl, bighāl, and ḥamīr — horses, mules, and donkeys — created li-tarkabūhā wa-zīnah — so you may ride them and as adornment. The distinction is purposeful. Anʿām were primarily for sustenance and trade; these three are primarily for riding and dignity. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the Arabs differentiated between animals eaten and animals ridden, and the Qur'an respects that distinction while reminding that both categories are created. Horses in particular were symbols of nobility, speed, and martial power. To own a horse was to possess both transport and status. By saying Allah created them for riding and adornment, the verse acknowledges that human beings care about how they appear and how they move, and it affirms that this care is legitimate when attributed to its Creator. Zīnah, adornment, is not condemned here; it is listed as a divine purpose.

**Why Mules And Donkeys Are Named**

Mentioning bighāl and ḥamīr alongside khayl shows the Qur'an's attention to the full range of society. Not everyone owned a horse; horses were expensive and required skill. Donkeys were the transport of the common person, the student, the farmer, the woman carrying water. Mules, offspring of horse and donkey, were valued for strength and endurance in mountainous terrain. By naming all three, the verse includes all classes. The poor man's donkey and the chief's horse are both created by Allah for human use. Al-Qurṭubī adds that this naming also has legal implications. The fact that anʿām were mentioned separately as food, while these three are mentioned for riding and adornment without mention of eating, was used by many jurists to argue that horse, mule, and donkey meat is not intended as food. The Prophet ﷺ explicitly forbade eating domestic donkeys on the day of Khaybar, as reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4216 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1801, and most schools extend the prohibition to mules. Horse meat is treated differently; some schools permit it, others discourage it because horses are more valuable alive for riding and defense.

**Adornment That Is More Than Vanity**

Wa-zīnah — and as adornment. The word zīnah in the Qur'an can mean both natural beauty and added decoration. Here it refers to the dignity and splendor that riding confers. Al-Rāzī explains that riding a horse, especially a well-kept one, gives the rider a sense of elevation, visibility, and honor. In a society where leaders were recognized by their mounts, this was not superficial. A rider could be seen, heard, and followed. The Qur'an does not dismiss this human desire to appear well; it places it within divine creation. You want to ride and look dignified — Allah created the means for that. This is consistent with the sūrah's broader method: it does not tell people to suppress natural desires but to see them as gifts that point to the Giver. Later the sūrah will mention garments for protection and garments for adornment (16:81), repeating the same pair: protection and beauty, necessity and delight. The pairing guards against two extremes: asceticism that rejects beauty and materialism that worships it.

**And He Creates What You Do Not Know**

Wa-yakhluqu mā lā taʿlamūn — and He creates what you do not know. This short clause opens the catalogue beyond the Arabian horizon. Al-Ṭabarī says it means Allah creates other animals and things beyond those named, which the listeners did not know at the time. Al-Qurṭubī expands: it includes animals in distant lands, creatures in the sea, and inventions that would appear later. Many modern commentators note that the phrase comfortably includes forms of transport and adornment that did not exist in the seventh century — ships larger than imagined, vehicles, aircraft — because the principle remains: Allah creates, whether directly as living creatures or by giving humans knowledge to make new carriers. The clause also functions as humility training. After listing familiar animals, the Qur'an reminds that creation is larger than your experience. You do not know all that Allah creates. This prepares for verses about rain, stars, and sea that also exceed immediate knowledge. It also answers the Makkan tendency to limit Allah's power to what they could see. If He creates what you do not know, then your ignorance is not a measure of His ability.

**The Bracketed Addition And Its Reason**

The translation begins with ˹He also created˺ in brackets because the Arabic verb khalaqa appears in verse 5 and is understood to continue, but verse 8's syntax begins with wa-l-khayla — and horses — with the verb implied. The brackets show that the translators supplied the verb for English clarity, not that it is missing in meaning. Classical Arabic often omits a repeated verb for brevity, relying on the previous verb to carry forward. This is called iḍmār or ḥadhf, elision for conciseness. The meaning is unanimous: horses, mules, and donkeys are also created by Allah. Recognizing this elision matters because it ties verses 5-8 into one creation passage, all governed by the same Creator. The sūrah is not listing random blessings; it is building a single argument: all these are from One.

**How This Verse Sets Up The Next**

Verse 8 ends with unknown creation, and verse 9 begins with guidance: *It is upon Allah to show the straight way*. The connection is deliberate. After establishing that Allah creates what you know and what you do not know, the sūrah says He also creates guidance and shows the way. Just as He did not leave you without transport, He did not leave you without direction. The physical carriers that prevent you from perishing in the desert have a parallel in the spiritual guidance that prevents you from perishing in confusion. Al-Rāzī makes this link explicit: horses carry bodies, guidance carries hearts, and both are created and shown by Allah. The mention of unknown creation also widens the reader's imagination before the next verses about rain, crops, and celestial bodies, which include phenomena that seventh-century Arabs knew existed but did not fully understand. By saying He creates what you do not know, the sūrah gives permission to keep discovering blessings without feeling that new discoveries threaten faith; they confirm it.
