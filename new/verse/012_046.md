## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:46

> ˹He said,˺ “Joseph, O man of truth! Interpret for us ˹the dream of˺ seven fat cows eaten up by seven skinny ones; and seven green ears of grain and ˹seven˺ others dry, so that I may return to the people and let them know.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Title He Is Given in a Prison Cell**

*Yūsufu ayyuhā al-ṣiddīq* — Joseph, O man of truth. The word *ṣiddīq* is an intensive form denoting a person for whom truthfulness is a settled characteristic rather than an occasional act. It is the same quality the family witness attributed to him in verse 27 and the same one the ʿAzīz's wife will confirm before the king in verse 51. Three attestations, separated by years and given by people with every reason to say otherwise: a relative of the accuser, a fellow prisoner who watched him in confinement, and the woman who prosecuted him. The commentators note two grounds for the cupbearer's use of the title here — Joseph interpreted his own dream truthfully, and he never told a lie in all the time they shared the cell. Both grounds are experiential. Nobody in Egypt has testified to Joseph's character on the basis of hearsay.

**The Dream Is Restated in Full, Not Summarised**

The messenger reproduces the vision image by image and number by number: seven fat cows eaten by seven lean, seven green ears and seven dry. He does not abbreviate, does not offer his own reading and does not ask a general question. The precision serves two purposes. It guarantees that Joseph is answering the dream the king actually had rather than a version of it, and it allows the reader to verify the interpretation against the original when it arrives in the next verse. The chapter's care with evidence extends to its messengers. A man sent on an errand of state repeats what he was told word for word, which is what makes the answer that follows admissible.

**"Interpret for Us" Speaks on Behalf of a Government**

The request is plural. The messenger is not asking for himself; he carries the question of a king and a council that has already failed to answer it. That plural changes the character of the exchange. In verses 36 to 41 two prisoners asked a fellow prisoner about their own fates, and the answer concerned two men. Here the state asks, and the answer will concern a country's food supply for fifteen years. The sūrah marks the shift by the register of the speech: no sermon, no theological preamble, no reference to creed. Joseph is addressed as an expert by an official envoy, and he responds as one. The envoy's commission is what lifts the exchange above a personal favour. He has been sent, he is expected to come back with something, and what he brings will be reported to a court. Joseph is therefore speaking into a record, and the chapter places his first official act not in the palace but in the cell, addressed to a messenger who has no idea whom he is addressing.

**"So That I May Return to the People and Let Them Know"**

The clause states the messenger's objective, and the commentators read it in two directions. The narrower reading is that the people — the king and his council — should know the interpretation itself. The broader adds that they should know Joseph's standing and knowledge, and therefore release him. Both are supported by the sequel, since the king's immediate response in verse 50 is to summon the man rather than merely to record the answer. What the clause establishes is that the errand has a second purpose from the start. The cupbearer is not only fetching information; he is presenting a person, and the presentation is what will move Joseph from the prison to the palace. The phrasing tells us how the cupbearer intends to account for himself at court. He is not laying claim to the interpretation; he is claiming to know the man who can give it. That distinction is what allows Joseph's words to reach the king unfiltered and in the first person, carrying the authority of their author rather than of a nervous intermediary.

**Joseph Sees Two Opportunities and Takes Both**

The commentators observe that Joseph recognised in this request both a route out of the prison and a chance to serve a country facing an agricultural catastrophe, and that he treated the two as compatible rather than as competing. Nothing in the text suggests he negotiated his release in exchange for the interpretation; he gives the answer and adds nothing about his own case, and the demand about his case comes later and separately (12:50). That separation is what makes verse 50 significant. He does not bargain with the interpretation. He answers the question as asked, and only afterwards insists that the record about him be corrected before he will walk out of the cell.

**The Answer That Follows Is a Programme, Not a Reading**

Verses 47 to 49 will contain almost no symbolism. Joseph does not explain that the cows signify years, although the identification is implicit and the biblical account states it explicitly. He moves directly to what should be sown, what should be stored, what should be eaten, what will be consumed and what will follow. The interpretation and the policy arrive in a single continuous statement, which is why the king's response is to appoint him rather than to thank him. A dream that has been decoded produces information; a dream that has been decoded into a fifteen-year storage plan produces an office, and the chapter understands the difference. It also fixes the sequence, which is easy to lose. The plan is delivered before the appointment, and it is delivered with no condition or bargain attached to it. Joseph names the years, the method and the discipline required, and only afterwards is he given authority to carry out what he has already designed — so the promotion reads as ratification rather than as reward.

**A Different Audience, A Different Content**

The contrast with verses 37 to 41 is exact and it deserves to be stated plainly. When two condemned men asked Joseph for an interpretation, he spent four verses on the oneness of God before answering them. When an envoy of the king asks for the same service, he answers without a word of creed. Nothing in the chapter suggests inconsistency. The two prisoners were idolaters with nothing to lose and years ahead of them; the envoy is a servant of a government with a famine approaching and no time. Joseph preaches where preaching can be received and serves where service is required. The sūrah presents both as prophetic conduct, and it leaves the reader to notice that the same man adjusted his speech to his audience without adjusting his convictions.

The title the envoy uses also tells the reader something about what happened in the prison. *Ṣiddīq* is not a form of address one official applies to a stranger, and it is not a compliment a servant invents on a king's behalf. It is what a man calls someone whose word he has watched tested over years — a fellow inmate whose interpretations came true and whose conduct in confinement matched his claims. The cupbearer has carried that assessment out of the cell and into the palace, and the chapter treats it as evidence: the same mouth that once forgot a message now delivers a character reference, and the reference is the reason a king will send for a prisoner he has never met.
