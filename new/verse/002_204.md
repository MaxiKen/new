## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:204

> There are some ˹hypocrites˺ who impress you with their views regarding worldly affairs and openly call upon Allah to witness what is in their hearts, yet they are your worst adversaries.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn From the Pilgrim to the Impostor**

The sūrah makes one of its characteristic pivots here: the hajj-legislation closes with the pilgrims gathered at Minā, remembering God in numbered days (2:203), and the next movement opens on a man whose speech impresses and whose heart is war. The contrast is the point of the placement — the community that has just been legislated through remembrance, flexibility, and mindfulness is immediately shown its internal opposite, the person who performs the language of faith while carrying enmity inside it. The verses that follow (204–207) form a diptych: this portrait of the eloquent adversary, and its counter-portrait of the one who sells his soul for God's pleasure (2:207). Between the two figures the sūrah lays out the heart's two economies — the speech that markets the self, and the life that spends the self — and asks the reader, freshly returned from the valley of gathering, which trade he is in.

**The Speech About the Life of This World**

The portrait opens with the man's instrument: his manner of speaking about worldly affairs delights you. The verb carries the senses the exegetes list — to please, to evoke wonder, to delight — and its object is specified: not talk of God or the Hereafter but of *this world's life*, its affairs, its interests, its management. The hypocrite's eloquence is worldly fluency: he speaks the community's practical concerns — provision, politics, the arrangements of the road and the market — with a mastery that wins the listener's admiration. The specification matters. The sūrah's opening portrait of the hypocrites described their claim, we are only peace-makers (2:11); this portrait describes their competence. The dangerous deceiver is not the one who talks badly about sacred things but the one who talks brilliantly about worldly things and carries the brilliance into the sacred. The listener's admiration — the verse addresses the Prophet himself, *impresses you* — is the mechanism of the harm: the community is disarmed not by argument but by aesthetic surrender, the pleasure of a well-managed sentence doing the work a false doctrine could never do alone.

**God Called to Witness the Heart**

The portrait's second stroke is the oath: he calls God as witness to what is in his heart. The performance is total — the deceiver does not merely speak well; he sanctifies his speech, invoking God as guarantor of his heart's contents, saying in effect what God knows is in my heart. Al-Ṭabarī records the case attached to the verse: Akhnas ibn Shurayq of Thaqīf displayed a pretended eagerness for Islam, protesting all the while that God was witness to his heart — the oath of interiority wielded by a man whose interior was the opposite of his words. The invocation is the most religious form deception can take: it borrows the language of the heart's transparency that true believers use before God and installs it at the service of a lie. And it is unfalsifiable by design — no human listener can audit another's heart, so the oath to what the heart contains claims exactly the territory where the community cannot check. The verse exposes the mechanism by naming God's actual relation to it: the Witness invoked is witness against the swearer, since what is in the heart is precisely what God knows and the man's audience does not.

**The Fiercest of Adversaries**

The third stroke reverses the whole surface: yet he is your bitterest opponent. The phrase carries a second reading al-Ṭabarī preserves — most crooked in disputation — and the two readings compose into one diagnosis: the man whose eloquence impressed you is your enemy in both senses, the one who works against you in fact and the one who twists against you in argument. The reversal is the verse's structural heart. Everything the listener received — the pleasing speech, the worldly fluency, the sacred oath — is reclassified in the final clause as the equipment of hostility: the eloquence is the weapon, the oath is the camouflage, and the admiration the listener felt was the wound being inflicted. The verse thus trains a discernment the community urgently needed in Medina, where the hypocrites lived inside the city's walls, prayed in its mosque, and fought no open battle: the enemy who cannot be identified by his position must be identified by his pattern, and the pattern the verse gives is the disjunction — the sweeter the speech about this world, the holier the oath about the heart, the sharper the suspicion the clause commands.

**Occasions and the Universal Portrait**

The exegetes record specific occasions for these verses and then, characteristically, prefer the general reading. Beside Akhnas, al-Thaʿlabī reports another incident behind the revelation: Meccan men feigned eagerness for Islamic instruction and requested that a party of teachers be dispatched to them; the men sent were waylaid en route and killed. But Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī agree in treating the passage as universal teaching on the nature of hypocrisy rather than as a locked historical caption — the portrait is drawn to be recognized in every generation. The preference is methodologically significant: a verse tied to one impostor would arm the community against a man; a verse describing the type arms it against the form. And the form is durable — the eloquent manager of worldly affairs, the invoker of God as heart-witness, the adversary inside the admiration — recurring in every community that judges its speakers by their speech. The universal reading also explains the diptych's second panel: the one who sells his soul for God's pleasure (2:207) is no less a type, and the two figures stand as the permanent alternatives of the religious life — the self marketed in God's name, and the self spent in God's cause.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Architecture of the Heart**

This portrait belongs to the sūrah's long anatomy of interior corruption, which began in its opening pages — the hypocrites who say we believe in God and the Last Day while believing not, who seek to deceive God and the believers while deceiving only themselves without awareness (2:8–9) — and now, two hundred verses deep, returns with its mature instruments: not the crude claim of belief but the accomplished performance of it. The progression is pedagogical. The opening portrait warned against the lie; this one warns against the beautiful lie, the one that arrives with worldly competence and sacred oaths, that impresses before it deceives. The hajj-context sharpens the warning: the pilgrim has just spent weeks in the state where the interior is legislated — the intention no one can audit, the rafath and jidāl forbidden even in the tongue's smallest movement, the remembrance required where no crowd can see — and the verses that greet his return describe the antipode of that formation, the man whose exterior is perfected and whose interior is enmity. The sūrah's two hundred verses have been building the community's heart; these verses name the counterfeiter who has been studying the same blueprint from the other side.
