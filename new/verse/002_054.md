## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:54

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people! Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf, so turn in repentance to your Creator and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves. That is best for you in the sight of your Creator.” Then He accepted your repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repentance With a Price Attached**

The verse files the calf's settlement, and the settlement is not a word. Moses addresses his people with the diagnosis first — you have wronged yourselves by taking the calf — and then commands a repentance with two movements: turn to your Maker, and execute among yourselves. The first movement is the interior return the word *tawbah* always names; the second is its public cost, a death-penalty carried out inside the community on the guilty. The pairing states the verse's whole theology of atonement: a sin of this size — shirk committed after the sea, under the mountain's shadow — is not dissolved by sentiment. The turn must be real, and its reality is measured by what the turners are willing to do. The sūrah's earlier filings had already shown the divine side moving fast: pardon filed at verse 52, the Book given at verse 53. Here the human side's movement appears, and it is the hardest thing the recital asks of anyone: a community asked to run the sword through its own calf-takers. The verse does not soften the command and does not explain it away; it reports the command, reports the acceptance, and ends on the two names that make both possible.

**“Execute Yourselves”: Whom the Swords Actually Found**

The literal phrase is *fa-q-tulū anfusakum* — kill yourselves — and the exegetes divide on who died and by whose hand. The dominant reading, and the strongest, is that the innocent executed the guilty: the calf-worshippers were killed by their own kinsmen and neighbors, which made the punishment more terrible than any external execution, since every stroke fell inside the camp and inside the family. The biblical account runs the same shape — Moses at the gate calling for whoever is for the Lord, and the sons of Levi taking swords against their brothers and companions (Exodus 32:27) — and the Qur'an's phrasing, read with 2:85's later charge that this community kills its own and expels its own, fits the same event. A second reading softens the distribution: the killed were martyrs, and the killers had their repentance accepted through the killing, so that all were forgiven and the episode functioned as collective purification rather than the innocent punishing the guilty. A third, esoteric reading takes the killing inward: slay your own egos, the command's real object being the passions that built the calf. The first reading carries the plain sense and the historical parallel; the third is devotion, not exegesis — it preaches the verse rather than reading it.

**Why the Verse Says Maker, Twice**

The address names Allah twice as *Bāriʾ* — your Maker, your Creator — and the repetition is the verse's chosen instrument. *Baraʾa* is creation in its fashioning sense: the making of a thing whole, sound, fitted — the same name that stands in the divine triad of Sūrah al-Ḥashr, the Creator, the Maker, the Fashioner (59:24). Against a community that had just manufactured a god with its own hands, the name does precise work. The calf was made; the Maker of the camp was not made. The verse insists on the distinction by putting the unmade Fashioner's name in both hinges of the sentence — turn in repentance *to your Maker*; that is best *in the sight of your Maker* — so the whole settlement runs between the people and the One who fashioned them, with the fashioned thing excluded from the grammar entirely. There is also a claim of jurisdiction in the name: the One who fashioned the body has standing over the body, including its death. A command to execute is bearable only from the Fashioner; from anyone else it would be murder. The name chosen twice is the name that makes the verse's severity lawful and its acceptance credible.

**The Better Thing in the Maker's Sight**

Between the command and the acceptance stands a valuation: *that is best for you in the sight of your Maker*. The demonstrative covers the whole package — repentance plus execution — and calls it *khayr*, better, the same comparative the Qur'an uses for the eternal against the temporal, for prayer against trade. The verse thereby inverts the obvious arithmetic. To the living, the killing of kin is the worst available outcome; in the Maker's sight, for this people at this moment, it was the best — best because it was the only route back, and because the alternative was not acquittal but the settled citizenship of verse 39. The valuation also answers a standing objection to costly repentance: that it is God demanding suffering for its own sake. The verse says the opposite — the suffering is instrumentally *best*, valued not in itself but as the road, and the road ends in acceptance within the same breath. Divine severity here is bounded by divine purpose on both sides: the command is severe because the sin was total, and the acceptance is immediate because the purpose was restoration, not payment. The sūrah's economy of verse 48 holds — no ransom is taken — but repentance is not ransom; it is the return the covenant always priced in.

**Tawwāb: The Name That Keeps Accepting**

The acceptance clause carries the sūrah's signature pair: *He accepted your repentance; surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful* — Tawwāb, Raḥīm. *Tawwāb* is the intensive form: not one who accepts once but one who accepts repeatedly, whose accepting is a settled attribute rather than an occasional act — and the word's double direction matters, since tawbah in Arabic runs both ways: the servant turns, and Allah turns back toward the servant. The pair appeared earlier in the sūrah at the first great fall: Adam received words from his Lord, and the same sentence closed his case — *"Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful"* (2:37). The identical names thus bracket the two founding repentances of the recital — the species' first and Israel's greatest — which is the sūrah telling its readers that the calf sits inside the human story rather than outside it. What Adam did at the tree and what Israel did at the calf are the same motion, and the same two names receive both. No sin in the recital is filed as unprecedented, and none as unforgivable while the pair is on the record.

**A Case File Still Addressed to You**

The verse is framed inside the second-person address that runs from verse 40: Moses speaks to *his people*, but the sūrah files the speech as *your* history — the Madinan descendants inherit the scene the way they inherited the sea and the manna. The inheritance is not guilt transferred across generations, which the Qur'an's own principle of burdens would forbid, but membership: a community that claims the covenant claims the case file, and the case file's first entry on repentance is this one. For that community in Madina, the verse also carried a pointed relevance — scholars who knew a prophet's description and had not yet confessed it were, in the sūrah's argument, standing at the same fork: a diagnosed wrong, a commanded return, and a valuation that called the costly road *best*. The verse's closing names are the ones every reader carries out of it. The Accepter of Repentance is not a name reserved for the wilderness generation; it is the standing title of the One who took this confession — the most expensive confession in the recital — and answered it with acceptance before the verse ended.
