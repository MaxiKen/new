## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:54

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people! Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf, so turn in repentance to your Creator and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves. That is best for you in the sight of your Creator.” Then He accepted your repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Diagnosis Before a Demand**

Moses opens with an accusation and closes with a treatment, and the order is deliberate. Before he tells the people what to do, he tells them what has happened to them: the worship of the calf was a wrong done to the wrongdoers themselves. The Arabic phrasing places the damage on the people who committed it rather than on God, and the sūrah has been saying the same thing about Israel's failures in every verse of this passage.

That diagnosis sets up everything that follows. A community that believes it has merely offended a rule can negotiate; a community that understands it has injured itself has a reason to accept a costly remedy. Moses names the wound first, then prescribes the cure, and the verse ends with the announcement that the cure was accepted.

**Repentance Directed to "Your Creator"**

The command to turn is addressed to a word that appears only here in this narrative: your Maker, the one who brought you into being. The Book's choice of that name in a passage about a manufactured idol is pointed. The people had turned to something they had made with their own hands; they are told to turn back to the one who made them, and the contrast is contained in a single noun.

Repentance in this verse is therefore not regret alone. The Arabic term describes a return, and a return requires a place to return to. The sūrah's earlier account of Adam used the same vocabulary and the same sequence — a failure, a turning, and an acceptance — which ties the first human being and the people of Moses to one pattern of recovery. The Book applies one remedy to both.

**The Command to Execute Their Own**

The instruction that follows is the hardest line in the passage. The people are told to kill one another, and the commentators explained the phrase in the way that fits the Book's other uses of the same construction: the guilty are to be executed by their own community, at the hands of their own relatives, without a foreign power being brought in to do the work. The reports add that those killed in this way are counted among the martyrs, and that the men who carried out the sentence had their repentance accepted as well.

Such a reading makes the punishment collective in effect as well as in instruction. Every household was implicated in the act of restoring the community, and the Book presents the severity as the measure of the offence rather than as an arbitrary cruelty. The alternative reading, which sees the command as an inner killing of the ego, is a later spiritual interpretation, and it does not remove the plain sense of the sentence.

**Where the Verse Says the Good Lies**

The sentence gives a reason: that is better for you in the sight of your Creator. The Book does not present the command as an arbitrary test or a demonstration of authority. It states a comparison and asks the people to accept the divine assessment of which option is better, even though the option named involves the loss of life.

That is the structure of the Book's moral instruction everywhere. God's commands in the Qur'an are consistently presented as the better of the available courses, and the human being's difficulty lies in his own assessment rather than in the command's reasonableness. Here the community is asked to accept that a painful, publicly administered punishment is better than the condition they had entered, and the verse reports their acceptance as a fact.

**An Acceptance Stated With Two Names**

The forgiveness is announced in the past tense by the verse itself: He accepted your repentance. The Book does not leave the community suspended between the command and its outcome; it reports that the arrangement worked. The names attached to the announcement — the Accepter of Repentance and the Most Merciful — are the same two names that closed the account of Adam's pardon, and the repetition connects the two scenes.

The Book uses the same pair wherever it describes God turning back to a person or a people, and the vocabulary is part of its standing promise rather than a comment on the severity of the case. The verse therefore ends where the sūrah's other accounts of failure end: at the mercy that follows a return, stated as an attribute of the one who grants it rather than as an exception granted to Israel.

**The Cost of a Pardon in This Life**

The narrative gives both halves of the arrangement without softening either. Something was lost — the guilty were executed — and the relationship was restored. The Book never suggests in this passage that the pardon erased the consequences of the act within history, and it never suggests that those consequences were the whole of the response. What the community received was forgiveness and a continuing place in the story being told about them.

That combination is what the sūrah's addresses to this people keep assuming. They are being spoken to as a community that has been repeatedly restored, and the restoration is not described as something the community earned. The verse names the remedy, reports the obedience, and states the pardon in a single sentence, and the reader reaches the end of it having seen the whole pattern that will be applied to Israel for the rest of the passage.
