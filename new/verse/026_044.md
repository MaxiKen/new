## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44

> So they cast down their ropes and staffs, saying, “By Pharaoh’s might, it is we who will surely prevail.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Magicians Fill the Stage With Imitation**

The magicians cast their ropes and staffs. The plural objects create a wide display, not a single private trick. Their craft is arranged for the eyes of the crowd, and their words accompany the performance with a declaration of victory. The scene combines material technique, public confidence, and political allegiance. They do not simply cast; they cast while announcing what the audience is supposed to conclude.

The ropes and staffs are ordinary objects placed under skilled manipulation. Their movement will affect perception, making the crowd feel that many things are alive or changing. The performance is powerful as spectacle because it multiplies the appearance. Yet the number of objects cannot create divine authority. A crowd may be overwhelmed by quantity while still failing to distinguish appearance from reality.

**They Swear by Pharaoh Instead of Seeking Truth**

The magicians invoke Pharaoh's might as the basis for their confidence. Their oath shows where their allegiance has been placed. They do not say that truth will prevail or that God will guide the outcome. They appeal to the ruler's power and treat his might as the guarantee of victory.

That statement reveals a spiritual problem beneath the technical performance. Human skill has become attached to a human sovereign. The magicians' craft is not merely employed by Pharaoh; it is symbolically consecrated to him. By swearing by his might, they turn political power into a source of certainty. Their confidence rests on the patron who pays and protects them, not on a careful examination of the sign before them.

**Certainty Can Be Performed Before It Is Possessed**

The phrase we will surely prevail sounds absolute. Public confidence can influence an audience before the result is known. A person who speaks without hesitation may appear to possess knowledge, and a group that repeats the same prediction can make doubt seem unreasonable. The magicians are creating an emotional result alongside the visual one.

Yet prediction is not proof. Their certainty may arise from confidence in their technique, loyalty to Pharaoh, or the pressure of performing before a crowd. None of these guarantees that their interpretation is true. The verse lets the audience hear their confidence before seeing the outcome so that it can compare the loudness of human assurance with the reality that follows.

**The Objects Become Instruments of Political Religion**

Ropes and staffs are used to make a ruler's claim appear victorious. Their performance is therefore not religiously neutral. The court has arranged the contest to defend Pharaoh's authority, and the magicians openly identify that authority as the source of their confidence. Objects that could have remained tools of craft become instruments in a campaign against Moses' message.

This pattern appears whenever skilled work is pressed into the service of a sacred political image. The tool itself may be harmless, but the use made of it can support falsehood. The magician who knows how to influence sight now helps the state influence judgment. The crowd must decide whether the spectacle deserves allegiance or whether it is being used to conceal the moral question about Israel.

**Multiplicity Creates the Appearance of Independent Proof**

Many ropes and staffs may look like many pieces of evidence. They are not. They are many applications of one human technique. Repetition can make an illusion feel confirmed because the eyes receive the same suggestion from several directions. The audience may think that abundance means truth, when it may only mean coordinated performance.

A similar error occurs in public debate when repeated claims are treated as independent support even though they all come from one source. The court has gathered many magicians, but the number of performers does not create many divine authorities. Their agreement may show common training or common dependence on Pharaoh. It does not settle what Moses' sign means.

**The Magicians' Confidence Makes the Later Turn More Significant**

At this moment the magicians appear certain that victory is theirs. They have the crowd, the objects, the preparation, and the ruler's favor. Their later response will therefore not be a small adjustment to a weak performance. It will be a reversal by people who entered convinced that their craft could prevail.

That reversal begins with honest sight. The verse preserves their initial allegiance so that the reader can see the depth of the change when it comes. They are not secretly sympathetic to Moses from the start in this account. They cast for Pharaoh and expect to win. The truth will have to break through professional confidence and political reward.

**A Performance Can Command Eyes Without Commanding Reality**

The magicians can organize what the crowd seems to see, but they cannot determine what the material objects truly are or what God's sign will do. Their power lies in perception and presentation. Moses' sign will expose the boundary. The crowd may be made to feel that ropes and staffs are alive, but a divine act is not merely a feeling produced in observers.

The verse thus prepares a contrast between appearance and reality without denying that appearance can be persuasive. People are responsible for more than what first catches the eye. They must ask whether the spectacle survives examination and whether it serves truth. The magicians' declaration of victory is loud, but the coming sign will answer it without needing to shout.

Their confidence also makes the audience responsible for more than excitement. The crowd has heard an oath to Pharaoh and seen a coordinated display. It must still ask whether loyalty and repetition have been mistaken for evidence. A public claim can dominate the ear for a moment; only truth can survive the event that follows.
