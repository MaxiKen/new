## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:39

> Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged. And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them ˹prevail˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Changed the Community's Posture**

*Udhina li-alladhīna yuqātalūna bi-annahum ẓulimū* — permission is granted to those who are being fought, because they have been wronged.

This is the verse most often identified as the first to authorize the believers to use force. Before it, the community's instructed response to persecution had been patience and withdrawal, and twice that withdrawal had taken the form of emigration: a small group to Abyssinia in 615, under the protection of the Christian Negus, and then the emigration of the Prophet and his companions from Makkah to Madīnah in 622.

The accounts of the occasion differ in detail. Some place its revelation at the point of the Hijrah itself, and al-Qurṭubī records that when Abū Bakr heard it he remarked that he had known all along that combat would eventually be permitted. Al-Ṭabarī connects it instead with a party of believers who set out to leave Makkah, were intercepted and turned back by their persecutors, and were then granted leave to resist.

The occasion matters for how the sūrah is read as a whole. This is a chapter whose earlier material — the Hour, the resurrection argument, the disputer without knowledge — belongs to the Makkan period, and it now contains a ruling that belongs to Madīnah. The mixture is not accidental: the sūrah's argument is that a community barred from its rites may recover them, and the recovery is what this verse licenses. The legislation is the argument arriving at its conclusion.

**The Grammar of Permission**

*Udhina* is a passive verb with no agent named. Nobody is doing the permitting in the sentence, which is the standard Qur'anic way of attributing an authorisation to God without making Him the subject of a finite verb.

The word matters more than it looks. What is granted is permission — *idhn* — not obligation and not enthusiasm. A command would have made fighting a duty of the same order as the prayer; a mere concession would have implied it was tolerated but regrettable. Permission sits precisely between them: it is lawful, it is not mandatory on every individual, and it is not celebrated.

That is the register the whole passage keeps. The sūrah has just forbidden treachery (22:38) and will immediately state the grievance that justifies the fighting (22:40) and the conditions attached to the power it may produce (22:41). Nothing here is a licence.

The passive is also how the Qur'an normally attributes acts of authorisation to God. A named subject would have made the sentence a piece of legislation among others; the agentless *udhina* presents it as something lifted — a restraint that had been in place and is now removed. The community is not being told to do something new so much as being told that what it had been forbidden to do is no longer forbidden.

**"Those Who Are Being Fought"**

The participle is passive: *yuqātalūna*, those who are fought against.

The grammar establishes the sequence, and the sequence is the justification. These people are not aggressors who have been granted a blessing on their aggression; they are the objects of force who have been allowed to stop being only objects. The passive participle carries the whole moral asymmetry of the verse in a single word.

This is why the verse is so often cited in discussions of the Islamic law of war. The permission is not attached to disbelief, to proximity, or to any quality of the opponent. It is attached to the fact of being attacked.

The restriction to the party that was attacked is stated as law elsewhere, and the wording leaves no room for extension: *“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Surely Allah does not like transgressors”* (2:190). The permission of this verse and the limit of that one are the same ruling seen from two sides: the object of force may respond, and the response has a ceiling.

**"Because They Have Been Wronged"**

*Bi-annahum ẓulimū* — because they were wronged. The causal particle *bi-anna* ties the permission directly to a specific injury, and the verb is again passive: wrong was done *to* them.

The clause is the verse's whole case, and it is stated in the most general available term. *Ẓulm* is the word for placing something where it does not belong, for a wrong that everyone recognises as one. It is not defined further here, because the next verse supplies the particulars: expulsion from homes, for no reason except a sentence about God.

The structure matters here: permission, then the reason, and the reason is not a threat assessment or a prophecy. It is a past fact of having been wronged, which can in principle be verified by anyone.

**The Alternative Readings**

The consonantal text admits more than one vocalisation, and the commentators record them. One reading makes the believers the subject of the verb of fighting: leave is given to those who are fighting, since wrong was done to them. Another reading supplies the subject of the granting: it is God who grants leave to those who fight, because they were wronged. Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī both note the variants.

The readings converge on the same claim. Whoever takes up arms in these circumstances does so as a party that has been wronged, and the permission is a response to that rather than an initiative. The difference between the vocalisations concerns only whether the Arabic presents the fighting as already under way or as being licensed to begin.

The legal consequence of the wording is where the variants stop mattering. Because the verse grants *permission* rather than issuing a command, the jurists who built the law of war on it did not treat fighting as an obligation falling on every individual in every circumstance. The most that follows from *idhn* is that it is lawful; whether it is required, and of whom, is settled by other texts.

**Why the Permission Falls Inside a Pilgrimage Sūrah**

The placement is worth a moment, because it looks like a digression and is not. This is the sūrah of the pilgrimage — the sūrah that was named for the rite its audience had been prevented from performing, and that declared the Sacred Mosque open to resident and visitor alike (22:25).

The people being granted permission to fight are the people who were barred from the House. The passage that follows will state the grievance in a sentence: they were expelled for saying that their Lord is God. What is being authorized is the recovery of a community's ability to worship where it was told to worship, and the authorisation is bounded by that.

That is why the sūrah can move from verses about sacrificial camels to verses about armed defence without any sense of incongruity. Both are about a community that has been prevented from practising its religion, and both are permissions granted to people who had been told to wait.

The sūrah has already said what the Mosque is for: it was made open to the resident and the visitor alike, and whoever intends to profane it in deviation will be made to taste a painful punishment (22:25). Those are the terms in which the fighting is authorised. The people who were kept out of the House by force are being permitted to remove the force, and nothing broader than that is licensed by the verse.

**"God Is Able to Give Them Victory"**

*Wa-inna Allāha ʿalā naṣrihim la-qadīr* — and God is, for their help, truly capable.

The closing clause reattaches the outcome to God, exactly as verse 38 did when it said that God defends the believers. The permission does not make victory a consequence of the fighting; the victory is God's to give, and the fighting is what the community has been allowed to do while waiting for it.

The emphatic *la-qadīr* is the note the sūrah ends on. A community that has been expelled, persecuted and prevented from emigrating is being told two things at once: it may now resist, and its resistance is not what will save it.

*Naṣr* is help that arrives from outside the struggle, and it is the same word the sūrah will use in verse 60 for the person wronged twice. What is being promised is not that the fighting will go well but that the outcome is not produced by the fighting. A community told both that it may resist and that its help comes from elsewhere is being given permission without being given the illusion of self-sufficiency.
