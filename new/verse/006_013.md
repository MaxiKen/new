## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:13

> To Him belongs whatever exists in the day and night. And He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership Measured in Hours Rather Than Miles**

The sūrah's first verse named the heavens and the earth, the widest claim in space. Here the same ownership is restated in time: whatever exists in the day and whatever exists in the night. The two clauses cover creatures that move at noon and creatures that appear only after dark, which means the claim has no shift in it, no interval in which the property is unsupervised. Elsewhere the same thought is joined to the alternation itself — to Him belongs the alternation of the day and night (23:80).

An owner is described by what he holds at every hour rather than by what he holds on the best day of the year. Stated this way, the sentence leaves nothing outside its scope: no hour is a holiday for the divine claim, and no creature active in the dark is a creature without a lord, however much the dark suggests otherwise to the people who use it.

**Why Hearing and Knowing Complete the Claim**

The two names that close the verse divide the invisible world between them. Hearing collects what is uttered; knowing collects what is intended, and also what was never uttered at all. A lord who heard but did not know could be managed by careful phrasing, and a lord who knew but did not hear would have no use for the words his creatures choose. The pair closes both doors at once.

The Book states the combination in a sentence that matches this verse exactly: whether you speak openly or not, He certainly knows what is secret and what is even more hidden (20:7). The order in that line — the open, the secret, then the deeper layer still — is the same order the verse implies. There is always one more level beneath the one the speaker has just secured.

**The Practice the Verse Makes Possible**

These two attributes are what make worship in the dark a reasonable act rather than a performance. The Book directs prayer into the hours when no audience is available: the dawn recitation is witnessed by angels (17:78); worship in the night is more impactful and better suited to recitation (73:6); the Prophet is told to rise in the last part of the night as an extra devotion (17:79). The sincere are described as people who abandon their beds, calling on their Lord with hope and fear (32:16).

A man who prays at three in the morning is not building a reputation. He is acting on the specific claim that the owner of the night is listening, and the practice exists in the Book because the claim in this verse is true. Without a hearer who never sleeps, night prayer would be speaking into a room.

**The Dark Hour Is Not an Unrecorded Hour**

If three people converse privately, God is their fourth; if five, He is their sixth; whether fewer or more, He is with them wherever they may be, and on the Day of Judgment He will inform them of what they did (58:7). That passage is the practical consequence of the two names in this verse: hearing supplies the words, knowledge supplies the meaning behind them.

The Makkans had their own use for the night. Councils about how to answer the Prophet, jokes about the resurrection, agreements about a boycott — all of it was transacted after dark on the assumption that darkness was a screen. The verse denies the screen without raising its voice. It simply states who owns the hours in which the arrangements were made, and adds the two attributes that make ownership of an hour into supervision of a conversation.

**A Lord Near Enough to Answer When Called**

Ownership of the night would be cold comfort if it were only ownership. The Book supplies the other half: when My servants ask you about Me, I am truly near, and I respond when they call upon Me (2:186). The same nearness is attached to the hours this verse owns. The Prophet is told to stay patiently with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure, and not to let his eyes wander past them toward the luxuries of the world (18:28).

Morning and evening, day and night — the vocabulary keeps circling the same two halves of the clock. What the verse provides is the reason those hours can hold a conversation with God at all. A claim over time that ended at ownership would leave prayer as a petition posted to an absent office. The two names at the end turn the claim into a promise that someone is on the other side of it.

**The Pair That Keeps Returning**

All-Hearing, All-Knowing is one of the Book's recurring pairs, and its reappearance is not decoration. Attributes in the Qur'an tend to be placed where a specific doubt has just been raised: knowledge is asserted where concealment is the problem, mercy where delay is the problem, and here hearing and knowing where the subject is what people do with hours that belong to someone else.

Read as a pair, the two names also close a loophole the earlier verses left open. A man can control his words and still be certain that his meaning is hidden; another can be careful about his intentions and careless about his speech. The verse's claim covers both, and it does so in six words attached to the ownership of every hour that has ever passed. Nothing spoken at night, and nothing meant in silence, falls outside the sentence.

**Two Words That Carry the Sūrah's Whole Case**

The sūrah assembles its portrait of God clause by clause, and each attribute arrives where an objection needs answering. Creation opens the case (6:1); the terms of life and death follow (6:2); knowledge of the hidden is stated when concealment is the problem (6:3); mercy is named when the question is why judgment has not yet fallen (6:12); and here hearing and knowing return to close the loop around words and intentions alike.

That placement is what the verse offers its hearer. The claim is not that God is powerful in the abstract, but that the one who owns day and night is an attentive owner rather than an absent landlord. For a believer mocked in the market and pressured at home, the recording never stops, and neither does the audience.
