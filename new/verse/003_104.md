## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:104

> Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil—it is they who will be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Tasks for a Group Within the Community**

The verse asks that there be a group among the believers that calls others to what is good, encourages what is right, and forbids what is wrong, and it ends by naming such people as the successful. The sentence describes a function rather than an office.

Calling to good is the general invitation; encouraging the right and forbidding the wrong are the two particular practices that give the invitation a content in daily life. The Book repeats this pair in several passages, sometimes as a description of the community as a whole and sometimes as an obligation, and the sūrah itself uses it again a few lines later when it describes the believers among the People of the Book.

**The Report That Sets Out How It Is Done**

A well-known report related from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī orders that a person who sees something abominable should correct it by his hand, and where he lacks the strength for that, by his tongue, and where he lacks the strength even for that, then in his heart — which the report calls the least of faith. The narration is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (49a) and is among the most quoted texts on this subject.

The report's detail matters for the verse's interpretation, because it orders the means according to capability and leaves the heart as the last resort for someone who can do nothing more. The sequence also shows that the practice is not aimed at producing conflict; where the hand is able, it is used, and where it would cause greater harm, the tongue or the heart remains. What the report forbids is indifference, and what it requires is the strongest response a person can responsibly make.

**What the Two Key Words Carry**

The word rendered as right means what is known and recognised, which is why it covers both what is good and what a community has agreed to treat as decent. Its opposite, rendered as wrong, means what is disowned or denied, and it carries the same double sense of the morally evil and the socially repudiated.

The choice of these two words rather than a pair drawn purely from legal vocabulary tells the reader where the practice begins. It begins with what a person already recognises and with the sense of disgust that wrongdoing produces in a healthy conscience, and only then rises to the level of formal instruction. A person who has lost the ability to be repulsed by what is wrong has nothing left to work with when he is asked to correct others.

**Who the Group Is**

The verse's phrase about a group has been read in two ways. On one reading it describes a distinct segment of the community that takes the task upon itself; on another, it is a statement of what the community should be, so that the whole body is addressed rather than a portion of it.

The case for a segment rests on the observation that not everyone can carry out the practice, since it requires knowledge of what is right and the standing to speak about it, and since the community is not composed only of those who can influence the conduct of others. The case for the wider reading rests on the fact that the community is described elsewhere in the sūrah as encouraging what is good and forbidding what is wrong (3:110). The two positions meet when the duty is understood as binding on the community as a whole and satisfied when a qualified part of it acts.

**The Practice and the People Who Field It**

Because the practice requires the ability to distinguish what is right from what is wrong, the commentators connected it with knowledge. A person who does not know what the law requires may end up forbidding what God has permitted, and the scholars therefore held that the obligation rests most heavily on those equipped to judge.

The Book's passage about the distribution of religious learning belongs to this discussion, since it describes a party from each group going out to gain understanding and then returning to warn their people (9:122). The two verses describe one system: expertise is developed in some and used for the benefit of all, and the community is expected to arrange itself so that the function does not lapse.

**Success Named in the Book's Vocabulary**

The verse closes by saying that such people are the successful, using the same word that opens the call to prayer, where success is placed beside the summons to prayer itself. In the Book's vocabulary success is not a measure of wealth or standing; it is the outcome of the final accounting.

That placement is deliberate in a passage about public duty. A person who corrects wrongdoing exposes himself to resentment, and the Book names the reward at the end of the instruction so that the effort is not measured by its reception. The success of the group is attached to the practice rather than to its results, since the practice is what lies within human control.

**How the Verse Has Been Used**

The command to encourage the right and forbid the wrong has shaped debate in the community about the limits of correction, the right to speak to those in power, and the balance between personal and shared responsibility.

Those discussions are consequences of the verse rather than its content, and the text supplies the criterion for them: the practice is directed at what is recognised as evil, carried out by those able to do it, and aimed at what is good. The next verses continue with the community's obligations and with the memory of what it had been saved from, so that the duty described here sits inside a passage about a community's common life rather than standing alone as an instruction to individuals.
