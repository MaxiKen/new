## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:193

> Fight against them ˹if they persecute you˺ until there is no more persecution, and ˹your˺ devotion will be to Allah ˹alone˺. If they stop ˹persecuting you˺, let there be no hostility except against the aggressors.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The War's Object Stated**

The verse completes the war-legislation by naming the war's end — in both senses: its purpose and its stopping-point. Fight them *until there is no fitnah and religion is for God*. The until-clause fixes the object the previous verses had implied: the persecution that was ranked greater than killing (2:191) is what the fighting exists to remove, and the state aimed at is a public order in which religion belongs to God — in which worship is not coerced away from Him, in which no power stands between human beings and their allegiance. The two halves of the object are inseparable: the absence of fitnah is the negative condition — no one tortured, expelled, or pressured for their faith — and religion-for-God is the positive condition, the freedom that remains when the pressure is gone. The exegetes read the positive clause with care: religion for God does not mean universal belief enforced but the sovereignty of God over the religious field — the end of the idols' political enforcement, the end of persecution as statecraft. What the verse legislates is therefore a war with a defined terminus that is not the enemy's annihilation or conversion but the persecution's extinction: the fighting continues while fitnah continues, and the object achieved, the permission is spent.

**Until There Is No Fitnah**

The fitnah named here is the same institution the previous verse ranked above killing, and the tradition's glosses keep it concrete: the persecution of the Meccan years — the torture of the early Muslims, the boycott of their clan, the expulsion from homes and sanctuary, the pressure that apostatized some and martyred others. The war against such fitnah is a war against a system of religious coercion, and the verse's until is exacting: not until the battle is won, not until the enemy is weakened, but until the coercion no longer exists. This is the Qur'an's answer to the question every war-legislation must face — when may the fighting stop? — and the answer ties the stopping to the injury rather than to the advantage: the permission lives exactly as long as the persecution it answers. The verse thus forbids the two corruptions of just-war practice at once. It forbids the war that continues past its object — fighting after fitnah has ended is transgression, the excess 2:190 named unloved by God. And it forbids the peace that leaves the object unachieved — a truce that preserves the persecution, that leaves believers still tortured and barred from worship, is a peace bought at the price of the very evil the war was permitted against. Between those two prohibitions the until-clause stands as the war's compass.

**Religion for God Alone**

The positive object — religion is for God — carries the legislation's theological core. The phrase excludes every rival sovereignty over conscience: the idols enforced by Quraysh's politics, the rulers whose state-religion the persecution served, the tribal order in which a man's worship followed his clan's feuds. Where religion is for God, allegiance is rendered to God and the political power withdraws from the soul — which is why the persecution's end and the phrase's fulfillment are one condition viewed twice. The verse's object is not the establishment of belief by force; belief compelled is not belief, and the Qur'an's own declaration that there is no compulsion in religion (2:256, in the legislation ahead) belongs to the same system: the war removes the compulsion that runs against God, and what remains is the field in which religion is rendered freely to its only proper Object. The tradition read the two verses together accordingly: the fighting until no fitnah is the removal of coercive idolatry's power, and the religion-for-God that follows is the free worship that power's removal makes possible — the sanctuary reopened, the pilgrim unbarred, the believer untortured, and every soul left, at last, alone with its allegiance.

**If They Cease, No Aggression**

The verse's second half is its brake, and the brake is absolute: if they cease, then no aggression — no iʿtidāʾ, no hostility — except against the wrongdoers. The cessation that ended God's enmity in the previous verse (the Forgiving, the Merciful) ends the community's here: the fighting stops at the persecution's stop, and continuing after the stop is itself the transgression the war-law opened by forbidding. The exception clause — except against the wrongdoers — was read by the exegetes in complementary ways: the aggression that remains lawful is against those who continue to wrong, so that the ceasing enemy is fully covered by the prohibition while the persisting wrongdoer remains within the permission; and some read the excepted wrongdoers as the persisting persecutors specifically, the fitnah's operators, against whom the enmity endures exactly as long as their wrong. Either reading yields the same law: the enmity is not against a people but against a wrong, and it survives neither the wrong's end nor its abandoner. The verse thereby closes the circle the passage opened — fight those who fight you (2:190), kill them where you find them within the limits (2:191), forgive when they cease (2:192), and stop when the persecution stops (2:193) — each command bounded by the next, the whole legislation a corridor with walls on both sides: the wall against passivity in the face of fitnah, and the wall against war beyond fitnah's end.

**The Oppressors and the Enmity's True Object**

The final phrase redefines the enemy one last time: the enmity is against the oppressors — the ẓālimūn, the wrongdoers, the ones who commit the ẓulm that fitnah is. The naming is deliberate and de-tribalizes the war completely. The enemy of these verses is never the Quraysh as a people, never the Arabs of Mecca as a population, never an ethnicity or a lineage; it is the oppressor as a moral category — whoever operates the persecution, and only while he operates it. The same category governs the enmity's end: the oppressor who ceases to oppress has left the category, and the enmity has no object left to hold. This is the verse's contribution to the whole passage's architecture: the war-law of 2:190–193 is indexed, start to finish, not to identity but to act — fight those who fight you, expel those who expelled you, fight fitnah until fitnah ends, and hold enmity only against the ones still wronging. The believers are legislated into a war they cannot racialize, cannot inherit, and cannot continue past its moral cause; and the verse's closing word leaves the community's posture toward the ceased oppressor governed by the previous verse's names — Forgiving, Merciful — with the enmity folded away and the wrongdoer, if he remains a wrongdoer nowhere but in his past, met by the pardon his cessation opened.

**The Passage as One Law**

Read as a unit, the four verses form the Qur'an's first complete war-code, and its shape is worth stating whole. The permission: fight in God's cause those who fight you, without transgression (2:190). The field-rules: the fighters wherever found, the expulsion bounded by the expulsion suffered, the fitnah named as the evil outranking killing, the Sacred Mosque held out of the war unless war enters it (2:191). The exit: the ceasing enemy met by God's forgiveness and mercy (2:192). The object and the brake: fight until persecution ends and religion is God's; if they cease, no aggression except against the persisting wrongdoers (2:193). Every clause is a limit on the clause before it — the permission limited by reciprocity, the field-rules limited by the sanctuary and the non-combatants the tradition drew from them, the object limited by the cessation, the cessation guaranteed by the divine names. A people thirteen years persecuted, freshly barred from their own sanctuary by treaty, are given this code — and its hardest sentence is the ranking of persecution above killing, while its last word about the enemy is pardon. The war these verses legislate is a surgeon's war: the fitnah cut out, the healthy tissue — the ceasing, the non-fighting, the asylum-seeking, the sanctuary — legislated inviolable, and the operation over the moment the disease is.
