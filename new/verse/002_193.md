## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:193

> Fight against them ˹if they persecute you˺ until there is no more persecution, and ˹your˺ devotion will be to Allah ˹alone˺. If they stop ˹persecuting you˺, let there be no hostility except against the aggressors.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Stated**

The verse states the purpose of the fighting, which is the removal of persecution and the establishment of devotion to God, and it closes with the statement that hostility is directed only at the aggressors. The sentence completes the short section on conflict that began with the permission to fight.

The Book's method of attaching a purpose to its commands appears throughout the passage, and the community's scholars read the sentence as the conclusion toward which the commands have been directed. What the reader receives is a statement about the condition the fighting is meant to produce rather than a description of the fighting itself.

**Persecution Removed**

The term the sentence uses for the condition to be removed is the one the sūrah has used for the pressure that drives people from their religion. The community's scholars explained the clause as a statement about the removal of the conditions in which a person is prevented from practising his faith.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere connects the term with the trials that the believers faced in Makkah and with the general condition of a society in which religious practice is coerced. What the sentence establishes is that the objective named is the end of that condition, and the reader is shown that the community's struggle is described in terms of what it removes rather than what it imposes.

**Devotion to God**

The sentence states that devotion will be to God alone, and the community's scholars explained the clause as a statement about the freedom of worship. The Book states elsewhere that there is to be no compulsion in religion, and the community's teachers read the two together.

The wording concerns the direction of worship rather than the identity of the community's rulers, and the phrase describes the condition in which people are free to devote themselves to God. What the sentence establishes is that the purpose of the passage's commands is the removal of an obstacle to worship, and the reader is shown that the Book's account of conflict is framed by the freedom that the community is to leave in place.

**The Limit of Hostility**

The sentence closes with the statement that hostility is directed only at those who are unjust, and the clause returns the reader to the principle of the passage's opening. The community's scholars read the ending as a prohibition of any continuation of the conflict after its cause has been removed.

The Book's language restricts the enmity to the aggressors, and the community's teachers drew from the clause the rule that a community's response is measured by the conduct that occasioned it. What the reader receives is a statement that the fighting is not directed at a people as such, and the sentence leaves him with a condition rather than a standing animosity.

**The Reports and the Interpretation**

The commentators read the passage with the community's history, and they described the circumstances of the confrontation with the Quraysh and the events that followed the migration. The Book returns to the subject in its later chapters, where it states the permission to fight for those who have been wronged, the instruction to incline toward peace when the opposing party does, and the prohibition of aggression. The community's scholars read the chapters together and set out the limits the Book places on fighting.
