## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:230

> So if a husband divorces his wife ˹three times˺, then it is not lawful for him to remarry her until after she has married another man and then is divorced. Then it is permissible for them to reunite, as long as they feel they are able to maintain the limits of Allah. These are the limits set by Allah, which He makes clear for people of knowledge.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Declaration's Consequence**

The verse legislates the count's terminus: if he divorces her — the third declaration the previous verse's twice-counted structure leads to — she is unlawful to him afterward until a second marriage has intervened and itself ended in divorce; and then the former pair may reunite without blame. The rule's shape is absolute in its bar and open in its sequel: the third ṭalāq ends the marriage beyond the husband's own power to restore, and the only path back runs through a complete other marriage — contracted, lived, and ended in its own divorce. The bar is the count's enforcement: without it, the twice-limit of 2:229 would be a formality, since the third declaration could be issued and the wife remarried at the husband's convenience, the cycle of divorce-and-return resuming where the code intended it to stop. The intervening-marriage requirement makes the third word irrevocable in the only way human arrangements can be made irrevocable — by putting the wife's life, and her marriage to another, between the declaration and any possible return.

**The Deterrence Built Into the Bar**

The commentators read the rule as the deliberate completion of the code's anti-harm architecture: an extension of the logic limiting declarative divorce (2:229) and the forswearing of marital relations (2:226). Their reasoning is precise. Were the husband free to remarry his thrice-divorced wife, he would be free to declare three more divorces and repeat the process continuously — cycling her through serial waiting-periods, married on paper, never free in fact, barred from every other suitor. The prohibition against remarriage until she has married and been divorced by another caps the damage available to him at a finite sequence, and it deters the declaration itself: the prospect of recovering a wife who has meanwhile been another's is, in most cases, unlikely — and the unlikelihood is legislated on purpose. The third ṭalāq is thereby made expensive in the exact currency the abuser values: permanent loss. The man who used divorce as a leash finds at the third word that the leash has severed, and the severance is final in every direction the abuser measured — she is free, he is barred, and the return he counted on depends on contingencies no one controls. The code's whole deterrent logic — count, cost, finality — converges in this verse.

**The Intervening Marriage's Conditions**

The jurists drew the rule's necessary conditions from its wording and purpose. Most hold that the intervening marriage must have been real — entered and lived — before the first pairing may lawfully resume (al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī) — the marriage must be real, not a paper transaction, since a nominal contract divorced without consummation would circumvent the bar as easily as the serial cycling it prevents. The consummation-requirement keeps the intervening marriage's dignity intact in the law's own terms: the wife's second marriage is a true marriage with its full rights and its own possible future, not a legal device — and its ending in divorce must be that marriage's own real event. The verse's scope-clarification completes the conditions: this bar applies to the thrice-divorced pair; remarriage after a single declaration belongs to the different legislation the code will supply — the completed-waiting-period remarriage of 2:232, where the couple's honorable consent suffices and no intervening marriage is required. The three-declaration case and the single-declaration case are thus governed by opposite logics: the first barred absolutely until another life has intervened; the second open as soon as the waiting ends — the law's severity exactly proportioned to the abuse each case represents.

**If They Deem They Will Uphold the Limits**

The return the verse permits carries its condition: no blame on the two to return to each other *where both judge that the bounds of God will this time be kept*. The deeming is prospective — a judgment about the future marriage, not a nostalgia about the past one — and its object is the limits: the whole ḥudūd the code has legislated, the retention in honor and release in grace, the reciprocal rights, the counts observed. The condition transforms the permitted remarriage from a romantic reunion into a covenantal assessment: the twice-separated couple may return only on the expectation that the third time will keep the law — that the harms which ended the marriage twice will not end it a third. The jurists read the deeming as a serious estimate, and its presence explains the bar's mercy: the intervening marriage is not only the deterrent but the test — years have passed, another life has been lived, and the two who return do so as people with evidence about themselves rather than as people with memories. The limits-language ties the verse to its predecessor's closing formula, and the tie is the code's self-reference: the limits whose transgression made the wrongdoers (2:229) are the limits whose future observance conditions the return — the same law serving as both the bar's reason and the gate's requirement.

**Clear to a People Who Know**

The verse ends with the ḥudūd-formula's rarest qualification: The bounds are made plain, the verse closes, for those endowed with knowledge. The clarity-clause has run through the sūrah's legislation — the signs made clear that people may be mindful (2:187, 2:221), the verses made clear that they may reflect (2:219–220) — and each instance names its audience by the faculty the legislation requires. Here the faculty is knowledge: the divorce-code is addressed to knowers, and the address is the code's final instruction about itself. The machinery this verse completes — counts, bars, intervening marriages, ransom economics, prospective deeming — is intricate, and its intricacy is not accidental but pedagogical: a law this precisely articulated against the abuses it foresees demands readers who can hold its structure, trace its deterrents, and apply its conditions case by case. The people who know are the code's intended administrators — the judges who impose divorce at the īlāʾ's deadline, the arbiters who assess the khulʿ's fear, the jurists who debate consummation and ceilings and the dual of the limits — and the verse names them as the clarity's recipients because the limits' keeping depends on their knowledge as much as on the spouses' obedience. Between the clarity and the knowledge stands the code's whole claim: the household's hardest cases are not left to power or custom but legislated in detail, made plain, and entrusted to understanding.

**The Code's Keystones**

Read with its neighbors, this verse is the divorce-code's keystone — the piece that locks the arch. The count (2:229) gave the structure its numbers; the bar and its sequel (2:230) give the numbers their consequence; the harm-prohibitions that follow (2:231–232) give the consequence its manners — the retention without injury, the remarriage without obstruction; and the nursing-legislation (2:233) extends the code's care past the divorce to the children it produces. The keystone's own logic gathers everything: the marriage is a covenant whose dissolution must be deliberate (the counts), whose final dissolution must be costly (the bar), whose restoration must be reformed (the deeming), and whose whole law must be understood (the knowers). The wife the pre-Islamic practice suspended for years now has a calendar; the husband who cycled declarations now has a terminus; the couple who return now have a condition; and the community now has a code whose severity — the third word's irreversibility — is entirely in the service of its mercy: that no marriage be a prison, no divorce a weapon, and no return a repetition of the harm the limits were made clear to prevent.
