## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:141

> and distinguish the ˹true˺ believers and destroy the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Clause Continued**

The verse carries on the sentence that began in the previous one, and it supplies a second purpose for the alternation of fortunes. The first purpose concerned the believers, and this one names both groups: the testing of those who believe, and the reduction of those who deny.

The grammatical construction caused the commentators some discussion. Al-Zamakhsharī noted that the sentence works with an implied phrase, so that it reads either as a statement that God arranged events in order to know the believers, or as a statement that God will know them and will act accordingly. Either way, the clause binds the events of the battle to a purpose rather than presenting them as accidents.

**What Assaying Involves**

The word rendered as assay belongs to the language of refining metal, and it describes the removal of what is impure from what is valuable. Al-Ṭabarī understood the process here as trials designed to test patience, which fits the way the Book uses the image elsewhere.

The Book states the same principle in several places: the community should not think it will be left alone without being tested, that those who claim belief will be left untested, or that entry into the garden comes without the struggle and endurance that the Book describes in this sūrah. The image of the refiner's fire suits a passage written for people who had just come through one.

**Blight and What It Means**

The second half of the clause says that God blights the deniers, and the word describes a reduction or a shortage, a diminishing so thorough that nothing of the thing can be seen. Al-Rāzī explained it in those terms, and the same root appears in the earlier sūrah where God states that He destroys interest and causes charity to grow.

The pairing of the two verbs is the point. One group is refined and the other is reduced, and both outcomes are attributed to the same arrangement of days. The verse's language avoids the suggestion that the fighting at Uḥud produced a result outside God's purposes, and it equally avoids the suggestion that the two groups were being treated alike.

**Two Outcomes for Two Groups**

The clause assigns a different result to each party, and the sūrah has been building toward this distinction since its account of the battle began. The community's losses were not the same in kind as its enemies' losses, even when both happened on the same field.

The difference lies in what each group was being fitted for. The believers were being separated from what might be mistaken for faith, and their enemies were being reduced in a matter that would eventually leave them with nothing. The sentence states both results in a few words, and the sūrah's earlier verses about the fate of those who deny the truth have prepared the reader for the second.

**The Two Battles in the Commentators' Reading**

The commentators read the reference to a people who had suffered a like wound as pointing to the enemies of the believers who had been defeated at Badr the year before. The wound at Uḥud answered an earlier wound, and the same community that had won the first engagement was present in both.

This reading gives the verse its historical anchor and explains why the sūrah refers to the alternation of days. The tradition of interpretation added an Arabic proverb that captures the sense of the passage: one day for you, and one day against you. The Book uses that rhythm as a teacher, and both battles belong to the lesson.

**Why Victory Is Not Simply Given**

Al-Qurṭūbī and others stated the principle that runs through these verses: God supports the believers, and when they disobey or lose their footing, their enemies gain the upper hand over them. Al-Rāzī put the matter in the same terms, observing that God does not support the deniers as such; He permits their success as a means of testing the believers through loss.

This explanation accounts for the whole sūrah's treatment of Uḥud. The community had been ordered to hold a position, had argued about the decision, and had left the position for the sake of spoils. The result came through the ordinary working of events, and the Book describes it as a test rather than as the withdrawal of a promise.

**The Knowledge of God in Sentences Like This**

The expression that God may know recurs in the Book wherever a trial is described, and the commentators addressed the theological question it raises in their treatment of the earlier passage in this sūrah about the community at the centre of the nations. The point they settled on is that God's knowledge is not increased by events; what changes is that a matter becomes visible in the world.

The Book uses the idiom the way people use it in ordinary speech. To say of a person that one knows no good in him is to say that he is not good, and the statement is about the reality that the events have brought to light. The verse therefore tells the community what the battle revealed about the people who lived through it, and the next verses apply the same principle in the form of a question.

**The Address to a Shaken Community**

The verse belongs to a sequence that handles the aftermath of a defeat step by step: the fact of the loss, the reminder that days alternate, the assurance of a purpose, and now the distinction between the two groups caught up in the same event.

Each step answers a conclusion that a person might otherwise draw. The last of them answers the most corrosive one, which is that the outcome showed no difference between the two sides. The sūrah denies that reading in a single clause, and the verses that follow turn to the community's own conduct before the battle, which is where the rest of the explanation lies.
