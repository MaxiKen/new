## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:91

> When it is said to them: “Believe in what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “We only believe in what was sent down to us,” and they deny what came afterwards, though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures! Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Why then did you kill Allah’s prophets before, if you are ˹truly˺ believers?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**“We Only Believe in What Was Sent Down to Us”**

The recital stages the confrontation directly now: the call is issued — *believe in what Allah has revealed* — and the answer comes back as a formula of loyalty: *we only believe in what was sent down to us*. On its surface the reply is the most respectable refusal possible. It claims no disbelief, only fidelity; it rejects nothing in principle, only what falls outside its inheritance. The exclusive particle — *only* — is the reply's whole architecture: belief is bounded by provenance, and provenance by possession. What was sent down *to us* is the criterion, which quietly converts revelation into property: the Torah as an estate held by a people rather than a truth addressed to whoever it reaches. The verse's first clause matters for the exchange's fairness — the call they answer is general: believe in what *Allah* has revealed, the sender named and the recipients unnamed, the invitation framed so that loyalty itself could accept it. If revelation is from Allah, then whatever Allah reveals carries the same authority as what Allah revealed first, and the reply's *only* has already decided the question: not by examining the new Book but by declaring in advance that nothing new can belong to them. The recital quotes the formula because it is the permanent shape of denominational closure — every community's version of *what was sent down to us* — and quotes it to dismantle, in the reply's own terms, the loyalty it claims.

**The Truth Confirming What They Hold**

The dismantling begins with a fact the reply cannot absorb: *they deny what came afterwards, though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures*. The confirming relationship — the one verse 89 filed at the Book's arrival — is now turned into an argument from their own side of the shelf. Their Scripture is true; they hold it; the Book they deny testifies to precisely that Scripture, carrying its confirmation as its credentials. The logic closes like a hand: to believe the Torah is to believe a book that announces confirmers; when the confirmer arrives with the Torah's own marks upon him, denying him is denying the thing that made the Torah credible. The verse's exclamation — *though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures!* — is the recital's astonishment at a refusal that saws off the branch it sits on. The exegetes mark the same structure in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ's ruling on selective belief, where those who believe in some and disbelieve in others are answered with the flatest verdict the Book possesses: they are disbelievers in truth (4:150–151). The principle generalizes past the occasion: confirmation is the Qur'an's own claim about its relation to all prior revelation — a Book that does not abolish what preceded but testifies to it — and a community that honors the testified-to while rejecting the testimony has not kept its Scripture but used it, holding the lamp while refusing the light it was made to show.

**The Retort: Why Did You Kill Your Own Prophets?**

The verse's second half hands the Prophet ﷺ the argument's killing move: *Ask them: Why then did you kill Allah's prophets before, if you are truly believers?* The question's construction deserves exact reading. The slain prophets were Israelites — Ibn Kathīr's point — messengers from within the community whose sole command was fidelity to the same Torah the reply invokes. If *we believe in what was sent down to us* were the operating principle, those prophets would have been its most unquestionable objects: same people, same Scripture, no provenance objection possible. They were killed anyway. The retort therefore proves that the refusal of the new Book stems from something other than faith in the old — the loyalty formula is a mask, and the community's own history of prophetic murder is the face beneath it. The question also carries a second charge in its tail: *if you are truly believers* — the conditional hangs because the answer is known. Believers in the Torah killed the Torah's prophets, and murder was forbidden in any case (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī): the formula of loyalty is contradicted not only by the rejection it excuses but by the graves it already dug. The verse's structure — call, reply, retort — is the recital's polemical method at its most economical: no assertion is made that the opponents can deny; they are simply asked to explain their own record, and the record does the work.

**Believing in Some, Disbelieving in Others**

The reply's formula places its speakers in a category the Qur'an legislates at length, and the category is the verse's doctrinal contribution. Selective belief — accepting some revelation, rejecting the rest — is not partial faith but a distinct position with its own logic, and the logic always runs on selection by appetite or identity rather than by truth. The sūrah documented its historical forms in sequence: the Scripture rationed by cost (85), the messenger judged by likability (87), the Book refused by lineage (89–90), and now revelation bounded by provenance (91). Each form selects on a different criterion — price, pleasure, tribe, property — and the common structure is the same: the believer's own preferences installed as the gate through which revelation must pass. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ's ruling names the result: such people are made disbelievers in truth by their selection (4:150–151), and the verse there adds the intermediate case the recital keeps meeting — belief claimed in all the prophets while the practice believes in some. The doctrinal edge for the hearing community is uncomfortable and deliberate: the Muslim ummah possesses a complete Scripture, which removes the provenance excuse but not the mechanism. Selection by appetite operates inside any canon — the verses honored and the verses quietly dropped, the commandments kept and the commandments reinterpreted into suggestion — and the verse's retort applies wherever it operates: show your record, not your formula, and the record will say which revelation you actually believe.

**The Question as Method**

The command framing the retort — *Ask them* — is the verse's instruction in prophetic argument, and its form is as significant as its content. The Prophet ﷺ is not told to assert, to condemn, or to threaten; he is told to question, and the question is historical rather than theological: not *why are you wrong* but *why did you do what your own dead record?* The method has three advantages the recital exploits throughout this passage. It is unanswerable: the killed prophets are a fact of the community's own tradition, not a claim of the questioner's. It is non-escalating: a question invites the interlocutor's own reasoning rather than triggering the defenses an accusation raises. And it is transferable: whoever hears the exchange — the tribes' members, the Muslims present, the recital's later readers — performs the calculation himself, and a conclusion the hearer computes is believed differently than one he is handed. Verse 80 ran the same procedure against the numbered days (*have you taken a pledge?*), and verse 87's closing question (*why is it that every time...?*) set this one up. The recital's polemics are consistently forensic because its subject is consistently a record: a people with documented covenants, witnessed pledges, and dated crimes, asked at each station to reconcile its claims with its file. The verse thereby teaches its audience an argument-form — ask, don't assert; use their record, not your rhetoric — and the form outlives the occasion as the Book's standing method with every community that claims a loyalty its history contradicts.

**Loyalty or Ownership**

The formula *what was sent down to us* contains the error the whole verse dissects: the *to us* of address mistaken for the *to us* of ownership. Revelation sent to a people is a mercy with addressees, not a deed with heirs — it obligates them, and obligation cannot be exclusive in the way property is, because the sender's authority follows the revelation wherever it goes. When Allah sends a further revelation confirming the first, the first community's loyalty is tested precisely by the confirmation: the faithful recognize their own book's testimony in the new one, and the proprietors discover that what they held as estate has arrived as witness against their management of it. The verse's closing conditional — *if you are truly believers* — leaves the test standing for every holder of every scripture: belief is verified by what it accepts when acceptance costs provenance, pride, or preference, and the communities of the recital failed the verification with their own prophets' blood on the record. For the Muslims hearing the sūrah in Madina, the lesson arrived as inheritance with a warning label attached: they had just been given the complete file of a people whose formula of loyalty concealed a history of selection and murder, and the file's final purpose was vaccination. The call the verse opens with — *believe in what Allah has revealed* — is therefore addressed, in the recital's design, to everyone who answers it: believe in what Allah has revealed, all of it, from whomever it comes, tested by confirmation rather than pedigree. The retort about the killed prophets remains available against every future *only*, and every generation gets to choose whether it needs answering.
