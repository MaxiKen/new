## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:91

> When it is said to them: “Believe in what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “We only believe in what was sent down to us,” and they deny what came afterwards, though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures! Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Why then did you kill Allah’s prophets before, if you are ˹truly˺ believers?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Invitation and the Reply**

The verse presents a scene that must have repeated itself often in Madinah: a believer invites a member of the Jewish community to accept what God has revealed, and the answer comes back that the community believes only in what was sent down to it. The Book records the exchange in two clauses and then proceeds to examine the position.

The reply has the shape of a principled statement. It sounds like fidelity to a tradition, and it was probably defended as exactly that. What the next clause does is test whether the loyalty it expresses is a loyalty to revelation or to a community's possession of it. What makes the exchange worth recording is its ordinariness. The invitation is the one the sūrah extends throughout this section, and the reply is the one it keeps meeting, so the verse serves as a specimen of the larger argument rather than as a report of a single incident.

**Belief Confined to What Was Sent to Us**

The phrase the community uses divides revelation by its recipients rather than by its source. The Book does not accept that division, and it states its own criterion repeatedly: all of the revealed scriptures come from one God, confirm one another, and address one another's peoples. The Qur'an describes itself as confirming what came before it and as a criterion over it, and it describes the earlier scriptures as guidance and light that were given to be followed.

The sūrah's opening had already presented the revelation as confirming the scripture that existed before it and as guidance for people aware of God, and the community's formula in this verse rejects the very continuity the Book is asserting. The sentence being quoted is therefore not a cautious position but a claim to own revelation exclusively.

**Denying What Came Afterwards**

The Book then states that the community denies what came after its own scripture, and it does so in a manner that treats the denial as the real position behind the formula. A person who believes only in what was sent down to him is not necessarily denying anything else; a person who denies what came after has made a decision about a specific text.

The distinction is not a technicality. The verse's charge is that the community reorganized its beliefs around the date of a revelation rather than around its content, and the following verses of the sūrah press the point when they ask why the same community killed prophets who preceded its own scripture. The Book dates the refusal to the arrival of the later revelation rather than to anything in the earlier one. Its charge is that a fixed point in history became the boundary of belief, so whatever came after it was dismissed for the fact of its arrival rather than for anything in its content.

**The Truth That Confirms Their Own Scriptures**

The clause in the middle of the sentence removes the last plausible ground for the refusal. What the community rejects is the truth, and the truth is described as confirming its own scriptures. The Book's argument is self-referential in the strongest sense: the community's own text, by its own standard, is evidence for the revelation it is refusing.

This is the same method the sūrah used in the previous verses, where it established that the community recognized the Book before it rejected it. The passage builds a consistent case: recognition first, confirmation second, refusal third, and the motive named last. The reader is left with a decision to make rather than a fact to weigh. The claim being made is that the two revelations agree, which means the refusal cannot be explained by a doctrinal clash. Later the same community is asked how it can reject a book that matches what it already has, and the question presupposes that no such clash exists.

**The Question About the Prophets**

The verse ends with a question the Prophet ﷺ is instructed to put to them. If the community truly believed in what was sent down to it, how does it explain the killing of the prophets who came before? The question is aimed at the notion that loyalty to a scripture produces loyalty to revelation in general, since the community's own history shows that it did not.

The Arabic form of the question is a challenge to the claim of belief, not an invitation to debate. The Book's method here is to hold the community to the record it cannot deny and to let the record contradict the position it has just declared. The question does not require the audience to accept any new premise; it works entirely from the community's acknowledged history.

**Why the Question Cannot Be Answered**

The community's answer in the previous clause was that it believes what was sent down to it. The question supplies a counter-example drawn from that same tradition: messengers sent by the same God to the same people were rejected and killed. If the formula meant what it said, the killings would be inexplicable.

The Book does not resolve the difficulty for the reader, because the point is the impossibility of resolution. A position that survives only by ignoring its own history is exposed by one question, and the sūrah lets the question stand without an answer. The next verse continues the historical record by recalling the calf, and the pattern of claim-and-contradiction repeats with a different offence. An answer would require the community to distinguish between messengers, and that is exactly what the Book's account of prophecy refuses. The Qur'an presents the messengers as one series carrying one message, so a community that accepts some and rejects others has broken the series rather than discriminated within it.

**The Standard the Passage Leaves Behind**

Read as a whole, the verse sets out a criterion for belief: what God has revealed is accepted because of its source, not because of the community it reached first. The claim to own a revelation exclusively is what the Book rejects in its own readers as well as in the community it is addressing here.

That criterion is what makes this passage still useful to a Muslim reader. The sūrah's account of a community that treated its scripture as an inherited possession is followed later by exhortations to the Muslims about their own treatment of the Qur'an, and the same test is applied to anyone who has received a book and made a privilege of it. Applied broadly, the standard is a criticism of every community that converts revelation into an inheritance. The verse therefore belongs beside the sūrah's warnings to the Muslims, since a people who receive a book and treat it as their property reproduce the position this community is being asked to defend.

**A Heritage Held as a Claim**

There is a difference between inheriting a scripture and belonging to it, and this verse turns on that difference. The community's answer identifies its revelation by the people it reached rather than by the God who sent it, and once that identification is made, later revelation arrives as a rival instead of a confirmation. The Book's response is to test the claim against the community's own history, and the test produces the question about the prophets. A position that treats divine guidance as property has no way to explain why its own ancestors treated guidance as an intrusion, and the sūrah leaves the question standing. The next verse continues the record with the calf, and the passage builds a case in which the community's own memory supplies every piece of evidence.

**Why the Prophets Question Still Lands**

The question at the end of this verse has an unusual force for a short sentence, and its strength lies in what it does not require. No new evidence has to be accepted, no authority from outside the community has to be admitted, and no interpretation of a disputed text has to be settled. The community's answer was that it believed what had been sent down to it, and the question simply asks how that statement fits the record of the messengers sent down to the same people with the same God behind them. The Book's method here is to let an accepted premise do the work. The historical fact of the killings is not in dispute, and the contradiction between it and the claim of exclusive fidelity does not need an outsider to point it out.
