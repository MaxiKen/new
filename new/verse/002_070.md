## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:70

> Again they said, “Call upon your Lord so that He may make clear to us which cow, for all cows look the same to us. Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Community's Position at the Fourth Question**

The community asks for further clarification because, on their own account, the animals look alike to them. The statement is a concession that the previous specifications have not settled their search, and it comes after two rounds of narrowing that should have made the target easier to identify. What the sentence discloses is not the difficulty of finding the animal but the community's unwillingness to stop asking.

The Book reports their words without comment, and it adds nothing about their motives. What the reader has is a community that has now asked three times about the same command, and the narrative's economy leaves the judgment to the pattern rather than to a verdict. The sūrah has shown the process from the beginning, and the process is the subject.

**"All Cows Look the Same to Us"**

The complaint has an obvious problem inside it, since the specifications already given excluded most animals in a herd: a colour that stands out and an age in the middle of a working life narrow the field considerably. The Book does not pause to point this out. It records the community's assertion in their own words and moves on to the answer.

The sentence's real content is the attitude it reveals about the whole enterprise. A community that has repeatedly asked for precision and now says that everything looks alike is describing its own relationship with the command rather than the state of the market. Its difficulty is not with the animal's description, and further inquiries will not resolve it.

**A Conditional Commitment**

The sentence ends with a promise: if the matter is made clear, they will be guided. The Book records the words with the qualifier the community itself attached, acknowledging that guidance belongs to God, and it treats the statement as evidence of the reservation inside it. The commitment to act is offered for a later date rather than for the present.

The Book's attention to the grammar of promises is consistent. Throughout the sūrah it reports communities who attach conditions to their obedience, and it records the qualifications without treating them as sincerity. What is being shown here is a people who have made their obedience a function of the quality of the information available to them, and who will be given a final answer in the next verse.

**The Detail the Answer Withholds**

The sūrah's next verse supplies the last description and stops there. No further questions follow, and the Book moves directly to the slaughter and to the community's hesitation over it. The narrative therefore ends the interrogation at the point where the community had offered a promise, which means the reader learns that the promise was kept with reluctance rather than with enthusiasm.

The Book's account of that hesitation appears in the same sentence as the act, so that the two are never separated. What the community did is recorded alongside how difficult it found the doing, and the sūrah's arrangement of the material keeps both facts in view.

**Why the Sūrah Repeats the Exchange in Full**

The Qur'an's usual method for narrating an incident from Israel's history is a single compressed verse, and this passage is an exception. Four verses are given to a conversation about an animal, and the Book does not use the space for historical detail or for the identification of a place or a person. The whole of the space is occupied by the exchange itself.

The effect of that choice is to make the community's method the subject of the passage. Anyone reading the account learns what a delayed obedience looks like from the inside, and the lesson does not depend on knowing which animal was bought or what it cost. The sūrah supplies the exchange and trusts the exchange to carry the point.

**What the Community Does Not Yet Know**

The readers of the sūrah know what the community did not. The animal's body is going to be used, and the purpose attached to it in the following verses is the production of a demonstration that life can be restored to a corpse. The community's arguing is therefore taking place in front of a requirement whose function will become clear later, and their insistence on knowing the animal's colour is made to seem what it is.

The Book does not state this contrast, and it does not need to, because the information is placed in the reader's hands by the order of the narrative. What the sūrah does is record the questioning faithfully and then report the outcome, and the sequence produces the meaning without assistance.

**Confession Made Without Submission**

The sentence is an example of a statement that concedes difficulty and withholds obedience. The community admits that the search is unclear to them, and it holds out the prospect of acting once the matter is settled. Nothing in the sentence is a refusal, and nothing in it is a commitment to act now. The Book records the words and lets the following verse supply the rest of the story.

That gap between admission and action is what the passage has been demonstrating from the beginning of the exchange. A community that names its uncertainty while leaving a command unperformed has converted its own difficulty into a reason for delay, and the Book's narratives about this people return to the same point in each incident. The sūrah's addresses to the community's descendants assume a reader who recognises the pattern.
