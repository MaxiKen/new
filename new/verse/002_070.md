## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:70

> Again they said, “Call upon your Lord so that He may make clear to us which cow, for all cows look the same to us. Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Question, and the Confusion That Grows**

The exchange's final question returns to its first's exact wording — *make clear to us which cow* — as if the two intermediate answers had never been given. Age specified, colour specified, the description now carrying marks no ordinary animal matches; and the questioners report the opposite of what specification should produce: *all cows look the same to us*. The verb is *tashābaha* — the things have become mutually similar, indistinguishable, confusable — and the report is the verse's central datum: information has accumulated and clarity has decreased. The dynamic is familiar to every trade and discipline. The person who must act on a general instruction finds it executable; the person who insists on total specification generates comparisons, and comparisons breed resemblances, until the field of candidates is simultaneously over-described and impossible to choose from. The questioners are not lying — the confusion is real — and its reality is the indictment. Confusion arrived as the fruit of their method: three rounds of interrogation conducted at a distance (*your Lord*, still, in this third question) have produced the epistemic state interrogation always produces when it replaces action — paralysis wearing the vocabulary of care.

**In Shāʾ Allāh Arrives at the Third Round**

The question ends with the narrative's first conditional formula: *then, Allah willing, we will be guided to the right one*. The phrase is the correct one — the Qur'an legislates it explicitly for every future-tense intention: *"And never say of anything, 'I will definitely do this tomorrow,' without adding, 'if Allah so wills!'"* (18:23–24) — and its arrival here, in the third round, carries the weight of its lateness. The exegetical tradition draws the inference directly: had the people coupled their first response to the command with *in shāʾ Allāh* — had they received the order with surrender instead of interrogation — any cow would have answered, and the negotiation would never have begun. The formula that makes asking lawful is the formula that makes acting possible, and the questioners discover it only after two rounds of its absence have cost them. The verse leaves the sincerity of the discovery open, and the openness is precise: *in shāʾ Allāh* can be the speech of submission or the idiom of a culture, and only the slaughter will show which it was. The narration's closing verdict on the whole exchange — that they slaughtered, but almost did not — suggests the formula arrived on the tongue before it arrived in the will. The conditional, spoken at the right moment, hands the outcome to Allah; spoken at the third round, it documents how long the questioners kept the outcome in their own hands.

**Tashābaha: Resemblance in the Looker**

The confusion-claim deserves its lexical weight, because the root does constant work in the Qur'an's epistemology. *Tashābuh* is resemblance, mutual similarity — the state of things whose differences the observer cannot hold. The questioners locate the resemblance in the cows (*cows look the same to us*), and the verse's staging relocates it in the lookers. The animals have not changed across the three rounds; the description has narrowed; the confusion grew as the information grew, which fixes its source. The spiritual commentators state the mechanism plainly: the multiplying question multiplies the candidate field it surveys, and every added specification is another property to compare — until the comparer stands before a herd rendered uniform by his own scrutiny. The pattern generalizes past cows. The believer who will not act until every doubt is resolved finds doubt regenerating at the resolution's threshold; the student who will not write until every source is read finds the sources branching; the repentant who will not return until every condition is clarified finds conditions accruing. Action narrows fields; interrogation widens them. The verse files the discovery as dialogue so the reader overhears the exact sentence in which paralysis announces itself — *the things look the same to us* — and recognizes it as the sound of looking having replaced doing.

**“We Will Be Guided” — to Which Guidance?**

The question's stated purpose is guidance: *then we will be guided to the right one*. The word is the Qur'an's greatest term — *hidāyah*, the guidance the sūrah opened by naming as its own content, the Book as guidance for the mindful (2:2) — and its appearance here, attached to cow-identification, is the verse's sharpest irony. The questioners use the vocabulary of salvation for the project of specification: guidance, in their sentence, means being shown which animal to buy. The narrowing is diagnostic. Guidance in the Qur'an's primary sense is directional — the whole of a life oriented toward Allah — and the command standing before these speakers was already guidance in that sense, complete and sufficient: *Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow*. To obey it was to be guided; the guidance was in the command, not in the cow's description. What the questioners seek is guidance's opposite in function — information without obligation, clarity purchased at the price of delay. The verse does not mock the word's use; it shows the word's demotion. When a divine command is received as a problem to be solved rather than a direction to be walked, the highest vocabulary the receivers possess gets conscripted into the solving — *we will be guided* — and the guidance shrinks to fit the task. The sūrah's readers are left holding the distinction the questioners lost: guided to the right cow, or guided, in the sense the Book was sent for.

**The Invoice Completes Itself**

The third question forces the final specification, and the final specification completes the narrowing: after this round the description will admit a single animal. The reports the tradition preserves give the completion its social form — one such cow in existence, an owner aware of its uniqueness, a price assembled from everything the three rounds had added: the age, the colour, the rarity, and now the total definition. The invoice's structure is the verse's lesson in one object. An indefinite command is free at the moment of receipt: the nearest cow, slaughtered in obedience, discharges it wholly. Every question is a line-item, and the line-items are not paid in information but in obligation — each answer narrowing the field and raising the cost of the field's remainder. The questioners experienced each round as clarification and were, in the exchange's real accounting, ordering: first the functional specification, then the beautiful one, then the total one, and with the total one the unique animal at its unique price. The counterfactual the tradition states — the nearest cow would have sufficed — is therefore not a rebuke of curiosity but an itemized receipt: sufficiency was on offer at the first word, and three questions bought it out. The verse stands in the recital as the sūrah's economics of obedience: commands are cheapest at arrival, and the price rises with every request to redefine them.

**The Adab of Asking**

The three rounds together compose the Qur'an's instruction on the etiquette of inquiry, and its positive model stands elsewhere in the same revelation. The disciples of ʿĪsā ask for a table spread from heaven — a request granted, but granted with the sternest conditional warning attached anywhere in the Qur'an: belief required, disbelief after the sign answered by a punishment unlike any other (5:112–115). The pattern across both scenes is constant: asking Allah's prophets to produce specifications and signs is possible, is sometimes answered, and always costs. The alternative the recital keeps filing is the believers' standing formula — *we hear and obey* (2:285) — the two verbs in their proper order, hearing completed by doing, with inquiry occupying the space action leaves rather than the space action needs. The distinction the verse installs is not between asking and never asking; Islamic law is built on questions put to the Prophet ﷺ and answered. It is between the question that serves performance — *how shall I do this?* asked by one already moving — and the question that replaces it, asked at a distance, in the conditional mood, while the command waits. The cow's questioners are the second kind's founding case, and their *in shāʾ Allāh* at the third round is the verse's mercy: the correct formula, arriving late, still carried them to the slaughter. The etiquette is learnable from their invoice: ask what obedience requires, couple every intention to the divine will from the first word, and remember that the field looks most uniform from the farthest distance.
