## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:48

> Those on the heights will call out to some ˹tyrants in the Fire˺, who they will recognize by their appearance, saying, “Your large numbers and arrogance are of no use ˹today˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address to the Proud**

The verse reports the address of the people on the heights to some of the people of the Fire whom they recognize by their appearance, and the address states that their numbers and their arrogance are of no use on that day. The identification is made from the elevated station, and the address follows the prayer of the previous verse. The people on the heights speak to both sides across the barrier, and the words in front of us are directed at the leaders among the condemned.

The phrasing of the address identifies a group rather than the whole company. The commentary tradition reads the addressees as the notables and chiefs whose rank and following had given them their standing in the world, and the sūrah's narratives of the communities supply the model, since each of them describes the notables of a people dismissing the messenger. The verse in front of us states the reckoning of that group in a single sentence.

**Numbers as a Measure**

The verse names the abundance of the condemned as a thing that no longer helps them, and the Book repeatedly warns its audience against the argument from majority. The sūrah of the Cattle states that if the Prophet were to obey most of those on the earth they would lead him away from the way of God (6:116), and the sūrah of the Table states that good and evil are not equal however much the abundance of the evil amazes the observer (5:100). The verse in front of us takes the argument from numbers and states its outcome.

The same measure appears in the sūrah's own account of the communities. The peoples of Noah, ʿĀd, and Thamūd were the majorities in their own lands, and the sūrah's narratives show what their numbers did for them when the term arrived. The sūrah of the Cow describes a people who left their homes in their thousands for fear of death and were told to die (2:243), and the verse in front of us applies the same verdict to the crowd that stands in the Fire.

**Wealth and Children That Do Not Avail**

The second item in the address is the arrogance that accompanied the abundance, and the Book attaches the two together in its descriptions of the prosperous deniers. The sūrah of Sheba reports the words of those who said that they were greater in wealth and children than the believers and that they would not be punished (34:35), and the sūrah of the Night Journey describes the Israelites being aided with wealth and offspring and made to outnumber their enemies, which did not protect them from the reckoning that followed (17:6). The sūrah of Noah reports the same reliance on wealth and children among the people who followed their leaders (71:21).

The Book's treatment of wealth and offspring as marks of divine favour in the world and as irrelevant to the verdict of the Hereafter appears in several passages, and the sūrah of Sheba states that neither wealth nor children bring a person near to God. The address in front of us names the two grounds of confidence that the great of the world rely upon, and it states that both have been left behind with the world that produced them.

**The Arrogance Named**

The address names arrogance alongside the numbers, and the Book uses the term for the disposition that refuses the command and despises those who accept it. The sūrah of Yūnus describes the chiefs of Pharaoh behaving arrogantly and persisting in their denial, and the sūrah of the Heights itself has already stated that arrogance has no place in the Garden and that the proud who refuse the signs will not enter it until the camel passes through the eye of a needle (7:13; 7:40). The verse in front of us states the same disposition at the reckoning.

The sūrah's treatment of the fault connects the narrative of the adversary with the history of the communities. The first refusal was made on a claim of superior nature, and the sūrah's later accounts of the notables who rejected the messengers repeat the claim with the terms of wealth, lineage, and power. The address in front of us names the common feature of all of them, and it states that the feature has no standing in the place where it is spoken.

**The Marks by Which They Are Known**

The verse states that the people on the heights recognize the condemned by their appearance, and the same statement was made in the previous verse about the recognition of both groups. The Book's descriptions of the day include visible signs that distinguish the saved from the condemned, and the commentary tradition explains the marks as the traces of the two conditions. The people on the heights in front of us identify the notables among the condemned and address them by what they see.

The recognition gives the scene its shape, since the people on the heights speak to a group they can identify without an introduction. The Book's habit of placing recognition at the center of the last-day scenes appears in its descriptions of the believers being known by their light and the condemned by their marks, and the verse in front of us uses the same arrangement to introduce the address.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands forty-eighth in the chapter, and it completes the speech of the people on the heights in the direction of the Fire. The verse that follows reports the question put to the condemned about the humble believers whom they had sworn would never receive mercy, and the answer that is given to the people on the heights themselves. This verse supplies the address that precedes the question.

The reader who has been given the station between the two outcomes now hears it speaking in both directions. The greeting, the prayer, and the reproach to the proud are all spoken from the same height, and the sūrah then states the mercy that the condemned had denied and the command given to those who hoped for it.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse asks whether these are the people concerning whom the condemned swore that God would not extend mercy, and it reports the command given to the people on the heights to enter the Garden with no fear and no grief (7:49). The reproach in front of us is followed by the vindication of the believers who had been despised, and the two verses complete the exchange across the barrier.

The scene thus reverses the judgment of the world. Those who were counted great by their numbers and their arrogance stand in the Fire, and those who were dismissed as too small to be shown mercy are told to enter the Garden; and the sūrah leaves the reader with the two verdicts in the order in which they were delivered.
