## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:75

> And what is it with you? You do not fight in the cause of Allah and for oppressed men, women, and children who cry out, “Our Lord! Deliver us from this land of oppressors! Appoint for us a saviour; appoint for us a helper—all by Your grace.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**What Is It With You That You Do Not Fight**

The question *wa mā lakum lā tuqātilūna fī sabīli Allāhi* — and what is it with you, you do not fight in the cause of Allah — is not a request for information; it is a rebuke that awakens. The sūrah has just called *let those who would sell this life for the next fight in God’s cause* and promised a great reward whether martyred or victorious (4:74). Now it asks why the broader community hesitates when the cause is not only the path of God but the distress of people. The lagger who called absence at disaster a blessing (4:72) and wished to have been there at bounty (4:73) is the type for whom the question is asked; the fighter who sells this life for the next is the answer. The sūrah elsewhere says *take your precautions and go forth in groups or together* (4:71) and *believers fight for God while disbelievers fight for the Devil whose schemes are weak* (4:76); this question is the moral pressure between those two, aimed at the gap between knowing the cause and moving for it. The cause — *sabīl Allāh* — was shown as the clear path God wills to make plain (4:26), the limits about wealth and marriage whose keeping is obedience, and the help for the weak; fighting in that path here means fighting where the weak cannot fight for themselves.

**For Oppressed Men, Women, and Children**

The addition *wa al-mustadʿafīna min al-rijāli wa al-nisāʾi wa al-wildāni* — and for the oppressed among men, women, and children — specifies whose cause is God’s cause at this moment. *Mustadʿafīn* — those made weak, deemed weak, oppressed — were explained as Muslims in Makkah and other places held under the power of oppressors, unable to emigrate or to defend. Among them were men, women, and children, the very groups the sūrah has been guarding: men and women with obligatory shares (4:7, 4:11–12), women with dowries and a firm commitment (4:19–21), children with tested competence and witnesses at handover (4:6). To fight for the oppressed of each is to bring the sūrah’s ethic of *iḥsān* — kind doing — to parents, relatives, orphans, poor, neighbours, companions, travellers, and those in possession (4:36) into the field. The Qur’an elsewhere praises delivering the oppressed as among the great acts, and the sūrah’s own logic of trusts — *return trusts to rightful owners and judge with fairness* (4:58) — becomes, at scale, deliverance for those whose trusts — freedom, property, worship — are denied. Fighting for the oppressed is therefore not a separate cause beside God’s; it is the form God’s cause takes where oppression exists.

**Deliver Us From This Land of Oppressors**

The quoted cry *alladhīna yaqūlūna rabbanā akhrijnā min hādhihi al-qaryati al-ẓālimi ahluhā* — Our Lord, deliver us from this land of oppressors, whose people are oppressors — was heard as a daily prayer of the detained, a *duʿāʾ* that names the place as *the town whose people are oppressors* rather than naming a tribe, showing that the character of its people — *ẓālim ahluhā* — makes it oppressive. The sūrah has named *ẓulm* throughout — consuming orphan wealth unjustly (4:10), devouring by falsehood deliberately (4:30), fabricating against God (4:50), and envy as *ḥasad* that withholds (4:54). To be oppressed is to live where those forms are the rule. The cry asks not only for removal — *akhrijnā* — deliver us out — but for governance after removal, which is why it continues *appoint for us a guardian and appoint for us a helper — all by Your grace* (4:75). The phrase *min ladunka* — from Your presence — matches *ajr ʿaẓīm min ladunnā* — great reward from Our presence (4:67, 4:74) — showing that the deliverance and the reward share the same source. The oppressed do not ask first for wealth or revenge; they ask for a *walī* and *naṣīr*, the two sufficiencies the sūrah said God Himself provides (4:45) and now provides through those who fight.

**Appoint for Us a Saviour, Appoint for Us a Helper — All by Your Grace**

The double request *wa ajʿal lanā min ladunka waliyyan wa ajʿal lanā min ladunka naṣīrā* — appoint for us, from Yourself, a guardian and a helper — was read as asking for leadership that is itself a mercy, not only rescue. *Walī* — guardian, protector who takes charge — and *naṣīr* — helper who brings victory — gather the sūrah’s two sufficiencies — *kafā bi Allāhi waliyyā wa kafā bi Allāhi naṣīrā* (4:45) — into a human form that God appoints. The oppressed recognize that the guardian who returns trusts and the helper who curbs the disbelievers’ might (4:84) must come *min ladunka* — from His presence — as grace. Early authorities linked this population to Muslims left in Makkah after the migration — al-ʿAbbās, Salmah, and others, including women and children — whose prayer was answered through the believers who fought. The prayer model matters: it is made by the weak, not by the strong describing the weak. The sūrah therefore teaches the community to hear the prayer as addressed to them through God. When the weak cry for a *walī* and *naṣīr*, the answer is the fighter who sells this life for the next (4:74) going forth with precautions (4:71) in God’s cause. The cause and the cry meet in the same act.

**A Question That Holds the Community to Its Name**

Placed after the promise to the fighter and before the distinction that believers fight for God and disbelievers for the Devil with weak schemes (4:76), this rebuke holds the community to its name as *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* — O believers — called at 4:71 and 4:59. To be among those who were told to obey and refer disputes, to return trusts, and to be kind in circles is to be among those the oppressed now call upon. The question *what is it with you* assumes that such people would fight, so the fact that some do not is surprising and needs reason. The next verses will give reasons some hesitate — fearing people as God should be feared, saying *why have You ordered us to fight, delay us* (4:77), and the lagger’s double talk at disaster and bounty — and will answer that the *dunyā* is little and the next better, and that death overtakes even in towers. Here the question is left as spur: the oppressed are men, women, and children — the same groups whose shares and dowries were guarded — crying for a guardian and helper from God’s grace. To hear that cry and remain as the lagger — among you yet not with you — is to be the one the question addresses. To hear it and fight — whether martyred or victorious — is to be the one the great reward awaits, and to become, by fighting, the *walī* and *naṣīr* from His presence for whom the oppressed prayed.
