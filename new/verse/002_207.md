## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:207

> And there are those who would dedicate their lives to Allah’s pleasure. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Soul Sold for Pleasure**

The diptych's second panel opens with the counter-figure: among people is the one who sells himself — his nafs, his soul, his self, his life — seeking the good pleasure of God. The verb is commercial and the verse means it: the same market-language the sūrah has used throughout — the traders who purchased error at guidance's price (2:16, 2:175), the concealers who sold revelation for a fleeting gain (2:174) — here receives its one holy instance. The seller is real, the merchandise is the highest thing a person owns, and the price is riḍwān Allāh, God's pleasure: not paradise as real estate, not safety as insurance, but the Divine good-will itself as the whole of the wanted. The transaction inverts the sūrah's bad trades completely. Those sold the eternal for the fleeting and received fire in their bellies; this one sells the fleeting — a life that ends regardless — for the eternal, and the verse's closing name will describe what the Buyer is: kind to His servants. The nafs sold is specified by the exegetes in its double sense, soul and life, because the sale runs at both levels: the self's desires given away, and where necessary the life itself laid down, in one undivided act of purchase-by-surrender.

**ʿUmar's Reading: The Man Who Enjoins and Is Slain**

The tradition's most cited application of the verse comes from the second caliph. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have read the seller as a man who sets out to enjoin the right and forbid the wrong — and is killed for it. The reading connects the verse to the Qur'an's great civic precept, al-amr bi'l-maʿrūf wa'l-nahy ʿan al-munkar, the commanding of good and forbidding of evil, a theme legislated across the scripture: let there be a community among you calling to the good, enjoining the right, forbidding the wrong — those are the successful (3:104); the believers, men and women, are protectors of one another who enjoin the right (9:71); and a dozen places more. On ʿUmar's reading the sale of the soul is not mystical withdrawal but public speech with a lethal price: the one who stands before a tyrant, a corrupt assembly, a community bent toward the corruption of 2:205, and says fear God — the very counsel the proud man of the previous verse rejected — knowing the saying may end him. The nafs as life makes the reading exact: the enjoiner sells his life in the sentence he speaks, and the verse records the transaction as the highest commerce available to a believer.

**ʿAlī in the Prophet's Bed**

The sīrah supplies the sale's most famous instance, reported by al-Thaʿlabī and the historians in connection with this verse. On the night of the Hijrah, with the Meccan assassins posted around the house waiting to strike at dawn, the Prophet commanded ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib to sleep in his bed and cover himself with his green mantle, so that the watchers, peering through the door's hole, would see a sleeper and believe him still present. ʿAlī — a young man lying down in a targeted bed, under a mantle that identified him as the target — asked whether the Prophet would be safe, was assured, and lay down; when the assassins rushed in at the hour they had chosen, they found ʿAlī and not Muhammad. The report needs no embellishment in the verse's terms: a soul sold, in full knowledge, for the pleasure of God — the sale priced in a night's certainty of death and paid, by God's keeping, with the morning's life. The tradition adds other candidates to the verse's roster: the Companions who spent their wealth on the emigration, or lost it behind them in Mecca, selling property and homeland in the same transaction of the road to Medina. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī gather the reports and keep, over all of them, the universal reading the passage prefers: whoever gives away his selfish desires or his life for God's sake has entered the virtuous bargain.

**The Virtuous Bargain**

The commentators' summary phrase for the verse — a virtuous bargain in the ultimate sense — states the economics the sūrah has been building since its opening pages. Every human being spends his nafs; the only question is the buyer. The traders of 2:16 spent their guidance on error and called the loss a trade; the concealers of 2:174 spent God's words on a fleeting gain and ate fire; the hypocrite of 204–206 spends his eloquence on this world's impression and is carried by pride into a sufficient Hell. The seller of this verse spends the same currency — the self, with its desires, its safety, its years — on the one counterparty whose payment does not fail: the good pleasure of God. The bargain's virtue is not that the seller receives more than he gives (he gives everything and receives a pleasure that is God's own) but that the transaction ends the selling: the self spent on the world must be re-spent forever, appetite renewing appetite, while the self sold to God is bought outright, and the buyer's name for the purchase follows in the same verse. The whole market-imagery of the sūrah — purchase, sale, trade, profit, loss — arrives here at its one transaction both parties will never regret.

**God Is Kind to His Servants**

The verse closes with the name that receives the sale: God is raʾūf — tender, deeply kind, full of pity — to His servants. The name's placement is the bargain's guarantee. A seller who gives his life fears one thing above all: that the price was wasted, that the buyer did not need the goods, that the sacrifice went unregarded. The verse answers with the divine tenderness: the Buyer of souls is not a hard master counting the minimum but the Raʾūf whose kindness precedes the sale — who made the servant, gave him the nafs he sells, and receives it with a tenderness the name denotes at its strongest in the Qur'an's usage. The clause also completes the diptych's contrast with the previous verse's ending: the proud man is paid with sufficiency — Hell suffices him, what an evil resting place; the selling man is received with tenderness — God is kind to His servants. The two panel-endings are the two divine dispositions the whole portrait-pair exists to display: the enough of judgment and the raʾfah of grace, each rendered to the heart that chose it. And the name binds the sale to the community: raʾūf bi'l-ʿibād, kind to the servants — plural — so that every seller in every generation sells into the same tenderness, and the bargain ʿAlī made in the Prophet's bed is open, at the same price, to whoever will lie down.

**The Diptych Complete**

The four verses now stand as one map of the heart's two economies. The first panel: the man whose speech about this world impresses, who calls God to witness a heart at war, who strives corruption in the land when he turns away, who is seized by pride into sin at the counsel to fear God, and for whom Hell suffices. The second panel: the man who sells his soul for God's pleasure — in the enjoiner's sentence, in the emigrant's bed, in the spender's emptied purse — received by the Kindness of God. Between the panels lies the hajj-legislation the diptych follows: the pilgrim who left the valley remembering God in numbered days, flexible in his options, mindful in his heart, gathered in his knowledge, walks now between the two figures and must recognize which one he has been becoming through the rite. The sūrah's method is visible in the whole arrangement — the law builds the community's shape, and the portraits test its heart — and the test this diptych administers is the market-question the sūrah has asked from its first pages: what is your nafs spending itself on, and who is receiving it? The eloquent self ends in sufficiency; the sold self ends in raʾfah; and the verse leaves no third buyer standing.
