## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:112

> He responded, “And what knowledge do I have of what they do?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah Refuses to Pretend He Knows Hidden Motives**

Noah answers the chiefs by asking what knowledge he has of what his followers do. His question is not an admission that he knows nothing about them. He knows their public lives and can see their commitment to the message. He refuses a different claim: that he possesses the hidden knowledge needed to declare their inner motives or final standing.

The chiefs want him to treat their contempt as evidence. Noah will not become a judge of hearts merely to satisfy their social standard. His task is to convey God's call and deal with people according to what is known. What they conceal, intend, or may become belongs to God.

The chiefs imply that poor followers must have secret material motives. Perhaps, they suggest, these people follow Noah to gain something rather than from sincere faith. Noah does not accept the accusation simply because it comes from influential men. He knows that social position cannot reveal the truth of a person's heart.

Neither does he claim that every follower is flawless. A believer can act wrongly and still be a believer who needs correction. Noah's point is that he cannot condemn the whole group through an unverified suspicion. Judgment must rest on knowledge, not on the chiefs' assumption that the lowly are incapable of spiritual conviction.

**Knowledge Has Moral Limits**

Noah's question models intellectual humility. A leader may know enough to teach, warn, and organize a community, while lacking knowledge of every person's inner reason. Recognizing that limit protects people from being judged by confident guesses. It also protects the leader from turning authority into an excuse for intrusion.

This humility does not paralyze action. Noah can respond to public wrongdoing and can call people toward faith. He simply leaves hidden intentions to the Lord. The distinction between known actions and unknown motives allows a community to uphold justice without pretending to possess God's complete knowledge.

The source tradition gives another reading of Noah's reply: he may be saying that he is not responsible for deciphering what his followers previously did or why they came. His duty is the message, not a speculative account of every biography. The reply therefore keeps the prophetic task focused.

A community can waste itself by demanding that a teacher explain every person's past before allowing anyone to belong. Noah refuses that burden when it is used to exclude believers. People can repent and change. Their present response to God cannot be dismissed because a chief invents a story about their motives.

**God Knows What Noah Does Not**

Noah's answer points toward God's knowledge without using ignorance as an insult. He does not say that the believers' actions are unimportant. He says that he does not have the knowledge the chiefs pretend to possess. God alone sees the complete record: former conduct, present repentance, hidden intention, and future persistence.

This arrangement gives the community a sound division of responsibility. Noah teaches and warns. Believers strive to live faithfully. The Lord judges the unseen and the final account. When human beings exchange these roles, they either become arrogant judges or careless people who refuse all accountability. Noah's measured reply avoids both errors.

Quran 6:52 instructs the Prophet not to dismiss poor believers who invoke their Lord morning and evening. That parallel confirms the principle at stake in Noah's response. The worth of a believer is not determined by the demand of chiefs who want religious space reserved for themselves.

The parallel also clarifies that no messenger is accountable for hidden worth in the way God is. The believers are responsible for their deeds, but the messenger is not required to expel them because elites suspect them. Revelation protects the poor from becoming a price paid for the powerful's attention.

Evidence concerns what happened and what can be responsibly known. Prejudice begins when a group is assigned motives because of its class, appearance, or reputation. The chiefs offer prejudice in the language of evaluation. Noah answers by asking whether they can actually know the claim they are making.

This is a discipline for every community. Ask what is known, what is inferred, and what is merely repeated because it protects status. The absence of knowledge does not require treating every person as innocent of every act, but it does forbid declaring a whole group unworthy without proof.

**Noah's Question Protects the Message From Elitism**

If Noah accepted the chiefs' suspicion, the message would become dependent on their approval. He would have to certify the poor according to a standard set by people who had not accepted God's authority. His reply keeps the call open to anyone who responds sincerely.

The question therefore preserves both justice and humility. Noah cannot see all that God sees, but he can refuse to make ignorance a reason for exclusion. The believers are not measured by the rank assigned to them by the chiefs. They are left under God's knowledge, where truth about their deeds and intentions will be judged correctly.

**The Wise Leader Knows What Belongs to God**

Noah's answer is a lesson in responsible leadership. He does not claim omniscience, yet he does not abandon his duty. He admits the limit of his knowledge, protects believers from prejudice, and leaves ultimate reckoning with the Lord. Such leadership is strong because it is not forced to pretend.

A community becomes safer when its leaders speak with this accuracy. They can address visible harm, welcome sincere repentance, and refrain from declaring hidden motives as facts. Noah's question stands against the arrogance that thinks social power gives knowledge of hearts.
