## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:48

> the Lord of Moses and Aaron.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Magicians Identify the Universal Lord Through His Messengers**

The magicians complete their confession by naming the Lord of Moses and Aaron. They do not leave the universal title at the level of an unnamed power. The signs have shown them that the Lord proclaimed by the brothers is the Lord of all worlds. This identification ties divine authority to the messengers who brought the claim without confusing the messengers with God.

The wording also confirms that Moses and Aaron speak together under one source. Pharaoh had tried to reduce their mission to a personal disturbance, but the magicians now recognize a shared commission. Their confession validates the relation between sign and messenger. What they saw did not point to a force detached from a message. It authenticated the Lord who sent these two servants.

**Naming Moses and Aaron Rejects Pharaoh's Interpretation**

Pharaoh called Moses a magician and implied that Moses' power came from a human teacher. The magicians' confession rejects that interpretation at its root. They do not say that Moses has mastered a superior school of magic. They call God the Lord of Moses and Aaron, placing the brothers on the side of servanthood and Pharaoh on the side of false authority.

This is a subtle but decisive reversal. The court had arranged the contest so that the magicians would stand as experts and Moses would be judged as a performer. The magicians now testify that Moses is a messenger whose Lord is above their craft. Their expertise does not make them rivals of Moses; it makes them witnesses to what distinguishes his sign from their practice.

**The Two Names Establish a Shared Mission**

Moses and Aaron are mentioned together because their work is joined. The earlier request for Aaron was not an attempt to create two competing authorities. It was a request for support in delivering one message. The magicians' words confirm that the brothers' difference in role does not divide the source of their mission.

This shared identification also prevents the court from isolating Moses. Pharaoh might attack Moses' history or speech, but Aaron stands with him as another witness. A message carried by two brothers is not validated merely by the number of speakers, yet their unity makes clear that the signs and proclamation are not the invention of one distressed man. The court has encountered a divinely ordered partnership.

**Lordship Is More Important Than the Messenger's Personality**

The confession names the Lord first and the messengers as those to whom He is related. The order preserves proper worship. Moses and Aaron deserve respect as messengers, but the magicians do not prostrate to them or call them lords. They identify the One whose authority the brothers carry.

This is a safeguard against replacing one ruler with another. Pharaoh has made himself the object of allegiance, while the magicians now place him under God's lordship. Moses' signs do not invite a cult of Moses. They invite recognition of God. The phrase Lord of Moses and Aaron makes that hierarchy plain: the brothers are honored by being servants, and their honor does not make them divine.

**The Confession Comes From Those Who Know the Difference**

The magicians' background gives the statement unusual force. They understand the methods of their own profession and have seen the contrast between their objects and Moses' staff. When they identify the Lord of Moses and Aaron, they are not speaking from ignorance of the contest. They are giving the judgment of people who know what their craft can and cannot do.

Their expertise also makes the court's later accusation harder to defend. Pharaoh may insist that Moses taught them a trick, but the magicians have just publicly separated their work from his sign. Their conclusion costs them the reward and exposes them to punishment. A witness who accepts danger to state what he knows is not easily dismissed as a hired partisan.

**The Name of the Messengers Connects Faith to History**

The magicians do not believe in an abstract principle detached from time. They believe in the Lord who has acted through Moses and Aaron in a particular encounter. Faith includes recognition of divine action in history. The Lord's universal sovereignty has entered a court, addressed a ruler, and displayed a sign through two servants.

That historical connection protects belief from becoming vague admiration. The magicians have a reason for their confession: the sign, the message, and the identity of the messengers fit together. Their faith is not built on a general feeling that some higher power exists. It is a response to what God has made clear through His chosen servants.

**The Court Cannot Separate the Sign From Its Source**

Pharaoh wants the people to see an isolated performance and judge it by human categories. The magicians' confession restores the missing relation. The sign belongs to the Lord, and Moses and Aaron are His messengers. Once that relation is acknowledged, the court's political story loses its power. The event is no longer about which group of performers controls Egypt's future.

It is about whether the ruler will accept that he stands under a Lord greater than himself. The magicians have answered for themselves. Their completed sentence leaves Pharaoh facing a new problem: the people he hired have become witnesses to the authority he sought to deny.

Their wording also teaches the right relation between evidence and worship. They do not stop at admiration for Moses or at fear of Pharaoh's failure. They identify the Lord who stands behind the sign and who sent the brothers. Recognition becomes faith when the witness follows the sign to its true source and accepts the authority that source carries.
