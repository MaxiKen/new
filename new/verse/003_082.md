## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:82

> Whoever turns back after this, they will be the rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Consequence of the Covenant**

The verse follows directly from the undertaking reported a line earlier, in which the prophets accepted an obligation and God took witnesses to it. Whoever turns back after that agreement, the verse says, belongs among the rebellious.

The sentence is short because its work has been done by the verse before it. The reader has just been shown a ratified covenant with witnesses on both sides; what remains to be stated is what the refusal of a ratified agreement amounts to. The Book's choice of the word for that condition is worth attention, since it is not a word for doubt or error. The term describes a departure from a state of obedience, and it is used throughout the Book for those who leave the way their own undertaking had set out for them.

**What Turning Back Means**

The Arabic describes a turning away and a reversal of course, and the Book uses the same root for apostasy, for the refusal of a summons, and for the conduct of a community that agrees to something and then abandons it.

The word therefore covers conduct rather than a mental state, which fits the verse's setting. The covenant was to believe in and support a messenger; turning back from it is not the failure to reach a conclusion but the abandonment of an undertaking. The Book's legal vocabulary is consistent on this point: an agreement obliges the one who accepted it, and the seriousness of a breach is proportionate to the standing of the party who was given the assurance. A covenant witnessed by God is the most serious kind, and the description of the violator follows from that fact rather than from any additional accusation.

**The Rebels as a Category in the Book**

The Book applies the term to several groups, and its range is instructive. It describes those who refuse what God has sent, those who set aside the obligations they accepted, and those who turn away after having seen the truth.

The consistent element in each case is the presence of something accepted earlier and then left. The Book does not use the word for people who never received a message, and it does not use it for those whose knowledge was too slight to oblige them. That is why the verse's placement matters so much: it comes after a covenant described in detail, with the acceptance and the witnesses reported, so that the rebellion in view is not a departure from an unfamiliar position but the abandonment of one the parties had formally taken up. The reader who has followed the passage from the story of Abraham to this sentence has been shown exactly what the obligation was and how it was contracted.

**Why the Verse Is Not Addressed to the Prophets Alone**

Read strictly, the covenant was made with the prophets, who by the Book's own account would not have turned back. The commentators resolved the apparent difficulty by noting that the obligation passes to the communities that inherit each mission.

The point is not a technical one. The Book treats the religious history it describes as a series of communities bound by what their messengers accepted on their behalf, which is why the sūrah can address the People of the Book about a covenant they themselves did not personally negotiate. A community's identity in the Book is corporate in exactly this sense: it inherits obligations, and it is judged by its fidelity to them. What the verse states is the consequence for the heirs when the terms are abandoned.

**The Severity and Its Source**

The language of the verse is severe, and the severity is drawn from the agreement it follows rather than from the persons of those who break it. That distinction is worth holding onto, because the passage as a whole has been careful to distinguish among the members of the communities it addresses.

The sūrah has already noted that a deposit may be returned faithfully by one member of a community and withheld by another, and it will shortly say in explicit terms that they are not all alike. The verse above states a rule about those who turn back, and the qualification runs through the surrounding verses: the Book names conduct and states its outcome without converting the statement into a verdict on an entire people. The next verse raises the question of what a person leaving such a covenant is actually looking for.

**A Sentence That Looks Forward**

The verse ends with the description of the rebels and leaves the reader at the edge of a question. If a person had a covenant and abandoned it, what destination did he think he was heading toward?

That question is taken up immediately in the following verse, which asks whether such people desire a way other than God's while everything in the heavens and the earth is in submission to Him. The two verses belong together, and the second one provides the scale against which the abandonment in the first is measured. What the reader receives from the pair is a definition of the offence in terms of its object: it is not simply a broken promise between people but a refusal of the arrangement in which everything that exists already participates.

The word chosen for the people who turn back also keeps the Book's account of human conduct consistent. Nobody in this passage is described as having been defeated by an argument; the failure named is a departure from a position that had been taken up with the parties' own agreement and God's testimony. The sūrah has used the vocabulary of turning away several times in the course of its argument with the same communities, and the term chosen here describes the end of that process rather than a single moment within it.
