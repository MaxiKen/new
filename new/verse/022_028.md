## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:28

> so they may obtain the benefits ˹in store˺ for them, and pronounce the Name of Allah on appointed days over the sacrificial animals He has provided for them. So eat from their meat and feed the desperately poor.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"That They May Witness Benefits for Themselves"**

*Li-yashhadū manāfiʿa lahum* — so that they may witness benefits belonging to them. The purpose of the pilgrimage is stated before any rite is described, and the word used is *manāfiʿ*, benefits: the ordinary term for what is useful and advantageous.

The commentators were explicit that the benefits are of both kinds. Al-Ṭabarī took the phrase as covering the trade and the markets that gather around the pilgrimage, and the attainment of God's good pleasure and reward in the Hereafter. Both are in the word, and nothing in the verse requires a choice between them.

That inclusion surprises readers who expect a religious gathering to be set apart from ordinary commerce, and the surprise is the point. The pilgrimage is not described as a withdrawal from ordinary life but as a place where ordinary life is transacted under better conditions: people meet, buy, sell, settle disputes, marry, and earn — and all of it is called a benefit they were summoned to witness.

**The Pilgrimage Is Not a Retreat From Commerce**

The Qur'an's willingness to name economic activity as part of the rite distinguishes it from the assumption that holiness requires distance from the market.

Sūrah al-Baqarah makes the same point from the other direction, in a verse that defends trade during the pilgrimage against those who considered it sinful. The logic of both texts is identical: what would corrupt a gathering is not buying and selling but the loss of the remembrance that frames them. The pilgrim is commanded to remember God *during the appointed days* (2:203), which presupposes that he is doing something else during those days from which the remembrance must not distract him — or rather, which the remembrance must pervade.

The practical consequence is a religion with no monastic withdrawal in it. The holiest days of the Islamic year are also, by this verse's own account, days of lawful gain.

**"Known Days"**

*Wa-yadhkurū isma Allāhi fī ayyāmin maʿlūmātin* — and mention the name of God during days that are known. The phrase is definite in the Arabic: days that are *known*, meaning identified, fixed, and established by the practice rather than specified here.

That is why the commentators had to supply the identification from elsewhere, and why they disagreed. Some took the known days as the first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, the month in which the pilgrimage falls. Others took them as the days of *tashrīq*, the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth of the same month, which are named in Sūrah al-Baqarah: *“And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺, so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺”* (2:203).

The disagreement is not over doctrine but over which span the rite occupies, and both readings place the remembrance inside a defined window. The point of the phrase is that the religion is not left to private timing: there are days for this, and they are known.

**"Over What He Provided Them"**

*ʿAlā mā razaqahum min bahīmati al-anʿāmi* — over what He provided them of the grazing livestock. Two features of the phrasing deserve attention.

The first is the verb: *razaqa*, to provide. The animal is described as provision before it is described as property. A person who slaughters a sheep at the pilgrimage is not disposing of his own asset; he is returning a gift to its giver, and the verse's vocabulary keeps that relationship in view. Ownership language would have made the sacrifice a transaction. Provision language makes it a response.

The second is the restriction to *bahīmat al-anʿām*, the grazing livestock — camels, cattle, sheep and goats. The sacrifice is drawn from the domestic animals that a household depends on, not from game or from anything wild. What is offered is what a person lives by.

**"So Eat of It"**

*Fa-kulū minhā* — so eat of it. The command is in the imperative, and it follows directly on the sacrifice.

The background explains the force of it. Pre-Islamic practice among some of the Arab groups included a refusal to eat the meat of animals they had slaughtered themselves for sacrifice, treating it as given over. The verse overturns that: the sacrificer eats.

The ruling is permission rather than obligation, as the commentators note — the pilgrim may eat and is not required to — but the permission is itself the point. A religion in which the worshipper consumes part of his own offering is making a claim about the offering: it is not a payment to a hungry deity, and it is not destruction for its own sake. It is a meal.

**"And Feed the Poor and Wretched"**

*Wa-aṭʿimū al-bāʾisa al-faqīra* — and feed the wretched, the poor. Two nouns where one would have served, and they are not synonyms.

*Al-bāʾis* is the one overcome by hardship, the person whose distress shows; *al-faqīr* is the one without means. The first describes a condition that has befallen someone, the second a standing lack. Together they name the person who is both afflicted and broke — the pilgrim's opposite number, since the person standing at the sacrifice has arrived with an animal to slaughter and the recipient has nothing.

This is why the command is attached to the sacrifice rather than placed alongside it as a separate charity. The animal is killed, the family eats, and the destitute eat from the same animal on the same day. The rite produces the meal that feeds them.

**The Two Halves of a Single Act**

Taken together, the last two clauses of the verse describe one action with two outcomes: eating and feeding. Neither is optional in the verse's rhetoric, and neither makes sense without the other.

A sacrifice from which only the sacrificer eats is a dinner. A sacrifice from which only the poor eat is a donation. What the verse describes is a single table at which both sit, which is why the pilgrimage's central act is neither a private devotion nor a general alms. It is a meal shared by the person who could afford the animal and the person who could not, on days fixed by God, over an animal described as His provision.
