## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:52

> They will ˹then˺ cry, “We do ˹now˺ believe in it ˹all˺.” But how could they ˹possibly˺ attain faith from a place so far-off ˹from the world˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words That Come Too Late**

*Wa qālū āmannā bih* — and they said, we believe in it. The declaration is the shortest possible statement of faith and it is grammatically flawless; nothing in the wording is defective. What has failed is the timing. The Qur'an does not accuse them of insincerity here, and the verse's reply does not suggest they are lying. They mean it. That is what makes the scene disturbing rather than merely just: belief has finally arrived, genuine and complete, at the one moment when it can accomplish nothing. The sūrah has recorded these same voices confidently denying that the Hour would reach them (34:3), mocking the claim of resurrection to a gathered crowd (34:7), and announcing that they would never believe in this Qur'an (34:31). The last of those was a *lan* — never. The never has expired.

**Belief That Is No Longer a Choice**

The reason the declaration fails is not arbitrary and the Qur'an supplies it consistently. Faith, in the Book's understanding, is trust extended to what has not been seen — which is why the believers are described at the opening of al-Baqarah as those who believe in the unseen. Once the unseen becomes visible, the act being performed is no longer faith but acknowledgement, and acknowledgement of a fact standing in front of you is not a moral achievement. The whole arrangement described earlier in this sūrah depended on that distinction: the world exists as a place where belief and doubt can become visible precisely because the outcome is not yet on display (34:21). A test that continues after the answers are published is not a test. What these speakers are offering is the right answer read off the board.

**"From a Place Far Off"**

*Wa annā lahum al-tanāwushu min makānin baʿīd* — how can they attain it from a distant place? The noun *tanāwush* means reaching for something, grasping at it, taking hold — a word with the physical sense of stretching out a hand. The image is of people reaching back toward something now out of range. The distance in question is not spatial. Faith was available in the life they have just left, at close quarters, cheaply; it is now being grasped at from the far side of death, and the reaching cannot span the gap. The rhetorical question *annā lahum*, how can they, is the Qur'an's standard form for an impossibility rather than a difficulty. Nothing is being refused to them out of severity. The thing they want is located somewhere they no longer are.

**The Distance That Reverses the Previous Verse**

One verse earlier they were seized *from a nearby place* (34:51); now they reach for faith *from a distant place*. The two phrases are set one line apart, using the paired opposites *qarīb* and *baʿīd*, and the reversal is the sūrah's most economical statement of their situation. Judgment reached them instantly, across no distance at all. The remedy is at a distance they cannot cross. This is exactly the inversion of their position during life, when the punishment seemed impossibly remote — they demanded a date for it and treated the absence of one as proof (34:29) — while repentance was available at any hour. The relative positions of the two have swapped, and nothing they do now affects either.

**Other Scenes of the Same Late Petition**

The Qur'an returns to this moment often and the request is always refused. Al-Sajdah shows the guilty with their heads bowed: *"Our Lord! We have now seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good. We truly have sure faith ˹now˺!"* (32:12). Ghāfir has them admitting their sins in the Fire. Al-Munāfiqūn records the wish of the man who dies holding his wealth: *"My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous"* (63:10). In every case the petition is well-formed, sincere, and unavailing. The consistency across these passages establishes it as a rule rather than a judgment on particular individuals. What all the petitioners have in common is that they are asking for a return to conditions under which their new conviction would have counted.

**What "It" Refers To**

The object of their belated belief is left as a bare pronoun — *āmannā bih*, we believe in it — and the Arabic does not say what *it* is. The candidates are all present in the sūrah. One is the truth, which the preceding verses described as thrown and landing (34:48–49). Another is the Qur'an, which they had sworn never to believe in (34:31). A third is the Hour itself, denied in the chapter's third verse and mocked in its seventh. A fourth is the Prophet, whom they called a man with a motive. Nothing forces a choice, and the ambiguity is probably the point: at that moment every one of these has become undeniable simultaneously, and a single pronoun covers the whole collapse. A man who has just been seized from the grave does not distinguish between the messenger, the message and the event. They arrive as one fact.

**What the Verse Asks of a Living Reader**

The scene is placed in the sūrah's final movement for an obvious reason, and it is aimed at people for whom the door is still open. Everything these speakers now believe was available to them at no cost the previous week, and the only difference between them and the sūrah's audience is a date. This is why the chapter ends here rather than with the arguments, the histories or the demolition of the idols. An argument can be answered and a history can be discounted; a scene of people who agree with you too late cannot be argued with at all. The following verse explains why the refusal is not merely a matter of timing but a consequence of how they had treated the unseen all along.
