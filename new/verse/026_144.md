## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:144

> So fear Allah, and obey me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Trust Must Become Obedience**

Ṣāliḥ has said that he is a trustworthy messenger. This verse states what the people should do with that claim: fear Allah and obey the messenger. The command does not ask Thamûd merely to respect Ṣāliḥ's honesty. It asks the community to let his guidance govern its choices.

The order keeps the messenger's role clear. Reverence belongs first to Allah, while obedience to Ṣāliḥ follows because he conveys God's command. The people do not replace direct worship of the Lord with attachment to a human leader. They follow the messenger as an act of submission to the One who sent him.


Fear of Allah gives obedience its right motive. A person may comply with a leader because of force, custom, or the promise of reward. Taqwa is different. It makes the servant aware that God sees the hidden intention and the public action. Obedience then grows from reverence rather than from fear of social consequences alone.

This inner foundation matters for Thamûd because its outward prosperity can hide inward independence. The people may obey one another's customs while refusing the Lord. Ṣāliḥ calls them to a deeper loyalty, one that remains present even when no human authority is watching.

**Obedience Reorders a Prosperous Society**

Thamûd's resources are not condemned simply because they are abundant. Obedience changes what abundance means. Land, crops, homes, and water become trusts instead of proof that the community may rule without limits. The strong must restrain themselves, and the community must protect those who can be overlooked.

A society can repeat the words of submission while preserving the same injustice. Genuine obedience reaches its institutions and habits. It influences whose claims are heard, how shared goods are used, and whether the powerful are allowed to turn prosperity into a license for harm.


Ṣāliḥ's command does not establish a rival kingdom for himself. He calls the people to Allah, not to his own name. A true messenger's authority is inseparable from his refusal to collect worship, wealth, or absolute loyalty for his person.

That distinction also protects obedience from becoming tribal politics. Thamûd is not asked to exchange one human master for another. It is asked to submit to God and to accept Ṣāliḥ's guidance only within that submission. The messenger's strength comes from the message, not from personal domination.

**A Repeated Prophetic Formula Carries Continuity**

The same call was directed to Noah and Hūd in the preceding accounts. Their peoples heard a combination of reverence and obedience because the core of prophetic guidance is stable. Thamûd cannot claim that Ṣāliḥ has invented a new demand simply because it challenges its own practices.

Quran 7:73 presents Ṣāliḥ calling Thamûd to worship Allah alone and identifying a clear proof from their Lord. That parallel shows the command is attached to a call to divine oneness, not to blind loyalty. Obedience to Ṣāliḥ is meaningful because it leads the people away from idols and back to God.


To follow Ṣāliḥ means more than attending to his speech. It includes stopping when the messenger identifies a boundary. Later, the community will face a test involving a protected sign and a shared resource. The present command establishes the principle before that test arrives: God's instruction must control appetite and power.

This is a general measure of obedience. A person who claims to follow revelation but refuses every limit has followed his desire instead. Taqwa becomes visible when a person can leave something alone even though he has the ability to take it.

**The Command Is Mercy, Not Humiliation**

Thamûd may hear obedience as a loss of status. Ṣāliḥ presents it as the way to preserve the community from its own pride. A people is not diminished by recognizing its Creator. It is freed from having to defend the illusion that its achievements make it independent.

The command also offers an honorable path for leaders. They can use their authority to implement justice rather than surrendering to the appetites of the strongest. Obedience to God does not erase responsibility; it gives responsibility a standard higher than popularity.


Ṣāliḥ's sentence is direct because the decision is direct. Either Thamûd will allow reverence for Allah to govern its conduct, or it will continue treating custom and ability as final authorities. The people cannot claim to honor the messenger while keeping his command outside the areas that matter most.

For the reader, the verse asks whether obedience is selective. God is feared truly when His guidance reaches wealth, family, public decisions, and private desire. Ṣāliḥ's call is simple, but it demands a whole life: fear Allah, then follow the messenger who faithfully brings His word.

The command also gives a standard for evaluating claims of religious leadership. A guide who asks for obedience while directing praise, wealth, and fear toward himself has reversed the order of Ṣāliḥ's words. The authentic messenger makes God's supremacy clearer and his own status smaller. His obedience is not a path into human domination but an escape from it.

In daily life, this means that reverence must survive when no dramatic sign is being discussed. It appears in fair contracts, careful use of land, restraint in conflict, and willingness to stop at a revealed limit. Thamûd is not being asked for a momentary gesture. It is being asked to let the command become the shape of its common life.

That common life includes the way a community responds when obedience costs it an advantage. Anyone can praise submission while the command agrees with existing interests. The test comes when truth limits a profitable custom or protects someone without influence. Fear of Allah gives the people a reason to accept that loss without treating justice as defeat.
