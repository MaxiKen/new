## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:132

> This was the advice of Abraham—as well as Jacob—to his children, ˹saying˺, “Indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith; so do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Bequest**

The verse records the patriarchs' final act as fathers, and the act is a genre the Qur'an treats with standing seriousness: the *waṣiyya*, the deathbed counsel — *this was the advice of Abraham, and Jacob likewise, to his children*. Two names, two generations-apart scenes, one instruction: the verse joins the millah's founder with the Israel-line's father — Jacob, grandson of Abraham, the patriarch whose twelve sons became the tribes the recital's hundred verses addressed — and joins them in the same act of transmission. The pairing is the verse's ecumenical stroke: the father claimed by the Arabs and the father claimed by Israel are shown doing one thing, saying one sentence, to their children — and the sentence is not about lineage, land, or precedence but about the faith's survival in the sons. The waṣiyya's solemnity in the recital's culture is its setting: the last words, spoken at the edge of the world's business, when the speaker's remaining property is truth. What the patriarchs spend that property on is the verse's ranking of goods — not estates, not offices, not the covenant's external marks, but the orientation of the children's souls. The tradition reads the bequest as the prophets' standing practice, the submission-pose of verse 131 transmitted at every prophet's deathbed to every prophet's house, and the two named here stand as the pattern's twin exemplars: the millah's founder and the tribes' father, the root and the trunk, both closing their files with the same charge.

**“Allah Has Chosen This Faith for You”**

The counsel's first clause states the children's position, and the statement is grace before demand: *indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith*. The verb is the iṣṭifāʾ of verse 124 and verse 130 — the divine selection, the same word used of the patriarch's own choosing — now applied to the religion itself as the children's portion. The clause's theology is precise in its prepositions: the faith is chosen *for you*, *by Allah* — the religion arrives as gift before it stands as task, the selection divine before the holding is human. The verse thereby installs the correct order of the religious life: the children are not told *choose this faith* but *this faith has been chosen for you* — their freedom operates inside a prior grace, the way the patriarch's own submission was commanded before it was answered (131). The named object — *this faith*, the millah in its submission-form — is the religion verse 131 defined: islām as the primordial orientation, the universal pose, what the prophets bequeath. And the choosing's agent answers the millah-contest one final time from inside the family scene: the faith the patriarchs transmit is not a tribal inheritance but a divine selection — held by the children not because their fathers held it but because Allah chose it for them, a ground that stands for every generation the choice reaches and falls away for every claim that cites the fathers alone. The counsel's first clause is thus the recital's whole doctrine of election in one sentence: chosen by Allah, chosen for the children, chosen as this — the submission the dialogue of verse 131 enacted.

**“Do Not Die Except in Submission”**

The counsel's second clause is its famous imperative, and its form is the verse's most striking feature: *so do not die except in a state of full submission*. Death is not chosen — no one commands the hour of his dying — and the command therefore addresses what the hour arrives to find: the state, not the event. The imperative is a command about living whose term is set at dying: arrange the whole of life so that death, whenever it comes, comes upon a submitter. The tradition named the doctrine this verse legislates — the good ending, ḥusn al-khātima — and its logic is the recital's own anthropology: the state at death is the sum of the life's direction, the heart's last posture continuous with its practiced posture, and the one who lives submitting dies submitting by the same necessity that verse 93 filed in the other direction: the heart made to drink its loves carries them past the boundary. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān legislates the identical clause to the Muslim community directly: be mindful of Allah as He deserves, *and do not die except in submission* (3:102) — the patriarchs' deathbed counsel converted into the ummah's standing law, which is the verse's proof that the waṣiyya was never addressed to one family. The command's severity is also its mercy: it makes every moment continuous with the last one, so that the deathbed requires no emergency conversion — the submitter arrives at death already in the state death is commanded to find. Between the two clauses the counsel's whole architecture stands: the faith given by selection, the life ordered toward the exit — gift and task, grace and vigilance, in the two sentences a dying father spent his last words on.

**Fathers, Children, and the Recital's Own File**

The verse's scene reads against the file the recital has just closed, and the contrast is its polemical edge. The Children of Israel are the children of this counsel — Jacob's sons and their descendants, addressed in the second person across a hundred verses — and the record between the bequest and the recital's present documents what children can do with a father's last words: the covenant cast at every station (100), the Book behind the backs (101), the hear-and-disobey (93). The verse does not draw the contrast explicitly; its structure draws it. The bequest was total — the faith chosen, the exit commanded — and the transmission's failure was therefore not the fathers' but the children's, which is exactly the covenant-law of verse 124 operating in narrative: the office and the promise do not ride the blood; each generation receives the counsel and answers it individually. The pairing of Abraham with Jacob sharpens the point across both lines: the Arabs' father and Israel's father gave the same charge, so neither line's children can plead a defective bequest — the counsel was perfect on both sides of the family, and the audit of verse 123's individualism runs on the children's answers alone. The verse thereby closes the recital's long argument with the claimants of the patriarchs: you hold the bequest's text and dispute its content; the content is submission, the exit-command is plain, and the ancestors named are witnesses against every child who received the waṣiyya and died in another posture. The counsel stands unrevoked in both houses, which is why the verse can state it once for two fathers: the millah's transmission has one wording, and the wording is the same at every deathbed the recital files.

**The Counsel to Every Child**

The verse's transfer is in its address: every reader is somebody's child, and the waṣiyya reaches whoever inherits a faith. The counsel's two clauses are the inheritance's two questions. Has the faith come to you as chosen — received as selection before it is held as identity, the gift-order of verse 128's petition intact? And is your life arranged toward the commanded exit — the daily posture continuous with the deathbed posture, the submission practiced at the scale of hours so that it stands at the scale of the end? The recital's audience heard both questions with their own bequest in hand: the Qur'an itself is a waṣiyya to its hearers, this sūrah's hundred-verses-plus of covenant, audit, and patriarch-file the counsel's elaboration, and the Messenger ﷺ raised from the prayed-for nation (129) its living transmitter. The verse installs the community's self-understanding as children of a bequest — not owners of a property but heirs of a charge, with the charge's terms stated: the faith is chosen for you, so do not die except in submission. Between the two patriarchs' deathbeds and the recital's Madinan moment, the counsel travels unchanged, which is the verse's final proof of the millah's identity: what Abraham said to his children and Jacob said to his, the sūrah says to its — one sentence, three generations of speakers, one posture required of every hearer, and the exit-command running until the Day verse 123 described, where the states the deaths found are filed, and no soul arrives except as it lived.
