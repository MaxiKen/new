## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:132

> This was the advice of Abraham—as well as Jacob—to his children, ˹saying˺, “Indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith; so do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Bequest Rather Than a Command**

The verse describes what Abraham and Jacob gave their children, and the Arabic word for advice carries the sense of a charge left by someone who expects to be gone. What the two prophets passed on was not a piece of information but an instruction about how to live and how to die.

The sūrah has presented Abraham's faith as a submission and his obedience as an example, and it now shows him handling the same legacy to the next generation. The verse therefore supplies the link between the founder of the line and the descendants whose claims the passage has been addressing. The Book's use of the term for a charge also indicates the expectation that the speaker would not be present to see it carried out. What Abraham and Jacob left was a standard for their children to keep when the parents were gone, and the same concern runs through the sūrah's account of the community that received the later revelation.

**Jacob Included in the Advice**

The mention of Jacob in the same sentence is important for the argument, since the People of the Book traced their descent through him, and the sūrah is showing that he carried the same religion as Abraham. The Book treats the patriarchs as bearers of one position rather than as founders of separate traditions.

Jacob appears elsewhere in the Book as the father of Joseph and as one of the prophets named in the list that the sūrah gives later, and the story told in the chapter of Joseph presents him as a man of patience and trust. The verse here identifies him with the same submission his grandfather had practised, and the following verse records his final words on the subject.

**"Allah Has Chosen for You This Faith"**

The phrase states that the religion came to the children as a selection made by God rather than as an inheritance they had earned. The sūrah has used the language of choosing for Abraham himself, and the advice here applies the same idea to the descendants in a form that makes the gift a responsibility.

The Book's treatment of chosenness in the passage is consistent: what is chosen is chosen for a purpose, and the receiving community is answerable for what it does with it. The advice therefore opens with a statement about God's action and closes with an instruction about the recipients' conduct. The clause also answers any assumption that the religion was inherited automatically. The Book presents it as a gift chosen by God and entrusted to the children, which is the same understanding of provision the sūrah has been setting out in its treatment of the community's favours.

**"Do Not Die Except in Submission"**

The final clause instructs the children about the state in which they should leave the world, and the wording presupposes that death can arrive at any moment while the condition of a person's life is what determines what he dies in. The instruction is therefore about perseverance rather than about a deathbed formula, and the commentators read it as a command to remain in submission until the end.

The Book makes the same point elsewhere when it tells the believers to be mindful of God as He deserves and to die only in that state. The verse in this place presents the instruction as the content of a prophet's bequest to his children, and the community that reads the sūrah is addressed by it as the descendants of the people it describes.

**The Advice to Children**

The Book's accounts of the prophets regularly include their concern for their families and descendants, and the sūrah has already recorded Abraham's prayer for a nation from his line and his request for a messenger among them. The advice in this verse continues the same theme in the form of a direct instruction rather than a petition.

The commentators noted the general principle that a person's household has the first claim on his religious concern, and the Book elsewhere instructs the believers to protect themselves and their families from the Fire. What the verse records is the practice of two prophets carrying that duty out at the level of their own children, and the wording leaves the reader with the model. The passage also shows the prophets doing what the Book commands the believers to do with their households. What the verse records is not a formal testament but the ordinary concern of a parent for the religion of his children, and the model applies to every reader who holds the same trust.

**A Testimony to Universal Submission**

The passage is one of the places the community's scholars cite for the universal meaning of submission in the Book, since the term is used of Abraham, of his son, of Jacob, and of their descendants before the final revelation. The religion being described in the sūrah is presented as the original one, and the bequest in this verse belongs to the chain of transmission by which it was carried.

The verse also supplies the answer to the claims that the sūrah has been examining. The communities that traced themselves to Abraham and Jacob had received the same advice, according to the Book, and the passage that follows records the words in which Jacob's children answered their dying father, so that the reader hears the confession of the descendants in their own voice.

**The Last Instruction of a Prophet**

The sentence records what a prophet chose to leave his children, and the Book returns to the same theme at the death of Jacob in the following verse. Two generations are shown closing their lives with the same concern, and the repetition is what gives the passage its force: the religion was transmitted as a charge from parent to child before it became a subject of dispute among communities. The content of the charge is short. God has chosen the religion for them, and they are to die in submission. The instruction assumes that death can come at any time and that the state in which a person leaves the world is what matters, and it therefore concerns perseverance rather than a final moment. The Book presents the bequest as the settled conviction of the prophets about what their families needed, and the community that reads the passage is addressed as the household that inherited the same charge from the same line.
