## Sūrah an-Naml 27:66

> No! Their knowledge of the Hereafter amounts to ignorance. In fact, they are in doubt about it. In truth, they are ˹totally˺ blind to it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Statements That Get Worse**

The verse describes one group three times, and each description is more severe than the one before. Their knowledge amounts to nothing. They are in doubt. They are blind. Arabic marks the escalation with *bal* before each clause, a particle that cancels the previous statement and replaces it with something stronger.

That structure is the verse's argument. It begins by addressing them as people who claim knowledge about the Hereafter — the previous verse had them demanding a date for the resurrection — and it strips the claim in stages. First the knowledge is emptied, then the emptiness is named as doubt, then the doubt is named as an inability to see at all. By the last clause the starting position has been dismantled entirely. Someone who began the passage asking a confident question ends it described as sightless.

**A Verb the Reciters Read Differently**

The opening phrase is *bal iddāraka ʿilmuhum fī al-ākhirah*, and the classical readers vocalised the consonants in more than one way, producing genuinely different senses. Al-Rāzī sets out the options.

Read as a question, it becomes a reproach: has their knowledge reached to the Hereafter? — meaning of course it has not. Read as a statement, al-Ṭabarī reports the sense that their knowledge *will* be complete in the Hereafter, when the thing they denied is in front of them and knowing it is worth nothing. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī note a third possibility, taking the verb as a contracted form of *tadāraka*, which yields the sense that their knowledge is completed there. The readings converge on one point and differ in tone. Nobody holds that these people currently know anything about the matter; the question is whether the verse mocks the claim now or predicts the moment it collapses.

**Doubt Named as the Actual Condition**

The second clause is the most precise: *bal hum fī shakkin minhā*, they are in doubt about it. This is the honest description of the position the previous verses were arguing against.

Meccan resistance to the resurrection was not a worked-out denial supported by reasons. It was an inability to take the prospect seriously, which is a different mental state and a more common one. The Qur'an names it accurately here rather than inflating it into a doctrine, and the accuracy matters because doubt and denial call for different responses. A denier has a position to be refuted. A doubter has no position at all, which is why this section of the chapter asks questions instead of mounting refutations. What the sequence of six questions was doing was giving a doubtful person something to stand on, one domain at a time.

**Blindness as the Final Term**

The third clause goes further than doubt: *bal hum minhā ʿamūn*, they are blind to it. A doubter has seen something unclearly. A blind man has not seen it at all.

The word connects to the chapter's fourth verse, where those who deny the Hereafter were described as wandering blindly with their deeds made attractive to them. Eighty-two verses later the same condition is named again with a related term, and the two passages frame everything between them. In the intervening narratives the chapter has shown what the condition produces: a court that called signs sorcery, a people who called a prophet bad luck, nine men who swore by God and planned a murder. Blindness here is not an excuse offered on their behalf. It is the accumulated result of the refusals the chapter has been documenting.

**Why the Hereafter Is the Fault Line**

Of all the disputed doctrines, an-Naml keeps returning to this one. The believers in verse 3 were marked by certainty about it; the condemned in verse 4 by disbelief in it; the questions of verse 64 by origination and return; this verse by knowledge, doubt and blindness concerning it.

The concentration is deliberate, because this is the belief with practical teeth. A person's view about the origin of the universe changes nothing about how he treats a debtor. His view about whether he will be questioned changes everything, which is why the chapter's villains are always people acting in the confidence that nothing follows. Thamūd's nine plotted at night. Pharaoh's court refused what they knew because conceding was expensive. In each case the operative assumption was that the ledger closes at death. What these three clauses describe is the epistemic state that makes such a life possible, and the objection the deniers themselves raise comes in the next verse.

**Knowledge Arriving Too Late**

One of the readings al-Ṭabarī transmits deserves separate attention, because it says something the other readings do not. On that vocalisation the clause is a statement rather than a question: their knowledge *does* reach the Hereafter — meaning it arrives there, at the point where knowing is useless.

The picture is grim and the Qur'an draws it elsewhere at length. The Book describes the deniers at the resurrection asking to be sent back, admitting what they had refused, seeing clearly at the one moment when clear sight purchases nothing. Knowledge in that setting is no longer a basis for decision; the decisions are over. What the reading adds to this verse is a completion of its three-stage description. Empty now, doubtful now, blind now — and then, at the end, full knowledge delivered to people who have nothing left to do with it. The value of understanding something depends entirely on when it arrives, and the chapter has been arguing since its opening verses that the interval available for acting on it is the thing being spent.
