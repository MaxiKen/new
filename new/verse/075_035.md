## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:35

> Again, woe to you, and even more woe!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Woe Is Not an Echo**

The sūrah repeats the threat almost word for word, and the repetition is the point rather than the redundancy. The Arabic opens with *thumma*, then or moreover, which in this context indicates sequence with an interval rather than simple addition. A second woe is being announced after the first, not alongside it. The effect is of a punishment that arrives in stages, each one complete in itself and each one followed by another. The Qur'an's rhetoric here is doing something a single intensified threat could not do: it is denying the listener the comfort of a bounded disaster. Whatever he imagines when he hears the first phrase, the second tells him there is more.

**How Many Woes Are Contained Here**

The phrase in the previous verse holds two occurrences of *awlā*, and this verse holds two more, which gives four across the two verses. Exegetes who counted them assigned the four to four distinct stages: the battlefield of Badr, the grave, the Resurrection, and the Fire. That assignment is a homiletical reading preserved in the tafsīr literature rather than a report with a chain, and it should be cited as such. What gives the reading its value is not the specific four but the principle underneath it: the punishment is not a single event but a condition that unfolds, and each stage is real rather than symbolic. The second possibility is simpler and should be stated alongside it: in Arabic rhetoric, repetition of this kind is a standard device of emphasis, and the doubled phrase may intend nothing more than to make the threat unmistakable. The two explanations are compatible. Emphasis and escalation both deny the hearer the comfort of a limit, and both leave him with the sense of something that has begun and is not going to stop. The Qur'an's own account supports the principle even where it does not license the count.

**A Pattern of Escalation Across the Sūrah**

This doubling is the last step in a rising series that began much earlier. Verse 11 stated flatly that there would be no refuge. Verses 22 through 25 described the two faces and attached to the second of them the anticipation of a back-breaking calamity. Verses 34 and 35 now address a single figure directly and double the threat against him. The sūrah's tone has been intensifying steadily, and the intensification is not rhetorical decoration. It mirrors the argument: as the evidence accumulates, the refusal becomes less excusable, and the language appropriate to it becomes heavier. A sūrah that opens with an oath and closes with a threat has moved through every register available to it.

**The Fitness of the Response**

The Qur'an's punishments are always described in terms that correspond to the fault, and the correspondence here is unusually tight. The man in v. 33 was swaggering on his way home to an audience that admired him. What is threatened to him now is the loss of every one of those supports: the woe at Badr removed his standing among his people, the grave removed his audience, and the Resurrection and the Fire removed his self-possession. The principle is stated elsewhere as a rule: *“Whoever does an evil deed will only be paid back with its equivalent.”* (40:40). Nobody is punished for something other than what he did, and the shape of the punishment is the shape of the act.

**Why the Punishment Is Still Not Described**

The sūrah could easily have named the fire, the chain, or the boiling drink, all of which appear elsewhere in the Qur'an in far more detail. It does not, and the omission is worth accounting for. A threat that names its content can be assessed, and assessment is the first step toward argument; a threat that names only its nearness cannot be assessed at all. The sūrah is addressing a man who has spent his life managing what other people see and think, and the one thing he cannot manage is an unspecified disaster he is told is close. The silence is the severity. There is a second reason for it, which the Qur'an supplies elsewhere: the thing being threatened is not a penalty imposed from outside but the consequence of the man's own state. *“Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day.”* (83:15) names the punishment as separation, and separation admits no description, because it is the absence of everything rather than the presence of something. A threat that names a fire gives the imagination somewhere to stand. A threat that names only an exclusion leaves it nowhere at all. The silence is the severity.

**Why the Sūrah Does Not End Here**

It is worth asking why the sūrah does not close on this note, since a doubled threat would make an effective ending. The reason is that a threat only moves a person who already accepts the premise — that there is a Day on which the threat will be executed. The denier of this sūrah does not accept that premise; he asked in v. 6 when the Day would be, in a tone that suggested it would never come. A threat built on an unaccepted premise is wasted. This is also why the threat is not softened in the telling. The sūrah is not trying to frighten a man into a convenient belief; it is establishing that the refusal has a cost, and it will then establish that the claim being refused is true. Fear and proof appear in the Qur'an in that order more often than readers expect, since fear is what makes a person willing to look at the argument he has been refusing. So the sūrah turns, in its final five verses, from threatening the man to proving the thing he denies, and it does so by an argument he cannot dismiss: his own origin.
