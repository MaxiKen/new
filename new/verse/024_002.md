## Sūrah an-Nūr 24:2

> As for female and male fornicators, give each of them one hundred lashes, and do not let pity for them make you lenient in ˹enforcing˺ the law of Allah, if you ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day. And let a number of believers witness their punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Specificity of the Hundred Lashes**

The statute opens with an exact numerical decree: *al-zāniyatu wa-al-zānī fajlidū kulla wāḥidin minhumā miʾata jaldah*. In classical Arabic phrasing, placing the female before the male marks a noticeable shift from the legal syntax of theft in 5:38, where the male is named first. Exegetes note that illicit intimacy involves mutual physical agency, but historical social vulnerability and the domestic consequences of lineage corruption fell with singular weight upon women. Placing the woman first emphasizes that the moral prohibition binds her directly as an autonomous moral agent, while immediately pairing her with the male ensures equal accountability under the whip. Neither partner is excused or treated as incidental to the crime.

The penalty itself is corporal rather than financial or custodial. The root *j-l-d* relates to the dermis, signifying blows that impact the skin without lacerating flesh, breaking bone, or inducing permanent injury. Classical jurists carefully defined the mechanical execution of the penalty. The whip used had to be moderate—neither fresh and supple like a cane, nor stiff and heavy like iron. The execution of the hundred lashes was traditionally administered across the broad surfaces of the torso and shoulders, strictly avoiding the face, head, and vital organs, thereby fulfilling punitive deterrence without cruelty.

**The Prohibition of Misplaced Compassion in Divine Justice**

The text anticipates the natural human discomfort associated with witnessing physical punishment: *wa-lā taʾkhudhkum bihimā raʾfatun fī dīni Allāh*. The noun *raʾfah* denotes a tender, visceral sympathy that instinctively seeks to shield another creature from distress. In private interpersonal interactions, tender mercy is a celebrated virtue. Within the judicial sphere, however, misplaced leniency threatens the structural integrity of the moral order. The qualifier *fī dīni Allāh*—in the religion and judicial law of God—establishes that judicial authorities do not possess private discretion to commute or diminish a verified divine boundary (*ḥadd*).

When a magistrate softens a mandatory sentence out of emotional sentimentality, the apparent kindness to the individual wrongdoer becomes an act of neglect toward the community whose social cohesion is undermined by unchecked transgression. Classical scholars explained that compassion is misapplied when it dismantles the protective barriers that safeguard public morals. Divine law commands that public justice remain steadfast, insulated from both vindictive malice and sentimental compromise.

**Faith in the Last Day as the Anchor of Judicial Duty**

The verse anchors judicial firmness in the foundational tenets of faith: *in kuntum tuʾminūna bi-Allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhir*. Connecting the execution of statutory law to belief in the Last Day reminds every magistrate, witness, and spectator that human justice does not operate in a cosmic vacuum. A judge might feel tempted to conceal a crime, show favoritism toward influential elites, or relent under social pressure. Invoking the final reckoning introduces an inescapable spiritual accountability into the courtroom.

The worldly embarrassment of enduring statutory discipline is insignificant when measured against the everlasting judgment awaiting unrepentant rebellion. Furthermore, the true believer recognizes that divine wisdom surpasses human intuition; the Creator who established the penalty understands the delicate balance required to preserve human dignity and societal stability far better than the sympathetic observer. To doubt the justice of the divine decree is to harbor deficiencies in one's conviction regarding God's wisdom and sovereign knowledge.

**The Public Dimension of Execution: Deterrence and Transparency**

The final clause commands the presence of a communal delegation during the administration of the sentence: *wa-lyashhad ʿadhābahumā ṭāʾifatun mina al-muʾminīn*. Classical jurists debated the numerical threshold implied by *ṭāʾifah*. Opinions ranged from a single upright witness to two, three (the linguistic minimum for a plural group), or four, matching the evidentiary standard required to establish the crime itself. Imām al-Shāfiʿī favoured four witnesses to mirror the evidentiary threshold of the courtroom, while others considered a modest gathering sufficient to validate public execution.

This public presence fulfills two vital functions. Outwardly, communal witness generates a profound psychological deterrence, impressing upon spectators the severe social and physical consequences of reckless passion. Inwardly, public execution ensures administrative transparency. When punishments are inflicted openly before upright citizens, corrupt officials cannot indulge in arbitrary cruelty, excessive brutality, or secret exonerations behind closed doors. The gaze of the community guarantees that the law is executed strictly within its humane boundaries.

**The Jurisprudential Debate Over Marital Status and Sanctions**

The universal phrasing of this verse generated extensive jurisprudential discourse regarding the scope of its application. The literal text commands one hundred lashes for every fornicator without distinguishing between unmarried and married individuals. The vast majority of classical jurists held that this verse governs only the unmarried offender (*bikr*), arguing that prophetic practice and consensus established the distinct punishment of stoning (*rajm*) for the married offender (*thayyib*). They viewed the Sunnah as specifying and qualifying the general language of the Qur'an.

Conversely, groups among the early Khawārij and several independent jurists throughout Islamic history maintained that the clear, unambiguous text of the Qur'an must remain absolute and uncompromised. They argued that solitary historical reports cannot override or append capital penalties to an explicit Qur'anic statute that prescribes solely lashes. They also pointed to verses that halve the punishment for enslaved individuals in 4:25, reasoning that death by stoning is mathematically indivisible, whereas one hundred lashes can readily be reduced by half.

**Evidentiary Rigor and the Practical Rarity of Conviction**

The severity of the prescribed penalty must be understood in connection with the extraordinary evidentiary standard the Qur'an requires for conviction. As established in subsequent verses, establishing the crime of illicit intimacy requires four upright eyewitnesses who observed the physical act of penetration simultaneously and with sensory certainty. In the absence of an explicit, uncoerced confession repeated multiple times by a sane adult, this standard is practically impossible to meet in ordinary life.

Islamic jurisprudence deliberately constructed a legal architecture that combines severe theoretical deterrence with nearly insurmountable procedural thresholds. The law aims to prevent the normalization of public debauchery while systematically discouraging voyeuristic surveillance, ensuring that private moral failures remain veiled beneath divine mercy rather than dragged into the public square. The primary social function of the statute is not to fill public squares with floggings, but to sanctify chastity as an inviolable communal norm.
