## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:21

> The man from Egypt who bought him said to his wife, “Take good care of him, perhaps he may be useful to us or we may adopt him as a son.” This is how We established Joseph in the land, so that We might teach him the interpretation of dreams. Allah’s Will always prevails, but most people do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Buyer Becomes the Patron**

The man who bought Joseph is described only as *the one from Egypt*, and the sūrah never names him. The commentaries supply the name found in Genesis 39:1 — Potiphar, rendered in the Arabic sources as Qiṭfīr or Iṭfīr — and the title by which the chapter will later address him, *al-ʿAzīz*. That title does useful work. *ʿAzīz* carries the senses of grand, powerful, cherished and honoured, and it denotes a high office rather than a crown. The distinction matters because this sūrah also has a king, addressed separately in verses 43, 50, 54 and 76, so the ʿAzīz cannot be the monarch. He is a minister, and some reports identify him as overseer of the treasury or as captain of the guard. The same title will later be applied to Joseph himself (12:78, 12:88), which is the chapter's quiet way of marking the transfer: the boy bought for twenty dirhams ends up holding the office of the man who bought him.

**"Take Good Care of Him" Is an Instruction to a Wife**

The command is *akrimī mathwāhu* — make his lodging honourable. It is addressed to the woman of the house, and it establishes two things at once. Joseph is not to be treated as labour; he is to be housed well. And the person responsible for his domestic arrangements is the same person who will attempt his seduction eight verses later. The irony is structural rather than incidental. The instruction is generous, and its generosity creates the conditions of proximity that the household will not be able to manage. The commentators add that the ʿAzīz was struck by the boy's bearing in the market and bought him for that reason, and that the couple had no children, which is why the second half of his sentence turns to adoption.

**The Same Sentence Was Spoken Over Moses**

The words are almost identical to those Pharaoh's wife used about a different foundling: *“˹This baby is˺ a source of joy for me and you. Do not kill him. Perhaps he may be useful to us or we may adopt him as a son.”* (28:9). Two powerful households, two boys with no protection, the same two possibilities named in the same order — usefulness, then sonship. That verse closes with a sentence this one only implies: *“They were unaware ˹of what was to come˺”* (28:9). The parallel is deliberate and it is doing theological work. In both cases the household that takes the child in is the household the child will eventually overturn or inherit. Pharaoh's palace raised Moses; the ʿAzīz's house raised Joseph. Neither man could have known it, and the verse records that they did not.

**Adoption in the Sense the Households Meant**

The phrase *we may adopt him as a son* describes a practice the Qur'an later addresses directly. In its Medinan legislation the Book distinguishes between care of a child and the fabrication of lineage: *“Nor does He regard your adopted children as your real children. These are only your baseless assertions”* (33:4), followed by the instruction that adopted children keep their fathers' names, since that is more just in God's sight (33:5). The ruling does not condemn what the ʿAzīz proposed; it regulates it. What the earlier dispensations allowed as full incorporation into a family, this law restricts to guardianship without falsifying descent. Reading verse 21 against 33:4–5 shows the chapter aware of the difference between sheltering a boy and claiming him, and shows Joseph's own history running through the first without ever acquiring the second.

**The Narrator Steps Into the Story**

Halfway through the verse the voice changes. The ʿAzīz stops speaking and God begins: *This is how We established Joseph in the land*. The verb is from *makkana*, to place firmly, to give a person footing and authority — the same verb the chapter will use again when the elevation is complete: *“This is how We established Joseph in the land to settle wherever he pleased”* (12:56). The interruption is a technique the sūrah uses sparingly and always at a hinge. It tells the reader that the purchase in the market was not a change of owners but a stage in an arrangement, and it does so before any of that arrangement is visible. The establishment named here is still thirty-five verses away from its fulfilment, and in between lie the seduction, the accusation and the prison.

**The Purpose Clause Explains Everything That Follows**

*So that We might teach him the interpretation of dreams* states the reason for the establishment, and the reason is educational. Joseph's rise will not come from wealth, birth or force; it will come from a skill given to him by God and exercised first on two prisoners and then on a king (12:36–41, 12:43–49). The clause also answers the promise his father made in verse 6, where choosing, teaching and completing favour were named as a single sequence. What looked like a favour in a Canaanite household is revealed as a curriculum. The well, the market, the palace and eventually the cell are all classrooms, and the sūrah is explicit that the syllabus was set before the pupil arrived.

**"Allah's Will Always Prevails" Is the Verse's Conclusion**

The final clause is a maxim, and it is aimed at the plot the reader has just watched. God is *ghālib* over His affair — prevailing, dominant, not thwarted. The brothers willed a well; the caravan willed a profit; the ʿAzīz willed a servant; God willed a prophet in a treasury. The verse does not say the other wills were unreal. It says they were outranked. The clause *but most people do not know* repeats a formula the sūrah uses twice more (12:40, 12:68) and the Book uses often elsewhere, and it identifies the specific ignorance at issue: not ignorance of facts but ignorance that events are governed. Every character in this chapter acts on the assumption that outcomes are in their own hands, and the reader is told, before the second act begins, that the assumption is false.
