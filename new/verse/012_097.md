## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:97

> They begged, “O our father! Pray for the forgiveness of our sins. We have certainly been sinful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Confession, Addressed to the Other Victim**

The brothers have already admitted their fault. In Egypt, immediately after the disclosure, they told Joseph that God had preferred him over them and that they had certainly been sinful (12:91), and they were forgiven in the next verse (12:92). Verse 97 repeats the admission to a different person, in almost the same words, and asks for something different: not forgiveness from the injured party but intercession with God. The chapter requires two confessions because the crime had two victims. What was done to Joseph was done to a brother; what was done to Jacob was done to a father who was deceived for decades and whose sight failed from the grief. The second confession completes the moral accounting. An offence against Joseph has already been named, admitted and forgiven by Joseph himself; the offence against the father has not, and it is the offence that produced the blindness healed in the previous verse. The brothers address the injury whose consequences they can still see, in the person whose eyes have just been restored.

**They Ask Their Father to Pray**

The request is specific in form. *Istaghfir lanā* — seek forgiveness for us. They do not ask Jacob to forgive them, although the deception was directed at him. They ask him to pray, which transfers the matter to God and places their father in the position of intercessor rather than judge. The distinction is precise and it reflects the chapter's understanding of offences. An injury between people can be waived by the person injured, and Joseph waived his (12:92). A sin against God cannot be waived by anyone; it can only be asked about, and the asking is properly done through someone whose standing with God is acknowledged. The brothers acknowledge theirs in the form of the request. The intercessory form identifies where they believe the remaining problem lies. Having been forgiven by Joseph in Egypt, they do not ask their father to forgive them; they ask him to approach God on their behalf. The distinction tells the reader that they understand the offence as having a dimension Joseph's amnesty did not close, and the following verse confirms that they are right.

**The Conversation Happens After the Return Home**

The commentators record that this exchange took place after the brothers came back from Egypt, which means the request is made in Canaan and not in the granary. The timing matters for the scene. Jacob has recovered his sight (12:96), has reminded his children of the knowledge he claimed (12:86), and is now facing the sons who deceived him decades earlier about the son whose shirt has just healed him. The confession arrives in that room. The chapter gives it a verse of its own and gives the answer another, which is the allocation it reserves for statements of weight. The allocation separates the request from its occasion. The brothers do not ask for intercession at the moment of their confession in Egypt, in front of Joseph, while the shame is fresh; they ask later, in Canaan, after everything has been reported to their father. The chapter gives the request its own space so that it reads as a settled intention rather than as a reaction.

**Why Not Joseph, Who Has Already Forgiven**

Joseph's amnesty in verse 92 removed the reproach that belonged to him and included a prayer: may God forgive you. The brothers nevertheless ask their father to pray as well. Nothing in the chapter suggests that Joseph's prayer was considered insufficient or that his forgiveness was incomplete. What the second request acknowledges is that the deception was a separate act with a separate victim, and that a person who was told his son had been eaten by a wolf, who wept until his eyes failed, and who was called deluded for hoping otherwise, is owed an admission of his own. The chapter treats the two offences as distinct even though they arose from a single afternoon at a well.

**The Wording Echoes Verse 91 Almost Exactly**

The confession is the same one, spoken to a different person. In verse 91 the brothers said they had certainly been sinful; in verse 97 they say we have certainly been sinful. The repetition is not careless. It records that the admission was made twice, in two countries, to two victims, and that the second was not weakened by the first having been accepted. A confession that has been forgiven is easy to repeat; the chapter does not make it easy. The brothers ask their father for prayer at a moment when he has just been vindicated against them and has every reason to withhold it, and the wording they use is the wording they used to the brother who had already pardoned them.

**No Explanation, No Mitigation, No Excuse**

The verse is short and it contains nothing but the request and the admission. The brothers do not explain why they acted, do not describe their youth or their jealousy, do not claim that their father's preference for Joseph provoked them (12:8), and do not repeat the mitigation Joseph himself supplied in verse 89 — that they acted in ignorance. They state the fact and ask for prayer. The chapter's economy here is a moral judgement as much as a narrative one. A confession that explains itself is partly a defence; a confession that does not is what verse 97 contains, and verse 98 shows it accepted. That distinction is what verse 98 answers. A request for intercession supported by circumstances would invite an assessment of the circumstances; a request supported only by an admission invites mercy, and mercy is what Jacob promises. The chapter builds the exchange out of two halves — a confession containing nothing but itself, and a reply addressed to the One who forgives.

**Jacob's Answer Comes in the Following Verse**

The chapter separates the request from the response, giving each its own verse. Nothing in verse 97 predicts whether the prayer will be granted, promised or refused. Jacob has reason to refuse: he rejected their first report outright (12:18), doubted their second (12:83), was rebuked by them for his grief (12:85) and called deluded for his hope (12:95). The reader who has followed the chapter knows what verse 98 will say, and knows it because Jacob has already been described in verse 64 as a man who places his sons in God's keeping rather than holding them to account. The verse ends on the request, and the pause before the answer is the chapter's. The pause is the only hesitation the chapter allows him. Jacob has been described as patient, as knowing and as refusing to despair, and here he is asked by his sons to intercede for them; the request is recorded and nothing else is, with the answer arriving in full in the verse that follows. The reader is shown a father taking the request seriously enough to defer it.

**The Confession Arrives After the Good News, Not Before**

Nothing obliged the brothers to make this request. Joseph has forgiven them, their father's sight has been restored, the family is about to migrate to Egypt, and the famine is still running. The admission in verse 97 is not required by any of those facts and is not prompted by anyone in the narrative. The chapter places it here, after the healing and before the reunion, which means it arrives at the moment when the brothers have least need of it and most capacity to make it. A confession extracted under pressure is a negotiation; a confession offered after the pressure has been removed is what verse 97 records. Verse 98 answers it, and the two verses together close the moral account the chapter opened in verse 8.
