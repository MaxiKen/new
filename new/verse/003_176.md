## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:176

> ˹O Prophet!˺ Do not grieve for those who race to disbelieve—surely they will not harm Allah in the least. It is Allah’s Will to disallow them a share in the Hereafter, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Consolation Addressed to the Prophet**

The verse tells the Prophet not to let those who rush toward disbelief cause him grief, and it then states that their conduct does not harm God in the least. The instruction follows a long passage about the community's own failures, and it turns the Prophet's attention to another group whose opposition was personal to him.

The Book addresses the Prophet's distress at the disbelief of others in several places, and the commentators gathered the passages together. One sūrah tells him not to let the conduct of those who deny cause him grief, another tells him not to let their speech cause him sorrow, and a third asks whether he will consume himself in regret over people who will not believe. The verses form a body of consolation addressed to the same experience.

**Who the Verse Concerns**

The commentators identified the group in more than one way. Al-Rāzī read the phrase as referring to the idolaters of Makkah, who were the Prophet's own kin and who worked hardest against him. Al-Qurṭūbī included the People of the Book, who had a scripture of their own and were expected to recognise what came after it, and the hypocrites, who claimed adherence while working against the community.

The three groups had little in common except the conduct described in the verse, and the sentence covers all of them. The Book's manner in such passages is to state the matter in terms that apply wherever the conduct is found.

**The Word for Hastening**

The verse describes the group as hastening toward disbelief, and the verb carries the sense of moving quickly and eagerly. The phrase describes people who did not drift into their position but pressed toward it.

The distinction is worth keeping when the sentence is read in a community's own situation. The Book distinguishes between people who fall into an error and people who rush to embrace it, and the phrasing in this verse places the group in the second category. The Prophet is told not to be grieved by what they do, and the wording of the verse describes the act rather than the persons.

**The Statement That They Harm No One**

The verse states that the group will not harm God in the least, and the same formula appears earlier in the sūrah when it tells the community that whoever turns back on his heels does not harm God at all. The Book uses the sentence to place the consequences of defection where they fall.

The harm, in the Book's account, belongs to the person who commits the act. The community that hears the sentence is being taught that its message does not depend on the number of its adherents, and the Prophet is told that the loss of any follower is not a subtraction from the cause he was sent to convey.

**What It Means to Have No Share in the Hereafter**

The verse states that God desires to give the group no share in the Hereafter, and the commentators explained the phrase as referring to a share in its good and its blessing rather than to the mere experience of the next life. The Book describes its enemies as present in the Hereafter and receiving their treatment there.

The sentence is therefore a statement about what the group has lost rather than about the annihilation of their existence. The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter consistently distinguish between being in it and having a portion in it, and the verse before the reader uses the second of those ideas.

**The Balance of the Sentence**

The verse gives the Prophet a command and then supplies the reasons for it, and the two statements that follow are arranged to relieve a burden rather than to increase it. The grief the verse prohibits is the grief of a person who feels responsible for what others choose.

The Book relieves that burden by stating what the disbelief does not affect and what the group forfeits by it. The Prophet is not told that the matter is unimportant; he is told that his own standing is not diminished by it, and the sentence lets him set down a weight he had been carrying. The Book's comfort in such passages is always the same in kind, and it is offered to the Prophet as a release from a responsibility that was never his to bear.

**The Book's Habit of Comforting the Messenger**

The repetition of this instruction across several sūrahs shows how persistent the experience was. The Prophet's distress at the refusal of his people is a recurring subject of revelation, and the Book answers it repeatedly rather than once.

The pattern is instructive for the community that reads these verses. Whatever grief a person feels over the rejection of the message by those he cares about, the Book treats the feeling as natural and supplies the same remedy: the cause belongs to God, and its outcome does not depend on the response it receives.


The verse's application extends beyond the Prophet. Any believer who invests himself in the response of others faces the same disappointment, and the sentence offers the same release from it.

The passage around this verse has been concerned with a community that had been tested and had lost members to apostasy and hypocrisy. The sentence tells the believers what such losses mean for the cause they carry, and it prepares the reader for the verse that follows, which restates the same principle in the language of trade. A community that has lost members to defection can measure the loss against what the sentence says was never at stake.
