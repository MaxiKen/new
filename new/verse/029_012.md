## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:12

> The disbelievers say to the believers, “˹Just˺ follow our way, and we will bear ˹the burden of˺ your sins.” But they would never ˹want to˺ bear any of the believers’ sins. They are simply lying.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Offer Designed to Remove the Cost**

The proposal is elegant in its cruelty. Return to the ways of your ancestors, and the consequences of that return will be carried by us. Every element of the sentence is calculated: the believers are addressed as people who hesitate because they can see the price, so the price is taken away; departure from the community is dressed as a small step, so the step is described as "following"; and responsibility for the deed is transferred to someone else, where the Book says it cannot go. The offer is also evidence of what the disbelievers understood about the new faith. They knew that its followers believed in accountability, that they feared the Day, and that the fear was strong enough to hold a person inside a difficult commitment. Attacking that fear with a lie about the ledger was a strategy aimed precisely where the believers were strongest.

**The Burden That Cannot Be Moved**

The refusal given in the verse is grounded in a rule the Book states again and again: no soul will bear the burden of another (35:18), and no soul will be requited for anything but what it worked for (53:39). The same declaration appears in several chapters with the same unyielding shape, and it governs the whole economy of the Hereafter: the record belongs to the person whose deeds produced it, and it cannot be reassigned, sold, gifted, or inherited. Anyone promising to carry a stranger's sins is promising something outside the structure of the system he lives in. The Book does report that leaders of misguidance will carry an additional burden for those they misled while those who followed them still carry their own, so that the weight accumulates for the one who caused it — but nothing in that arrangement lightens the follower's load by a single sin.

**A Promise Made to Be Broken**

The verse separates two counts in the indictment against the offer, and the distinction matters. The first is that the promisers would never want to bear any of those sins. Their proposal was never intended for performance; it was a device for extracting compliance, and even the sincerity of the offer was false. The second count is capacity. Even a willingness would have failed for lack of means, as the rule about individual responsibility shows. The Book's phrasing places the first count first because the intention is what makes the man, and because the cruelty of the sentence lies there: the person addressed is being asked to trade a real consequence for a sentence with no content. The blunt conclusion, that they are simply lying, follows naturally from the demonstration. The proposal also reveals what the promisers thought of their audience: not as partners in a decision but as votes to be collected, and the verse's verdict refuses to dress that transaction in the language of generosity.

**The Ransom That Will Not Be Taken**

The Book closes the same door from another direction when it describes the Day for which the offer would be owed. No soul will be of help to another on that Day; no intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help given (2:48), and the sentence is repeated almost word for word in the same sūrah (2:123). A ransom is what the proposal in this verse actually amounts to: someone offers to pay a debt that is not transferable, in a currency nobody accepts. The Book's insistence on the point, stated in two nearly identical verses, suggests that the temptation to look for a sponsor is permanent. People in every generation prefer a system in which someone else's merit can be arranged to cover their own account, and the Book announces the same answer each time: the account is yours.

Refusing a ransom also settles what the judgment is about. If the dispute could be closed by an exchange of goods, it would be a disagreement about property; the verse describes it as a question of truth and conduct, and the Book's account of the Hereafter never allows wealth to substitute for either.

**Why the Sūrah Uses the Word Liars**

The sūrah has already used this vocabulary as its central dividing line, promising a distinction between the truthful and the liars (29:3). The return of the word here is deliberate. The disbelievers' offer is a lie in the plain sense — the promise will not be kept — but it is also a lie in the sūrah's deeper sense, since it invites men to structure their lives on a claim that does not hold. The Book frequently joins the two senses, because the moral vocabulary of this chapter is not a set of compliments and insults; it is a description of what is load-bearing. A lie in this usage is anything a person can stand on that will not support him. The verse's closing words therefore turn a tactical deception into a statement about the architecture of reality.

The word is chosen to fit the moment. An offer made with no intention of being honoured is not an error or a misunderstanding, and the Book's vocabulary marks the difference between people who cannot carry a burden and people who promise to carry it in order to end a conversation.

**What the Offer Was Meant to Prevent**

The proposal in this verse was not aimed at strangers; it was aimed at the community's own dissidents, and the Book records the anxiety behind such efforts in a warning to believers about yielding to a group among the People of the Book who would turn them back from belief to disbelief once they had accepted it (3:100). Apostasy in this setting was a public event with consequences for the group that lost a member, and the men making the offer were negotiating the return of their own people. What they asked for in exchange was nothing concrete, which is why the Book treats the offer as a device rather than a bargain. Its purpose was to restore the old arrangement without any admission that the new claim had been right.

The purpose of the offer becomes clearer beside the pressure the community was under. A believer who carried his burden despite the pleading of his relatives was the one thing the town's arrangements could not absorb, and the sentence describes leaders attempting to remove that embarrassment by negotiation rather than by force.

**The Same Exchange Refused Elsewhere**

The Book rejects this kind of transfer with a short formula in another sūrah, where the Prophet is told to tell the disbelievers that they will not be held accountable for the believers' misdeeds, nor will the believers be accountable for theirs (34:25). The sentence is neat because it refuses in both directions at once, denying the offer and declining any claim in return. Nothing about responsibility in the Book is collective in the sense the offer assumes, though much of it is collective in its effects: a person's errors can harm a community, and a community's guidance can help a person, and none of that changes who answers for what. The offer in this verse would have been refused even if it had been made honestly, and the chapter's next move is to explain what the promisers actually will be carrying.
