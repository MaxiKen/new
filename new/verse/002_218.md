## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:218

> Surely those who have believed, emigrated, and struggled in the Way of Allah—they can hope for Allah’s mercy. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse of Comfort**

The verse descends, the reports say, directly into the crisis the previous verse addressed. When the ruling on the sacred month came down — the concession of the sin, the weighing against the graver crimes — ʿAbd Allāh ibn Jaḥsh and his companions are said to have despaired, doubting that any merit remained for their action even though they had been absolved of crime; and this verse arrived as comfort and promise to them (al-Rāzī). The comfort's precision is worth seeing: the party had been told their act carried a sin, and their fear was that the sin had erased the sacrifice — the months of scouting, the risk of the mission, the service of the community's defense. The verse answers by naming, not the act, but the actors and their three defining deeds: those who believed, emigrated, and struggled in God's way. The mission's timing-error does not cancel the biography; the persons stand before God as the sum of their faith, their exile, and their striving, and it is to that sum — not to the single flawed operation — that the promise attaches.

**The Three Deeds**

The triad the verse names — belief, emigration, struggle — is the early community's complete biography in three verbs. Believed: the inner assent, the creed's foundation. Emigrated: the hijrah, faith made geographical — property left in Mecca, homes abandoned, the material cost of allegiance paid in full, the act that gave the community its calendar and its name (the Emigrants, al-muhājirūn). Struggled: the jāhadū, the striving in God's way with wealth and person — the expenditure the previous verses had legislated (2:195) and the fighting the verse before had prescribed (2:16). The three are joined by a single article — *those who* — making one class of people defined by all three together: the believing emigrant striver, the person whose faith cost him his home and whose home-loss issued in striving. The Qur'an returns to this triad repeatedly, and wherever it appears it carries the community's highest social-spiritual rank: the sūrah of the Spoils will give the emigrants who strove a promise of provision without measure, and the sūrah of Repentance will rank the strivers and emigrants at the summit of the believers' degrees. The verse here installs the triad as the identity the promise addresses.

**The Theology of Hope**

The promise itself is couched in a word the exegetes weighed carefully: they *hope* for God's mercy — yarjūn. Al-Rāzī unfolds both readings the word supports. The first is doctrinal: hope reflects the principle that no reward is certain, that everything remains in God's hands, that a person might still die a disbeliever — so the greatest deeds are completed not in certainty of payment but in hope of mercy. The verse thus refuses the community the merchant's confidence: even the believing emigrant striver does not hold God's reward as a debt but approaches it as a hope. The second reading is descriptive and practical: hope here indicates the attitude believers are called to hold — approaching God with hope and fear together, the two wings the tradition's spirituality never lets fly apart. The two readings compose: because the reward is not certain, hope is the correct posture; because hope is the correct posture, certainty is refused even to the triad's heroes. And the sūrah connects the hope to its earlier portrait of the humble — those who reckon that they will meet their Lord and to Him they return (2:46): the hoping and the reckoning describe both what believers do and what they are called to do, the expectation of the meeting shaping the life that precedes it.

**The Names That Receive the Hope**

The verse closes with the two names that make hope rational: God is Forgiving, Merciful. The pairing answers the party's case exactly. They feared the sin had voided the merit; the Forgiving — ghafūr — is the name that covers the sin, and the verse had already reported their absolution. They hoped the biography might still carry weight; the Merciful — raḥīm — is the name that receives the biography in mercy rather than measuring it in strict account. The names also complete the hope-theology from the other side: if no reward is certain because all is in God's hands, then the character of the hands is the whole question — and the verse names the character. The hope is not a gamble against an unknown disposition but a reliance on a declared one; the uncertainty concerns the servant's deeds, never the Lord's mercy. This is the same structure the pilgrimage-legislation ended on — the pilgrim commanded to ask forgiveness at his rite's summit and told that God is Forgiving, Merciful (2:199) — and here the structure consoles a community whose rite of war had carried an error inside its sincerity.

**Emigration as the Verse's Social Text**

The triad's middle term deserves its social weight, because the verse speaks to a community of displaced people. The Emigrants were the trial-verse's addressees in person: the property left behind, the taunts of the locals and hypocrites about sacrificing for a losing cause (2:214), the years of exile in a city not their own. To such people the verse names emigration as a constitutive act of faith — not a misfortune endured but a deed counted, ranked beside belief and striving in the single clause that defines the promised class. The hijrah's cost becomes its credential: the homes and wealth left in Mecca are not losses written off but expenditures credited to the same account as the striving. And the verse's hope-clause answers the exile's particular temptation — the sense of having paid everything and received nothing yet: the payment is seen, the biography is the promise's addressee, and the mercy hoped for is the exile's eventual homecoming in a form larger than Mecca. The community that would return to the Kaʿbah within years first received this verse, and its hope-structure — deeds counted, reward hoped, mercy named — carried the Emigrants through the remaining seasons of the taunt.

**The Verse Between the Scales and the Questions**

Within the movement's architecture the verse is the hinge between war and domestic law. Behind it: the trial (2:214), the charity roster (2:215), the fighting prescribed despite dislike (2:216), the sacred month's scales (2:217). Ahead: the question-verses of wine and gambling, orphans, marriage, menstruation, oaths, divorce, and nursing — the great household legislation that fills the sūrah's remaining pages. The hinge-verse carries the whole transition: the community that has been legislated for war is assured of its hope before being legislated for home. The triad itself spans both legislations — belief undergirds the household's rules, the emigration built the households that the rules will govern, and the striving includes the patience the domestic law will require. And the verse's final word-order leaves the community inside the names: Forgiving, Merciful — the same names that closed the bequest-legislation (2:182), the pilgrim's istighfār (2:199), and the cessation of hostilities (2:192). The hope for God's mercy is the atmosphere the whole sūrah breathes: every law given inside it, every error absolved under it, every biography — believing, emigrating, striving — received at last by it.
