## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:70

> They said, “Have you come to us so that we would worship Allah alone and abandon what our forefathers used to worship? Then bring us what you threaten us with, if what you say is true!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge to the Messenger**

The verse reports the answer of the people to the reminder of the previous verse, and the answer states what the messenger's call would require of them: the worship of God alone and the abandonment of what their forefathers worshipped. The objection is stated as a description of the demand rather than as an argument against it, and the phrasing tells the reader what the community valued. The verse in front of us supplies the reason for the refusal in the speakers' own words.

The appeal to the practice of the forefathers is the same one that the sūrah has already addressed in its passage on the shameful deeds (7:28), and the Book reports it from the disbelievers in several passages, including the sūrah of the Cow (2:170), the sūrah of the Table (5:104), and the sūrah of Luqmān (31:21). The verse in front of us places the appeal in the mouth of a community within the sūrah's narratives, and the answer that follows states the ground on which it is rejected.

**The Demand for the Punishment**

The second half of the verse reports the demand that the messenger bring upon the people what he had threatened, if his claim is true, and the demand appears in the sūrah's other narratives. The sūrah of the Sand Dunes reports the people of ʿĀd asking their messenger whether he had come to turn them away from their gods and calling on him to bring the punishment if he was truthful (46:22), and the sūrah of Yūnus and the sūrah of the Prophets report the same challenge from the audience of the Prophet. The verse in front of us states the challenge at the close of the second warning.

The demand treats the warning as a claim to be tested rather than as a warning to be heeded, and the Book's answer to it appears in several passages. The sūrah of the Prophets states that the people would not believe the signs even if they saw them, and the sūrah of the Cattle reports the audience's demand for a sign and the answer that the signs belong to God. This verse supplies the challenge that the messenger answers in the next verse.

**The Appeal to the Forefathers**

The worship that the people refuse to abandon is described as what their forefathers worshipped, and the Book treats the appeal to ancestry as a recurring obstacle in its accounts of the communities. The sūrah's earlier passage answered the same appeal with the statement that God never commands what is shameful and with the question of how the people attribute to God what they do not know (7:28). The verse here states the appeal in its simplest form, and the next verse names the objects of the ancestral worship.

The commentary tradition notes that the ancestral practice in the case of ʿĀd included the worship of idols and of natural forces, and the sūrah of Noah names five idols that the people of that messenger worshipped. The present verse states the community's attachment in terms of inheritance rather than of conviction, and the answer that follows addresses the practice rather than the ancestry. The Book's argument in both cases is that a practice does not acquire authority from repetition.

**The Two Halves of the Refusal**

The verse joins two elements that the sūrah's narratives keep together: the refusal to change the inherited practice and the demand for immediate proof. The two belong to the same position, since the community that will not reconsider its customs asks for an event that would make reconsideration unnecessary. The Book's descriptions of the demand for signs connect it to the refusal to accept the message on the terms on which it is offered, and the sūrah's answer states the consequence of the demand.

The Book's treatment of the request for the punishment appears in its accounts of the audience's challenge to the Prophet, where the demand is answered with the statement that the term belongs to God. The verse under discussion places the same demand in the narrative of ʿĀd, and the answer of the messenger in the next verse states what the demand will produce. The community's challenge is thereby turned back on itself, and the narrative that follows states the outcome.

**The Recurring Pattern of the Refusal**

The verse repeats the pattern of the refusal that the sūrah's accounts establish, and the pattern now includes the appeal to the ancestors and the demand for proof. The first account supplied the charge of error, and the second adds the two elements that the sūrah's audience in Makkah used in its own resistance. The Book's arrangement of the narratives in series makes the audience's arguments part of the history of the earlier peoples.

The sūrah's later accounts of Thamūd and of the people of Shuʿayb repeat the refusal in their own terms, and the sūrah of Hūd reports the same arguments in its versions of the narratives. Here the verse supplies the answer of ʿĀd, and the sūrah's reader is shown that the arguments against the message have a history and an outcome.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands seventieth in the chapter, and it follows the reminder of the favours and precedes the reply of the messenger. The verses that follow state the messenger's answer and the destruction of the people (7:71–72), and the second account then gives way to the third. This verse supplies the community's final answer to the call.

The reader who has heard the reminder of what the people had received now reads their response to the messenger who brought it. The worship of God alone is refused because it would require the abandonment of the ancestral practice, and the proof is demanded in the form of the punishment itself; and the sūrah's next verse states the messenger's answer and the terms on which the demand is met.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the reply of Hūd, that the people were already subject to the wrath and torment of their Lord, that their gods were names invented by them and their forefathers with no authority from God, and that he would wait with them for the outcome (7:71). The challenge in front of us is answered with the statement that the punishment is not a proof to be summoned but a consequence already in motion, and the account then proceeds to its end.

The two verses together state the last exchange between the messenger and his people. The community asks for the punishment as a test of the message, and the messenger states that the terms of the test have long been settled; and the sūrah's following verses record the rescue of the believers and the removal of the people who denied the signs.
