## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:24

> **And when it is said to them, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “Ancient fables!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Exposes Attitude**

Wa-idhā qīla lahum mādhā anzala rabbukum qālū asāṭīru al-awwalīn — and when it is said to them, what has your Lord revealed, they say, ancient fables. The question mādhā anzala rabbukum, what has your Lord revealed, is asked to test response to revelation. Rabbukum, your Lord, is personal, the One who created, sustains, knows. The expected answer from a grateful heart would be to acknowledge blessing. Instead, they say asāṭīru al-awwalīn — fables of ancients, tales of old peoples, myths. Asāṭīr is plural of usṭūrah, story, fable, often with connotation of fabricated tales that entertain but have no truth. Al-awwalīn means ancients, earlier peoples. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Makkan deniers called Qur'an asāṭīr because it contains stories of previous nations like Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and they claimed these were merely old tales copied, not revelation. The same accusation appears in 6:25, 8:31, 23:83, 25:5, 27:68, 68:15, 83:13. It is a way to dismiss without engaging: label it as old stories, not relevant, not divine.

**Why They Call Revelation Fables**

Calling revelation fables serves two purposes for deniers. First, it avoids having to argue against its content. If Qur'an is from Allah, then its warnings about Hereafter and its calls to abandon idols must be taken seriously. If it is merely fables, it can be ignored as entertainment. Second, it protects social status. To admit that what Muhammad ﷺ recites is from your Lord would require changing worship, economy, and tribal customs. To call it fables allows continuation of existing ways while appearing to have a reason. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that asāṭīru al-awwalīn implies that these stories are not only old but also false, invented by ancients and now retold. The accusation is therefore that Prophet ﷺ is not a messenger but a storyteller who learned old tales from others, as 25:5 says: *And they say, fables of ancients which he has written down, and they are dictated to him morning and evening* (25:5). The sūrah will answer this accusation later by pointing to linguistic inimitability and by noting that the Prophet ﷺ was unlettered and had no human teacher for such stories.

**The Contrast With Believers' Answer**

Verse 30 will give contrasting answer from those mindful of Allah: when asked what has your Lord revealed, they say *all the best*. The two answers are placed to show difference between hearts: one sees revelation as fables, other as goodness. The question is same, answers opposite, revealing inner state. Al-Rāzī points out that the question mādhā anzala rabbukum is asked to both groups, but answers differ because hearts differ: one heart is munkiratun mustakbirūn, denying and arrogant, so it says fables; other heart is muttaqī, mindful, so it says goodness. The sūrah thus uses same question as test. What you say revelation is reveals what you are. This is why verse 23 said Allah knows what they conceal and reveal and does not like arrogant: their open answer fables reveals concealed arrogance.

**Fables Accusation And The Theme Of Knowledge**

The accusation asāṭīru al-awwalīn is ironic because the stories in Qur'an are presented as true accounts with lessons, not fables for amusement. The Qur'an says *In their stories is a lesson for those of understanding; it is not a fabricated tale* (12:111). By calling them fables, deniers show they do not reflect on purpose of stories. The stories of destroyed peoples are warnings, not entertainment. The sūrah opened with command at hand and said do not hasten it, then listed blessings as signs, then argued idols are dead. Stories of ancients who denied and were destroyed fit this pattern: they are evidence that command does come. To call them fables is to refuse to be mindful, yadhakkarūn, and to refuse to understand, yaʿqilūn. The accusation therefore is not literary criticism but moral evasion.

**How The Qur'an Answers The Fables Charge**

The Qur'an answers the fables charge in several ways throughout. One is by challenging to produce similar speech: if it is merely human fables, then produce something like it. Another is by pointing to Prophet's unlettered status: he did not read previous scriptures, so how would he know detailed stories of ancient peoples with accurate lessons? Another is by pointing to prophecies and knowledge of unseen that fables would not contain. In al-Naḥl specifically, answer comes via blessings: if Allah can create heavens and earth, cattle, rain, crops, sea, mountains, can He not reveal stories of past peoples as lessons? The One who creates what you do not know can reveal what you did not know. The accusation also forgets that fables of ancients, even if old, may be true; old does not mean false. Al-Qurṭubī notes that ancients' stories include both true and false; Qur'an distinguishes and confirms true.

**Linguistic Note On Asāṭīr**

Asāṭīr from s-ṭ-r means writings, lines, stories written down. Usṭūrah is a story, often with connotation of something written and passed down, sometimes with implication of falsehood. Al-awwalīn means first ones, ancients, earlier generations. The phrase asāṭīru al-awwalīn became a stock dismissal used by Makkans against Qur'an. Its use here shows that opposition to revelation often takes form of labeling rather than argument: call it myth, legend, old wives' tale, to avoid engaging. The verse's brevity — question and answer in one sentence — captures a scene repeated many times in Makkah: believers ask deniers what they think of revelation, and deniers dismiss with a wave: fables. The scene is familiar to anyone who has seen truth dismissed with a label.

**The Cost Of Calling Revelation Fables**

To call revelation fables is not a neutral literary judgment; it has consequences that verse 25 will detail. When a community's leaders label divine guidance as old tales, they block others from considering it. A common person who hears a respected elder say fables may never open the Book to check. The label becomes a barrier. Al-Qurṭubī notes that this is why the Qur'an treats mockery of revelation as serious wrongdoing, not mere opinion. It is a form of misguidance without knowledge, as verse 25 says. The label also reveals arrogance: to call Allah's speech fables is to place one's own judgment above divine speech, as if one knows better what is true. The verse therefore captures both the words and the attitude behind them, showing that speech reflects heart.

**How Believers Should Respond To Such Dismissal**

The Qur'an teaches believers not to respond to fables accusation with anger alone but with clarity and patience. The sūrah later says *Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good exhortation* (16:125). When someone says revelation is fables, the believer is to show that it is not: it contains guidance, law, mercy, knowledge of unseen, linguistic miracle, and consistent message of tawḥīd that fables do not contain. The believer also recognizes that label fables often comes from lack of reflection on blessings. A heart that has not reflected on cattle, rain, and stars as signs will also not reflect on stories of past peoples as lessons. The remedy is same: reflection, remembrance, humility. The verse therefore invites believers to be patient with deniers while remaining firm that revelation is from their Lord, not fables.
