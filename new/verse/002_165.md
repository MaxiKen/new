## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:165

> Still there are some who take others as Allah’s equal—they love them as they should love Allah—but the ˹true˺ believers love Allah even more. If only the wrongdoers could see the ˹horrible˺ punishment ˹awaiting them˺, they would certainly realize that all power belongs to Allah and that Allah is indeed severe in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Equals Loved With Divine Love**

The verse describes the inner structure of shirk, and the description is not about belief but about love. Some among people take *andād* — equals, rivals, competitors, the same word the sūrah used in its early challenge (2:22) — and they love them *as God should be loved*. The exegetes mapped the possible senses and the range is instructive: they love their idols the way believers love God; they obey their leaders the way God should be obeyed; they love these equals as they themselves claim to love God, dividing the love between heaven and its rivals; they glorify and follow human agencies with the total allegiance owed only to the Divine. All the readings share one claim: shirk is not primarily an intellectual error about how many gods exist but a misplacement of the heart's supreme attachment. The idolater may never formulate a theology; he performs one, in the structure of his loves — and the verse prosecutes the performance.

**The Believers' Love Is More Ardent**

Against the divided love the verse sets a comparison: *but the believers love Allah even more* — *ashaddu ḥubban*, stronger in love. Al-Rāzī's explanation is arithmetic rather than emotional: the love of the believers burns hotter because it is undivided, poured entirely into one true object, while the affection of the associators is split among rivals and is, in crisis, revealed as opportunistic — the same people who love their idols in ordinary times call on God alone when the ship is overwhelmed (31:32). The comparison quietly redefines strength of love as concentration. The idolater's affection may be passionate in each of its channels, but a passion distributed among competing objects is weaker at every point than a passion gathered into one; and it fails the only test that matters, since at the wave like a mountain the rivals are forgotten and the true love surfaces — proving all along which object the heart had actually ranked first. The believers' greater love is thus not a claim about feelings' temperature but about loyalty's unity: one object, no reserve, no emergency exit to a second.

**The Qur'an's Theology of Love**

This verse is the Qur'an's first great statement about the love between God and human beings, and the Book returns to it with a developed doctrine. The test of love is made practical: *"If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me; Allah will love you and forgive your sins"* (3:31) — love validated by following, and answered by being loved in return. The community is defined by mutual love: Allah *"will replace them with others who love Him and are loved by Him"* (5:54). And love is put to its sharpest test in the sūrah of Repentance: if parents, children, siblings, spouses, wealth, trade, and cherished homes are *more beloved to you than Allah and His Messenger and struggling in His Way*, then wait for God's command (9:24) — the ranking of loves made the criterion of faith itself. Beyond these, the Qur'an speaks dozens of times of what God loves and does not love — the virtuous, the repentant, the patient, the just, on one side; the transgressor, the arrogant, the corrupter, on the other — so that divine love is not a sentiment but a moral alignment. Human love for God is described far less often than God's love for people, and the asymmetry is theological: every true love of God is downstream of His love first.

**"You Will Be With Those Whom You Love"**

The practical consequence of love's ranking was given by the Prophet in a report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (6171). Anas ibn Mālik narrated: a man asked, when will the Hour be established, O Messenger of God? The Prophet said: what have you prepared for it? The man answered: I have not prepared much prayer or fasting or alms — but I love Allah and His Messenger. And the Prophet's answer was that a person will be in the company of the ones he loves. Anas added: nothing after Islam made us happier than that sentence. The report is the verse's eschatology in pastoral form. If love determines company — and the verse shows love determining even a person's gods — then the poor in deeds have one remaining capital: the direction of their love. The man who could not produce works produced an attachment, and the attachment was accepted as his ticket of proximity. Read with 2:165, the ḥadīth completes the picture from both ends: love misplaced binds a person to what he loves in punishment, and love rightly placed binds him to the Beloved in company. The Hour, in this teaching, does not sort people into strangers; it delivers each person to the object of his supreme love.

**Two Kinds of Love, and the Higher One**

The commentators used this verse to draw the classic distinction in the Islamic science of the heart. The first kind is desirous love — *ḥubb al-hawā* — loving a thing for what it does for the lover: God loved for His gifts, worship performed for its rewards. This love is legitimate and incumbent on all believers, since glorification, obedience, and righteous behavior are pursued in it for the peace and happiness God gives. The second kind is love for the sake of the beloved — arising not from benefit but because the perfection, beauty, and goodness of the beloved ignite the heart without intermediary. The higher lovers want the acts God loves not for what they gain but because He loves them; abandoning a thing the Beloved loves costs them more than serving their own interests ever would. The Sufis named the two poles metaphorical love (*majāzī*) — romance, the world, the self — and true love (*ḥaqīqī*), love of God alone and of all other beings through Him. Al-Rāzī added the epistemology of the higher love: one loves God to the degree one knows Him, and knowledge arouses love in return — even slight familiarity with the Beloved kindles the desire to know more, and that desire is itself love's signature. The discipline built on this circuit is *dhikr*, constant remembrance: the lover banishing from the heart all that is not the Beloved.

**Power Unveiled Too Late**

The verse ends by projecting the whole economy of love onto the Day: *if only the wrongdoers could see* — when they see the punishment — *that all power belongs to Allah and that Allah is severe in punishment*. The conditional is tragic in structure: the knowledge is certain and the timing is fatal. The rivals they loved as equals will be unveiled as powerless — the bonds cut, the leaders disowning the followers in the scene the next verses stage — and the power they had attributed to the loved objects will be seen residing wholly in the One they ranked second. An alternate reading reported by the exegetes turns the clause into an address to the Prophet: *if you saw* the wrongdoers when they see the punishment, you would see that power belongs entirely to God — the vision given to the reader rather than the wrongdoer, making the verse a warning delivered to whoever can still re-rank his loves. The severity named at the end is the verse's final argument against misplacement: love allocates allegiance, allegiance allocates destiny, and the severity of the punishment is simply what love-of-the-powerless amounts to when power arrives. The believers' stronger love is stronger because it was placed where the power actually is — before the seeing was forced.
