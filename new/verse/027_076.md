## Sūrah an-Naml 27:76

> Indeed, this Quran clarifies for the Children of Israel most of what they differ over.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Different Audience Named**

Every verse since the chapter's narrative section ended has addressed the Meccan idolaters. This one turns to a community that was not in the room: the Children of Israel, the inheritors of an earlier scripture.

The shift is abrupt and it widens the chapter's claim considerably. A book that answers Arabian paganism is a local corrective; a book that settles disputes internal to the older revealed traditions is claiming a different kind of authority. The verse makes that second claim explicitly, and the placement repays attention — immediately after the statement that everything hidden is in a clear record. A Book that resolves what people cannot resolve among themselves is being connected to a source that has the matter written down.

**The Verb Is About Narration**

The Arabic is *yaquṣṣu ʿalā banī isrāʾīl* — it recounts to the Children of Israel. The verb comes from the root behind *qaṣaṣ*, story or narrative, and it means to relate an account.

That choice of verb is more modest than a verb of adjudication would be, and more precise. The Qur'an does not say it issues rulings on their disputes; it says it recounts the matters to them. What settles a disagreement about what happened is a reliable account of what happened, and the Book presents itself here as supplying one. This is consistent with how the Qur'an handles the earlier traditions elsewhere, where it describes itself as confirming what came before and as a criterion for distinguishing within it, rather than as a replacement.

**What They Actually Differed Over**

Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī identify the disputes as internal quarrels among the Israelites, pursued to the point of mutual denunciation. Al-Rāzī specifies matters connected to what he calls the distortion of the Torah and the Gospel; al-Qurṭubī adds disagreements over the obligations of the divine law.

Both categories are recognisable. Communities with a long scriptural history generate disputes about the text itself — what belongs, how it was transmitted, what was altered — and disputes about practice, which obligations remain binding and which were provisional. The Qur'an engages both kinds in other chapters, correcting the account of ʿĪsā, addressing what was permitted and then forbidden to the Israelites, and adjudicating the status of Ibrāhīm. This verse is the general statement of which those are instances. A broader reading also circulated among the commentators, taking the disputes as questions about the nature of God, which the Qur'an answers universally.

**"Most of What They Differ Over" — Not All**

The limitation is explicit and it is unusual. *Akthara alladhī hum fīhi yakhtalifūn* — most of what they differ about, not the whole of it.

That qualification is easy to pass over and hard to explain away, since an expansive claim would have been rhetorically stronger. What it indicates is a selection principle: the Qur'an addresses the disputes that matter for the relationship between a person and God, and leaves alone the ones that do not. Questions of ritual detail, historical minutiae and legal particulars internal to another community's law are not the Book's subject. The restriction is consistent with how the Qur'an actually behaves. It says a great deal about the identity of ʿĪsā and nothing about the genealogies that occupy so much of the earlier scripture.

**Why the Children of Israel Appear at This Point**

The placement can look arbitrary in a chapter aimed at Mecca, and it is not. An-Naml has just spent fifty verses recounting the histories of Mūsā, Dāwūd, Sulaymān, Ṣāliḥ and Lūṭ — four of whom belong to the Israelite tradition and its prehistory.

The chapter has therefore been doing, in narrative, exactly what this verse describes in summary. It told the story of Mūsā at the fire, of Sulaymān and the queen, of the household of Lūṭ, and in each case it gave an account that differs in emphasis and sometimes in substance from what circulated elsewhere. Verse 9 of this chapter set the divine self-disclosure at the fire beside its biblical counterpart; verse 44 recorded a queen's submission that no other tradition preserves. This verse names what the chapter has been doing and identifies its second audience after the fact.

**A Claim That Could Be Checked**

The assertion is unusual for its period in one respect: it was verifiable by the people it concerned. Medina held substantial Jewish communities with scholars, texts and a long tradition of internal debate, and within a decade of this chapter's revelation the Prophet would be living among them.

A book claiming to resolve their disputes is inviting exactly the audience best equipped to test the claim, and the Qur'an keeps making it rather than quietly dropping it once contact was established. The later Medinan chapters engage the Israelite tradition at far greater length and in greater detail than the Meccan ones — the covenant, the calf, the sabbath, the prophets, the dietary restrictions. That is the behaviour of a text growing more confident about the claim under conditions where it could be contested, not less.

**Clarification Is Not the Same as Judgment**

The verse says the Qur'an recounts the disputed matters. It does not say the disputes end. Two verses later the chapter will state that the actual judgment between the parties belongs to God and happens in the Hereafter.

Reading the two together prevents a natural misunderstanding. A community holding a book that clarifies could easily conclude that it also holds the verdict, and is entitled to enforce it. The chapter separates the functions and gives the second one to nobody in this world. What is available now is a clear account; what is not available now is the decision about who was right. That distinction governs how the whole section should be read, and it is the reason the passage ends with an instruction to the Prophet to rely on God rather than an instruction to settle anything.
