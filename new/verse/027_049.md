## Sūrah an-Naml 27:49

> They vowed, “Let us swear by Allah that we will take him and his family down by night. Then we will certainly say to his ˹closest˺ heirs, ‘We did not witness the murder of his family. We are definitely telling the truth.’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Swearing by God to Commit Murder**

The conspirators bind themselves with an oath, and the oath is *taqāsamū bi-Llāh* — by God. They invoke the name of the deity whose messenger they are planning to kill, as the guarantee of their agreement with one another.

This is the sharpest irony in the chapter and it is not accidental. Thamūd were not atheists. They acknowledged God in the way their society acknowledged many things, as a name to swear by, and Ṣāliḥ's demand that they worship Him is what they are conspiring against. An oath in His name is the strongest bond available in their culture, and they reach for it instinctively to secure a murder. What the detail exposes is the difference between recognising a name and submitting to what it refers to. Verse 14 described a court that knew and refused; this is a smaller version, where the knowledge has been reduced to a formula for making promises binding.

**The Plan Is Made at Night for a Reason**

The verb is *nubayyitannahu* — we shall come upon him by night. Arabic has a specific term for a night attack, and it names a recognised form of tribal raid: fall on a household in the dark, when defence is impossible and witnesses are asleep.

Two purposes are served by the timing. The practical one is that the target cannot resist. The other is concealment, and it is the one the rest of the verse elaborates. Al-Ṭabarī notes that they swore to kill Ṣāliḥ and to provide alibis for one another afterward, so the darkness is part of an evidentiary strategy rather than merely a tactic. Men who intend to lie about their whereabouts choose an hour when nobody can contradict them. The plan is thus two plans — an attack and a cover — and the second is discussed at greater length in this verse than the first.

**The Family Is Included**

The target is not Ṣāliḥ alone: *nubayyitannahu wa ahlahu*, him and his household. The Qur'an states it flatly and adds no comment.

Extending the attack to a man's family had a logic in a tribal society, since a surviving son or brother carries the obligation of retaliation, and a killing that leaves claimants alive is unfinished business. That logic is what turns an assassination into a massacre. Whatever these nine told themselves about Ṣāliḥ deserving what he got — the accusation of ill omen from two verses earlier supplies a candidate justification — no version of it covers the household. The chapter's fourth verse described people whose deeds are made appealing to them, and a plan that has grown from silencing a preacher to killing his family in their sleep is that process completed. Nobody in the room objects.

**The Alibi Is Prepared in Advance**

The second half of the verse is a rehearsed statement: we shall tell his heir that we did not witness the destruction of his family, and we are telling the truth. They are drafting testimony before the event it concerns.

Al-Qurṭubī explains the target of the lie: they wanted an excuse ready for the person on whom retaliation would fall — the *walī*, the heir who inherits the right to demand redress. This is a legal calculation, made inside a functioning system of blood justice. The conspirators are not planning to escape law; they are planning to satisfy it with a false statement. That tells us what kind of men they are more precisely than the murder does. They expect to be questioned, they expect the questioning to matter, and they have decided that a coordinated lie will hold. Nine notables who own the city's credit and its dignity have reason to think their word will be accepted.

**"We Are Definitely Telling the Truth"**

The rehearsed speech ends with an assertion of honesty: *wa innā la-ṣādiqūn*. They are composing, in advance, the sentence with which they will vouch for a lie, and they are adding the emphatic particles that Arabic reserves for insistence.

There is a dark precision in this. The most heavily emphasised claim to truthfulness in the chapter comes from men constructing a perjury, and the emphasis is itself the tell — a truthful witness does not usually need to certify himself. The Qur'an has been building a vocabulary around truth and certainty since verse 3, where the believers were marked by *yaqīn*, and verse 14, where Pharaoh's court was certain and denied anyway, and verse 27, where Sulaymān said he would see whether the hoopoe was truthful. Here the word arrives in the mouths of conspirators as decoration on a fabrication. The verse leaves them at the moment the plan is complete, and the next verse says what happened to it.

**The Timing in the Wider Account**

The Qur'an's other chapters supply the surrounding events. Thamūd had demanded a sign, been given a she-camel, and hamstrung it; Ṣāliḥ then told them they had a fixed number of days left. Several commentators place this conspiracy three days after the camel was killed, in the interval Ṣāliḥ had named.

If that placement is right, it makes the plot considerably stranger. These men had been told a deadline and are using the time inside it to arrange a murder — hastening the evil before the good, exactly as verse 46 described. They are also, on that reading, attempting to kill a man whose prediction of their destruction has not yet been falsified, which is a poor use of a countdown. The chronology comes from the commentators rather than from the text of this chapter. [UNVERIFIED: the three-day interval between the hamstringing of the she-camel and this conspiracy is a tafsīr reconstruction; the Qur'an does not date the plot in this sūrah.]
