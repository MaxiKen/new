## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:3

> The disbelievers say, “The Hour will never come to us.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Yes—by my Lord, the Knower of the unseen—it will certainly come to you!” Not ˹even˺ an atom’s weight is hidden from Him in the heavens or the earth; nor anything smaller or larger than that, but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Denial Phrased as a Personal Exemption**

The objection is not that an hour of reckoning is impossible in principle. It is narrower and more revealing than that: *lā taʾtīnā al-sāʿah* — it will not come **to us**. The pronoun carries the weight. These speakers are not cosmologists denying an end to the world; they are men claiming an exemption from one. That is why the reply, when it comes, fastens on the same pronoun and turns it back: it will certainly come **to you**. Elsewhere the Qur'an records the same posture in fuller form, where those addressed dismiss the promise as guesswork: *"We do not know what the Hour is! We think it is no more than speculation, and we are not convinced ˹that it will ever come˺"* (45:32). The tense in the Arabic here is the imperfect, which covers the habitual as well as the future, so the sentence describes a thing they kept saying rather than a single remark. An opinion repeated often enough stops feeling like an opinion, and the sūrah is describing men who have talked themselves into safety.

**An Oath the Prophet Is Ordered to Swear**

The response begins with *qul balā* — say, yes. *Balā* is a specialized Arabic particle with no single English equivalent: it is used only to contradict a negative statement, so it means "no, the opposite is true" while sounding like agreement. Then comes the oath: *wa-rabbī*, by my Lord. This is unusual. The Prophet is rarely instructed to swear, and searching the corpus of these translations shows only three places where he is told to answer a denial about the next world with a personal oath by his Lord — this verse, *"Yes, by my Lord! Most certainly it is true! And you will have no escape"* (10:53), and *"Yes, by my Lord, you will surely be resurrected"* (64:7). All three concern the same disputed fact. The oath is not there to add information; the argument that follows does that. It is there because a claim about the unseen cannot be settled by producing evidence in the ordinary way, and the messenger is made to stake his own honesty on it in public. The attached title — Knower of the unseen — names precisely the qualification the deniers said nobody had.

**The Smallest Unit the Language Could Supply**

*Mithqāl dharrah* is usually rendered "an atom's weight," and the phrase deserves accuracy rather than anachronism. *Mithqāl* is a weight-unit from the vocabulary of the market scales; *dharrah* in classical usage means the tiniest visible speck, the mote that shows in a shaft of sunlight, and was also used of the smallest species of ant. The expression is therefore drawn from ordinary commerce and ordinary eyesight: the least quantity a merchant's balance could register. Nothing in the word requires modern physics, and nothing in it excludes a smaller referent either, which is why the verse immediately adds *nor anything smaller*. The same measure appears where deeds are being weighed — *"So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it"* (99:7) — and where fairness is being guaranteed — *"Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom’s weight"* (4:40). Choosing a market unit to describe divine knowledge is itself an argument aimed at merchants. The men of Mecca understood exactly how small a quantity their scales could catch, and the verse tells them the reckoning is finer than their instruments.

**Both Directions Are Closed: Smaller and Larger**

Having named the smallest unit, the verse blocks the two escapes available to anyone who wants to slip beneath or above the audit: *nor anything smaller or larger than that*. The first half answers the man who assumes his small sins are beneath notice; the second answers the man who assumes his large crimes are too big to be filed, too tangled in politics and circumstance to be assigned to him personally. Both assumptions collapse in the same clause. Luqmān presses the smaller half into a father's advice to his son, complete with the hiding places a child might imagine: *"˹Even˺ if a deed were the weight of a mustard seed—be it ˹hidden˺ in a rock or in the heavens or the earth—Allah will bring it forth"* (31:16). The rhetorical construction is worth noticing. The verse does not assert exhaustive knowledge as an abstract attribute and leave the reader to apply it. It names the borderline case, then extends the rule past the border in both directions, so that the mind cannot finish the sentence with an exception still standing.

**A Perfect Record Turns Knowledge Into Evidence**

The final clause shifts from knowing to writing: everything is *fī kitābin mubīn*, in a clear and perfect Record. Knowledge that is never externalized can be doubted by the person it concerns; a record can be produced. The Qur'an keeps this vocabulary consistent. The same phrase closes the verse on the falling leaf and the buried grain (6:59), and the same phrase closes a verse addressed to the Prophet about every act performed while the revelation is being recited (10:61). Elsewhere the Book speaks of scales set up on the Day of Judgment so that *"no soul will be wronged in the least"* (21:47), and of a *"Preserved Tablet"* (85:22) in which the whole is inscribed. Bringing writing into an argument about the Hour also meets the deniers on their own ground. Commercial Mecca lived by written contracts and witnessed debts precisely because memory is disputable and ink is not. A reckoning backed by a document is a reckoning that can be contested in the open, which is what the verse is promising.

**Why This Answer Is Placed Before Any Description of the Hour**

The sūrah could have replied to the denial by describing the terrors of the Day, and later passages do exactly that. Answering first with an argument about knowledge is a structural choice. If the objection is that the Hour is a fabrication, then descriptions of it merely restate the thing under dispute; what the objection actually requires is a demonstration that the universe is the kind of place in which an exact reckoning is possible at all. The two verses just passed built that case — ownership of both realms (34:1), and knowledge of everything moving in either direction (34:2) — so this verse can now draw the conclusion the deniers were trying to avoid. A world fully owned, fully known and fully recorded is a world that can be judged. What remains unstated here, and comes next, is what such a reckoning is finally for.
