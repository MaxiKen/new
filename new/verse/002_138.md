## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:138

> This is the ˹natural˺ Way of Allah. And who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And we worship ˹none but˺ Him.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Behind the Translation**

The Arabic term rendered here as the natural way of God is built from a root meaning to dye or to colour, and it carries the image of cloth being plunged into a liquid until it takes on its colour. Several older translations rendered the word as the baptism of God, since immersion is common to both images, and the range of the term is what makes the sentence so suggestive.

Read with that image, the verse describes the believers as having been dipped into something that has changed what they are, so that the religion is not an ornament worn on the surface but the colour of the substance itself. The Book uses physical images for spiritual matters frequently, and this is one of its most memorable.

**The Primordial Nature**

Commentators connected the term with the natural disposition God has instilled in human beings, and the sūrah's later verse about the natural way of God states that this disposition admits no change. The connection gives the sentence its force in the argument of the passage.

The religion being defended is not presented as one tradition among several that a person might adopt by choice. The Book describes it as the original condition of human beings, which the prophets and their scriptures restore, and the image of dyeing fits that claim. A colour that is already in the fabric is not added to it but revealed by it, and the verse presents the religion of Abraham in that light.

**The Range of Readings**

The commentators offered several explanations of the term. One took it as the natural disposition that human beings bear as cloth bears its original colour. Another read it as the way God deals with His creation, since the Book uses the same root for the established pattern of His action that does not change. A third identified it with worship, purification, or the religion itself.

The readings do not compete. God's way, His religion, the purification of the worshipper, and the unspoiled nature of the human being all converge on the same point, which is that the religion taught by the messengers corresponds to what is already written into the human creature and to the pattern by which God deals with His servants.

The differences among the recitations do not change the sense of the sentence, since each of them points to the same idea of an inborn disposition or an appointed pattern. The community’s scholars used the variety to show that the verse concerns something placed in the human being rather than a rule imposed from outside. What the reader takes from the verse is that the religion it names is older than the communities that argue about it.

**The Contrast With a Ritual Claim**

Some commentators noted that the communities addressed in this passage practised a rite of baptism, and they read the verse as asserting that the true dyeing and the true purification come from God rather than from any human ceremony. The sentence would then be answering a claim of the same kind as the claim to guidance in the previous verse.

The point does not depend on that historical reference. What the verse states is that the change of the person belongs to God, and that no community administers it as a rite. The community reciting the sentence affirms that its colouring comes from its Lord, and the image excludes the possibility that it could be applied by any human authority.

**"Who Is Better Than Allah in Ordaining a Way?"**

The middle clause is a rhetorical question of the kind the sūrah uses to close an argument, and it turns the comparison into a statement about authority. If God has given a way, the question of which way is better is settled, and the interrogation simply invites the hearer to acknowledge it.

The Book uses similar questions throughout this passage, and each one reduces a dispute to a point on which the answer is already known. The community is not being asked to weigh competing traditions; it is being asked whether any ordinance could be preferable to the one God has given, and the sentence leaves the conclusion to the reader.

**The Closing Confession**

The sentence ends with a declaration of worship, and the Arabic places the community's devotion before the pronoun in a form that emphasises exclusivity. What the believers say is that they worship Him alone, and the clause matches the confession of Abraham in the surrounding passage, who was described as one who was not a polytheist.

The verse therefore completes the answer given to the two communities in the preceding verses. They invited the believers to adopt their religious identity, and the sūrah answers with a statement about the way God has ordained and about the worship the community offers. The claim being made is not that Islam is one option among several but that it is the way God has given and the relation to God that human beings were made for.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Sequence**

The sentence follows immediately on the assurance that God will spare the believers from the evil of those who oppose them, and it supplies the footing on which the community stands while the dispute continues. What it offers is not a strategy but an identification: this is the way, this is the One who ordained it, and this is the worship the community offers.

The sentence belongs to the group of statements that close the passage on Abraham, and it takes up the vocabulary the sūrah has used from its opening pages onward. The religion is not described here by the name of a community or by the practice of a particular shrine; it is described as a disposition woven into the human constitution by the One who made it. The reader who has followed the sūrah from the account of Adam through the building of the House arrives at a sentence that gathers the whole sequence into a single claim about what people were made for.

**The Image as a Whole**

The image of dyeing is one of the few in the Book that describes the religion as something done to a person rather than something a person does. The two halves of the religion are held together in the sentence: the colouring is God's act, and the worship is the community's response. The verse leaves the reader with both, and the confession of faith in the previous verse supplies the content that the following verses will develop.
