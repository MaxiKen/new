## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:138

> This is the ˹natural˺ Way of Allah. And who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And we worship ˹none but˺ Him.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Word, One Occurrence**

The verse in Arabic opens with two words only: *ṣibghat Allāh* — the dye of God, or as this translation renders the sense, the natural Way of God. The root *ṣabagha* means to dye, to plunge cloth into coloring liquid, and more generally to cause someone to enter into something. *Ṣibghah* is the dye itself, or the coloration it leaves. This is the word's only occurrence in the entire Qur'an — a hapax — and the singularity is part of its power: a term with no prior Quranic usage to lean on, chosen once, for this one place, at the climax of the argument about whose religion is true. Because the word is unique, the exegetes have read it in several directions at once, and the readings turn out to converge rather than compete: God's coloration is His nature stamped on the soul, His unchanging way with creation, His religion, and His purification, all in one image.

**The Color Worn From Birth**

The primary reading takes the dye as the *fiṭrah* — the primordial nature every human being bears the way a cloth bears its original color. Al-Thaʿālibī's explanation is exact: the ṣibghah is the upright religion God instilled in human nature, the religion named in the sūrah of the Romans — *"the natural Way of Allah which He has instilled in ˹all˺ people"* (30:30) — the same verse that uses the word *ḥanīf* which appeared in the answer about Abraham's faith three verses back (2:135). The chain of connection is deliberate: the faith of Abraham, the upright, is the faith of unspoiled nature, and God's dye is that faith as a fact of creation rather than a choice of sect. A person does not acquire the color at conversion; he is dipped in it at his making, and every religion that arrives later either matches the dye or paints over it.

**An Answer to a Rite**

The reading was sharpened by the religious landscape of Arabia, where Christian communities practiced baptism as the rite of entry into faith — immersion in water as the sign of belonging. Some commentators understood this verse to answer that rite directly: God's baptism, His dyeing of the soul at creation, is superior to any water a church administers, because the divine coloration is original, universal, and indelible, while the human rite is later, particular, and external. The question the verse poses — "who is better than Allah in ordaining a way?" — is rhetorical in the strictest sense: there is no candidate. Every instituted rite, on this reading, is measured against the instituting act of God Himself, who dyed the whole species in the color of upright recognition of Him before any priest existed to pour water. The claim is not that rites are worthless but that they are second — responses to a coloration that came first.

**The Dye as God's Unchanging Way**

A second family of readings takes ṣibghah as *sunnat Allāh* — God's wont, His unchanging manner of acting toward the world. The Qur'an uses the phrase repeatedly and always with the same emphasis on constancy: *"˹This has been˺ Our way with the messengers We sent before you. And you will never find any change in Our way"* (17:77), and *"˹This is˺ Allah's way, already long established ˹in the past˺. And you will find no change in Allah's way"* (48:23). Under this reading the verse says that the religion being preached is not an innovation but God's established procedure: the way He has always colored souls, sent prophets, and judged communities. The rhetorical question that follows then carries a historical weight — who is better than God at this, than the One whose method has never varied across the whole procession of nations the sūrah has just recounted?

**Religion, Purification, and One Image**

Other exegetes heard in ṣibghah simply God's religion (*dīn*), and others heard purification — the dye as the washing that cleanses. Al-Rāzī mapped the grammatical options with care: the phrase can stand as an imperative, "follow the dye of God"; it can reach back to Abraham's creed in 2:135 as the thing to be followed; or it can attach adverbially to the confession of 2:136, "we believe in God — dyed, as it were, in His religion." The interpretations are complementary rather than contradictory, because the image of dyeing contains them all at once. Dyeing is a religion's entry rite, a purification, a permanent change of state, and the revealing of the cloth's intended color. A single well-chosen metaphor can carry a whole theology, and this one carries the theology of the passage: God's coloring of the human soul is original, it is His own doing, no rival can match it, and the sects that quarrel over it are arguing about whose water is better while the dye was never water's to give.

**"And We Worship None but Him"**

The verse ends by dropping the metaphor and speaking plainly: and we are His worshippers. The shift from image to declaration does the final work. All the readings of ṣibghah — nature, wont, religion, purification — issue in one posture, and the posture is worship of the One who does the dyeing. The first-person plural continues the voice of the community's confession from two verses earlier: this is what we say, we who believe without distinction between the prophets. The grammar of the whole movement is worth naming: the creed was stated (2:136), its consequence for others drawn (2:137), and now its ground is given — the coloration of God Himself — and its fruit declared: worship. A religion that began as a list of things believed ends as an act performed. That is the shape the sūrah wants faith to have, and the hapax at its center is the pivot on which belief turns into belonging.
