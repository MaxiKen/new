## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:5

> As for those who strive to discredit Our revelations, it is they who will suffer the ˹worst˺ torment of agonizing pain.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Effort, Not Mere Opinion, Is What Is Being Punished**

The Arabic is *alladhīna saʿaw fī āyātinā muʿājizīn*, and the first verb decides the meaning of the sentence. *Saʿā* is a verb of physical exertion — to run, to walk briskly, to put effort into a task; it is the same root used for the ritual hurrying between Ṣafā and Marwah, and for earning a living. The people described here are not doubters who kept their doubts to themselves. They worked at their opposition. *Muʿājizīn* then names the aim of that work: to render something *ʿājiz*, incapable, frustrated, unable to deliver on what it promised. So the picture is of men labouring to make the revelation look impotent — arranging for the recitation to be drowned out, buying stories to rival it, spreading the rumour of madness that this sūrah will later quote in their own words (34:8). The distinction between honest disbelief and organised sabotage runs through the whole Qur'anic treatment of the matter, and this verse punishes the second. A man may fail to be convinced; that is one thing. Setting out to ensure that nobody else is convinced is a different act with a different consequence.

**A Sentence Built as the Exact Mirror of the One Before It**

Verse 4 and verse 5 are constructed as a matched pair, and reading them as a unit shows how tightly the Qur'an builds its contrasts. Both begin by naming a group by what it did rather than what it called itself. Both use the emphatic construction *ulāʾika lahum* — it is **they** who will have — which in Arabic isolates the group and assigns the outcome to them specifically, with the implication that it belongs to nobody else. The one promises forgiveness and an honourable provision; the other promises torment of agonizing pain. Even the rhythm of the two verses matches. This is not ornament. Placing the two destinations in identical grammatical frames removes the suggestion that reward is the system's normal output and punishment an exception forced on a reluctant judge, or the reverse. Both are outputs of the same reckoning described in verse 3, produced by the same record, and distinguished only by what the parties did with the revelation that reached them.

**Doubling the Words for Pain, and the Old Reading That Explains It**

The closing phrase piles one word of suffering on another: *ʿadhābun min rijzin alīm*, a punishment of *rijz* that is painful. *Rijz* is an unusual choice. It appears in the Qur'an for the plague-like scourges sent on Pharaoh's people (7:134), and in al-Anfāl for the defilement or agitation that Satan casts into a heart, which the rain washes away (8:11). Its root carries the sense of something unstable, unclean and distressing at once. Early readers of the Arabic text noticed that the vowel on *alīm* could be read two ways, attaching the adjective either to the punishment or to the *rijz* itself, which yields "a painful punishment of defilement" alongside "the torment of a painful punishment." Both readings survive in the tradition and neither changes the doctrine. What the doubling achieves is a refusal of the comfortable idea that punishment in the next world is simply the absence of reward. The vocabulary here is drawn from plague and pollution — conditions that spread, cling, and cannot be washed off by the sufferer.

**The Same People, Tracked Across the Book**

This exact description recurs, which lets the phrase be read as a standing category rather than a one-off insult. Al-Ḥajj places the identical clause in a verse that names their address: *"But those who strive to discredit Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Hellfire"* (22:51), immediately after describing the believers in the same words Sabaʾ used a verse earlier. Al-Jāthiyah attaches the same closing phrase to a different subject: *"And those who deny their Lord’s revelations will suffer the ˹worst˺ torment of agonizing pain"* (45:11). Al-Rūm supplies the courtroom image: those who denied the signs and the meeting with their Lord *"will be confined in punishment"* (30:16). The sūrah itself will return to these people once more before it finishes (34:38), which makes them one of the few groups Sabaʾ names twice. The repetition across chapters is how the Qur'an builds a definition: not by supplying a dictionary entry, but by showing the same description in enough settings that its boundaries become clear.

**What "Our Revelations" Covers, and Why the Breadth Matters**

*Āyāt* is among the widest words in the Book. It means a sign, a mark, a token, and it is used for the verses of the Qur'an, for the natural order read as evidence, for the miracles given to earlier prophets, and for the events that precede the end of time. Nothing in this verse narrows it, so all of these are in range. That breadth changes who is implicated. A man who never argued a line of scripture in his life but worked hard to make sure nobody around him could read the evidence of the created world as evidence — that man is inside the description as written. Sabaʾ makes the connection itself a few verses later, when it points at the sky and the ground overhead and underfoot and calls the arrangement *"a sign for every servant who turns ˹to Allah˺"* (34:9). Signs of the second kind are harder to discredit than recited verses, which is perhaps why the sūrah keeps returning to them when the argument over the Qur'an's authorship reaches deadlock.

**A Warning Aimed at Organisers Rather Than Ordinary Deniers**

The social reality behind this verse is worth stating plainly, because it explains the severity. In Mecca the opposition to the new message was not a diffuse mood; it was coordinated by men with standing, money and reach, and the sūrah later records their side of the argument in detail — the mockery of resurrection (34:7), the charge of fabrication and madness (34:8), the boast of wealth and children (34:35), the accusation of sorcery (34:43). Those verses describe speech. This one describes work. The people it condemns are the ones who converted private rejection into a programme, and the Qur'an consistently treats the leadership of misguidance as a heavier matter than being misled — a distinction the sūrah itself will dramatise later when followers and leaders argue before their Lord. Having set the two outcomes side by side, the passage now turns to a third group, whose response to the revelation was neither sabotage nor indifference.
