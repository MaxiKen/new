## Sūrah Fāṭir 35:12

> The two bodies of water are not alike: one is fresh, palatable, and pleasant to drink and the other is salty and bitter. Yet from them both you eat tender seafood and extract ornaments to wear. And you see the ships ploughing their way through both, so you may seek His bounty and give thanks ˹to Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Difference Does Not Cancel Shared Provision**

The verse begins by insisting that the two waters are not alike. One satisfies thirst; the other is too salty and bitter for ordinary drinking. Their difference is obvious to anyone who encounters them, yet the Qur'an does not stop at contrast. It asks the observer to notice what Allah brings from both. The waters retain their distinct qualities while becoming sources of food, ornament, travel, and livelihood.

This is a lesson in the variety of divine provision. Allah need not make all parts of creation identical in order to make them useful. A person may prefer the fresh water, but the sea has its own gifts and functions. The distinction between the two also keeps gratitude from becoming narrow. Blessing is not limited to what directly meets the first human need. The world contains layers of usefulness that appear when people observe carefully and work lawfully within the order Allah established.

**Food Comes From Environments People Cannot Create**

Fresh seafood is available from both bodies of water. The verse calls it tender meat, pointing to a living resource that human beings do not manufacture. They may catch, preserve, and prepare it, but they do not originate the creatures beneath the surface or command their abundance. The gift is therefore a visible sign of provision hidden in an environment that people cannot fully control.

The Qur'an makes a similar point in 16:14, where the sea is described as a source of food, adornment, and vessels that move through it. The repeated pairing of nourishment and movement shows that the sea serves both subsistence and civilization. Yet the benefits remain vulnerable to waste and injustice. A grateful society takes from the water without pretending it owns the order that makes taking possible. It protects the gift because gratitude includes restraint.

**Beauty and Utility Meet in the Same Gift**

Ornaments are mentioned alongside food. Pearls and coral, identified in 55:22, turn the sea's hidden life into objects of beauty. The Qur'an does not treat adornment as automatically vain. Beauty can be enjoyed when it remains lawful, modest, and detached from arrogance. The danger lies not in the existence of ornament but in allowing ornament to define a person's rank or to become a cause of oppression.

The pairing of food and adornment also broadens the meaning of provision. Allah supplies what keeps the body alive and what brings legitimate beauty into human life. The same sea can provide a meal for one person and material for a craft or trade for another. Diverse needs are met through one created realm. This variety directs praise toward the Provider rather than toward the object. The pearl is beautiful, but its beauty is a sign of the One who placed value and form within creation.

**Ships Reveal Human Skill Within Divine Permission**

Ships plough through both waters. The verb suggests a deliberate cutting path, and the image includes human craftsmanship, navigation, trade, and courage. The verse does not deny human skill; it places that skill inside the larger gift of water, wind, material, buoyancy, and knowledge. A ship is an achievement, but the conditions that allow it to move are not an achievement of the shipbuilder.

That balance matters for civilization. People may seek provision through travel and commerce, but they should not turn success into a claim of self-creation. The sea can carry a vessel and also expose its fragility. A calm voyage may be followed by danger, and a port's prosperity may depend on conditions no merchant commands. The Qur'an uses the ship as a sign of both competence and dependence. Skill deserves thanks, while the power behind its field of operation deserves worship.

The sea therefore becomes a school of proportion. Take what is permitted, acknowledge what cannot be controlled, and do not confuse access with ownership. A profitable voyage may produce gratitude when it reveals dependence, but it produces arrogance when success is credited entirely to human cleverness. The verse calls the beneficiary back to the Giver at the moment of benefit.

The verse quietly rejects a life of extraction without acknowledgment. Eating, trading, and wearing are not morally complete until the heart recognizes the One who made them possible. Gratitude turns use into stewardship and prevents abundance from becoming permission to damage the sea or exploit the people whose labor reaches it.

For the grateful heart, benefit becomes a reminder of the Benefactor.

**Seeking Bounty Ends in Giving Thanks**

The final purpose is not consumption alone. People travel, trade, eat, wear, and profit so that they may give thanks to Allah. Gratitude here follows reflection: once the distinct waters and their shared benefits are noticed, the beneficiary is directed to the Benefactor. Thankfulness includes speech, but it also includes lawful earning, fair exchange, avoidance of waste, and generosity toward those who lack access to the same gifts.

The verse also teaches that seeking bounty is not opposed to devotion. Work on the sea, fishing, travel, and trade can be acts of worship when they remain within lawful limits and are accompanied by recognition of Allah. Profit is not the final purpose; it is a means placed inside a larger moral purpose. The two waters differ, but both are governed by one Lord. Their food, ornaments, and ships are signs that creation is full of varied paths to gratitude.
