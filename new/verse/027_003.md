## Sūrah an-Naml 27:3

> ˹those˺ who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Belief Defined by Three Tests, Not by a Claim**

The word *believers* from the previous verse is now given content, and the content is specific enough to be checked. Two of the three items are actions with times, amounts and witnesses; the third is an inward certainty that shows itself in how a person treats everything else. No sentiment appears in the list, no declaration of loyalty, no self-description. A reader who wants to know whether the promise of guidance and good news applies to him is handed three measurements rather than a mood.

The structure is worth noticing. Prayer is a duty owed to God alone, alms is a duty owed to God through other people, and certainty about the Hereafter is the conviction that makes both worth performing. Vertical, horizontal, and the foundation underneath. Al-Qurṭubī reads the triad as covering the whole of religion in miniature: worship of the Creator, obligation to the creation, and belief in the accounting that gives each its weight. Remove any one and the other two lose their shape — prayer without the Hereafter becomes ritual habit, charity without it becomes philanthropy, and certainty without either becomes an opinion.

**"Establish" Is a Stronger Verb Than "Perform"**

The Arabic does not say they pray; it says they *establish* prayer, *yuqīmūn al-ṣalāh*. The verb comes from the root *q-w-m*, to stand upright, and in this form it means to set something up straight, to make it stand and keep standing. A crooked wall can be built; it cannot be *established* in this sense. Applied to prayer, the word covers the physical uprightness of the worshipper, the correctness of the act, and its steady repetition over time.

Commentators have drawn out each of those layers. Ibn Kathīr takes establishment to include performing prayer at its proper times with its conditions met, not merely going through its motions. Others add constancy: a prayer offered once is performed, but a prayer offered through every season of a life is established. The distinction has a practical edge. Arabic has a perfectly ordinary verb for doing a prayer, and the Qur'an consistently avoids it in favour of this one. What is being described is not an event in a person's schedule but a fixed structure in a person's life, and the difference is exactly the difference between a tent and a building.

**Zakāh: A Word That Means Growth Before It Means Payment**

*Zakāh* is usually translated as alms-tax, and the translation is accurate for the institution. The word's root, *z-k-w*, means to grow, to increase, and to be pure. Both senses feed the term: wealth from which the due portion is taken becomes lawful and clean, and the act of giving is described as the thing that makes the remainder grow rather than shrink. Arabic thus names the obligation by its effect, not by its cost.

The pairing of prayer and alms runs through the whole Qur'an, appearing dozens of times in almost every kind of chapter, early and late. The combination survives across every legal register of the Book — in commands to the Children of Israel, in descriptions of Ismāʿīl, in the terms offered to former enemies. Its persistence answers a permanent temptation, which is to treat devotion and generosity as alternatives, so that ritual excuses a closed hand or charity excuses an empty prayer mat. The Qur'an never lets them separate. Where one appears as an identifying mark of faith, the other is nearly always standing beside it, and this verse keeps the pattern exactly.

**Certainty Is a Higher Bar Than Belief**

The third item shifts vocabulary. The verse does not say they *believe* in the Hereafter; it says they are certain of it, *yūqinūn*, from *yaqīn*, a word for knowledge that has no doubt left in it. Arabic distinguishes the two states, and the Qur'an uses the distinction deliberately. Belief can coexist with a private hesitation. Certainty is what remains after the hesitation is gone.

Setting the bar there has consequences, because the Hereafter is the one article of faith with no sensory support at all. Prayer can be seen; poverty relieved by alms can be counted; the resurrection cannot be inspected. The Qur'an nonetheless asks for *certainty* on precisely this point, and the reason becomes visible in the next verse of this chapter, where disbelief in the Hereafter is identified as the root from which a whole distorted life grows. Ibn Kathīr understands the certainty here to cover the full set — resurrection, the reckoning, the Garden and the Fire — rather than a vague expectation of something after death. The definite content is what makes the conviction operational. A person who is sure there will be an accounting behaves differently from a person who suspects there might be.

**The Same Three Marks, Placed in a Different Argument**

This verse appears almost word for word at the opening of Sūrah Luqmān: *“those who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.”* (31:4). The repetition is exact, which makes the surrounding difference instructive. In Luqmān the Book is called a guide and mercy for *the good-doers*; here it is a guide and good news for *the believers*. Same description, two different labels attached to the same people.

Al-Baqarah performs a related move with a different list, marking its people by belief in the unseen, prayer, spending, acceptance of earlier revelation, and then closing on the same clause: *“and have sure faith in the Hereafter.”* (2:4). Certainty about the Hereafter is the fixed element across all three openings while the other items vary. That consistency is a claim in itself. Whatever else distinguishes the Qur'an's model believer, the non-negotiable is that he lives as though the accounting is real. The following verse turns to those who do not, and traces what happens to a life built on the opposite assumption.
