## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:12

> **And He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon. And the stars have been subjected by His command. Surely in this are signs for those who understand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Subjection Of The Great Bodies**

Wa-sakhkhara lakum al-layla wa-l-nahāra wa-l-shamsa wa-l-qamara — and He has subjected for your benefit the night and day, sun and moon. Sakhkhara, from s-kh-r, means to make subservient, to compel into service, to make something do work for another without resistance. The same verb was used for sea and will be used for birds. Here it is applied to celestial phenomena that humans cannot control: day and night alternate, sun and moon move, yet their alternation serves human life. Al-Ṭabarī explains taskhīr here as making them serve human interests: night for rest and concealment, day for work and seeking bounty, sun for light, heat, and ripening, moon for months and tides and gentle light. The phrase lakum, for your benefit, appears again, tying this verse to the previous catalogue where everything was for you. Even the most majestic bodies are for you, not you for them. This reverses the pagan tendency to worship sun and moon as gods. If they are subjected for you, they cannot be gods over you.

**Stars Subjected By His Command**

Wa-l-nujūmu musakhkharātun bi-amrihi — and the stars have been subjected by His command. Nujūm, stars, includes all stars, planets, and constellations. The construction musakhkharātun bi-amrihi emphasizes that their subjection is by His command, His amr, the same word that opened the sūrah. The stars follow orbits, appear and disappear, guide travelers, yet they do so by command, not by independent will. Al-Rāzī notes that stars are mentioned separately because their benefit is different: they guide at night on land and sea, they mark seasons, they adorn the sky. The Qur'an says elsewhere *And He made the stars for you to be guided thereby in darkness of land and sea* (6:97). In pre-Islamic Arabia, star lore was advanced; Arabs navigated by stars and attributed rain to star positions, saying *we were given rain by such-and-such star*. The Qur'an corrects this by saying stars are musakhkharāt, subjected, not actors. They are commanded, not commanders. This subtle polemic against astrolatry and star-based superstition is part of the sūrah's campaign against shirk.

**A Sign For Those Who Understand**

Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yaʿqilūn — surely in this are signs for those who understand. Yaʿqilūn comes from ʿa-q-l, to bind, to restrain, to understand, to use intellect that restrains from error. The shift from yatafakkarūn in verse 11 to yaʿqilūn here is purposeful. Tafakkur is reflection on details, like crops from one water; ʿaql is understanding of larger systems, like day and night and celestial orbits. Al-Zamakhsharī explains that day and night, sun and moon require ʿaql to see their regularity and to infer a Regulator. Anyone can see crops grow; it takes deeper understanding to see that night and day are subjected for you and to conclude that their Subjector must be One, Powerful, and Caring. The plural āyāt, signs, rather than āyah, indicates multiple signs within this one system: the alternation itself, the benefit of each part, the precision of timing, the fact that they serve without tiring.

**Day And Night As Mercy And Measure**

The Qur'an often pairs night and day as two mercies: *And among His signs is your sleep by night and by day and your seeking of His bounty* (30:23). Night is not absence of mercy but a mercy itself: it provides rest, coolness, cover, and a time for reflection. Day is mercy for work, seeking provision, and seeing. Together they create a rhythm that matches human biology. Modern science confirms that human circadian rhythm depends on light-dark cycles, but the Qur'an noted the benefit long before: night for sukūn, tranquility, day for movement. Al-Qurṭubī adds that without night, humans would be exhausted by constant light and heat; without day, they could not farm or travel. The subjection includes both the existence and the alternation. Allah did not create only day or only night; He created both and made them alternate in measure, as in 73:20: *Allah measures night and day*. This measurement is itself a sign of wisdom, not randomness.

**Sun And Moon: Two Lights With Different Roles**

Al-shamsa wa-l-qamara — sun and moon — are mentioned together throughout the Qur'an as two lights with different functions. Sun is diyā', intense light and heat; moon is nūr, reflected gentle light, as in 10:5: *He made the sun a radiant light and the moon a reflected light and measured for it stages that you might know number of years and calculation* (10:5). Sun ripens crops mentioned in previous verse; moon marks months for fasting, pilgrimage, and trade. Both are subjected, meaning they follow fixed courses that humans can rely on. Al-Rāzī notes that their subjection includes that they do not collide, do not deviate, do not need rest. They serve continuously. This continuous service, without wage or complaint, is presented as evidence that they are commanded, not divine. A god does not serve without ceasing for the benefit of others; a subjected creature does. The verse thus uses their very usefulness to disprove their divinity.

**How This Verse Connects To Guidance**

Verse 9 said upon Allah is to show the straight way. Verse 12 shows that He also shows the physical way via stars and celestial bodies. The link is subtle: just as stars guide travelers in darkness of land and sea, revelation guides hearts in darkness of ignorance. Both are subjected by His command. The sūrah will make this explicit in 16:16: *and landmarks, and by the stars they are guided*. The same verb hadā, to guide, is used for stars and for revelation. The reader is being taught to see guidance everywhere: in sky and in Book, both from same Commander. The verse ends with la-āyātin, surely signs, in plural, because the system contains many signs, yet it is one system under one command, pointing to One God. The invitation to yaʿqilūn is therefore both intellectual and spiritual: use your intellect to bind your understanding to its true object, Allah alone.
