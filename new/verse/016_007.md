## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:7

> **And they carry your loads to ˹distant˺ lands which you could not otherwise reach without great hardship. Surely your Lord is Ever Gracious, Most Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Beasts That Make Distant Lands Near**

Wa-taḥmilu athqālakum ilā baladin lam takūnū bālighīhi illā bi-shiqqi al-anfus — and they carry your heavy loads to a land you would not reach except with great hardship to yourselves. Athqāl is plural of thiql, heavy load, baggage, goods that weigh down. The verse describes a specific function of cattle, especially camels, that made civilization possible in Arabia: long-distance transport of goods, water, and people across deserts where human strength fails. Al-Ṭabarī explains balad here as any distant town or region that a person needs to reach for trade or migration. Without these animals, the journey would require shiqq al-anfus, literally splitting of selves, an idiom for extreme effort that tears body and soul. The Qur'an uses vivid physical language to make the reader feel the alternative: without these carriers, you would have to carry the loads yourselves, and you would break. The blessing is therefore not only that loads move, but that you are spared breaking.

**Trade, Migration, And The Making Of Society**

For Makkans, this verse was not poetry but economics. Quraysh were traders. Their two annual journeys, to Yemen in winter and Syria in summer, mentioned in Sūrat Quraysh (106:2), depended entirely on camels bearing athqāl. Without camels, there is no trade; without trade, there is no Makkan prosperity. By saying these animals carry your loads to distant lands, the Qur'an reminds Quraysh that their wealth, which they attributed to their own skill and to the protection of their idols, in fact rides on the backs of animals Allah created and subjected. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the verse also applies to migration, hijrah, which would soon become central to the Muslim community. Those who would leave Makkah for Abyssinia and later Madinah would do so with camels carrying their families and goods. The verse thus anticipates the very experience of its listeners: the loads they would soon carry as emigrants are made possible by the same Creator they are being called to trust.

**Why The Verse Ends With Two Names**

Inna rabbakum la-ra'ūfun raḥīm — surely your Lord is Ever Gracious, Most Merciful. This is the first occurrence of divine names in the blessings catalogue, and it comes after mentioning hardship spared. Ra'ūf comes from ra'fah, a tenderness that prevents harm before it occurs. Raḥīm is mercy that brings good and forgives. The pair appears often in the Qur'an where human weakness is highlighted. Al-Rāzī explains that ra'fah is relevant here because carrying loads would otherwise cause shiqq al-anfus, a splitting of the self. To spare humans that splitting is ra'fah — gentle prevention. To then provide the animals that not only spare but also enable trade and growth is raḥmah — mercy that gives beyond mere protection. The names also answer verse 4's description of humans as contentious challengers. Even though humans challenge, the Lord remains gracious and merciful, continuing to provide carriers for their loads. The lordship, rabbakum, your Lord, is possessive and nurturing, not distant. It is the Lord who raised you from a drop who now spares you from breaking under your own baggage.

**The Concept Of Taskhīr Behind The Scene**

Though the word taskhīr, subjection, does not appear in this verse, the idea underlies it. Animals that are stronger than humans — a camel can kill a man — nonetheless allow themselves to be loaded, led, and directed. This obedience is not natural in the sense of automatic; it is a subjection placed by Allah. The Qur'an makes this explicit later: *And We have subjected them to them, so some they ride and some they eat* (36:72). Al-Qurṭubī points out that a child can lead a camel that could crush him, which shows that its docility is a divine gift, not a human achievement. The verse invites reflection on this: why does a powerful animal submit to a weaker human? The answer in the sūrah is that Allah subjected it for you. Gratitude therefore requires acknowledging the Subjugator, not merely using the subjected. When the Makkans loaded their caravans and thanked their idols for protection, they misdirected thanks. The camel's submission came from Allah, not from Hubal.

**Hardship As A Measure Of Blessing**

The phrase illā bi-shiqqi al-anfus — except with great hardship — quantifies blessing by imagining its absence. The Qur'an often does this to make ingratitude visible. It asks you to imagine life without the blessing, then to feel its weight. Without cattle carrying loads, every family would have to carry its own goods, every trader his own merchandise, every migrant his own children and water. The society that Quraysh prided itself on would collapse. By forcing that imagination, the verse makes the blessing unmistakable. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that shiqq can also be read as shaqq, with different vowel, meaning half, so the phrase could mean you would not reach the land except with half your selves gone, meaning after losing half your strength or half your number. Both readings converge on the same point: without these animals, reaching distant lands would cost you yourselves. The blessing is therefore measured in saved lives and preserved strength.

**Translation Note On Distant Lands**

The translators add ˹distant˺ in brackets before lands to capture the implication of balad here. Balad alone means town, land, country, but context indicates a distant destination that requires effort, not a neighboring village. The brackets show interpretive addition. Athqālakum, your loads, includes both physical loads and metaphorical burdens, though tafsīr focuses on physical goods. The word shiqq in classical lexicons means hardship that splits or cleaves, from the root sh-q-q, to split. Lisān al-ʿArab defines shiqq al-anfus as reaching the point where the soul is almost split from the body due to effort. By using such strong language for the alternative, the Qur'an elevates the animal from mere tool to savior from self-destruction. The verse thus completes a three-step argument: you are weak from a drop, you are contentious, yet your Lord is gracious and merciful enough to create for you carriers that prevent your breaking.
