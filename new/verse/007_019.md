## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:19

> ˹Allah said,˺ “O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat from wherever you please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Dwell in the Garden**

The verse reports the address to Adam to dwell with his wife in the Garden, and it belongs to the sequence of commands and permissions that the Book gives in its accounts of the first human pair. The sūrah of the Cow contains the parallel command, in which the pair are told to eat freely of the Garden and are warned against one tree (2:35). The two accounts agree on the liberty granted and on the single boundary.

The Book's presentation of the Garden in these passages is one of abundance and ease. The sūrah of Ṭā Hā states that the first human being was told that he would not go hungry or naked there, nor thirst nor suffer the heat (20:118–19). The verse in front of us states the same arrangement in its own words, and the single prohibition stands out against the extent of the permission.

**The Wife of Adam**

The verse names the wife of Adam, and the Book does not give her name in any of its accounts. The tradition of the community supplies the name Ḥawwāʾ, and the commentators discuss her creation on the basis of the sūrah of the Women, which states that God created people from a single soul and created from it its mate and spread abroad from the two a multitude of men and women (4:1). The verse in front of us presents the pair as the two human beings who receive the command.

The narrative's silence about her name is matched by its silence about any separate origin, and the Book's accounts of the creation and the prostration treat the two as the recipients of the same command and the same warning. The verses that follow the temptation state that both were deceived, both ate, both were exposed, and both repented. The sūrah's narration therefore keeps the pair together throughout the account.

**The Tree That Was Forbidden**

The verse forbids the approach to a particular tree, and the Book does not identify the tree by species. The sūrah of Ṭā Hā calls it the tree of everlastingness in the report of the tempter's words (20:120), and the commentary tradition offers identifications drawn from the reports and the earlier scriptures, including wheat, the fig, and the vine. The verse in front of us keeps to the prohibition, and the prohibition is what the narrative requires.

The wording forbids the approach rather than the eating alone, and the commentary tradition notes the wider boundary that the phrasing sets. What is forbidden is not only the act but the movement toward it, and the Book's instructions to the community elsewhere use the same manner of speaking when they forbid the paths that lead to a transgression. The temptation in the next verse is directed at that boundary.

**Permission and Boundary Together**

The verse combines a wide permission with a narrow prohibition, and the arrangement is characteristic of the Book's treatment of human conduct. The sūrah of the Cattle states the general principle when it says that God has not forbidden most of what the audience imagined to be forbidden, and its list of the actual prohibitions is short. This verse gives the first human pair the whole of the Garden except one tree.

The commentary tradition draws from the arrangement a lesson about the nature of the test. The prohibition is small in comparison with what is permitted, and the disobedience consisted in crossing a boundary that was plainly marked. The sūrah's later treatment of the audience's self-imposed food rules and its forbidden practices presents the reverse error, since the audience forbade what God had permitted while taking liberties that God had forbidden.

**The Warning of Wrongdoing**

The verse states that approaching the tree would make the pair wrongdoers, and the warning names the consequence in the same vocabulary the sūrah uses for the communities that were destroyed. The Book's description of wrongdoing as a harm that the person brings on himself appears in the sūrah of the Cow and the sūrah of Yūnus, and the warning in front of us states the outcome before the act. The pair's own confession after the fall uses the same word when they say that they have wronged themselves (7:23).

The choice of the word connects the first disobedience with the crimes of the nations. The sūrah's audience is warned about wrongdoing through the accounts of the destroyed communities, and the narrative of the Garden supplies the original instance. The wrong is committed first against the self, and the sūrah's teaching about the reckoning follows from that.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands nineteenth in the chapter, and it follows the sentence passed on the adversary and precedes his temptation of the pair. The narrative then reports the whisper, the false promise, the oath, the fall, the exposure, and the confession. The command in front of us establishes the conditions under which the temptation and the fall take place.

The reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening has been given the revelation, the command to follow it, the warning about protectors, the account of the reckoning, and the story of the adversary. The passage in front of us places the human pair inside the same narrative, and the sūrah's address to the children of Adam in the following passage is built on what happens to them.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports that Satan whispered to the pair in order to expose what had been hidden of their nakedness, and that he told them their Lord had forbidden the tree only to prevent them from becoming angels or from living forever (7:20). The prohibition in front of us is answered by the whisper, and the response of the pair follows in the verses after that.

The two verses together show the method of the adversary at work. A boundary is set, the boundary is described as a deprivation, and the false promise is offered as the reason for crossing it. The sūrah then recounts the crossing and its consequences.
