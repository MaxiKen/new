## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:25

> Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Warning and Promise, Paired**

The sūrah has just described a Fire prepared for those who reject. It now describes a Garden prepared for those who do not, and it does so without a pause. The pairing is the sūrah's constant method, and a reader who takes one half without the other has misread it: the same Book that threatens also promises, and in this opening passage the threat and the promise are separated by a single verse.

The instruction is addressed to the Prophet: *give good news* (2:25). His office was described earlier as *warner*, and the sūrah now names the other half of it. A messenger who only warned would be incomplete, and the community that receives only warnings would obey for the wrong reason — from fright rather than from desire.

**Gardens With Rivers Flowing Below**

The description is concrete and it is chosen for a desert audience. Water is the scarcest thing in the world the first hearers inhabited, and the promise leads with it: not a garden, but a garden *under which rivers flow* (2:25).

The preposition has caused discussion, and the point is worth getting right. It does not mean the water runs underground. It means the rivers run below the vegetation — beneath the trees and plantings of the Garden — which is a picture of irrigation from beneath, in a landscape where water on the surface is rare and moving water rarer. Others take it to mean the rivers run *through* the Gardens, or simply that the Gardens have water in them the way a garden has fruit in it. All three readings describe the same thing: growth that does not depend on rain.

**Fruit That Is Familiar and Not**

The strangest detail in the verse is the conversation. Given fruit, the inhabitants say *this is what we were given before* (2:25) — and the explanation offered is that they are served what is *mutashābih*, similar in appearance. The natural reading is the one the translation gives: the fruit looks the same and tastes different. Others took the similarity to mean uniform excellence, with no inferior specimens; others again held that the statement is an expression of wonder rather than a comparison, since nothing in the Hereafter shares anything with this world except a name.

The detail is doing something specific. It establishes that the inhabitants remember. *Aforetime* refers to this life, and the verse is spoken from the point of view of a person in the Garden recalling the world he came from. The life before is not erased; what is gone from it is the bitterness. The Quran states the same thing about the people of the Garden elsewhere: *there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve* (2:62).

**Pure Spouses**

The companions of the Garden are described as *pure spouses* (2:25). The commentators take the purity to cover what is physically distasteful — injury, menstruation, excretion, the ordinary indignities of a body — rather than to make a claim about virginity, which the major commentaries do not associate with this verse.

A parallel passage fills out the picture: *There they will have pure spouses, and We will place them under a vast shade* (4:57). The emphasis across these descriptions is on the absence of what spoils companionship in this life. What is promised is not intensification of appetite but the removal of everything that has ever made a relationship cost something.

**They Will Be There Forever**

The closing word is *khālidūn* — those who stay, those who remain. The refrain is one of the most frequent in the Quran and is applied to the inhabitants of both destinations. Its lexical sense is duration, and when it is promised by God it is equivalent to permanence, though several verses add phrases that have generated long debates about the exact sense.

For this verse's purposes the function is simpler. The Garden's defining feature, on the Quran's own account, is not its abundance but its permanence. Everything the first hearers valued was perishable by definition — water dried up, flocks died, a man's standing in his tribe lasted as long as he did. The promise here is of a good that cannot be lost, which is the one thing their world had no category for.

**Why the Pictures Are Material**

The objection is standard and old: why is the highest state described in terms of food, water, and spouses? The answer the commentators give is that the descriptions are not a substitute for spiritual realities but their expression. The Quran's claim is that the concrete goods of this life are genuine goods, given by God, and that the Garden is where they are finally freed from everything that made them temporary.

The wisdom, from the point of view of a person still living, is the familiarity. A promise made entirely in unknown terms would not be a promise at all — it would be a noise. The sūrah gives its hearers the shape of what is coming in the only vocabulary they have, and then tells them the one thing that matters about it: it will not end.

The verse also completes the arc that opened the sūrah. The first five verses described a community that believes in the unseen, prays, spends, and is certain about the Hereafter. This is the destination that certainty referred to. The sūrah has now stated both ends of the road it has been describing since 2:6, and from here it turns to the long work of showing what the road itself looks like.
