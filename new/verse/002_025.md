## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:25
> Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever.


**Expanded Commentary**

**Glad Tidings Set Against a Fire**

The previous verse ended with a Fire prepared for disbelievers, whose fuel is people and stones (2:24). This verse opens with the opposite preparation: *“Give good news to those who believe and do righteous deeds, that theirs are Gardens”* — *wa-bashshiri alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt*. The *waw* — “and” — that joins the two verses does not merely add information; it completes a symmetry: Fire for the persistent and the doubting, Gardens for the believing and the doing. The same verb *uʿiddat* — prepared — will reappear for the Gardens as for the Fire (*uʿiddat li-l-muttaqīn*, 3:133), so that the two abodes are presented as two preparations already made, toward one of which every reader is moving. The command is to the Prophet — *bashshir* — “give glad tidings,” so that the announcement is not a detached doctrine but a prophetic act of conveying joy, as the warning of the Fire was a prophetic act of conveying caution.

**Belief and Doing — Not Belief Alone**

The tidings are for *those who believe and do righteous deeds* — *āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt*. The pairing is not rhetorical doubling. The mindful were defined first by *who believe in the unseen* (2:3) and then by *establish prayer and spend* (2:3) and *are certain of the Hereafter* (2:4). The Gardens are now promised to those whose belief has taken the same two forms: assent and act. The sūrah has already shown that belief can be professed without being held (2:8), that deception can be attempted without perceiving it (2:9), that disease can be in the heart (2:10), that corruption can be renamed as reform (2:11), and that a trade can be profitless (2:16). The requirement *wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* excludes each of those counterfeits by requiring deeds that are *ṣāliḥāt* — sound, wholesome, set right — the same root *ṣ-l-ḥ* that the hypocrites claimed for themselves as *muṣliḥūn* (2:11) and that the earth was made to be after being *set right* (7:56). The doing that is promised a Garden is the doing that sets things right, not the doing that calls the corrupting right.

**Gardens With Rivers Flowing Beneath — Not Underground**

*Jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — “Gardens under which rivers flow.” The source notes that *min taḥtihā* does not mean the rivers are underground, as an English “below” might suggest, but that the water runs lower than the Garden’s vegetation, along the ground that the Garden covers (Ṭ), or that the rivers run *through* the Gardens (Z), or simply that such Gardens possess flowing water as a garden possesses grapes (R). The image inverts the hypocrites’ storm, where water poured from the sky as darkness, thunder, and lightning (2:19). Here water flows at the feet, ordered, perennial, and gentle. The desert listener who knew water as a wadi that appears and vanishes, who knew rivers as things that are almost never *upon* the ground and almost never flowing, hears in this Gardens-water a reversal of scarcity: the settling earth (*firāsh*, 2:22) that was given fruits as provision now yields Gardens where provision is not seasonal but flowing.

**“This Is What We Were Given Before” — Likeness Without Identity**

*“Whenever they are given a fruit therefrom as provision, they say, ‘This is what we were given before,’ and they were given a likeness of it”* — *kullamā ruziqū minhā min thamaratin rizqan qālū hādhā alladhī ruziqnā min qablu wa-utū bihi mutashābihā*. The scene is domestic: provision (*rizq*) arrives, and the recipients remark on its familiarity. The commentary preserves several hearings of the likeness (*mutashābih*). One hearing is that the fruits resemble earthly fruits in appearance but surpass them in taste, so that the likeness is in form with difference in reality; another is that all the fruit of the Garden is of the choicest kind, with none inferior, so that the provision does not vary in quality; a third is that the dwellers’ exclamation is not a botanical comparison at all but an expression of wonder, since there is nothing in common between the two worlds except names (IK). A further nuance, noted by Zamaksharī and Qurṭubī (Z, Q), is that *min qablu* — “before” — could refer not to earthly life but to earlier provision *within* the Garden, so that the remark is made about successive servings of heavenly fruit. Each hearing preserves the same point: the Garden’s provision is both continuous with and discontinuous from what was known.

**Why Familiar Objects Are Promised for an Unfamiliar World**

The wisdom of giving heavenly reward in familiar terms is not to reduce the heavenly to the earthly but to give earthly desire a ladder. The listener who knows fruit as a provision that arrives through rain between a floored earth and a roofed sky (2:22) is told that the Garden’s provision will arrive as fruit that looks like that fruit, so that the longing that was trained by earthly provision can be transposed. The verse adds the perspective of the Garden dweller precisely to show that the transposition is not forgotten: the dweller remembers *“what we were given before”* — whether before in the world (*dunyā*) or before in the Garden — and the memory is not bitter. The frequent Qur'anic refrain about the Garden — *“no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve”* (2:62) — assures that the continuity of names does not bring the continuity of anxiety. The provision is remembered without the scarcity that once accompanied it.

**Pure Spouses and the Purity They Signify**

*“Therein they have spouses made pure”* — *wa-lahum fīhā azwājun muṭahharah*. The commentators describe the purity as freedom from injury, from what is considered distasteful among bodily functions, and from the associations that trouble earthly intimacy, while noting that the verse itself does not dwell on the details that later literature elaborates (Ṭ, Z). The adjective *muṭahharah* — made pure, purified — uses the passive, indicating that the purity is given, not achieved, as the Gardens themselves are given. That passivity connects the verse to the larger pattern in which the earth was *made* a *firāsh* and the sky a *bināʾ* (2:22), and provision was *brought forth* as *rizqan lakum*. The Garden’s purity is the same kind of givenness at a higher degree. The *azwāj* — spouses, companions — are therefore not an addition to the Gardens but an image of the Gardens’ own character: companionship that is purified as the rivers are ordered and the fruits are familiar yet surpassing.

**Therein They Shall Abide — A Verb That Becomes a Promise**

*Wa-hum fīhā khālidūn* — “and therein they shall abide.” The phrase *khālidūn* and its variants — *they shall abide therein* — recur as a refrain for both Garden and Fire, lexically meaning to remain, to stay, to endure. As a promise from Allah, staying becomes perpetuity. The commentators note the supplementation that elsewhere makes the perpetuity explicit — *“abiding therein forever”* (*khālidīna fīhā abadan*, 4:57; 4:122; 9:100; 98:8) and the exception that appears in other contexts — *“save as your Lord wills”* (11:108) — which theologians have debated in the essay on death and afterlife. The verse in al-Baqarah itself adds *abadan* through the translation — *“there forever”* — so that the abiding is heard as everlasting. That everlasting is the counterpart to the Fire’s everlasting preparation (2:24). Both have been prepared; both are abiding; the human being, who was summoned to worship so that he may become mindful (*laʿallakum tattaqūn*, 2:21), is the one who, by believing and doing what is sound, is given the abiding that mindfulness was meant to secure.

**A Likeness That Protects Wonder**

Finally, *mutashābih* — likeness — protects wonder in both directions. For the earthly listener, it says the Garden will not be unrecognizable; for the Garden dweller, it says the earthly provision will not be forgotten, yet its bitterness will be. The provision that was once grown *for you* between earth and sky (2:22) and then doubted (2:23) and then defended by the Fire’s preparation (2:24) is now promised again, but as provision that looks like that provision and is not it, remembered as that provision and surpassing it. The likeness is the final form of the Book’s pedagogy: to give what is longed for in a shape that is known, and then to make the known shape the bearer of a joy that the known shape could only hint at.
