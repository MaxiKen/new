## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:25

> Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Good News, and the Shape of the Sentence**

The verse opens with an instruction to the Prophet ﷺ to announce glad tidings, and the Arabic verb carries the sense of news that changes a person's face. It arrives immediately after a warning about a fire prepared for the disbelievers, and the pairing is standard throughout the Book: two destinies, stated in consecutive sentences, with the hearer addressed in both.

What distinguishes the announcement here is the description attached to the people who receive it. They are named by belief and by righteous action in a single clause, so that the promise is not attached to an inner state alone. The sūrah has already described the mindful in similar terms, and the Book maintains that pairing consistently: the destination is promised to people whose faith has produced something.

**Rivers Running Under Gardens**

The Garden is described by its water, which is a description chosen for an audience that lived on the edge of a desert. The Arabic says the rivers run beneath the gardens, and the commentators took the phrase to mean that the water flows lower than the vegetation, which is the arrangement of any well-tended orchard and the opposite of what a traveller in Arabia expected from water in the ground.

The Book's paradisal water arrives elsewhere in greater variety: *“The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that in it are rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink, and rivers of pure honey. There they will ˹also˺ have all kinds of fruit, and forgiveness from their Lord.”* (47:15). Another verse summarises the permanence of the garden's resources in the same breath as its shade: *“The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that under it rivers flow; eternal is its fruit as well as its shade.”* (13:35). Water, fruit and shade are the three goods a desert dweller values most, and the Book promises all three in durable form.

**"This Is What We Were Given Before"**

The inhabitants of the Garden receive fruit and remark that they have been given this before, and the verse explains the remark: what they are served resembles what they knew. The commentators debated what the resemblance amounts to. Some held that the fruits are identical in kind with those of this world, so that the recognition is literal. Others read the sentence as surprise rather than memory, since nothing in the next life resembles this one except the names. The most commonly adopted reading is that the forms are similar while the taste is not, which fits the phrase the verse uses for likeness.

There is a practical generosity in the arrangement. A reward that could not be pictured at all would mean nothing to the people being addressed, so the Book describes a garden in the vocabulary of the gardens they knew, and then quietly raises what is inside it beyond comparison. The sūrah later uses the same root for verses whose full meaning eludes the reader, which suggests that likeness with a difference is one of the Book's ordinary ways of describing what human language can only approach.

**Spouses Made Pure**

The Garden is then said to contain spouses who are purified, and the commentators explained the purification in terms of this world's discomforts rather than in terms of status. They listed freedom from injury and illness, from the bodily functions and conditions that make people reluctant to be near one another, and from whatever would disturb the peace of the household. The major commentaries do not read the phrase as referring to virginity — a point worth stating because the translation's *pure* can suggest otherwise to an English reader.

What the clause contributes to the verse is companionship without friction. The Book's descriptions of the Garden regularly include relationships as well as goods, and it treats the household as part of the reward rather than as an accessory to it. A community that had endured exile, division and the loss of family ties in the migration is told that the home it lost is not the last word on the subject.

**A Stay Without an End**

The sentence closes with permanence, and the Book repeats that note so often that it becomes a refrain of the descriptions of both destinies. The Arabic word for abiding carries the sense of remaining, and the Book sometimes strengthens it with an explicit phrase for eternity, and sometimes qualifies it with a clause about God's will, which the commentators took as an acknowledgement of divine sovereignty rather than as a threat to the promise.

The promise of an unending stay is the part of the description that carries the most weight in the Book's moral argument. A reward that ends would eventually turn into a loss, and the sūrah's account of the hypocrites earlier used exactly that kind of arithmetic: a trade that produced no profit. The verse here uses the opposite language. What is given to the people described in it is given without a term limit, and the promise is attached to belief and good action rather than to tribe, lineage or wealth.

**The Two Destinies Stated Side by Side**

The verse was revealed in the same breath as the warning before it, and the pairing is one of the sūrah's structural habits. A fire is described as prepared; a garden is described as promised; the same audience is addressed in both sentences. What the Book is doing with this arrangement is not balancing terror with comfort but stating the two outcomes that follow from two ways of responding to the same revelation.

The order matters as well. The warning comes first because the argument of the passage has just reached its conclusion, and the good news then arrives to describe what the invitation leads to. Throughout the sūrah this pattern recurs: an argument, a warning about what refusal costs, and an announcement of what acceptance produces. In this verse the announcement is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ as an instruction to deliver it, which makes the glad tidings a duty rather than an optional comfort.
