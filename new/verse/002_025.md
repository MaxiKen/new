## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:25

> Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counter-Announcement, in the Same Breath**

One verse after the Fire is prepared, the imperative turns: *wa-bashshir* — and give good news. The command is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ and through him to everyone who will ever carry a message of joy, and its recipients are named in the Qur'an's standing formula: *alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — those who believe and do righteous deeds, faith joined to its works in the pairing this sūrah used at 2:3. The juxtaposition with verse 24 is not decoration but structure: the two announcements share one grammar of preparedness, one audience of the living, and one present moment in which the hearer must locate himself. Good news in the Qur'an is never generic comfort; *bashārā* is announcement of a specific, named, delivered good — here, gardens — and its force depends entirely on the specificity of the alternative announced one verse earlier. The sūrah has now completed its two horizons, and every subsequent ruling it issues will be issued between them.

**Rivers Under the Gardens: An Inverted Desert**

The gardens are described as *tajrī min tahtiha al-anhār* — rivers flowing from under them — and the commentators are careful with the preposition: the water is not buried, as the English might suggest, but runs *below the greenery*, visible and audible from beneath the canopy of the trees. For the first audience the phrase was not scenery but inversion. In Arabian experience water hides — in wells, in underground channels, in rock — and greenery is bought at the price of digging. Paradise is announced as the country where the terms are reversed: the water flows openly, on display, underfoot and everywhere, with the gardens standing on top of an un-concealed abundance. The formula then becomes the Qur'an's most repeated description of reward, recurring in dozens of sūrahs as a fixed refrain — each repetition re-addressing people whose daily relation to water was fear, and promising them its opposite. The rivers are also, in the exegetical reading, the visible form of provision no longer rationed: *rizq* without scarcity, flowing where verse 22's rain fell only at intervals.

**“This Is What We Were Given Before”: Memory Inside Paradise**

The strangest and richest clause in the verse is the dwellers' own remark. Given fruit, they say: *this is what we were given before*. The majority reading takes "before" as the earthly life — the fruits of Paradise arriving in the forms of the fruits of this world, so that the first bite in eternity triggers recognition. The commentators then draw the theology out of the homesickness: the world's fruit was the sample, Paradise is the delivery; the familiar form is kept so that the reward is *recognizable as the fulfillment of a life actually lived* — this world is not forgotten there, only its bitterness removed, which is why the Qur'an's standing description of the dwellers is that no fear comes upon them and they do not grieve (2:62). A minority reading places "before" inside Paradise — each fruit resembling the one previously tasted there. Both readings serve one purpose: continuity of personal memory across the boundary of death. The reward is not absorption into novelty but the completion of a biography the dweller still owns and can quote.

**Mutashābih: Same Face, Different Taste**

The verse explains the recognition with one loaded word: they are given fruit *mutashābih* — resembling, similar, the translation glossing it *looks similar ˹but tastes different˺*. The exegetes read the resemblance three ways: identical appearance with transformed taste; or uniform excellence — no fruit of Paradise has an inferior grade, so every specimen resembles every other in perfection; or the remark as wonderment rather than comparison, since the dwellers are recognizing names and forms while the realities exceed both. The word choice carries a second cargo for the attentive reader: *mutashābih* is the same term Sūrah 3 will use for the Qur'an's elusive verses (3:7) and that Sūrah 39 uses for a Book whose parts resemble each other in harmony (39:23). A lexicon links Paradise's fruit to the Book's texture: familiarity on the surface, inexhaustible difference underneath, recognition that deepens instead of exhausting. The dwellers' conversation over fruit is, in miniature, the reader's conversation with revelation — every return to the familiar thing yields an unfamiliar taste.

**Pure Spouses: Purity as Completed Companionship**

The verse's third gift is *azwāj muṭahharah* — spouses made pure. The commentators define the purification concretely and consistently: freedom from menstruation, from excreta, from every bodily process the classical lists call distasteful, and from injury and defect of every kind — purity as the completion of companionship, everything that strains or interrupts human intimacy removed from it. One scholarly caution deserves preservation: the major commentators do not attach sexual purity or virginity specifically to this verse; the purification named is comprehensive, of body and condition, not a single attribute. The gift also answers a loneliness in the passage: the men of the parables stood alone in a dark plain, and the storm's victims were described only as a plural of fear; here companionship is named as part of the reward, and the plural becomes company. Whatever the modern reader makes of the imagery's cultural skin, the verse's structural claim is plain — Paradise is furnished with persons, not only produce, and the highest created good listed here is a companionship that cannot spoil.

**Khulūd: The Clause of Permanence**

The verse closes on the word that seals both houses: *hum fīhā khālidūn* — they will be there abiding, *khulūd* the Qur'an's standing refrain for the inhabitants of the Garden and the Fire alike. Lexically the root denotes staying, duration, a thing's persistence in its place; from the mouth of Allah the commentators read it as the promise that upgrades duration into eternity, since His word cannot expire. The Qur'an sometimes reinforces the clause — abiding *forever* — and sometimes qualifies it, *as long as the heavens and the earth endure, except what your Lord wills* (11:108), which the exegetical tradition debates without dissolving, ending in a verse the same passage supplies: *a generous giving, without end*. This verse states the permanence flatly, and the flatness is part of the good news: a joy announced as temporary would not be news at all, since the heart's objection to every earthly good is exactly its expiry. The rivers flow, the fruit recurs, the spouses remain, and the clause of *khulūd* removes from all of them the one defect that made every provision of verse 22 bittersweet — the deadline.
