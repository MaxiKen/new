## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:223

> who gives an ˹attentive˺ ear ˹to half-truths˺, mostly passing on sheer lies.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Eavesdropping Is Not Possessing Knowledge**

The verse describes a listener who gives an ear to fragments and then carries them into human speech. The image is one of partial access, not reliable knowledge. Hearing something from an unseen source, or claiming to have heard it, does not turn the listener into an authority. The information may be incomplete before it is repeated, and the purpose of repeating it may be dishonest from the beginning.

The Quran's earlier account of guarded revelation makes the distinction clear. The devils are barred from the protected source, while corrupt people may still chase scraps, rumors, and impressions. A fragment that contains a small truth can become more dangerous when it is used to give a large lie an appearance of credibility.

**Half-Truths Are Often the Best Cover for Lies**

A complete invention may be easy to reject. A statement that begins with something true can travel farther because listeners recognize a familiar piece and then trust the rest without checking it. The person who gives an attentive ear to half-truths is therefore not merely curious. He is willing to become a channel through which confusion gains authority.

This pattern exists in ordinary human communication as well as in the verse's unseen imagery. A rumor may contain one accurate detail and several invented conclusions. A speaker may quote a rule while hiding the condition that limits it. The listener who wants excitement more than truth becomes part of the lie's distribution.

**Most Lies Does Not Make the Truth Safe**

The word mostly leaves a narrow distinction: something heard may occasionally correspond to reality, but the dominant character of the report is falsehood. That limited truth does not justify trusting the carrier. A poisonous mixture is not made safe by containing a small amount of nourishment.

The point is also not that every statement from a flawed person is automatically false. Quran 49:6 commands verification of news brought by an evildoer because harm can follow untested reports. The believer tests the claim, seeks context, and refuses to pass on what cannot be established. Careful verification is different from accepting a message because it contains an attractive detail.

**The Listener Becomes Responsible When He Repeats**

A person may not have invented the original lie, but he becomes responsible when he repeats it without care. Speech can injure honor, divide families, and turn a private suspicion into public certainty. The ear is therefore a moral gate. What it welcomes and passes onward can make the listener a helper of truth or a helper of falsehood.

The verse's imagery warns against fascination with secret reports. A person who claims special access to hidden information may be seeking influence rather than guidance. The Quran directs the believer away from such dependence and toward revelation that is publicly conveyed, protected, and subject to responsible understanding. A secret fragment cannot compete with a clear message from Allah.

**Truth Needs Humility and Restraint**

The right response to uncertain information is not to decorate it with confidence. Say what is known, mark what is uncertain, and stop the report when passing it on would harm people. The person who does this resists the path described in the verse, even when rumor would bring attention or advantage.

The contrast is decisive. The satanic channel thrives on fragments and lies; the believer seeks complete truth and accountability before Allah. Listening becomes worship when it leads to verified obedience. It becomes corruption when it turns half-heard claims into a market of false certainty.

The verse's structure shows how a false report travels. There is a receiver who gives an ear, a source that supplies fragments, and a public that receives the final story. Responsibility exists at every stage. The source may deceive, the listener may fail to question, and the repeater may add confidence that was never present in the original. Falsehood grows as it moves because each person wants the report to do something for him.

This is why the Quran does not praise curiosity without discipline. A person may want secret information because it makes him feel important or gives him influence over people who cannot check his claim. The desire to know the unseen can become a desire to control the seen. Revelation closes that route by teaching that the protected message is received through a trustworthy chain, not through private eavesdropping.

The word mostly also guards against a crude conclusion that no information ever reaches such a listener. A fragment of truth can be used to make a lie believable. The believer should not be impressed by the occasional accuracy of a source whose general method is deception. A correct detail does not validate the authority of the carrier or justify the harm caused by the false majority around it.

The same discipline applies to public speech. Do not repeat a claim about a person because it sounds spiritually serious, and do not attribute secret knowledge to a speaker merely because he predicts something that later occurs. Verify what can be verified, admit what cannot, and protect people's honor from reports that have not earned confidence. Quran 49:6 gives this principle a direct social form.

The contrast with revelation is therefore complete. The Quran is not a rumor assembled from overheard fragments. It is a protected message brought by the trustworthy spirit and conveyed to a prophet for warning. The believer honors that message by listening carefully, learning its context, and refusing to make uncertain speech compete with clear guidance.

An attentive ear should finally be an ethical ear. It should hear the suffering a rumor may cause, the duty a command creates, and the correction Allah is sending. When listening produces humility and action, it becomes a path to truth. When it produces fascination, slander, and false certainty, it repeats the pattern exposed by the verse.
