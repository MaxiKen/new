## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:13

> **so that you may sit firmly on their backs, and remember your Lord’s blessings once you are settled on them, saying, “Glory be to the One Who has subjected these for us, for we could have never done so ˹on our own˺.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose of the Mount Is a Moment of Remembrance**

The verse completes the sentence begun in verse 12. God made ships and animals to ride so that you may settle on their backs, and then so that you may remember. The particle li, "so that", governs both clauses, and the second is the surprising one. The obvious purpose of a riding animal is transport. The verse names a further purpose: to produce, at the moment of being seated, an act of memory. The word for settling is tastawū, from istawā, to become level and steady, the moment when the rider is fixed in his seat and the animal rises. That moment, when a person feels a body larger than his own bear his weight and move at his direction, is chosen as the moment for dhikr. The verse thus makes an ordinary daily act into an occasion for worship, and it does so not by adding a ritual but by asking the rider to notice what he is doing. The point of the animal was never only to move him. It was to be remembered on.

**"Glory Be to the One Who Subjected These for Us"**

The rider is given words. Subḥāna lladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā: glory be to Him who made this subservient to us. Subḥān is the declaration that God is free of every defect and beyond every comparison, and it is the usual word when a person is struck by something only God could do. The verb sakhkhara means to subject, to make something serve a purpose it did not choose. The Qurʾān uses it for the sun and moon, the sea, the winds, and here for the animal under the rider. The choice of subḥān rather than al-ḥamd, praise, is instructive. Praise thanks God for a benefit. Glorification marvels at a power. The rider on a camel is not only grateful for the ride; he is amazed that a creature of this strength lets him direct it, and the amazement points beyond the animal to the One who made it docile. The same words are used by Muslims today when they step into a car or an aircraft, and the transfer is exact: the machine, like the camel, is stronger than its rider and is under his hand only because the world was made in a way that permits it.

**"We Could Never Have Done So": Muqrinīn**

The prayer continues: wa-mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn. The word muqrin comes from qarana, to bind or yoke together, and a muqrin is one who is able to bring something under control, to match it, to be its equal. The phrase means: we were not able to master this; we could not have brought it under our power by ourselves. Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī all read it as an admission of incapacity. A man cannot make a camel carry him. He cannot make the sea hold a ship. What he has is the use of arrangements he did not make and could not have made. The admission is the real content of the prayer, because it is the exact opposite of the attitude the sūrah is attacking. The Quraysh treated their wealth, their trade, and their animals as their own achievement and as evidence of their standing. The rider's prayer says: none of this was within our power. The same lesson is taught in 31:20, where God has subjected everything in the heavens and the earth to human beings, and people still dispute about Him without knowledge.

**The Prophet's Practice on Mounting**

The prayer in this verse became the Prophet's ﷺ own habit, and the record is preserved with unusual detail. Ibn ʿUmar reported that when the Messenger of God ﷺ mounted his camel to set out on a journey, he would say Allāhu akbar three times and then recite these very words, Subḥāna lladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa-mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn, continuing through the return to the Lord in the next verse, and then adding his own requests: that God grant righteousness and piety on the journey and deeds that please Him, that He make the journey easy and fold up its distance, and that He be the companion on the road and the guardian of the family left behind. This is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1342. The report shows how the Qurʾānic prayer was used: not as a text to be read but as the opening of a longer conversation with God at the start of a journey. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī note that for sea travel the parallel prayer is the one Nūḥ spoke on boarding the ark in 11:41, in the name of God be its course and its anchoring. The verse's words were meant for the road, and the road is where the Prophet ﷺ said them.

**Remembering the Blessing Turns a Journey into Worship**

The verse's instruction is to remember niʿmata rabbikum, the blessing of your Lord, at the moment of mounting. The word niʿmah means a favour granted freely, and the possessive "your Lord" reminds the rider whose favour it is. The Qurʾān lists the blessings of livestock and ships in many places, and this verse asks for one thing in return for all of them: that the person using them recall, at the moment of use, who provided them. This is a low demand and a high one at once. Low, because it costs nothing but a sentence. High, because it requires a habit of attention that most people never form. The rider who says the prayer every time he mounts is training himself to see the world as given rather than owned, and that training is the beginning of the recognition the sūrah is asking the Quraysh for. They admitted in verse 9 that God made the heavens and the earth. This verse asks them to admit, every morning, that He made the camel kneel. The prayer's final clause, which the next verse completes, turns the earthly journey toward a longer one.
