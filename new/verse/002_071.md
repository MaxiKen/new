## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:71

> He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish.’” They said, “Now you have come with the truth.” Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Specification, and What Remained**

The last answer adds two details that describe the animal rather than its appearance: it has never been worked and carries no defect. The requirements have now covered its age, its colour and its history, and the description could only match a beast that had lived in a herd without being used in it. The book of Numbers describes a heifer in the same terms, without blemish and without a yoke, which is why the parallels between the two accounts have been examined closely.

What is distinctive in the sūrah's account is the placement of the physical description at the end of a conversation rather than at the beginning. The community asked three times for details and received them in stages, and the last of the answers is the one that would have been hardest to satisfy from an ordinary herd.

**"Now You Have Come With the Truth"**

The community's response to the final answer is an acknowledgement that Moses has finally brought them what they were waiting for. The Book records the sentence and lets the reader notice what it implies: the previous answers had also been true, and the acknowledgement had been withheld through three rounds of questioning. The community was satisfied only when the description was complete to its own specifications.

The phrasing also distances the community from the command rather than joining it. They are speaking about the instruction as something delivered to them by a man rather than as something binding on them, and the Book's narrative keeps that voice intact. The next clause reports what they then did, and the reader is left with the comparison between the words and the act.

**The Hesitation Reported in the Same Breath**

The verse ends with the slaughter and with a statement about the reluctance that accompanied it. The Book describes the community as having almost not done it, and the commentators supplied two explanations: the animal was rare and expensive, or the identification of the killer that would follow the ritual would expose a division inside the community. Both readings give the hesitation a motive, and the text leaves the motive unstated.

What the sentence does state is that the act happened. The command was carried out, and the Book records neither a punishment nor a further reproach for the manner of the obedience. The community's difficulty is reported as part of the event, and the narrative moves on to the use of the animal's body.

**The Cost of Precision**

The story's final shape is the one the reader has been watching form since the first question. A simple order was converted into a set of specifications, the specifications narrowed the search until a single animal qualified, and the community that demanded precision paid a price it would not have paid for an ordinary cow. The Book does not present this as a punishment for asking; it presents it as the natural cost of the approach.

That arrangement is what makes the passage usable as instruction. Anyone who reads it can see that the questions were reasonable in themselves and that their accumulation made the duty harder. The Book's narratives about this community consistently operate this way, and the effects are always presented as consequences rather than as sentences.

**The Order of Obedience in the Book**

The sūrah's account places the community's obedience after three delays and reports it with a qualifier, and the Qur'an's instruction elsewhere is framed as the opposite: belief and submission offered without waiting for the account to be complete. The Book states the proper sequence in its description of the believers: they hear and obey, and they ask for forgiveness rather than for further particulars.

The contrast is stated in the sūrah's own words when it describes the men who responded to the invitation with the words of belief and obedience. The narrative in this passage gives the reader the other case in full, and it makes the two positions comparable without commentary, since the exchange and its outcome are enough.

**What the Animal Was For**

The readers of the following verses learn what the community learned when the act was done: the cow's body was to be used to restore a dead man long enough for him to identify his killer. The command that had been interrogated for four verses was the instrument of a demonstration of the power that gives life, and the community that hesitated over the price was purchasing the answer to a crime it had concealed.

The sūrah does not spell out the irony, because the order of the narrative accomplishes it. What the reader is left with is a completed exchange: a command, four questions, a purchase, an act performed with reluctance, and a demonstration that will follow in the next two verses.

**Why the Requirements Were So Precise**

The specifications that cost the community so much were attached to an act whose function is disclosed two verses later, and the Book's arrangement of the material makes that function the answer to the whole exchange. The animal was the instrument of a demonstration: a dead man was to be struck with part of it and restored long enough to settle a case. The requirements were therefore not arbitrary precision but the marking of a particular beast for a particular use, and the community's demand to know everything about it before acting turned preparation into obstruction.

Read that way, the passage is a study in the difference between doing a task and understanding it first. The Book's commands are frequently like this: their purposes are stated, sometimes later, and a person who waits for the full rationale before moving never arrives. The community that argued over the cow's age and colour ended by carrying out the act with reluctance, and the sūrah reports both the act and the reluctance without further comment.
