## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:71

> He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish.’” They said, “Now you have come with the truth.” Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Portrait**

The last answer completes the description, and its two new marks close the animal's biography rather than its appearance. *Not employed to till the soil or water the fields* — a beast that never bore the yoke, whose strength was never spent in labor, whose hide carries no plow's mark and no well-rope's scar. *Wholesome* — *musallamah*, sound, intact, free of the defects that sacrificial law everywhere excludes. *Without blemish* — *lā shiyata fīhā*, literally no streak in it, no patch of any other colour breaking the specified yellow: the saturation of verse 69's *fāqiʿ* now confirmed as total. Across the three rounds the command's object has been assembled piece by piece — age, colour, work-history, soundness — until the description admits no substitute: the field that began as *any cow* has narrowed to one animal answering to a portrait. The specification of unworked strength is the most expressive of the marks. A yoked ox is the ordinary case of a useful life spent in the world's work; the commanded cow is the animal kept out of that spending — full strength, uncommitted to the soil, available wholly for the act of sacrifice. The offering Allah's definition describes is not a worn-out remainder but an unspent whole.

**“Now You Have Come With the Truth”**

The questioners' acknowledgment is the negotiation's verdict on itself: *now you have come with the truth*. The word *now* convicts the preceding rounds — by their own sentence, the truth arrives only at the description's completion, which means the earlier answers, though given by the same mouth in the same formula (*Allah says*), did not register with them as truth. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss states the acknowledgment's content plainly: now you have made it clear. The clarity they celebrate is total specification — a definition with nothing left to ask about — and its arrival closes the exchange by exhaustion: no fourth question is possible, because no property remains undescribed. The sentence is also, without the speakers' knowing it, the exchange's confession. Truth, in their usage, meant the description matching their demands; truth, in the verse's staging, meant the command they had been avoiding since the first word. They had the truth at *Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow* and did not call it truth until it cost them a search. The acknowledgment thus marks the point where stalling becomes impossible and only the deed remains — and the verse's final clause reports how the deed arrived.

**“They Almost Did Not”**

The narration's last sentence is its deepest probe: *yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly* — *wa-mā kādū yafʿalūn*, and they were not near to doing it, the Arabic idiom of an act performed at the outer limit of reluctance. The obedience happened; the willingness did not. The exegetes give the near-refusal two grounds, and both readings carry weight. The first is economic: the specified cow, the reports say, was found in single possession and priced accordingly — the three rounds' accumulated specifications arriving as an accumulated invoice, and the camp hesitating at the cost its own questions had manufactured. The second is darker: the murderer was among them, and the hesitation was his — the sacrifice's purpose still undisclosed to the community, but its completion certain to expose a killing, so the guilty party and those entangled with him dragged at the deed. The two grounds are compatible: a costly obedience with a hidden scandal attached is exactly the situation in which *almost not* is the honest report. The clause's theological precision is in what it refuses to round off. A neater narration would end at the slaughter — command obeyed, case closed. The Qur'an keeps the reluctance on the record because the reluctance is the episode's finding: an obedience extracted across four rounds of questioning, at maximum cost, with the knife taken up and nearly put down, is obedience in its barest legal form — the deed present, the surrender absent.

**The Nearest Cow Would Have Sufficed**

The tradition's summary of the whole exchange is a single prophetic sentence: it is preserved in the exegetical record that the Prophet ﷺ said, had they taken the nearest cow and slaughtered it, it would have sufficed them. The sufficiency statement is legislation in miniature, and it generalizes into the rule the ḥadīth corpus states in full: *"Leave me with what I have left to you, for those who were before you were destroyed because of excessive questioning"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1337). The cow's negotiation is the destroyed-peoples archetype the ḥadīth names — the case study behind the rule. Its mechanism is now fully visible in the completed episode: a command received with general terms is cheap and sufficient; the same command interrogated acquires specifications, and each specification transfers cost from the law to the law's addressees, until sufficiency has to be purchased back at the market's ceiling. Islamic jurisprudence built its interpretive ethics on the counterfactual. The schools' standing maxim — do not ask into what your obligations could become — descends from this verse's invoice; the jurists' preference for leaving general texts general, and for acting on commands at their plain face, is the nearest-cow principle made method. The verse thus legislates twice: once in its narrative, showing the cost of interrogation, and once in its silence — the sufficiency that stood at the first word, unclaimed, while three questions assembled its replacement.

**The Unspent Offering and the Cherished Gift**

The completed portrait — unyoked, sound, unblemished, saturated in colour, pleasing to the eye — describes the most valuable animal the definition could construct, and its value is the verse's quiet claim about sacrifice. The Qur'an's rule of giving runs in the same direction: *You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish* (3:92) — the measure of the gift being the love of the object given. The cow's specification assembles exactly such an object: not the old beast past work, whose slaughter is disposal, but the sound one in its full strength, beautiful to look at, expensive to buy. Islamic sacrificial practice keeps the principle in its own rulings — the ʿīd animal to be sound, mature, unblemished, the best within the offerer's means — and the cow-episode is the principle's narrative filing: what is offered to Allah is offered from the unspent and the cherished, not from the remainder. The questioners assembled this offering without intending it. They sought specifications to postpone a sacrifice and received the description of the finest sacrifice their region contained; the stall produced the standard. The verse leaves the irony standing and draws its lesson: the object of obedience, fully defined, always turns out to be costly — which is not the definition's defect but its purpose, since the cost is where the offering becomes an offering.

**The Soul That Never Bore the Yoke**

The esoteric reading the recital invites completes here its most exact application. Ibn ʿAjībah's commentary takes the cow as the egotistical soul and reads each specification as the soul's required condition at the sacrifice: in its middle age, neither calcified nor unformed (verse 68); beautiful through goodness, the colour of a soul pleasing to its Lord (verse 69); and here — *not employed to till the soil or water the fields* — a soul never broken to the world's plowing, whose strength was not spent cultivating appetites and irrigating desires. *Wholesome*: free of the defects that bind — the crack of greed, the limp of attachment. *Without blemish*: no streak of another colour in its yellow, no divided allegiance in its surrender. The reading keeps the history primary — the cow was slaughtered in a wilderness camp by a reluctant people — and rides on the text's own choice of marks: the unyoked animal is an image the narrative supplies, not one the commentator imports. Its pastoral edge is the hesitation clause. The soul's sacrifice, fully specified, is always *almost not* performed: the knife is easy and the readiness is the expensive item, which is why the verse ends where it ends — not on the portrait but on the flinch. The commanded sacrifice of every reader stands at the same point: the description complete, the object identified, the hand approaching. The sūrah files the outcome without comment, and the comment was never needed: they did it. The hesitation is on the record so that no later sacrificer mistakes his own for a disqualification.
