## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:34

> Woe to you, and more woe!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Threat in an Idiom**

The sūrah has finished describing the man — neither believing nor praying, declaring the message false, turning his back, and swaggering home to his people — and it answers him with a phrase rather than an argument. That is the first thing to notice. Argument was offered earlier in the sūrah and was refused; what is offered now is a threat, and the Qur'an's movement from proof to threat is itself a diagnosis. A person who has declared the signs false is not short of reasons. He is short of fear, and the sūrah addresses what is actually missing. The sequence matters for how the Qur'an is to be understood generally: proof is given first, refusal follows, and only then does the threat arrive. Anyone who reads the threat without the preceding thirty-three verses will mistake warning for vindictiveness, and the sūrah has taken care that this cannot be done honestly.

**What the Phrase Means**

The Arabic is *awlā laka fa-awlā*, and it is an idiom the grammarians discussed at length because its literal sense and its usage pull in different directions. The word *awlā* is an elative meaning nearer or more fitting, so the literal sense is *nearer to you, and nearer still* — nearer, that is, than anything you are expecting. As used by the Arabs, however, the phrase functioned as a threat, and the early authorities recorded that this was its force: a warning that the thing dreaded is close at hand rather than distant. Sībawayhi and the Basran grammarians treated it as an idiom of menace. The two readings converge rather than compete. What is near is the woe, so a rendering that makes the nearness explicit and a rendering that makes the woe explicit are describing the same sentence from two sides.

**Why the Threat Is Not Filled In**

No punishment is described. There is no fire here, no chain, no duration, no description of what the person will undergo, and the absence is deliberate. A specified punishment can be measured against a person's estimate of his own deserts and found excessive; an unspecified one cannot be argued with. The Qur'an uses the same economy elsewhere, and its threats are frequently at their heaviest when they are at their vaguest. A man told that woe is nearer to him than his own next step is left to supply the content, and what he supplies is shaped by everything he knows about what he has done. The Qur'an uses the explicit word for woe elsewhere, and the objects of it are instructive: *“Woe on that Day to the deniers—those who deny Judgment Day!”* (83:10–11). The woe attaches to the denial of the Day rather than to a list of particular sins, which is the same emphasis the sūrah has maintained since its opening oath. The man being threatened here is not a monster. He is someone who declined to believe that this Day would arrive.

**Who Is Being Addressed**

The address is to a single person in the second person singular, and the reports preserved in the tafsīr literature name him as Abū Jahl, one of the Prophet's most determined opponents in Makkah. A related report assigns the four occurrences of the phrase across these two verses to four stages: defeat at Badr, the grave, the Resurrection, and the Fire. These are exegetical reports rather than authenticated narrations, and they are cited as such. [UNVERIFIED: the identification of the addressee with Abū Jahl and the fourfold assignment were not located this session in a chain-carrying source; they belong to the asbāb material of the tafsīr tradition.] The generalising function is unaffected. Whoever matches the description in vv. 31–33 is the person being addressed, which is why the Qur'an names the type rather than restricting the threat to one man.

**The Answer to the Swagger**

The placement of the threat is exact, and the Qur'an's sense of fitness here should not be missed. The man in v. 33 drew himself up, lengthened his stride, and went home to be admired. The reply tells him that something is nearer to him than his own stride and that it is coming for him. Pride is met with nearness, because the one thing arrogance cannot survive is the collapse of distance. A man can shrug off a punishment he regards as remote; he cannot shrug off one he is told is at the door. The nearness is not a rhetorical flourish invented for this verse. It is the Qur'an's standing description of the situation: *“˹The time of˺ people’s judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away.”* (21:1), and *“The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺.”* (54:1). Every person alive is closer to his own reckoning than he was yesterday, which is what makes the phrase *nearer to you* literally rather than merely rhetorically true. A man can shrug off a punishment he regards as remote; he cannot shrug off one he is told is at the door. That is why the sūrah frames the threat in the vocabulary of proximity rather than in the vocabulary of severity.

**A Threat Is Also an Address**

The hardest thing to hold on to in a verse like this is that it is spoken to someone still alive. A threat delivered to a man in this life is a warning, and a warning presupposes that the thing warned against can be avoided. The Qur'an never threatens the already-damned; its threats are always served in advance, and the sūrah has already stated the alternative in the plainest terms available: *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The severity of the phrase is the measure of what is at stake, not of what has been decided.
