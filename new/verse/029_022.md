## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:22

> And you cannot escape Him on earth or in heaven. Nor have you any protector or helper besides Allah.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Denial That Closes Both Directions**

The sentence removes every route of escape by naming the two regions that exhaust the geography available to a human being or an angel. There is no hiding place on earth and no exit into the sky; the Book states the same impossibility when it tells its audience that they can never escape God on earth and have no protector or helper besides Him (42:31), and the jinn themselves affirm that they know they cannot frustrate God on earth nor escape from Him into heaven (72:12). The wording is not a threat about capture but a description of the structure of existence. Anything that exists exists within the domain of its maker, and the idea of fleeing to a place outside that domain turns out to be an idea with no address. The Book strips the fantasy in a single line.

**The Verb Behind the Word Escape**

The Arabic expression rendered as escaping is the language of frustration and incapacitation, and it is used for the person who thinks he can put his opponent in a position where the opponent's power no longer reaches. The Book applies it to God's enemies in several passages and always denies the possibility, and the form of the denial is instructive. It does not say that escape is difficult or unlikely; it says that no one possesses the capacity for it. The claim being answered is usually not a public doctrine but a private calculation, the assumption that a man can manage his exposure by moving, concealing, or aligning himself with useful people. The sentence denies all three strategies at once, and it denies them without needing the evidence that the rest of the sūrah has been assembling.

The verb describes an attempt to get out of range rather than merely to move quickly. What the sentence denies is the possibility of reaching a place where the authority under discussion does not apply, which is why the Book uses the image of being overtaken rather than of being chased.

**Protector and Helper, Two Different Offices**

The pair of terms at the end of the verse names two distinct things. A protector is one who takes charge of a person, guards what belongs to him, and stands between him and harm; the word carries the sense of nearness and responsibility. A helper is one who advances a person's cause, lending strength so that a claim may succeed. The Book denies that either office exists besides God, stating that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Him alone and that people have no guardian or helper besides Him (2:107), and repeating the denial with the added note that He alone gives life and causes death (9:116). The denial is aimed at the two practical uses to which people put their gods: protection from harm and assistance toward advantage. Neither service is available from any source but the One being addressed.

**The Patronage the Sentence Takes Away**

For a believer under pressure, the practical temptation is not to worship a stone; it is to look for a patron. The powerful of Makkah could offer protection, a well-placed tribe could offer leverage, and a compromise could purchase safety. The sentence removes that entire market in one stroke, and it does so at the point in the chapter where the reader most needs the reassurance that follows from it. If no protector exists besides God, then no enemy can be protected against Him, and nobody who is held by Him can be lost through the incompetence of an ally. The verse's apparent severity is therefore a form of security. It tells the believer that the only power in the universe capable of helping him is one that cannot be alienated by a stronger party.

The removal is stated as a consequence rather than as a punishment. A person who has placed his confidence in patrons discovers that the relationship was never reciprocal, and the verse's earlier question about protection becomes, in this sentence, the loss of the assumption the question rested on.

**The Comfort Inside a Refusal**

A reader who considers the sentence carefully notices that its denial is also the ground of the Book's calm about the powerful. Governments, armies, and majorities are all real, and the Book never pretends otherwise; it says instead that none of them can decide a matter that belongs to God. The deniers plotted against Abraham and were made the lower party, and the Book reports the outcome with no drama because the contest was never in doubt. This is why the verse sits so naturally after the promise that God punishes and shows mercy as He wills: the same authority that determines outcomes is also the reason no other authority can determine them, and the two sentences together declare that the field of struggle contains no hidden ally and no hidden lethal power.

The sentence also gives the reader a settled ground before anything changes around him. Nothing in the verse depends on the audience's circumstances improving, and stating who holds the patronage in the middle of a chapter about persecution is itself a form of reassurance.

**The Question Put to Those Who Plot**

The Book does not rely on the impossibility of escape as a bare fact to be accepted; it turns the fact into a question and leaves it with the people it concerns. The formulation in another chapter asks those who devise evil plots whether they feel secure that God will not cause the earth to swallow them, or that the torment will not come upon them in ways they cannot comprehend (16:45). The phrase about ways they cannot comprehend is the answer to the confidence of a clever man. Plots are designed against known dangers, and the Book's warning is that the arrival will not be on the list the plotter prepared. The verse under discussion supplies the geography of that impossibility; the question supplies the practical consequence for anyone still deciding how much confidence his arrangements deserve.

Putting the matter as a question also leaves the plotters to answer it themselves. The Book has used the same technique with the town's prosperity and with its rainfall, and each time the audience is handed the premise of an argument it would rather not finish.

**Where the Same Two Names Appear Again**

The Book pairs protector and helper in several passages that describe the day of reckoning, and it consistently joins the denial of both with a statement about ownership of the heavens and the earth. The repetition is not redundant; it establishes the vocabulary that the chapter's later arguments depend on. When the sūrah turns to the story of Abraham's rescue and the ruin of the communities that denied their messengers, it does so in a universe already described by this verse: a place with no escape routes, no alternative guardians, and a single governing authority. The remaining question for every character in the story, and for the reader, is not whether such a power exists but how one should stand in relation to it. The answer the chapter develops is delivered by the stories it tells: standing in relation to that power means worship, patience under pressure, and the refusal to treat anything else as a source of security. The sentence contributes the negative half of that answer, since it removes every alternative a person might reach for, and the verses that follow supply the positive half by showing what the men who trusted God were given. Read as a single unit, the two halves leave a person with one source of protection and no excuse for looking elsewhere, which is the position the rest of the chapter assumes its reader occupies.
