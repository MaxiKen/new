## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:23

> And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Challenge That Waits for Doubt**

The verse does not respond to an argument; it responds to a suspicion, and it offers a procedure for settling one. The condition is doubt about what has been sent down, and the remedy proposed is a competition. Anyone who thinks the text is the work of a human being is invited to demonstrate it in the one way such a claim can be demonstrated: by producing something comparable and letting the comparison speak.

Nothing in the sentence forbids doubt or punishes it. The Book's method here is to meet the objection on the objector's own ground, and the later part of the sūrah does the same thing when it tells the Prophet to announce that whoever wishes may believe and whoever wishes may disbelieve. The challenge is not the voice of an author closing the discussion. It is the voice of one carrying a case that is prepared to be tested.

**"Our Servant," and What the Title Confers**

The revelation is described as what has come down to *Our servant*, and the epithet is the highest title the Book gives the Prophet ﷺ. The same word marks the gift at the head of another sūrah: *“Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant, so that he may be a warner to the whole world.”* (25:1). In this Book the servant, not the lord, is the one who carries revelation, and the irony of the challenge rests on that relationship. Men who were willing to accept a messenger from among themselves were being asked to attack the message rather than the messenger.

The title also frames the terms of the contest. If the reciter of the Qur'an is a servant who received what he carries, then the question of authorship is a question about the source. The Book does not claim literary credit for the Prophet; it claims that what he recites came from its giver, and the challenge is addressed to anyone who says otherwise.

**One Sūrah, the Smallest Unit of the Demand**

The challenge asks for a sūrah, not for a book, a law code or a civilisation. The word names the shortest recognised unit of the revealed text, so the demand has been reduced to its smallest workable size, and the Qur'an grades the same test elsewhere at several levels: *“Or do they claim, ‘He made it up!’? Tell them ˹O Prophet˺, ‘Produce one sûrah like it then, and seek help from whoever you can— other than Allah—if what you say is true!’”* (10:38). Other passages raise the bar to ten sūrahs (11:13), and one raises it to the whole of the Qur'an with every possible ally enlisted: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other.’”* (17:88).

The scale of the request matters, because it is easy to misread it as an impossible demand dressed up as fair play. One short passage of comparable quality is the whole thing being asked for, and the Book repeats the offer across at least four passages, including a taunt that reduces the task further: *“Or do they say, ‘He made this ˹Quran˺ up!’? In fact, they have no faith. / Let them then produce something like it, if what they say is true!”* (52:33–34).

**Helpers and Witnesses Named Inside the Challenge**

The next instruction is to call upon helpers and allies, and the invitation is remarkable for how little it withholds. The Arab poets, rhetoricians and soothsayers of the time were being offered the entire field; the sentence does not restrict the assistance to one tribe or one art. Any power short of God is admissible. The point is not that the challenge is unwinnable because the rules are tilted; it is that the rules have been written to exclude every excuse.

The Book elsewhere describes the human material it is made of, which is the same material the audience used every day: letters, words, speech. Sūrah Yūnus states the relationship in similar terms, and the sūrah's own opening letters make the claim that the revelation is built from the alphabet its hearers owned. The challenge stands on that basis. If the raw material is common, then what is being compared is arrangement.

**Inimitability, a Doctrine Built on This Demand**

Later Muslim scholarship named this claim with a term the Qur'an itself does not use, *iʿjāz*, and treated the text's power to silence challengers as the central miracle of Islam. The miracle, on this account, is not an event that happened once to an audience; it is a property of the revelation that any generation can test. The Book's self-descriptions supply the terms: the Qur'an calls itself a light and a clear Book (5:15), and it presents clarity as one of its own features rather than something the reader must supply.

What the doctrine rests on is the comparison this verse invites, and the comparison has been taken up repeatedly over fourteen centuries. The sūrah's next sentence assumes the outcome of that test before it happens, and it draws a conclusion about which the audience is asked to think carefully. The argument of the passage is cumulative: the Book offers itself for examination and then states what it believes the examination will show.

**"If What You Say Is True"**

The verse ends by handing the standard of proof back to its audience. The claim being made by the doubters was that the Qur'an is human work; the sentence says, in effect, that the claim should be supported the way any claim about human work is supported, by demonstration. The Book's own case is on the table, and the burden it places on the other side is the ordinary burden that anyone making an assertion accepts.

That is the shape the passage gives to the whole dispute. It offers an invitation, defines the smallest possible test, removes every restriction on assistance, and leaves the final clause with the people who were objecting. What follows in the next verse is the conclusion the Book draws from its own challenge, and it continues to address the same audience on the same terms.
