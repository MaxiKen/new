## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:23

> And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Doubt Addressed to the Doubters of the Book Just Described**

The sūrah has summoned humanity to worship the Lord who created and settled them (2:21-22) and then, without delay, anticipates the objection that such a summons inevitably provokes: doubt about the Book that conveys it. *“And if you are in doubt about what We have sent down upon Our servant”* — *wa-in kuntum fī raybin mimmā nazzalnā ʿalā ʿabdinā* (2:23). The doubt (*rayb*) is the same noun whose absence was proclaimed about the Book in (2:2) — *“no doubt about it.”* What was denied as a property of the Book is now acknowledged as a possibility in its hearers. The sūrah does not suppress that possibility; it invites the doubters to act on it. That invitation is the Qur'anic challenge (*taḥaddī*): to meet doubt not with dismissal but with a test whose terms are public.

**Our Servant — A Title That Grounds the Challenge**

*ʿAbdinā* — “Our servant” — names Muḥammad without naming him. The possessive *Our* gathers the revelation under divine agency, and the noun *servant* gathers the recipient under the same relation that all humanity was just summoned to — *worship your Lord* (*uʿbudū rabbakum*, 2:21) and *do not set up equals in worship* (*fī al-ʿibādah*, 2:22). The Prophet is not presented as an autonomous author whose talent might be debated; he is presented as the servant upon whom something is being *sent down* (*nazzalnā*). The verb is the same *n-z-l* that described revelation to the Prophet in (2:4) — *what was revealed to you* — and the shift to the first-person plural *We* emphasizes the Sender. Doubt about the Book is thereby referred to its proper locus: not whether a man could have composed it, but whether the One who created and provisioned (2:21-22) is the One who sent it down to His servant.

**Produce a Sūrah Like It — The Measure of Likeness**

*Fa-ʾtū bi-sūratin min mithlih* — “then produce a sūrah like it.” The challenge does not say “produce a book like it” but “a sūrah like it” — a single unit among 114. That specification matters because it scales the test to human capacity. A sūrah can be three verses (*al-Kawthar*) or 286 (*al-Baqarah*); the challengers are free to choose the shortest that would count as a sūrah. *Min mithlih* — “like it” — has been heard in two ways: “like the Qur'an” in its beauty, clarity, and levels of meaning, or “like the servant” — like a sūrah from one like Muḥammad, an unlettered man. Both hearings converge on the same difficulty: the Qur'an is described as *muʿjizah* — that which incapacitates — not because its opponents lack language, but because the particular combination of concision, resonance, and layered meaning it achieves, in the language the opponents claim to master, cannot be matched when attempted.

**Call Your Helpers Other Than Allah — Witnesses, Partners, Intercessors**

*Wadʿū shuhadāʾakum min dūni Allāh* — “and call your helpers — your witnesses — other than Allah.” *Shuhadāʾ* here has been glossed as witnesses who would testify to the likeness, helpers who would assist in the composition, or the very equals (*andād*, 2:22) just prohibited, now summoned to corroborate the doubt. The phrase *min dūni Allāh* — “other than Allah” — gathers any agency the doubters might invoke — poets, soothsayers, *jinn*, patrons, institutions — and says: bring them. The challenge is not *“produce a sūrah alone”*; it is *“call whomever you can other than Him.”* That breadth prevents the later excuse that the challengers lacked collaborators. The sūrah that began by forbidding equals in worship now challenges the doubters to summon those equals as literary witnesses, so that the theological claim and the literary test become one.

**If You Are Truthful — Doubt as a Claim That Must Be Owned**

*In kuntum ṣādiqīn* — “if you are truthful” — attaches the challengers’ doubt to their own truthfulness. Doubt is not treated as a private hesitation to be respected at a distance; it is treated as a claim — that the Book is not from Allah — and claims have witnesses. The same conditional *if you are truthful* will appear in other challenges — *“bring your proof”* (2:111) — and in each case it shifts the burden from the Book to the doubter. The Book has been presented with its credential — creation, settlement, provision, and the servant’s known character as *unlettered* (7:157). The doubters who met that presentation with *rayb* are now asked to vindicate the *rayb* with a *sūrah* that would vindicate the *sidq* they implicitly claim for their doubt. The conditional keeps the door open while making the door’s threshold explicit.

**Similar Challenges in Mecca and Medina**

The source notes that this Madinan challenge echoes earlier Meccan challenges — to produce ten sūrahs (11:13), to produce one sūrah (10:38), to produce the like of the Qur'an altogether even if humans and *jinn* gathered (17:88). The Madinan placement does not supersede the Meccan; it re-situates the same challenge after the community has been formed, after the Book has been described as *“no doubt”* and *“upon guidance”* and after the hypocrites have been shown to trade guidance for error. The challenge is therefore not an isolated proof-text but the Qur'an’s standing offer, renewed in Medina in the same terms it was offered in Mecca, so that the new audience — who did not witness the Meccan contests — can be addressed without requiring a new kind of evidence. The *unlettered* Prophet remains the background that makes the production of a sūrah by his like the relevant test.

**Inimitability as Incapacitation, Not as Assertion**

The term *iʿjāz* — inimitability, the power to incapacitate — is not used in the Qur'an for itself, yet the phenomenon it names is presented here as the Book’s own credential. The Qur'an does not say “believe because I say I am inimitable”; it says “if you doubt, produce.” The incapacitation, if it occurs, will be experienced by the challengers as the inability to meet the test despite being given the most lenient form of the test — a single sūrah, with all helpers other than Allah permitted. That is why the classical commentaries link this verse to the Prophet’s *ummiyyah* — his unlettered status — not to magnify the Prophet but to remove the rebuttal that the sūrah was produced by training. The servant’s known lack of the training that would be required to produce such a sūrah is part of the test’s fairness.

**A Challenge That Becomes a Criterion of Mindfulness**

Finally, the challenge returns to the opening definition of the mindful. To be mindful (*muttaqī*) was to be guided by a Book about which there is no doubt (2:2), to believe in what was revealed to the Prophet and before him (2:4), and to be upon guidance (2:5). To be in doubt (*fī rayb*) about what was sent down upon the servant is to stand at the opposite pole of that definition, not as a separate class but as the same humanity summoned to worship (2:21) now hesitating at the instrument of worship’s guidance. The challenge does not expel the doubter; it invites him to resolve doubt by the most direct means available to a literary culture — to imitate, and, failing to imitate, to recognize that the inability to imitate is itself a sign that the Book’s claim to be “no doubt” was not a boast but a description. The next verse will state what follows if the production fails — and will, in stating it, remove the pretense that the challenge was hypothetical.
