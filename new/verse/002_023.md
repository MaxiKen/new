## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:23

> And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge**

The sūrah has asserted that it is from God, described the people who accept it and the people who do not, and now does something unusual: it names the test. *If you are in doubt* — then do this. The claim is not withdrawn into the realm of private conviction. A procedure is offered.

What is asked for is not belief, or submission, or silence. It is a piece of writing. The Quran's central argument about itself is, in the first instance, a literary one, and the verse invites the reader to settle it the way any literary question is settled: by producing something comparable.

**"Our Servant"**

The Prophet is called *Our servant* (2:23), and the title is doing work. The accusation being answered was that he had composed the Quran himself, or been taught it by a human. The reply does not argue about his character; it names his station. A servant is not the author of what he delivers.

The background the Quran supplies elsewhere makes the point sharper. The messenger is described as *the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel* (7:157). His opponents knew his history and knew what he had and had not been exposed to. The challenge, in that context, is a claim about the disproportion between the man and the book: if this is his composition, more of it should be easy.

**What Is Being Asked For**

The unit demanded is a *sūrah* — and the shortest sūrahs in the Quran run to three brief lines. The challenge is therefore not one of length or stamina. Nobody was being asked to produce six hundred pages. The demand is for the smallest complete unit of this discourse: three lines that do what these three lines do.

That is what makes the challenge difficult rather than merely provocative. The Quran's shortest chapters are among its most recited, and they are not short because they are slight. A demand to match three lines, on the terms on which those three lines exist, is a demand for the same thing that makes them work — and the sūrah's argument is that nothing else has it.

**The Parallel Challenges**

This is the most repeated challenge in the Book, and the repetitions vary in a way that matters. Produce *a sūrah like it* (2:23; 10:38). Produce *ten fabricated sûrahs like it* (11:13). *Let them then produce something like it, if what they say is true!* (52:34). And the largest version: *If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other* (17:88).

The escalation is the argument. The demand is raised from one chapter to ten to the whole book and to the combined effort of every creature, and in each form the claim is the same: it cannot be done at any scale. A challenge that could only be issued in its largest form would be rhetorical; a challenge issued at the smallest scale and then raised is a claim about the material.

**What Inimitability Is Not**

The term later used for this — *iʿjāz*, the power to incapacitate — does not appear in the Quran, which is worth saying because the vocabulary has sometimes suggested a doctrine the text does not contain. The Quran does not claim to be miraculous in the sense of violating anything. It claims to be unmatched in Arabic, and it says so to people who were the acknowledged masters of Arabic and who had every incentive to prove otherwise.

The challenge is also not a claim that the Quran is untranslatable or that its beauty is self-evident to everyone. It is a specific challenge about composition, issued to a specific audience with specific skills, and the sūrah treats the failure of that audience as evidence rather than as proof. The difference matters: an argument addressed to people who can judge it.

One more thing is often missed. The invitation is *call your helpers other than Allah* (2:23) — bring everyone you like. The challenge is not issued to an individual working alone but to a coalition, and the sūrah names the condition explicitly because the argument would otherwise be vulnerable to the charge that the contenders were excluded or outnumbered. Whatever anyone can bring, the claim is, it will not be enough.

**Why the Failure Counts**

The historical claim embedded here is falsifiable, which is more than most scriptural claims offer. If a comparable sūrah had been produced and accepted by the Arabic-speaking public of the seventh century, the Quran's central claim would have failed on its own terms. The sūrah's confidence — stated outright in the verse that follows — is a prediction about what will not happen.

For a reader centuries later, the argument has lost its immediacy and gained a different one. What remains available is the disproportion the challenge was pointing at: a book produced by a man with no literary training, in a language with a fierce poetic culture, which reorganised that culture and has not been matched in it since.

That asymmetry is worth weighing honestly, because it is the part of the challenge that survives the loss of the original audience. The Quran was not preserved by a literary establishment. It was preserved by people who were conquered by the argument it contains, and it became the standard against which Arabic prose and poetry were subsequently measured. Whatever a reader concludes about its origin, the fact that a single book reorganised a literary tradition that had no shortage of masters is the kind of disproportion that asks for an explanation.
