## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:23

> And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Challenge Issued at the Level of Doubt**

The verse addresses a specific interior state and takes it seriously: *wa-in kuntum fī raybin* — if you are in *rayb*, the doubt-with-suspicion named in this sūrah's second verse as having no place in the Book. The challenge does not wait for denial or hostility; it descends to the earliest stage of resistance and offers it a test. That pedagogical choice is worth weighing. A prophet confronting deniers might demand belief or pronounce judgment; this verse instead proposes an experiment, phrased as a fair conditional — if such is your doubt, then attempt such-and-such. The invitation converts an interior fog into an exterior task with a public outcome. Doubt that will not test itself is revealed, by the refusal alone, to be something other than doubt. And the task is calibrated to be attemptable by anyone: it requires no faith, no purity, no membership — only the ability to write. The verse is, in form, the most open door the Qur'an offers its critics, and the most exacting.

**“Our Servant”: The Title That Carries the Claim**

The revelation is described as *mā nazzalnā ʿalā ʿabdinā* — what We have sent down upon Our servant — and the single word *ʿabd* carries the verse's whole biographical argument. The Qur'an does not name Muḥammad ﷺ here but titles him, and the title is the lowest and highest a person can hold: slave, servant, one who owns nothing and speaks for Another. The choice answers the objectors' actual case, which was never that the text lacked beauty but that its author was visible — a known man, in a known town, with a known history of neither scribal training nor poetic apprenticeship. The Qur'an supplies that biography as evidence rather than embarrassment: the *unlettered Prophet*, *"whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel"* (7:157), who could not read the scriptures he is said to have absorbed. A forgery requires sources; this author had none. The servant-title therefore does humility-work and argument-work in the same breath — the man is small enough that the book cannot be his.

**One Sūrah: The Deliberately Minimal Quantum**

The demand is graduated down to its floor: *bi-sūratin min mithlih* — one sūrah of its like. Not the whole Book, not ten chapters — a single unit, and the Qur'an elsewhere records the demand having been pitched higher before: bring ten sūrahs like it, forged if you must (11:13); bring a Book like it (28:49 — cited in the classical lists of the graduated challenge). The gradation is a documented feature of the challenge verses, which appear in Yūnus, Hūd, and al-Isrāʾ as well, and the Isrāʾ formulation removes the final limit of even collective effort: *"If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other"* (17:88). One sūrah is thus the minimum testable unit of a claim whose maximum version spans two species. The commentators note this verse is Madinan while its parallels are Makkan — the challenge was not exhausted by its first audiences but re-issued to a new city with new doubters, and stands re-issued to every century since.

**“Call Your Helpers Other Than Allah”**

The second clause extends the challenge from the individual to his whole alliance: *wa-dʿū shuhadāʾakum min dūni Llāh* — summon your witnesses, your patrons, your gods-beside-Allah. The phrase *min dūni Llāh* is the same construction verse 22 used for the equals set up in worship, and the reuse is a hinge: the helpers invoked in the literary test are of the same order as the rivals forbidden in the creed. The verse quietly argues that every false god is now on trial as a co-author — if the idols are real powers, let them co-sign a sūrah. The objectors are invited to mobilize everything their worldview contains: poets, soothsayers, ancestral deities, jinn confederates of the kind the Qur'an names as whisperers of elegant deception (6:112). The demand is phrased as generosity — bring all the help you trust — and functions as exposure: the challenge will be refused by the same mouths that professed the helpers, and the refusal will itself testify which of the two claims the claimants actually believed.

**Iʿjāz: The Doctrine This Verse Built**

From verses of this shape the Islamic sciences developed the doctrine of *iʿjāz* — the Qur'an's inimitability — a technical term the Qur'an itself does not use, built by scholars out of the challenge's fourteen-century record. The root means to incapacitate: a *muʿjizah*, miracle, is what disables the opponent's capacity to respond, and the Qur'an is Islam's central miracle in precisely this sense — not a staff turned serpent but a standing public test that no one has passed. The classical analysis locates the inimitability in several simultaneous registers: the linguistic surface — eloquence and a prose form that is neither poetry nor speech; the structural layer — coherence across a text revealed piecemeal over twenty-three years; the semantic depths — layers of meaning that reward centuries of reading without exhaustion. Each register was separately attemptable; their combination was not. And the miracle's modality suits its claim: a staff can be watched only by those present, while a text challenges every generation with the same demand, at the same floor of one sūrah, in the reader's own lifetime.

**The Silence of Fourteen Centuries as Evidence**

The challenge's career is part of its argument, and the verse leaves that career open on purpose. The condition — *in kuntum ṣādiqīn*, if you are truthful — converts every subsequent century into testimony. Hostile regimes possessed the motive, the poets of two classical golden ages possessed the craft, and the Arabic grammatical tradition itself grew up around this text with every tool needed to imitate it; the record contains no surviving work accepted as meeting the demand, from enemies who would have gained everything by meeting it. Skeptics can discount the record — victors preserve archives — but the burden the verse assigns is public and permanent: the test remains runnable today, by anyone, with the floor still at one sūrah. That permanence is rare among miracles and deliberate among claims. A wonder that expired with its witnesses requires faith in a report; a challenge still standing requires only an attempt. The verse's final word to the doubter is therefore not *believe me* but *try* — and the next verse states what follows when the trying fails, as the verse already knows it will.
