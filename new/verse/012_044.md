## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:44

> They replied, “These are confused visions and we do not know the interpretation of such dreams.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Classifying the Dream as Meaningless**

The answer is a diagnosis. *Aḍghāthu aḥlām* — jumbled dreams, dreams of confused things. The first noun is a plural whose singular denotes a tangled bundle, a handful of mixed sticks or plants gathered together without order. Applied to a dream it describes imagery with no coherent content, fragments assembled by a sleeping mind rather than signs carrying a message. The classification is technical, not dismissive in tone: the notables are saying that what the king saw belongs to a category which has no interpretation, and therefore no interpreter can be faulted for failing to produce one. The reply is well formed, professionally worded, and wrong.

**Categories Behind the Answer**

The tradition the notables are drawing on divides what people see in sleep into kinds. A true vision comes from a quarter that gives it content and can be traced back to a reality. A distressing dream comes from a devil's suggestion and is meant to frighten. A third kind is the residue of waking thoughts, the mind replaying what it was occupied with. Only the first is interpretable, because only the first corresponds to anything outside the dreamer. The Qur'an's own distinction between *ruʾyā* and *ḥulm* rests on the same ground, as does the Prophetic instruction that a bad dream be met with seeking refuge rather than with analysis. The king's council has placed his vision in the second or third category, which is a decision about the origin of the dream rather than about its difficulty.

**"We Do Not Know" Is Also a Confession**

The second clause can be read narrowly or broadly, and the commentators preserve both. Narrowly: we are not skilled in interpreting dreams of this confused kind — an admission limited to the category just named. Broadly: we are not skilled in dream interpretation at all, which is an admission about the entire court. The broader reading is the more likely in context, since a king who asks his council *if you can interpret visions* has evidently received no such service before, and the council's answer does not offer to fetch someone who can. Either way the confession is total. The governing establishment of Egypt has been asked a question by its ruler and has answered that it has no answer, which is the vacuum the next verse fills.

**An Answer That Is Also Convenient**

A king's dream is a dangerous object. If the notables had offered an interpretation and it failed, the failure would be theirs. If they had offered a grim one and it proved wrong, the offence would be worse. Declaring the dream meaningless costs them nothing, protects them from error, and closes the enquiry in a single sentence. The Qur'an does not accuse them of this calculation, but it does not need to; the structure of the reply is sufficient evidence. This is the same posture the household adopted in verse 35, where people who had seen the proofs chose the option that ended the matter rather than the one that followed it. Twice in the Egyptian section the chapter shows an establishment resolving a problem by declaring that no problem exists.

**Taʾwīl, the Word They Claim Not to Possess**

The term they use for interpretation is *taʾwīl*, from a root meaning to bring a thing back to its origin. In Qur'anic usage it covers the interpretation of dreams, the explanation of events whose meaning is not apparent — as when Moses promises to explain what his companion could not bear patiently (18:87) — and the handling of verses whose sense is not self-evident, which 3:7 assigns to God alone while noting that those with deviant hearts pursue their own. In later Islamic usage the same word came to designate the interior hermeneutic of a text, set alongside *tafsīr*, its outward exposition, though the distinction was never rigid and al-Ṭabarī titled his commentary with *taʾwīl* rather than *tafsīr*. The notables' sentence therefore denies competence in a discipline with a settled name and a recognised method.

**Experts Failing Is the Point of the Verse**

Structurally the chapter needs this answer. If the court had produced a reading, Joseph would have had no route out of the prison and no route into the palace. The sūrah arranges the failure before arranging the reminder: first the council disqualifies the dream, then the cupbearer remembers the man who could not have been disqualified. The order matters because it establishes that Joseph's interpretation is not one opinion among several but the only one offered anywhere in Egypt. When it arrives it meets no competitor, and its verification over the following fifteen years is therefore uncontested. The verse is the mechanism by which a prisoner becomes indispensable.

The failure also disposes of an objection a later reader might raise. Egypt in this period had a professional class of dream interpreters, and the biblical account shows the king summoning all the magicians and wise men of the country for the same purpose (Genesis 41:8). Their inability is reported in both traditions, and the Qur'an's version is the more pointed because it records their reasoning rather than simply their silence. They did not fail for want of effort; they applied a settled classification and reached a settled conclusion. The chapter thereby locates the gap that Joseph fills not in the court's diligence but in the limits of its discipline, which no amount of further effort inside that discipline would have overcome.

**The Dream Was Not Confused at All**

The reader knows something the council does not. The vision had a structure — two series, one number, one relationship between the terms — and its content was about to determine the fate of a country and, through that country, of a family in Canaan. Calling it *aḍghāth* was not a modest admission of limits but an error with consequences: it would have left Egypt unprepared for a famine it could have survived, and it would have left Joseph in a cell for the rest of his life. The chapter allows the error to stand uncorrected in the mouths of the men who made it, and corrects it only by showing what happens next. That is its method throughout. False accounts are not argued with; they are outlived.
