## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:139

> Do not falter or grieve, for you will have the upper hand, if you are ˹true˺ believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Commands Joined in One Sentence**

The verse forbids two things at once and then gives a reason for the prohibition. The community is told not to weaken and not to grieve, and it is told why: its position is one of ascendancy, given the condition the sentence names at its end.

The pairing of the two prohibitions is not accidental. Weakness and grief belong together in a community's life after a defeat: the first is the practical loss of confidence, and the second is the emotional form the same loss takes. The verse addresses both, and the reason it supplies is meant to treat them at their common root.

**The Word Behind "Falter"**

The verb rendered as falter also carries the sense of becoming weak, faint, or diminished, and one commentator glossed it as meaning that the believers should not consider themselves weak. The prohibition is therefore directed as much at self-assessment as at outward conduct.

The Book uses the same root again when it tells the believers in another sūrah not to lose heart and not to call for peace while they have the upper hand. The instruction in both places concerns the estimate a community forms about itself, because that estimate shapes what it attempts. A community that decides it is finished will behave accordingly, and the Book addresses the decision at the point where it is made.

**Grief and What It Was About**

Al-Ṭabarī read the prohibition of grief in this verse as directed at mourning over the losses of the battle, the men killed and the ground given up on the day of Uḥud. The grief the verse addresses is not sorrow in general but sorrow over the specific cost of the fighting.

The distinction is worth keeping. The Book does not forbid the community to feel the deaths of its people, and it does not treat the memory of a defeat as a subject that must be avoided. What the verse forbids is a grief that settles into the conclusion that the cause has been lost, and the reason that follows in the next clause explains why that conclusion is wrong.

**The Promise of Ascendancy**

The reason given for the two prohibitions is a statement about the outcome: the believers will be on top, or higher, if the condition that follows is met. The word denotes elevation, and the commentators explained it as victory over the enemies of the community on the one hand, and as the superiority of the community's final state over theirs on the other.

The second reading has the advantage of holding true in every period of the community's history, since the fighting does not always go its way. The Book's promises to believers in this sūrah have already shown the same care about outcomes: what is guaranteed is not the result of every engagement but the standing of the community with its Lord, and the difference between those two things is what the community most needed to learn.

**The Conditional Clause and Its Two Readings**

The verse ends with a condition: if you are believers. Some commentators read the particle as meaning since, so that the sentence becomes a statement that the community's ascendancy follows from its faith rather than waiting on a further condition.

Both readings place the weight on the quality named at the end of the sentence. The Book regularly attaches its promises to faith and to the conduct that accompanies it, and the effect of the clause here is to turn the community's attention back to the ground of its standing. The promise is not being made on account of numbers, equipment, or the possession of a city.

**The Verse's Place After the Wound**

The verse follows a description of the community's moral condition and precedes the direct reference to the wound the believers received. It functions as the interpretation of the battle before the battle is mentioned again.

The sūrah's method in the whole passage is to describe the community's position before it describes the event, so that the event is heard in light of the position. The believers are told what they are in the sight of God, and only then are they reminded of what happened on the field. The order is the same one the Book uses when it introduces its descriptions of earlier peoples, and it makes the loss easier to place.

**The Book's Other Promises of Support**

The Book states the same assurance in other places with different words. One passage tells the believers that if they are mindful of God, He will grant them a criterion by which to distinguish right from wrong, wipe out their misdeeds, and forgive them. Another tells them that if they support the cause of God, He will support them and make their footing firm.

The pattern is consistent: the promise of help is tied to the community's own condition, and it is stated in terms of standing and firmness rather than of guaranteed victory in every engagement. The clause at the end of this verse belongs to that pattern, and the community that had just been routed needed the distinction.

**What the Verse Requires of Its Reader**

The verse asks for a decision about how the community will describe itself after a loss. It rules out both panic and despair, and it grounds the ruling in the community's relation to God rather than in an assessment of its remaining strength.

That is why the sentence remains useful long after the battle that occasioned it. Every generation of believers faces situations in which the visible facts suggest that the cause has been abandoned, and the verse supplies the response in a form short enough to be carried in memory. The commands and the reason they rest on are all contained in one line, and the community's history has shown how often it has needed exactly that.
