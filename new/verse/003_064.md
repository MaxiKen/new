## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:64

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! Let us come to common terms: that we will worship none but Allah, associate none with Him, nor take one another as lords instead of Allah.” But if they turn away, then say, “Bear witness that we have submitted ˹to Allah alone˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Invitation Addressed to the Scriptured Communities**

The sūrah turns from the dispute about Jesus to a direct address to the People of the Book, and the address begins with an invitation rather than a charge. The Prophet is told what to say, and what he says is *let us come*.

The form of the sentence is worth noticing before its content. An invitation belongs to a relationship of equals setting out to reach an agreed position; a proclamation would not have used the first-person plural. The Book stages the encounter between the Muslim community and the communities that held earlier scriptures as a meeting in which the other party is asked to join the speaker on a shared footing. Nothing in the verse abandons the claim the sūrah has been making about the truth of its own account. What it does is to place the terms of agreement where both parties can recognise them.

**The Word Sawāʾ and What "Common Terms" Means**

The phrase rendered "common terms" translates a single Arabic word whose root sense is levelness, evenness, and fairness — the same root that gives the expression "the middle of the road" for the correct way of proceeding. The commentators found two readings, and they overlap more than they appear to.

Some took it to mean that the word being offered is a just and fair one, in the sense that both parties are treated equitably in it. Others took the term to describe what the two sides already share. Al-Rāzī's gloss captures the first reading well: come to a word in which there is fair treatment from each of us to the other. The translation's choice of "common terms" follows the second reading, and it fits the three items that follow, since each is a proposition that the earlier scriptures themselves assert.

**The Three Propositions**

The invitation names three commitments: worship of none but God, the association of nothing with Him, and the refusal to take one another as lords instead of God. The three form a single position stated from different angles.

The first two concern God directly, and they are the ground of everything the sūrah has said about Jesus: a messenger whose birth was a wonder is not a partner in divinity. The third concerns human authorities. The commentators offered two readings of it — that it forbids the prostration paid to religious leaders, and that it forbids obedience to them carried to the point of setting aside what is true. The Book states the same charge elsewhere in harsher language, describing people who took their rabbis and monks as lords apart from God, along with the Messiah, though they were commanded to worship one God (9:31). The clause in this verse is that criticism in the form of an invitation to its remedy.

**Who Is Being Addressed**

Commentators differed about the audience. Some held that the address covers the Jews of Madinah and its surroundings; others that it covers both communities; a third group restricted it to the Christian delegation whose argument about Jesus occupies the earlier part of the sūrah.

The three positions are not exclusive, since the verse's terms apply wherever the conditions obtain, and the sūrah will continue to address both communities in the verses that follow. What matters for the reader is the level at which the invitation operates. It does not ask the other party to accept a new book or to renounce an ancestry. It asks for a statement about God that the earlier scriptures themselves support, which is why the Book can present it as a basis for agreement rather than a demand for surrender.

**"If They Turn Away, Bear Witness"**

The verse closes with a provision for refusal. If the invitation is declined, the Prophet is told to say: bear witness that we have submitted. The response to rejection is not argument and not retaliation; it is a statement of the speaker's own position, made in public.

The closing formula is the same one the sūrah has used before, and it echoes the disciples' request in the earlier passage, where they asked to be counted among the witnesses. A community that has stated its case and been refused does not need to win the conversation. It needs to have said clearly what it holds, and the clause given here is the Book's instruction on how to leave an argument with dignity. The witness is borne, the offer remains on record, and the matter is left.

**What the Verse Reveals About the Sūrah's Method**

The sūrah has spent several verses establishing that a messenger can be created without a father and that God has no partner; it now summarises the position it has been defending in a form the other party can be asked to accept.

That is the method the whole passage follows. The Book argues its case at length, and then narrows the disagreement to its minimum in order to offer a way forward. The reader who has followed the narrative from the family of ʿImrān to the challenge of the disputants is now shown the grounds on which discussion can continue. The verses that follow take up the shared ancestor of the communities involved, which is the next place where the Book looks for common ground, and shows what both parties had been making of him.
