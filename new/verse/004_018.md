## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:18

> However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, “Now I repent!” nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.

### Expanded Commentary

**The postponed confession**

The verse distinguishes repentance from a declaration made when death has removed the possibility of continued obedience. A person may knowingly persist in wrongdoing, treating warnings as distant, and then announce repentance when the unseen becomes visible. That statement may be emotionally sincere, but it is not the tested transformation described in the previous verse. The difference is not that God lacks mercy at the deathbed; it is that the person has reached the end of the arena in which repentance can become a life.

Pharaoh’s confession as he drowned is the Qur’anic example: recognition came only when punishment was undeniable, and it could no longer produce faithful action. The same principle appears in the Qur’an’s account of people who ask to return after death. A confession that costs no surrender of desire and cannot restore a harmed person is not equivalent to the turning of the living heart.

**A warning against spiritual procrastination**

The verse also addresses those who die as disbelievers. It does not encourage human beings to declare another person’s final state in individual cases; that judgment belongs to Allah. It warns the believer not to build a plan around delaying faith until certainty is forced upon him. Faith is a willing response to signs, not a word extracted by the arrival of the final consequence.

**Repentance and the rights of others**

The two verses together make a practical point about injustice. A guardian who consumed an orphan’s property cannot wait until death and assume that a sentence of regret cancels the child’s claim. Restoration must begin while the assets, victims, and opportunities for repair remain accessible. Likewise, a person who has harmed a spouse, employee, or neighbour should make amends now rather than convert fear of judgment into a last-minute performance.

The verse is severe because delay is a form of choice. Yet its severity is also mercy: while the reader is alive, the prohibition has not become the description of him. The proper response is immediate return, not despair.

**The ethics of timely repair**

The verse is especially urgent where another person has been harmed. A delayed apology may leave evidence lost and the victim unsupported. Return the property, correct the allegation, seek treatment, and ask forgiveness while these steps can still change the outcome. Deathbed regret may be sincere, but it cannot give back every opportunity that delay destroyed.
