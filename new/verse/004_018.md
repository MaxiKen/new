## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:18

> However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, “Now I repent!” nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Return That Comes Too Late**

The previous verse defined the repentance that is accepted: evil done ignorantly or recklessly, then a turn soon after (4:17). This verse defines the repentance that is not: *wa laysat al-tawbatu lilladhīna yaʿmalūna al-sayyiʾāt ḥattā idhā ḥaḍara aḥadahumu al-mawtu qāla innī tubtu al-ān* — repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until death presents itself and then they cry, Now I repent. The image is not of a person who fell and then struggled to return; it is of a person who kept doing wrong while deferring the return, telling himself there would be time later. *Ḥattā idhā ḥaḍara aḥadahumu al-mawtu* — until death is present — places the utterance at the moment when choice is ending. The Qur’an elsewhere describes the same scene with Pharaoh, who believed only when drowning overtook him and was told that his faith then avails nothing, because the trial by which faith is tested has closed. The principle is consistent: repentance is a deed of the living that mattered while life allowed alternatives; once the alternative has been removed by the arrival of death, the words now I repent lose the condition that made them meaningful, which was willingness while unwillingness was still possible.

**Nor Those Who Die as Disbelievers**

The verse adds *wa lā alladhīna yamūtūna wa hum kuffār* — nor those who die as disbelievers. That second group is not deferring repentance; they have rejected the very address to which repentance would return. The pairing shows two ways the window closes: by delay until the last breath, and by death in a state that never turned at all. Both end in the same preparation: *ulāʾika aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāban alīmā* — for them We have prepared a painful punishment. The verb *aʿtadnā* — We have prepared — stresses that the recompense is not improvised at the moment; it has been readied, as Gardens were readied for those who obey limits (4:13). The symmetry between the two endings is deliberate. Two verses earlier, those who obey limits enter Gardens to stay forever; here, those who persist until death face a penalty already prepared and described as painful — *alīm* — because the wrong persisted was itself a cause of pain to others and to the soul that chose it. The sūrah elsewhere says *“whoever does this sinfully and unjustly, We will burn them in the Fire, and that is easy for Allah”* (4:30), and this verse specifies one of the easiest ways to fall into that Fire — by treating time as endless.

**Knowingly Persisting Versus Acting Ignorantly**

The contrast between 4:17 and 4:18 turns on the state during the act. Verse 17 spoke of *bi jahālah* — ignorantly, heedlessly — while 4:18 speaks of persistence — *yaʿmalūna al-sayyiʾāt* as a continuing practice until death arrives. Persisting was understood by early commentators not as a single grave sin committed once in ignorance and then regretted, but as a settled habit where the person knows what he does and keeps doing it while postponing return. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“they do not persist in what they did while they know”* (3:135), praising those who, when they err, remember and do not persist. That praise implies that the blameworthy pattern is knowing and persisting. The sūrah has already shown what persisting looks like in other domains: consuming an orphan’s wealth hastily before he grows up to demand it (4:6), mixing wealth to make use easier (4:2), inheriting women against their will and then pressuring them to surrender dowries (4:19). Each is a case where the actor knows the roster of right yet continues because the victim cannot yet speak or because death seems distant. This verse collapses that distance and says the time to speak is while speaking remains a choice.

**Why Now I Repent Fails at the Throat**

The phrase *innī tubtu al-ān* — now I repent — fails not because the words are wrong but because the time that gives them weight has passed. The Prophet ﷺ said that Allah accepts the servant’s repentance so long as the death rattle has not yet reached the throat, a ḥadīth transmitted in the Sunan and explained as marking the point where the unseen becomes seen and faith ceases to be a trial. The sūrah’s language matches that threshold without needing to detail physiology: *ḥaḍara aḥadahumu al-mawtu* — death is present to him — the moment when the person sees what he had only been told, and when fear replaces willingness. At that moment, to say now I repent is to react to sight, not to turn to the unseen. The Qur’an elsewhere says that faith at the moment of seeing the punishment does not benefit those who had denied before, because the benefit of faith lies in trusting before seeing. The same applies to turning. A return that is accepted is one given while the return remains hard, when the act could still be continued and is instead abandoned. A return forced by the sight of death carries no such cost, and therefore no such pardon, because it does not choose God while another choice was still genuinely open.

**Prepared Punishment and the Sūrah’s Pattern**

The closing *aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāban alīmā* gathers this verse into the sūrah’s larger pattern of prepared recompense. Those who keep limits receive Gardens prepared and described as flowing water (4:13); those who eat orphan wealth unjustly are told they eat fire in their bellies and will burn in a blazing Hell (4:10); those who cross limits receive a humiliating punishment and abiding in Fire (4:14). This verse adds the temporal border to that pattern: the Garden remains open while repentance remains soon (4:17); the painful punishment becomes prepared when soon is replaced by until death. The lesson for the living is not to measure the exact length of soon but to remove delay as a plan. The Qur’an elsewhere praises *“those who, when they commit an indecent act or wrong themselves, remember Allah immediately and seek forgiveness”* (3:135), and the praise rests on immediacy. The sūrah’s earlier promise that *“if you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of lesser misdeeds”* (4:31) also depends on the habit of turning quickly from smaller faults before they harden into major persistence. Delay is the bridge between small and great; this verse cuts that bridge by naming its end.

**The Mercy That Remains While Breath Remains**

Read with the verses that opened the sūrah, this warning is also a mercy. The call to be mindful of the Lord who created all from a single soul (4:1), to return orphan wealth with witnesses under God as Reckoner (4:6), and to accept discipline and then be relieved when mending appears (4:16) — all of this assumes that breath remains and that a turn is still possible. The sūrah does not treat a person who fell ignorantly as finished; it says Allah will pardon those who turn soon (4:17). It distrusts only the plan to keep persisting until the last hour and then to speak. That distinction keeps the community from two harms: despair that says no return will be accepted at all, and presumption that says the return can be postponed indefinitely. Both are corrected by time. While death has not yet presented itself, the door stands open; when it presents itself, the open door is the one the person chose not to enter. For them, the sūrah says, a painful punishment has been prepared — not as a surprise, but as the stated end of a path that was walked knowingly, step after step, until the time for choosing ran out.
