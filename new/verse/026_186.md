## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:186

> Also, you are only a human being like us, and we think you are indeed a liar.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Humanity Is Treated as a Reason for Rejection**

The people now present two claims about Shuʿayb. He is human like them, and they regard him as a liar. The first claim is true in one sense: a messenger eats, speaks, and lives among human beings. The error is turning that shared humanity into proof that God could not choose him to carry guidance.

Quran 17:94 records the same objection in direct form: some people found it strange that a human messenger should be sent. The objection misunderstands the purpose of a messenger. If guidance came through a being who never shared human weakness, people could dismiss obedience as impossible to imitate. A human messenger gives the command a visible form within ordinary life.

Because Shuʿayb belongs to their world, the people know his voice, habits, and social position. Familiarity should allow them to test his character, but they use it to reduce him. They see the person they have known and refuse to see the responsibility God may have placed upon him.

The same community can say that someone is too ordinary to be a messenger and too familiar to be trusted. Both judgments avoid the central question: does he speak truthfully for God? Shared humanity is not evidence of lying. It is the condition in which a prophetic example can be understood and followed.

**Calling Him a Liar Is a Verdict Without Examination**

The phrase about lying gives no reason for the verdict. Shuʿayb has not been shown to seek payment, and his warning asks the people to return what belongs to others rather than to enrich himself. His claim must still be assessed, but a bare accusation does not perform that assessment.

A lie about God is a grave charge, so it should not be used as a casual name for an unwelcome speaker. The people have the means to examine his conduct, his message, and its continuity with the call of earlier prophets. Instead, they pronounce judgment before answering the moral facts placed before them.

**A Messenger's Humanity Makes the Call Near**

The prophets in this sūrah speak the language of their people and face the same world of hunger, fear, trade, and family pressure. That nearness makes their guidance practical. Shuʿayb can command honest dealing without pretending that he has never faced temptation or social cost.

The human messenger does not become divine by being chosen. His authority comes from the One who sends him. This distinction protects both sides of belief: the messenger deserves obedience as a conveyor of God's command, while worship remains for Allah alone. The people's phrase confuses the messenger's nature with the source of his authority.

**Rejection Often Begins by Making the Guide Smaller**

A community may shrink a warning by shrinking the person who delivers it. He is called ordinary, ambitious, confused, or false, and the hearers feel released from examining themselves. Yet a claim is not refuted by lowering the speaker in the eyes of the crowd.

Shuʿayb's answer is not recorded here because the verse concentrates on their judgment. The record is enough to expose the defect in it. They treat humanity as disqualification and suspicion as proof. A sound response would recognize that the ordinary human setting of a messenger is not a weakness in revelation; it is part of its mercy and intelligibility.

A human messenger also exposes the false demand that guidance must arrive without any ordinary means. The people want a messenger who is unlike them, yet they would then claim that his obedience cannot be copied. Allah's wisdom places revelation in a human life so that patience, worship, honesty, and courage can be practiced within the same pressures that confront the audience.

The charge of lying should have been especially difficult for this community to sustain. Shuʿayb was not offering a private trick. His call required him to oppose the practices that gave influential people advantage. A liar seeking easy status would normally flatter those who could reward him. His warning instead placed his own standing at risk, which should have prompted a fair examination of his motive.

Familiarity can still be useful evidence when it is used honestly. The people knew whether Shuʿayb had a history of deceit, whether he had hidden financial demands, and whether his conduct matched his words. They could distinguish a messenger's human limitations from a fabricated claim. Instead they treated the very possibility of ordinary humanity as a reason to stop looking.

The verse reaches every community that prefers a distant ideal to a nearby example. It is easier to praise righteousness in a figure who cannot correct us than to follow a truthful person who shares our language and sees our excuses. Prophetic humanity removes that escape. The guide stands within the world and calls it to submit to the Creator.

The phrase like us can also conceal a demand for a messenger who confirms the community's preferences. If Shuʿayb were not human, his authority would be easier to imagine as distant and irrelevant. Because he is human, his example threatens the excuse that honest measure is too difficult for ordinary people. His life says that obedience can be practiced amid trade, family, fear, and social pressure.

Calling him a liar then protects the community from the force of that example. Once the messenger is declared false, people can keep their old habits without asking whether those habits have made them unjust. Yet the claim of falsehood should have been tested against the content of his call. He asks them to worship Allah and stop taking people's property; he does not ask them to enrich him or make him the object of worship.

The verse thus exposes a refusal to distinguish the messenger from the message. Human nature describes the carrier, while divine revelation explains the authority behind the call. Confusing the two allows a community to reject guidance for the wrong reason and then call its rejection rational.
