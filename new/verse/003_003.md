## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:3

> He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb That Says the Book Came Down**

The Arabic of the verse uses two forms of one root for two acts of revelation: *nazzala*, "He sent down", attached to the Book given to the Prophet, and *anzala*, attached to the Torah and the Gospel. A reader who works only in translation sees the same English verb twice and misses the variation.

Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī read the variation as intentional and proportionate: the Qur'an came down in portions across twenty-three years, so the intensive form suits it, while the earlier books arrived as complete units, so the plain form suits them. Al-Ṭabarī answers that the two forms are used interchangeably throughout the Qur'an and the distinction cannot be pressed. His objection carries weight, but so does the fact that the Qur'an elsewhere says the same thing about itself plainly: *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace.”* (17:106). The difference between the two positions is not what happened; it is whether the wording was chosen to tell us about the timing. On the text alone, al-Ṭabarī is the more careful reader, and the portioning of the Qur'an is established by verses that do not depend on the grammar of this one.

**"In Truth" — a Claim About Content, Not Tone**

The phrase *bi-l-ḥaqq* is not an adjective of style. It asserts that what was sent down corresponds to what is, that the Book is not a useful fiction or a political instrument dressed in religious language. The Qur'an makes the same claim about revelation generally when it says that the Book was revealed *“with the truth, as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority on them”* (5:48).

The consequences of the claim are heavy. A Book that is true cannot be improved by its readers' preferences, and its statements about the past, about God, and about the final outcome cannot be discarded by people who dislike them while the rest is retained. The Qur'anic critique of the People of the Book later in this sūrah turns on exactly this point: they are not charged with reading too little. They are charged with knowing what the revelation says and preferring a part of it.

**Confirming What Came Before**

*Muṣaddiq* is the word translated "confirming", and it describes a witness, not a replacement. The new revelation testifies that the earlier ones were genuine, and the Gospel does the same for the Torah before it: *“Then in the footsteps of the prophets, We sent Jesus, son of Mary, confirming the Torah revealed before him. And We gave him the Gospel containing guidance and light and confirming what was revealed in the Torah—a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing.”* (5:46). The Book's relation to its predecessors is stated most compactly in 2:41: *“Believe in My revelations which confirm your Scriptures.”*

Jesus puts his own mission in the same verb when he announces a messenger still to come (61:6). So the chain runs forward as well as backward: each revelation attests the one before it and points to the one after, and no link in the chain is presented as the cancellation of its predecessor. Confirmation and correction are compatible; erasure is not what the word means.

**The Two Books Named Here**

Tawrāt denotes the revelation given to Moses; Injīl denotes the revelation given to Jesus. The names are not used in the Qur'an for the texts as they happen to exist in a given century, and this is the point where much of the modern discussion gets stuck. The Qur'an speaks of a book revealed *to a prophet*, and its unit of measure is God's speech, not the history of a manuscript tradition. When the Qur'an addresses the People of the Book, it addresses communities that possess a scripture, and its charges against them concern conduct and interpretation as much as anything else.

The two names sit inside a sentence whose subject is the Book given to the Prophet. He is the one addressed, and everything else in the line is collateral to that address.

**Revelation as a Calendar of Descents**

A report from Wathilah ibn al-Asqaʿ puts the descents in sequence inside one month: the Scriptures of Abraham on the first night of Ramadan, the Torah on the sixth, the Gospel on the thirteenth, and the Qur'an on the twenty-fourth (Musnad Aḥmad, from Wathilah ibn al-Asqaʿ). The report speaks of the beginning of each revelation, the night its descent commenced, not of the whole of any book arriving at once.

Read against this verse, the report supplies a shape. Revelation in the Qur'an's account is not one event in one place; it is a recurring act of speech spread across the calendar and the centuries, one author addressing different peoples, in the language of each, with the same news about God. The night mentioned in 97:1 is the night this sūrah's Book began to come down.

The report also settles a smaller question of tone. If the descents were spread across the month and across the ages, then the Qur'an's arrival is not the discrediting of an earlier revelation but its continuation in a new address. The verse's word for this is "confirming", and its grammar places the earlier books in the same sentence as the later one, under one revealing subject. When the sūrah turns, a few verses on, to the responsibilities of the communities that hold those books, it will do so from inside that shared history rather than from outside it.

**The Two Verbs for Sending Down**

The verse uses two forms of the same Arabic verb for sending down: nazzala for the Book given to the Prophet, and anzala for the Torah and the Gospel. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī read the difference as a statement about manner, since the intensive form suggests that the Book came down in portions across a period while the plain form suggests that the earlier scriptures were delivered whole. Other commentators hold that the two forms are used interchangeably across the Book and that nothing should be built on the variation. Both readings agree on the point that matters to the argument here: the three texts named in the sentence all come down from God, and none of them arrived by human arrangement.

**How the Term for the Criterion Has Been Read**

The word rendered as the Criterion, al-furqān, appears in the Book in more than one connection. It usually names the Qur'an itself, and the passages that use it call the revelation the means by which a person separates truth from falsehood. Its range is wider than that, since the same noun is attached to what was given to Moses and Aaron (2:53; 21:48), and some commentators therefore took this verse to be speaking of discernment as such, the capacity to tell right from wrong that revelation supplies. Others held that the word here names the Psalms, and al-Rāzī offered a further reading on which the Criterion is not a fourth book but a second description of the three already named, all of them containing what distinguishes the permitted from the prohibited.

**What the Torah and the Gospel Name**

The Torah is normally understood to mean the five books of Moses, and the Gospel the revelation given to Jesus, and the commentators asked how those names relate to the scriptures as they now exist. The Book says that the revelation given to the Prophet confirms what came before it, and al-Rāzī raised the difficulty of how a text from the past can be spoken of as present, answering that the earlier lore is treated as present because it is so widely known. One line of explanation takes the charge against the People of the Book to concern their interpretation rather than the wording of their books, which is the position of those who speak of distortion of meaning (2:75; 4:46; 5:13, 41). Another allows that the Gospel given to Jesus is conveyed by the Gospel the Christians possess without being identical to it, so that what they have carries the message while also containing material of a different kind. The discussion is not settled by the verse, which states the relation between the books and leaves the detail of their transmission to be examined.
