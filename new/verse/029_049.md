## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49

> But this ˹Quran˺ is ˹a set of˺ clear revelations ˹preserved˺ in the hearts of those gifted with knowledge. And none denies Our revelations except the ˹stubborn˺ wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Book Carried in Memory Rather Than on a Shelf**

The sentence answers the previous verse's argument about writing by stating where the revelation actually lives. The Qur'an is a set of clear verses preserved in the hearts of those who have been given knowledge, and the image is precise: the Book is housed in people rather than in a manuscript that could be lost, altered, or monopolized. The Book elsewhere says of itself that it has been made easy to remember, and it repeats the question of whether anyone will take heed (54:17). What the verse describes is the mechanism by which a text survives without a printing press or an archive: a community that carries it in its chests, recites it in its gatherings, and transmits it to its children.

**"Clear Revelations" and What Clarity Means Here**

The Arabic describes the verses as clear signs, and commentators stress the transparency of the language and the memorability of the phrasing. The Book's own claim about itself is that it is an explanation for people, guidance, and mercy, and it repeats the characterization in several places. What the verse adds is the location of that clarity: not in the mind of one interpreter, not in an institution, but in the knowledge of those who receive it. The point is important in a chapter that has just discussed disputes with other communities that possessed written scriptures. The argument is not that one set of documents beats another, but that this revelation is present and legible in the understanding of its bearers.

Clarity, in the Book's usage, is a property of the message rather than of its packaging. The verses are called clear because they can be understood by the people they reach, and the Book habitually locates the obstacle elsewhere, in the use a hearer makes of what has already been explained to him.

**Who Has Been Given Knowledge**

Two readings of the phrase appear in the commentaries. One takes it as a reference to the Muslims who memorized, recited, and interpreted the Qur'an, and it fits the Book's repeated use of the term knowledge for the understanding that comes with faith. The other takes it as a reference to those among the People of the Book who found the signs of the Prophet in their own scriptures and recognized him. Both readings have support in the surrounding verses, which have just reported that faithful People of the Book believe in the revelation. The two groups are not mutually exclusive, and the sūrah has already described scholars of earlier scriptures joining the community of those who submit.

The gift is attached to people rather than to institutions. The Book does not say that the scholars of a school preserve the revelation, but that those given knowledge hold it, which is why the sentence applies to a believer in a hostile town with no library and no academy within reach.

**Denial Repeats With a Sharper Word**

The previous verse ended by naming the stubborn disbelievers as the only ones who deny God's signs; this one ends with the stubborn wrongdoers, and the substitution is deliberate. The change of term shifts the complaint from a matter of belief to a matter of justice. Rejecting a clear sign is not merely an intellectual position; it is an injury to the person who commits it, which is the understanding of wrongdoing that the whole sūrah is built on. The two endings together cover the same offence from two sides, and the fact that the Book repeats the verdict with a different word indicates that the denial described has the character of both unbelief and self-harm.

The sharper wording reflects what the hearer has already been given. A community denying a claim it has never examined is one case, while the denial described here comes after clear revelations have been set in front of the audience, and the Book's vocabulary marks the difference between not knowing and refusing to know.

**A Community That Cannot Be Dispossessed**

A reader in the position of the first audience, being pressured to abandon a religious claim in a city that opposed it, would hear something further in this verse. The Book does not reside in a building that can be seized or a document that can be burned; it resides in the memory of the believers, and no authority in Makkah had jurisdiction over that. The sūrah has already told its audience that the protection they need cannot come from any creature, and this verse shows the corresponding asset: a text that travels with its carriers. The community's possession of revelation is not threatened by its political weakness, and the verse makes the observation without any appeal to resistance or to material strength of any kind.

**Why the Final Verdict Names the Deniers Rather Than the Doubters**

The sentence reserves its harshest description for people who deny signs they have understood, and the distinction has been a thread through the entire chapter. The wavering believer of the earlier verses was shown as someone whose claim failed under pressure, and the Book addressed him as a subject of warning rather than as a denier. The people described here are doing something different: they are actively refusing what has been made clear, and the sūrah has already given a catalogue of the communities that did the same thing. That catalogue is what gives this short sentence its weight. The reader has just watched five nations disappear because of the behaviour that the verse now names.

Part of the reason for the distinction is that it leaves room for a reader who is still working through the case. The sentence closes one door and keeps another open for someone with questions he has not yet answered, and the later verses of the sūrah turn to those who strive, which describes effort rather than a settled position.

**A Heart Opened and a Heart Hardened**

The Book's location of the revelation in the hearts of the knowledgeable connects to a wider contrast it draws between two conditions of the same organ. One passage asks whether those whose hearts God has opened to submission, so that they are enlightened by their Lord, can be like the misguided, and then pronounces woe on those whose hearts are hardened at the remembrance of God, calling them clearly astray (39:22). The verse under discussion describes the first condition in its settled form. The verses are not merely stored inside people; they are held in hearts that have been prepared to receive them, and the difference between a community that carries the Book and one that has heard it and forgotten is described in the same passage as the difference between light and hardness.

**The Book's Own Account of How It Is Kept**

The claim that the revelation is preserved in the hearts of knowledgeable people connects to a wider set of passages about how the Book is held. The Book says of itself that it has been made easy to remember (54:17), describes itself as a reminder and a clear recitation rather than poetry (36:69), and says that God has taken responsibility for collecting and reciting it (75:17–18). The mechanism these verses describe together is one in which the community's memory is not an accidental vessel but a designed feature of the revelation's survival. The verse under discussion states the result of that design: the signs are not locked in an archive that a hostile power could seize but carried by people who recite them in their homes and in their prayers, which is precisely the practice the sūrah will command a few verses later. A community whose only asset is a text in its memory is difficult to disarm, and the Book presents that arrangement as one of its own provisions rather than as a fortunate accident of history.
