## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:28

> But no! ˹They only say this˺ because the truth they used to hide will become all too clear to them. Even if they were to be sent back, they would certainly revert to what they were forbidden. Indeed they are liars!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refusal That Opens the Sentence**

The verse begins with a word of correction, a blunt denial of what the previous sentence reported. The promise made in front of the Fire is struck down before it can be examined, and the striking down happens in a single syllable. The Book uses this device when it wants a claim cancelled rather than argued with, and the cancellation here is total: what the speakers offered as evidence of their sincerity is dismissed as a symptom.

The word also tells the reader how to hear the rest of the verse. What follows is not a debate with the people in the scene but an explanation of them, given to whoever is listening now. The Book explains why men promise reform in the fire of the Hereafter while they never reformed in the safety of the world.

**What Was Hidden Becomes Plain**

The reason given is knowledge rather than honesty: the truth they used to hide will become all too clear to them. What was concealed in life, whether the realities of the resurrection and the judgment that the leaders dismissed in public, or the deeds that were kept out of sight, is displayed on the day the secrets are tested (86:9). Their promise is therefore not a change of heart. It is what a person says when he can no longer say anything else.

Commentators offer several identifications of what was hidden: the disbelief that hypocrites concealed, the truth about the Prophet that the People of the Book suppressed, or the evil deeds that the wrongdoers kept from view. The Makkan context favours the last two readings in combination, since the sūrah's opponents had both concealed their own conduct and worked to keep the message from circulating.

**The Counterfactual the Book States With Confidence**

The verse then makes a claim about a world that did not happen. Even if they were sent back, they would certainly revert to what they were forbidden. This is not a prediction about individuals whose future is unknown, since the Book elsewhere records that a return to the world would have produced the same conduct (32:12), and it reports the pattern of a generation that swore the most solemn oaths to believe if a warner came, and then was driven further away when he did (35:42).

That swearing is the key to the sentence. The Makkans had already been given a version of the second chance: they asked for a sign, swore they would believe on its arrival, and when signs came they renamed them. A return with better information would not produce better character, which is why the verse refuses the request on grounds of character rather than of evidence.

**Why the Promise Was a Lie**

The judgement at the end of the verse is severe: indeed they are liars. The promise is a lie, and the Book is not accusing them of insincerity at the moment of speaking. They meant the words as they said them. But a promise made under the pressure of pain and abandoned the moment the pressure lifts is a lie in the only sense that counts, because it was never grounded in the person's own will.

That distinction is the difference between regret and repentance. Regret is a response to consequences, and repentance is a response to the act. The scene records the first in its purest form, and the verse reports that the men who produce it are telling the truth about their feelings and a lie about their intentions.

**The Second Chance That Would Not Work**

Some commentators take the verse as evidence that the destiny of such people was already fixed, and read the return as pointless by decree. Others read the same sentence as a statement about character and habit: a person who returns to the same conditions, with the same appetites and the same social pressures, will repeat the same choices, and the Book's confidence reflects what it knows about human beings rather than about an abstract schedule.

The second reading keeps the verse connected to the world the audience knew. The leaders of Makkah had been offered the message for years, under no compulsion, and had chosen repeatedly. A return would restore the same freedom with the same appetites, and the Book states what use they would make of it.

**What the Book Does With Its Hypotheticals**

The counterfactual here is unusual in the Book, and its rarity is instructive. Ordinarily scripture sends its listeners to the past — ruining towns, destroyed generations, the fate of those who mocked the messengers — and lets the record do the arguing. This verse does the opposite. It invents a future that will never happen, puts the disbelievers in it, and reports what they would have done. The Book is so certain of the outcome that it can conduct the experiment on paper and publish the result.

That certainty is itself a claim about human nature. Appetite, habit, and social position do not change when a person is given a second trip through the same conditions, and the Book states elsewhere that the hearts are alike across generations, so that the demands of one age are the demands of another (2:118). Knowing this, God still grants the span of a life, and the granting is mercy rather than oversight. The men in the scene mistake the delay for an invitation to negotiate, and the verse states in advance why the negotiation would have gone nowhere.

The passage has been describing men who, on the day their options run out, promise to be different. The reader who holds the text is in the position they no longer occupy: still able to change, still able to be different, with the plain words of the message in front of him. That is the purpose of these verses in the sūrah, which is not describing the afterlife for its own sake but using it to make the present urgent.

The sentence that follows takes the reader to the same detained crowd and records a different question, asked by a different speaker, waiting for a different answer. The verse in front of us explains why the answer cannot be the one the crowd was hoping for.
