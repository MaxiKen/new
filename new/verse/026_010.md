## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:10

> ˹Remember˺ when your Lord called out to Moses, “Go to the wrongdoing people—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Story Begins With a Command**

The verse opens Moses' account not with his birth or his first miracle, but with a summons. God calls, and the call gives the prophet a direction: go. Prophethood is therefore not a self-appointed project built from private insight. Moses is sent by a Lord who knows the people he must face and the task he must carry. The imperative makes the mission concrete. Revelation will not remain an inward experience on a mountain; it will become an encounter with a society organized around wrongdoing.

The command also creates a contrast with the opening verses of the sūrah. The Prophet's grief over disbelief could make the mission feel like a personal struggle with an unresponsive crowd. Moses' story shows that such resistance belongs to the history of revelation. The messenger is called into the very place where rejection is expected. His courage will not come from favorable conditions, but from the One who sends him.

**Wrongdoing Names a System, Not Only a Person**

The people Moses is told to approach are described as ẓālimūn, those who act with ظلم, wrongdoing that places things out of their proper place. The label is plural before Pharaoh is named. That wording prevents the story from reducing injustice to one villain's private temper. A ruler can command, but officials, customs, beneficiaries, and silent witnesses can help an unjust order endure. The mission addresses the whole field in which oppression operates.

The phrase does not mean that every member of the society has identical guilt. It identifies the public condition that requires warning. Some may be coerced, some compromised, and some actively violent. A prophet goes to a people as a community because communal power can normalize what an individual conscience would recognize as wrong. The command calls that condition by its moral name before it enters into debate about Pharaoh's claims.

**Moses Is Sent Toward the Center of Power**

Later accounts describe Moses' approach to Pharaoh in greater detail, including the encounter at the fire and the signs given there. Here the narrative is compressed so that the destination stands out. He must go to the people whose authority reaches across Egypt. This is not a mission chosen for ease. The prophet is sent toward the institution most capable of punishing dissent and most invested in preserving its own image.

The direction of travel has a theological meaning. Truth does not begin by asking whether power will approve it. It goes to power because injustice becomes more dangerous when it is protected by rank. The message will also reach ordinary people, but the first command identifies the ruling environment whose conduct affects an entire population. Moses is not being sent to win Pharaoh's favor. He is being sent to place Pharaoh's order under God's judgment.

**A Calling Gives Fear a Place Without Giving It Rule**

The next verses will record Moses' concerns, but this opening command already explains why those concerns arise. Fear is not evidence that the call was unreal. A difficult assignment can reveal the seriousness of obedience. Moses knows the people are wrongdoing people; the command does not hide the danger behind polite language. It asks him to proceed with clear knowledge of what he is approaching.

This is different from reckless courage. The prophet does not invent a risk to display bravery. He receives an order and will ask for the help needed to fulfill it. The command sets the direction; divine support will answer the weakness of the servant. The story therefore makes room for both courage and truthful self-assessment. A messenger can be afraid of rejection or harm without being free to abandon the task.

**The Address Recalls Earlier Missions**

Moses' summons is expanded in 20:9–48 and 28:29–35, while 79:15–19 also recalls the call in a shorter form. The differences among these accounts are not contradictions. Each passage selects the detail needed for its immediate argument. Here al-Shuʿarāʾ is preparing a series of prophetic confrontations, so the opening emphasizes the wrongdoing of the audience and the divine origin of the mission.

That selective retelling is itself instructive. A sacred history does not need to repeat every detail each time it tells an event. It brings one event into a new setting so that a particular truth becomes visible. The same Moses can teach about fear, signs, liberation, or the failure of tyranny depending on the passage. In this verse the first truth is simple and severe: God's call sends His servant to confront injustice where it has become public power.

**The Mission Starts With Obedience Before Outcome**

No result is promised in the command. Moses is not told that the people will accept, that Pharaoh will yield, or that the journey will be safe. He is told to go. This order separates obedience from visible success. A prophet acts because God has commanded him, while the outcome remains under divine control.

That principle gives the opening its lasting force. The first question in a moral mission is not whether the powerful will applaud it. It is whether the servant has heard the right command and is prepared to carry it with wisdom. Moses' story will show the cost of doing so, but it begins with the source of his authority. The Lord calls; the messenger goes; the wrongdoing people are placed before the truth.

**The First Command Makes the Mission Public**

Moses is not sent to cultivate a private circle that can remain untouched by the surrounding order. He is sent to the people whose conduct has public consequences. A divine call may begin in a servant's private encounter with God, but it reaches its purpose when it addresses the habits that injure others. The command therefore joins worship to justice from its first line.

This public direction also protects the message from being reduced to self-help. Moses is not being asked merely to manage his fear or perfect his inner state. He must carry truth into a society that has made wrongdoing normal. Personal purification and social responsibility will meet in the mission, because a Lord who is worthy of worship is also the Lord of those who have been wronged.
