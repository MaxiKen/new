## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:47

> Ask, “Imagine if Allah’s punishment were to overwhelm you with or without warning—who would be destroyed other than the wrongdoers?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Ways for a Punishment to Arrive**

The verse names the two possibilities an audience would have feared: a punishment that comes without warning, and one that comes openly. Commentators explain the pair as covering the night and the day, or as covering a coming that is felt before it strikes and a coming that arrives unannounced. The Book uses both patterns in its accounts of destroyed peoples, and it records towns in the Qur'an as having been destroyed at night while their inhabitants slept, or in the morning while they were playing (7:4; 7:98).

The choice presented in the question is therefore not between two punishments but between two timetables. Neither option leaves a gap in which a person could claim he did not see it coming, since even the open arrival gives only enough time to recognise what is happening rather than to change anything.

**The Question That Answers Itself**

Who would be destroyed other than the wrongdoers? The interrogative form is the same device the sūrah has used for the greatest wrong (6:21) and for the greatest of religious inventions. Here it is used to deliver a conclusion the audience had no interest in resisting, and the conclusion is that the punishment is aimed rather than indiscriminate.

That is the sense in which the sentence is a mercy as much as a warning. A person who has understood that God's destructive acts are directed at wrongdoing, and not at whoever happens to be standing nearby, has been told that he can remove himself from the target by removing the conduct that draws it.

**The Difficulty the Verse Leaves Open**

The Book also records worldly destruction falling on whole societies, righteous and wicked alike, as in the case of Noah's people and the towns of Lot, and it warns the believers about a trial that will not befall only those among you who do wrong (8:25). Commentators acknowledged the difficulty and answered it in different ways, with some restricting the verse to the destruction of the guilty in the Hereafter, and others holding that the righteous who are destroyed in this world lose nothing, because their reward with God is secure.

Al-Rāzī's treatment of the question is the one that fits the following verses best. He argued that even when a worldly punishment wipes out a people together, the righteous receive their reward with God in the Hereafter, while the wrongdoers have lost both this world and the next. The verse's verdict about who is destroyed concerns what each group ends with rather than who was standing in the street on a particular morning.

**Why the Timetable Was Left With God**

The sentence is addressed to a people who were pressing the Prophet to hasten something, and the answer removes the question from his hands entirely. The Book records their demand in another sūrah where they called for stones to rain down from the sky if the message was true (8:32), and it reports people asking why the torment is not sent down (29:53). The verse treats the demand as a symptom of unbelief rather than as a request for information.

The Book states the underlying reason plainly: the postponement is not a sign that nothing is coming but a period in which the wrongdoer is given time, and the Book elsewhere describes the delay as a rope rather than as an acquittal (3:178). The verse in front of us supplies the warning side of that arrangement, and the next one describes the messenger's only role.

**What the Words "With or Without Warning" Imply About God**

A deity who warns at all is a deity who does not want the destruction to be a surprise, and the Book treats the warnings sent through messengers as exactly that. The same pattern runs through the sūrah's earlier passages, where messengers were sent and the people were tested with hardship so that they might be humbled (6:42), and where the destruction followed only after the reminders had been forgotten (6:44).

So the question in this verse carries an implicit account of God's conduct. If the punishment can come at night or in broad day, the difference between the two for a guilty community lies only in how much time it is given to reconsider, and the Book holds that the warning had already been delivered in either case.

**The Two Groups Named in the Future**

The verse ends by naming the wrongdoers as the ones who will be destroyed, and the term is not accidental. The sūrah has been describing conduct rather than a label: obstructing others from the message (6:26), disputing after listening (6:25), demanding signs while rejecting the signs already given (6:37), and hardening after suffering (6:43). The wrongdoers in this sentence are those whose record contains those items, and the verse leaves each hearer to check the list against himself.

The next sentence describes the mission that had been given while that check is still possible. Messengers come as bearers of news and warnings, and the Book's habit of stating the messenger's role immediately after a threat is its way of keeping the door visible.

The demand behind this question is recorded elsewhere in a form that shows how the Makkans actually phrased it. They prayed that if the message was truly from God, He should rain down stones on them from the sky or bring a painful punishment upon them (8:32), and the Book also notes that the wrongdoers have a share of torment like that of their predecessors, with an instruction not to ask for it to be hastened (51:59). The verse in front of us answers the tone of such demands rather than their content. A person asking for the punishment to come early is not requesting justice; he is testing whether anyone is listening.
