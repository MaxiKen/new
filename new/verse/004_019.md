## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:19

> O believers! It is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will or mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry ˹as a ransom for divorce˺—unless they are found guilty of adultery. Treat them fairly. If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Inheriting Women Was Not Inheritance**

The verse opens with a prohibition that corrects a pre-Islamic practice by naming it for what it was: *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā yaḥillu lakum an tarithū al-nisāʾa karhan* — O believers, it is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will. In the custom around the revelation, when a man died, his male kin might claim authority over his widows as part of the estate, either marrying them without their choice, marrying them off to others for a bride-price they would keep, or blocking their remarriage until they ransomed themselves with their dowry or inheritance. The Qur’an calls that *irth* — inheritance — to expose its logic: the woman was treated as property to be passed on. By prohibiting it with *lā yaḥillu* — it is not lawful — the verse removes that logic entirely. The woman is not an asset to be divided after debts and bequests; she is an heir in her own right (4:7, 4:12) and a party to a covenant described as *“a firm commitment”* (4:21). The prohibition therefore stands at the head of the sūrah’s marriage reforms for a reason. After settling that wives have a fixed share and that dowries belong graciously to the woman herself (4:4), the sūrah must ensure that widowhood does not become a loophole where those rights are seized back through control of the person.

**Mistreatment to Reclaim the Dowry**

The second prohibition *wa lā taʿḍulūhunna li tadhhabū bi baʿḍi mā ātaytumūhunna illā an yaʾtīna bi fāḥishatin mubayyinah* — nor mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry as a ransom for divorce, unless they are found guilty of adultery — addresses the companion abuse. A husband who dislikes his wife might make her life straitened — *ʿaḍl* and *ḍirrār* — confining, harassing, or withholding rights, so that she will seek release and offer back her bridal gift to buy her freedom. The Qur’an names that strategy and forbids it, except in the case where the wife has committed a clear indecency — *fāḥishatin mubayyinah* — where scholars differed on whether open adultery lifts the restriction or whether it permits the husband to seek separation without penalty. Apart from that exception, the dowry, once given as a *niḥlah* — a free gift (4:4) — and even where it was a heap of gold — *qinṭār* — as the next verse will say (4:20), is not to be taken back by pressure. The sūrah has already said *“if they waive some of it willingly, with a contented soul, then enjoy it freely”* (4:4), making willingness the only lawful path to any return; this verse shows what unwilling extraction looks like when it is pursued as a tactic, and it blocks it.

**Treat Them Fairly**

The positive command *wa ʿāshirūhunna bi al-maʿrūf* — treat them fairly, live with them in kindness — uses the same root *ʿ-r-f* that elsewhere describes decent speech and customary good — *qawlan maʿrūfā* (4:5, 4:8) and *qawlan sadīdā* (4:9). *Maʿrūf* here is not only avoidance of harm; it is active companionship that is recognized as good — provision without stinginess, conversation without contempt, and intimacy without coercion. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“do not kill yourselves”* (4:29) and describes God as ever Merciful (4:29); that mercy is to appear in the home as well. The command to live with women in kindness was read to include what the next verses will detail: giving the dowry, not reclaiming it unjustly, not marrying the former wives of fathers (4:22), and respecting the full list of prohibited degrees (4:23). To live with a wife *bi al-maʿrūf* is therefore to keep the whole marriage ethic the sūrah is building: consent in entry, generosity in maintenance, equity if there is more than one wife (4:3, 4:129), and fairness in separation where it comes. The short phrase carries a long practice, because what is recognized as good is what a faithful community would recognize, not what a private preference would justify.

**If You Happen to Dislike Them**

The verse adds a counsel that reaches the heart at its most private: *fa in karihtumūhunna fa ʿasā an takrahū shayʾan wa yajʿalu Allāhu fīhi khayran kathīrā* — if you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing. Dislike — *kurh* — is not denied; the verse assumes it will occur. A wife’s character, habits, or circumstances may not match what a husband wishes, especially after time, grief, or dispute. Rather than treating dislike as proof that separation must be pressured, the verse asks for patience and for trust in an unseen turn. The same theme recurs elsewhere: *“perhaps you dislike a thing and it is good for you”* (2:216). Here the good is described as *khayran kathīran* — abundant good — which commentators linked to affection that returns, to righteous children born of the marriage, to the training of patience itself, or to the barakah that comes from keeping a covenant that was called firm. The sūrah’s earlier refrain that *“humankind was created weak”* (4:28) and that God wishes to lighten burdens (4:28) is already anticipated here: dislike is a weakness, and the law lightens it not by forcing immediate release through harm but by asking for fair companionship and by promising that what is kept for God’s sake may yield more than what is seized in haste.

**A Protection for Widows and Wives Alike**

Read together, the two prohibitions cover both ends of a marriage: its ending through death, where kin might claim the widow, and its middle where a husband might force a wife to buy her freedom. Both are blocked by the same principle that property belongs to its owner and that persons are not property. The verse generalises that principle with *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* — O believers — addressing the whole community, not only husbands or heirs, because the abuse depended on social acquiescence. A man could only inherit a widow if others accepted that she was inheritable; a husband could only harass his wife into ransoming her dowry if family pressure made divorce shameful. By speaking to believers as such, the verse recruits the community to enforce the change. The next two verses will complete the dowry protection — not to take back even a heap of gold (4:20) and the reminder of *“how could you take it after you have enjoyed each other and she has taken a firm commitment from you?”* (4:21) — so that the entire law of dowry from gift to retention to release is made coherent. This verse is the hinge in that law: it forbids treating the woman as an estate and forbids treating the estate as a tool to coerce the woman.

**From Creation to Kind Companionship**

This verse returns the sūrah to its first note: *“He created you from a single soul, and from it its mate, and spread from both countless men and women”* (4:1), and *“Allah is ever Watchful over you”* (4:1). If humanity shares one origin and the Watcher is present in every transaction, then the widow is not an extra to be claimed and the wife is not a debtor to be squeezed. Both are the mate created from the single soul, the partner in spreading, the one whose womb-tie shares a root with mercy. To live with them *bi al-maʿrūf* is therefore not an additional virtue beyond law; it is the law’s completion. The sūrah has moved from dividing estates to dividing households, and it keeps the same standard: fixed rights honored, voluntary gifts left voluntary, and dislike handled with patience rather than pressure. A husband who finds himself disliking his wife is told to remain fair in company and to trust that God may place abundant good in what he currently dislikes. That trust is the counterpart to *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6) for orphans and to *“Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing”* (4:12) for heirs. Here, the name behind the trust is the One who can turn dislike into blessing when the marriage is kept with kindness.
