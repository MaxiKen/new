## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:26

> It is Allah’s Will to make things clear to you, guide you to the ˹noble˺ ways of those before you, and turn to you in mercy. For Allah is AllKnowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Will to Make Things Clear**

The verse opens with *yurīdu Allāhu li yubayyina lakum* — it is Allah’s Will to make things clear to you. After twenty-five verses of intricate law — orphan wealth, marriage limits, dowry, prohibited degrees, and the lawful field beyond them — the sūrah pauses to name the intention behind the detail. Clarity is not incidental to the legislation; it is the aim. The verb *yubayyin* conveys explaining until a matter becomes distinct, until what is lawful and what is barred can be seen without blur. The sūrah has just distinguished the father’s former wife from other prohibitions (4:22), blood from nursing from marriage ties (4:23), the married woman as forbidden from the lawful field beyond (4:24), and the free woman from the believing bondwoman for those of limited means (4:25). Each distinction removed an ambiguity that custom had left cloudy. To say that Allah wills to make this clear is to say that the burden of confusion is not placed on the believer; the map is being drawn plainly. The same concern for clarity appears elsewhere where the Qur’an says it was sent to explain — *li tubayyina* — so that people may reflect, and here that explanation takes the form of precise family law.

**To Guide You to the Ways of Those Before You**

The second purpose *wa yahdiyakum sunana alladhīna min qablikum* — and guide you to the noble ways of those before you — places the new community inside a longer history of guidance. *Sunan* — ways, paths, established practices — were explained as the upright practices of prophets and righteous communities who lived by limits around property and family, who honoured covenants and guarded chastity. The Qur’an elsewhere says that previous peoples were given similar boundaries for inheritance and marriage, and that deviation from those ways led to social disorder. By guiding to those ways, the sūrah tells the Medinan believers that they are not inventing a new ethic from nothing; they are being returned to a pattern that earlier communities knew when they were faithful. That framing matters after a list of prohibitions that might feel restrictive. The limits are not a novel hardship for this generation alone; they are the restored noble way that earlier peoples walked when they were closest to revelation. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse therefore links law to precedent: what is commanded here aligns with what was commanded to those before, so that obedience feels like joining a lineage rather than breaking from one.

**And to Turn to You in Mercy**

The third purpose *wa yatūba ʿalaykum* — and turn to you in mercy — shows that clarity and guidance are directed toward pardon. *Yatūba* here is God’s turning, His acceptance, as when the sūrah earlier promised *“Allah will pardon them”* for those who repent soon after acting ignorantly (4:17). The sequence matters: first He makes clear, then He guides to the path, then He turns to those who take it. Clarity without mercy would be exposure; guidance without acceptance would be demand without help. The sūrah therefore presents law as a means to mercy, not as its opposite. The same verse that promises to make things clear also promises to accept the return of those who had previously blurred the lines — whether by consuming orphan wealth hastily (4:6), reclaiming dowries (4:20–21), or marrying where it was hateful (4:22). The sūrah will repeat that desire to turn in grace in the next verse — *“Allah wishes to turn to you in grace”* (4:27) — and will add that He wishes to lighten burdens because humankind was created weak (4:28). Here the turn is already offered as the outcome of understanding: once you see the distinction, walk its way, and you will find Him turning toward you.

**All-Knowing, All-Wise Over Detail and Time**

The closing *wa Allāhu ʿAlīmun Ḥakīm* — for Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise — matches the two purposes that came before. *ʿAlīm* knows every family configuration, every temptation to treat a widow as inheritable property (4:19), every pressure to reclaim a heap of gold (4:20), and every case where means fail and a believing bondwoman becomes the lawful choice (4:25). That knowledge is what makes the distinctions precise; the law is not guesswork about what might harm lineage but knowledge of what will. *Ḥakīm* places that knowledge inside wisdom: the distinctions are not only accurate but beneficial, producing households where trust can be kept and where wealth can be divided without rancour. The same pair closed the inheritance shares (4:11) and the dowry flexibility (4:24), showing that both arithmetic and mercy belong to the same wisdom. The sūrah’s detailed legislation could have been heard as burdensome without that closing; with it, the detail itself becomes assurance. The One who knows each tie’s sensitivity is the One who draws its limit, and He draws it so that the path of those before you becomes walkable for you, and His turning toward you becomes near.

**A Verse That Holds the First Third Together**

Read after twenty-five verses, this verse gathers the opening third of al-Nisāʾ into a sentence about divine intent. It tells the reader that the laws about a single soul and womb-ties (4:1), about testing orphans and witnessing handovers (4:6), about obligatory shares after debts and bequests (4:11–12), about disgust for marrying a father’s widow (4:22), and about lawful seeking with wealth in chastity (4:24–25) are not a collection of disconnected rulings but a single clarifying, guiding, and pardoning will. The sūrah has moved from creation to trust to property to family formation, and this verse names the thread: Allah wills to make all of it clear, to place you on the noble way others walked, and to turn to you when you do. That naming prepares the next two verses, which contrast that will with the wish of those who follow desires to see you deviate entirely (4:27) and which add that weakness is the reason burdens are lightened (4:28). Clarity, precedent, and mercy together form the ground on which the lighter burden can be borne. Without the will to make things clear, the law would remain opaque; without guidance to the ways before, it would feel rootless; without the turn in mercy, it would feel unattainable. With all three, the community is given both the map and the help to walk it.
