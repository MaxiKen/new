## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:76

> you and your ancestors?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Reaches Beyond One Generation**

The continuation of Abraham's question includes both the present worshippers and their ancestors. He does not allow the people to place all responsibility in the past. Their forefathers may have begun or preserved the practice, but the living generation has adopted it again and is now answerable for what it worships. The question therefore crosses time without confusing the responsibilities of different people.

This is why inherited religion cannot be defended merely by pointing backward. The ancestors are part of the object of examination because their example has shaped the present. Yet the present people must look for themselves. They cannot borrow certainty from the dead, just as they cannot borrow accountability. Each generation receives the capacity to recognize truth and must decide whether it will use it.

**Ancestry Does Not Give an Idol New Powers**

The people have already admitted that the idols cannot benefit or harm. Abraham now asks them to consider whether that admission applies equally to the idols honored by their ancestors. Time has not changed the nature of the objects. An image does not become capable of hearing because many generations have stood before it, and a powerless object does not gain authority from the age of its shrine.

This is the quiet force of the question. The community may feel that the idols are validated by a long chain of devotion, but the chain only proves that a practice was transmitted. It does not prove that the object is worthy. A custom can be ancient and still be false; it can also be recent and true. Age describes a practice's history, not its truth.

**The Living Cannot Hide Behind the Dead**

When people say that their forefathers did the same, they often turn an argument about truth into an argument about loyalty. Abraham refuses that substitution. He honors neither the living nor the dead by allowing a false object to receive worship simply because it was familiar to the family. Real respect must remain subordinate to what is right.

The question places a moral burden on the present hearers. They have listened to Abraham's challenge, recognized the idols' lack of power, and still have a choice. Continuing after understanding is not the same as receiving a custom without reflection. Knowledge makes the decision more conscious. The community must ask whether its loyalty to ancestry is becoming disloyalty to the Lord.

**A Family Tradition Can Become a Spiritual Inheritance**

Traditions carry memories, obligations, and a sense of belonging. A person may fear that abandoning an inherited idol means abandoning parents, language, festivals, or the story of the community. Abraham's question does not deny the emotional weight of these ties. It asks whether belonging should be organized around a powerless object.

A true spiritual inheritance is not the repetition of every practice found in the family. It is the transmission of submission to God, moral seriousness, and a willingness to correct error. If the ancestors themselves were dependent on the Lord, then the descendants do not honor them by treating their mistakes as sacred. They honor truth by refusing to turn family memory into a rival lord.

**The Question Separates Explanation From Justification**

The ancestors can explain why the idols are present. They cannot justify why the idols deserve worship. This distinction is essential whenever custom is used as proof. History tells us how a belief arrived; it does not tell us whether the belief is sound. Abraham's question draws attention to that missing step.

A community can preserve an error for centuries through repetition, authority, and fear of change. The length of the process may make the error difficult to challenge, but it never turns repetition into revelation. Abraham asks the people to move from “this is what they did” to “this is why this object deserves worship.” If they cannot make that movement, their defense remains only imitation.

**The Question Includes Abraham's Own Ancestors**

Abraham is not asking the people to despise family as such. He is showing that every human lineage stands under God's truth. His own prophetic call involves breaking with the worship around him rather than accepting it because it is familiar. That gives the question a personal seriousness. He is not criticizing a problem that belongs only to other families.

A messenger's relation to his ancestors does not exempt him from seeking the truth. Nor does a community's pride in its lineage exempt it from examination. All people inherit limitations as well as gifts. The question about ancestors therefore humbles both sides: the idolaters cannot claim ancestral perfection, and the reformer must rely on truth rather than on his own family name.

**Blind Following Is Not the Same as Faithfulness**

Faithfulness means remaining loyal to what is true, even when the truth requires correction. Blind following means repeating a practice because it was received, without asking whether its object and direction are right. Abraham's question exposes the difference. His people call their imitation loyalty, but the verse invites them to see that loyalty without knowledge can preserve falsehood.

This warning applies wherever people use forefathers, teachers, institutions, or majorities to end a question. Reliable guidance deserves respect, but no created authority can replace the obligation to recognize God. The authority of a predecessor is a means to truth only when it points beyond itself. Once it blocks honest examination, it has become an obstacle rather than a guide.

**The Question Prepares the Declaration of Exclusive Allegiance**

By asking about the people and their ancestors together, Abraham clears the ground for a different account of worship. The issue is no longer which generation began the custom. It is whether any created thing deserves the heart's ultimate dependence. The answer will require a contrast between the false objects and the Lord of the worlds.

This verse therefore holds the community at the point of decision. Its past has been named, but the past cannot answer for the present. The ancestors' practice may explain the idols' authority in society, yet it cannot establish divine authority. Abraham's question returns responsibility to the worshippers standing before him: consider what you have inherited, and do not call it true merely because you inherited it.
