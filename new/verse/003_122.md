## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:122

> ˹Remember˺ when two groups among you ˹believers˺ were about to cower, then Allah reassured them. So in Allah let the believers put their trust.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Groups Named in the Tradition**

The verse recalls a moment when two groups among the believers nearly lost their resolve, and it adds that God was their protector and that the believers should place their trust in Him. The community remembered which groups were meant.

A narration from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh identifies them as the two Madinan tribes of Banū Ḥārithah and Banū Salimah, and adds a detail that has the flavour of the first generation's way of reading the Book. Jābir said that he would not have wished the verse had not been sent down, precisely because of the clause in which God is named as the protector of the two groups. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (4051) and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (2505).

**What It Means to Be About to Cower**

The verb describes an inclination rather than an act. The two groups considered faltering, and the Book records the thought without describing it as a deed, which keeps the verse's accusation at the level of temptation.

The Book is consistently careful about this distinction. It reports the inner intentions of people whose outward conduct did not follow, and it treats the withdrawal from a resolve as a different matter from the abandonment of a duty. What the two groups carried was a heaviness that could have turned into action, and the verse's first clause names the condition without condemning the men who felt it.

**God as Their Protector**

The verse's answer to the hesitation is a statement about protection, using a word that describes a guardian or an ally. The two groups did not overcome their hesitation by their own strength, and the sentence attributes their steadiness to God.

The word also appears in the sūrah's account of the covenant, where God is described as the ally of the believers, and it carries the sense of a relationship in which a stronger party takes responsibility for a weaker one. What the verse tells its readers is that the men who nearly gave way were held by someone else. The protection is stated as a fact about the past, and it explains why the hesitation never became a departure.

**Some Read the Clause as Present Tense**

Grammatically, the clause about God's protection can be read as a statement about the past, describing what happened on the day, or as a statement of what is always true of such people. Al-Rāzī noted the second reading and drew from it a sign of mercy: God is described as the protector of the two groups even as the verse reports the weakness they were struggling with.

The difference between the readings is a matter of emphasis rather than of meaning. On either, the community is told that the danger came from within and that the remedy came from outside, and the sentence that follows turns that fact into an instruction.

**The Command to Place Trust in God**

The verse ends by commanding the believers to put their trust in God, and the instruction is attached to the recollection rather than offered as a general principle. Trust is given as the practice that answers the kind of fear the verse has described.

The Book's word for trust describes reliance on another after taking whatever steps are available, and the sūrah's passage contains both elements. The Prophet had positioned his forces and given his orders, and the community had done what it could; what remained was the part beyond its control, which is the point at which the instruction applies. The verse therefore teaches trust as the companion of preparation rather than as a substitute for it.

**The Book's Honesty About Its Community**

The verse records a moment of weakness among the believers, and the Book records such moments repeatedly. It describes the community's fear at Badr, its hesitation before the battle of the Trench, and its afflictions in between, and it never presents the first generation as a body of people who were never afraid.

The honesty serves the sūrah's purpose. A later reader who finds himself afraid is told that the people whose example he is meant to follow also found themselves afraid, and that what distinguished them was the outcome rather than the absence of the feeling. That is why Jābir's remark about the verse is such a telling piece of the tradition: the community valued the record of its own wavering because the verse attached God's protection to it.

**Trust as the Passage's Instruction**

The verse's final clause is the second time in this passage that the believers are told to rely on God, and the repetition is deliberate. The sūrah is preparing its readers for an account of a battle that went wrong.

The instruction is given before the account rather than after it, so that the reader encounters the failures of the day with the verse about trust already in hand. The community is being taught a habit in advance of the test, and the next verse supplies the reminder of a victory that the same community had already been given.

**The Word the Book Uses for Losing Heart**

The Arabic term in this verse describes a slackening of resolve, the moment when a person's determination weakens before any action has been taken. It is the vocabulary of an inner movement rather than of a visible failure.

That choice of word sets the standard the passage applies to the community. The Book does not accuse these men of desertion; it reports that they were close to it and records that they held. The distinction matters for how later readers measure themselves, since it places a person's private struggle inside the same story as his conduct. What the verse honours is not the absence of fear but the refusal to be governed by it, and the protection attributed to God is the reason the refusal was possible.
