## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:177

> Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets; who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make; and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle. It is they who are true ˹in faith˺, and it is they who are mindful ˹of Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Not the Direction but the Whole Life**

The verse opens by declining a definition. Righteousness — *birr* — is not in turning faces east or west. The occasion the reports preserve explains the target: after the migration to Medina the Muslims observed the Jews praying westward toward Jerusalem and the Christians eastward, in the period when the qiblah itself had been the community's defining dispute (2:145). The verse does not abolish the direction — the sūrah had just spent a dozen verses fixing it by revelation — but it refuses to let the direction be the sum. *Birr* is defined by the exegetes as obedience to God well established in the heart, or the total of the acts of obedience and devotion that bring a person near Him; the same word names the duty owed to parents in the phrase for filial devotion. The structure of the verse is therefore a correction of proportion: orientation is a part of righteousness, and a commanded part; the whole is the architecture this verse builds — creed, charity, worship, covenant-keeping, and endurance — of which the facing is one brick.

**The Five Articles Before the Deeds**

The definition begins in belief, and the list it gives became canonical: belief in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets. These five are the principal articles on which the Islamic creedal statements are built, and they reappear as a unit in the sūrah of the Women's command to believe in Allah, His Messenger, the Book revealed to him, and the Scriptures revealed before (4:136). Their fullest canonical exposition is the famous ḥadīth of Gabriel, preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb: the archangel, appearing as a traveling man, asks the Prophet to define *īmān*, and the answer enumerates belief in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and the divine decree in its good and its evil — the same list, with the decree added. The ordering in this verse is instructive: God first, then the Day that gives moral life its horizon, then the unseen agents, then the books, then the messengers — creed before conduct, because the conduct that follows is unintelligible without it. The righteous are not defined as those who *do* the listed deeds but as those who *believe* the listed truths and do the deeds out of them.

**Wealth Given While It Is Still Loved**

The first deed named is charity, and its qualifier is the verse's psychological core: giving wealth *despite loving it* — or, on the reading that takes the pronoun as God's, *because of love for Him*. Al-Ṭabarī's explanation covers the first sense: parting with property even when one fears poverty or yearns for what wealth provides. The phrase has a twin in the sūrah of Man, where the righteous feed the poor, the orphan, and the captive — *and give food despite their desire for it* (76:8). The gift counted here is the one that costs, made while the hand still prefers to keep. The recipients are then listed in the order of social nearness and need: kinsfolk first — charity begins where obligation bleeds into love; orphans, whose guardianship the sūrah has legislated repeatedly; the indigent; the wayfarer stranded among strangers; the beggars; and the freeing of captives — wealth spent not on a person but against an institution, buying human beings out of bondage. Many jurists noted that this giving must be *beyond* the zakāh, since the alms-tax is listed separately later in the same verse: first the costly charity of love, then the fixed levy of law. The recipient list recurs at 2:215 and 4:36 and across the Qur'an's legislation of war-booty — one standing roster of the people a righteous economy is measured by.

**Prayer, Alms, and the Kept Pledge**

Three more acts complete the worship section: establishing the prayer — not performing but *establishing*, the word that denotes the settled, regular, communal maintenance of the rite; giving the zakāh, the fixed purifying levy that makes charity law rather than mood; and keeping pledges when they are made. The third is the verse's social keystone. The oath and the promise are the instruments by which human beings extend their wills into the future and bind themselves to others — contracts, treaties, vows to God, words given in marriage and market — and the righteous are defined as those whose spoken word survives the change of their interests. The Qur'an's insistence on this virtue runs through its portraits of the prophets — Ismāʿīl, who kept his word to his people — and its laws require the same fidelity of ordinary believers in 5:1 and throughout. Placed between the ritual duties and the patience that follows, covenant-keeping bridges the verse's two halves: it is worship performed in the medium of other people's trust, and it is the first form the coming patience will have to take, since the pledge is hardest to keep precisely when keeping it hurts.

**Patience in the Three Extremes**

The final deed is the sūrah's standing virtue, now specified by its three testing grounds: patience in misfortune (*baʾsāʾ*), in hardship (*ḍarrāʾ*), and in moments of peril. Al-Ṭabarī's glosses separate them: misfortune as challenges like poverty — the loss of means; hardship as matters like sickness — the loss of health; and the moments of peril as battle, the meeting of the enemy, the hour when the body's fear argues against the will's resolve. The three grounds cover the full geography of affliction — the purse, the body, the battlefield — and together they recall the test announced at 2:155 with its fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. The verse thus closes its definition where the passage began: the righteous are the patient of 2:155–157, described now in full dress. And the peril-clause points forward with deliberate timing: the sūrah is about to legislate retaliation (2:178), then war itself (2:190 onward), and the community about to receive those laws is first told that the law's operators must be defined by patience — in the feud's heat, in the wound's pain, in the battle's first hour.

**The True and the Mindful**

The verdict that closes the verse is double: *it is they who are true, and it is they who are mindful of Allah*. The first word — *ṣadaqū*, they were truthful, they proved sincere — answers a question the verse's own opening raised: who is telling the truth about righteousness? Not the one who points at his direction of prayer but the one whose creed, charity, worship, pledges, and patience are on record. Truthfulness here is demonstrated rather than claimed, which is why the sūrah elsewhere reserves the same word for those whose faith survives testing. The second — the reverent, the *muttaqūn* — names the quality the whole sūrah has been circling since its second verse, the Book given as guidance to the mindful. The restrictive construction of both clauses ("it is *they*") excludes the counterfeits the passage has just catalogued: the concealers who traded the Book (2:174–176), the far-schismatics, the direction-merchants of righteousness. Between the traders of revelation and the definition of birr the sūrah has drawn its clearest boundary line — righteousness is a life structured by believed truth, costly giving, established worship, kept words, and endured extremes; and the verse leaves no direction of the compass, east or west, with any excuse to stand in for it.
