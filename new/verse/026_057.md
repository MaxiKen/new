## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:57

> So We lured the tyrants out of ˹their˺ gardens, springs,

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Draws the Tyrants Out From Their Security**

The verse turns from Pharaoh's boast to God's action. The tyrants are brought out from gardens and springs, the settings of luxury that helped sustain their confidence. Their departure is described as something God caused, not as a victory won by the pursuing army. Pharaoh believes he is moving by his own decision, yet the narrative places his movement within a divine plan.

The wording translated lured can also be understood as made to leave or brought out. Commentators considered whether the phrase emphasizes God's direct removal of the Egyptians from their possessions or the desire placed in their hearts to chase the Israelites. Both readings fit the contrast. Pharaoh's army leaves the security of its wealth because it thinks pursuit will restore control, while God is drawing it toward the judgment prepared for its arrogance.

**Luxury Becomes a Trap When It Feeds Pride**

Gardens and springs are signs of provision, but the tyrants treat them as evidence that their power is secure. The beauty and abundance around them create a world in which consequences seem distant. They can imagine that the palace, farms, and water will remain theirs because they have learned to confuse possession with permanence.

God's action exposes that confusion. The same comforts that reassure the tyrants cannot accompany them when they chase the Israelites. Their wealth does not make the pursuit wise, and their gardens do not protect them from the Lord who gave them. A blessing becomes a trap when it produces contempt for the One who provides it and for the people whose labor sustains it.

**The Tyrants Leave Their Own Evidence Behind**

The verse names the possessions from which they are brought out. The list makes their loss concrete. These are not empty people with nothing to lose; they are people surrounded by abundance who still choose oppression. Their wealth cannot be used as proof that their rule is righteous. It may instead increase their responsibility because they possess enough to act justly and still refuse.

The details also prevent a shallow account of judgment. God does not merely defeat an army in an abstract battle. He removes a class of rulers from the spaces that embodied their social power. Gardens and springs represent a way of life built around control of land and resources. The tyrants leave that way of life behind when they make their wealth serve the pursuit of the oppressed.

**The Pursuit Draws Power Away From Its Foundation**

A ruler's strength often depends on remaining within the institutions and resources that support him. Pharaoh's court has cities, advisers, wealth, and military supply. By drawing the tyrants out, God moves them away from the setting in which those advantages appear stable. The pursuit separates them from the comfort that makes their rule look permanent.

This does not mean that God deceives innocent people into danger. The people being drawn out are tyrants whose choices have already placed them in opposition to justice. The movement is judgment on a settled pattern of oppression. Their own confidence supplies the means: they leave safety because they believe force will recover what they have lost.

**Gardens and Springs Cannot Be Carried Into Judgment**

The possessions remain behind because material abundance is tied to a place and a time. A person may own land, water, residences, and treasure in the world, but none of these can be carried as proof of worth before God. The tyrants leave their visible glory behind while pursuing the people they tried to own.

This is not a condemnation of every garden or every form of wealth. Creation is good, and provision can be used gratefully. The judgment falls on those who turn provision into a basis for domination. Wealth should make a person more responsible, not more certain that he can defy the Lord. The tyrants' departure reveals the emptiness of a security that depends on possession.

**The Language of Being Drawn Out Exposes Hidden Direction**

Pharaoh's speeches have made him appear to be the author of the chase. The verse reveals another level of causation. The tyrants choose and God judges; their intention is real, but it operates within a divine plan they do not control. They set out to recover Israel and are drawn toward the consequence of their own wrongdoing.

This balance protects the story from two errors. It does not turn Pharaoh into a puppet with no responsibility, and it does not let Pharaoh imagine that his plan exists outside God's rule. Human pride makes choices; divine sovereignty places those choices within judgment. The gardens and springs are the first visible signs that the tyrants are leaving the world they thought they owned.

**The Wealth of a Tyrant Does Not Make His Pursuit Noble**

A splendid setting can make a ruler look respectable. The verse strips away that visual power by describing the tyrants being brought out from it. Once separated from gardens and springs, their claim to greatness must be judged by what they do to the vulnerable. Their wealth cannot turn a chase into justice.

The passage therefore teaches a way to read political grandeur. Look past the residences and cultivated land to the people whose freedom the ruler denies. If abundance is guarded by oppression, the beauty surrounding the court is also evidence of responsibility. God can remove the tyrant from that beauty and leave the public to see that the tyrant's greatness was never self-sustaining.

The gardens and springs therefore function as witnesses as well as possessions. They show what the tyrants enjoyed, what they believed would remain, and what they were willing to abandon in anger. A life surrounded by provision can still be spiritually poor when it uses provision to keep others captive. God's removal of the tyrants begins by separating them from the signs of abundance they mistook for security.
