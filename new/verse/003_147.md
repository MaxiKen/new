## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:147

> And all they said was, “Our Lord! Forgive our sins and excesses, make our steps firm, and grant us victory over the disbelieving people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Restriction in the Sentence**

The verse reports that the only words of these people were a prayer, and the restriction is the point of the sentence. Faced with a defeat, the devoted men did not debate, did not cast blame, and did not announce what the community should have done differently; they asked God for four things.

The Book notices such restraint, and the verse records it as part of the description of the people it praises. The community that had just come through Uḥud is being shown what the right response to a setback looks like in the form of speech, and the model given is short enough to be memorized and repeated by anyone.

**The First Petition and the Two Kinds of Sin**

The prayer begins with a request for forgiveness of sins, and then asks for forgiveness of prodigality in affairs. Al-Qurṭūbī suggested that the first term covers the smaller faults and the second covers the greater ones, so that the two clauses together leave nothing outside the request.

The distinction, if it is accepted, reflects a pastoral concern that runs through the Book. A person who has made a serious mistake and a person who has accumulated minor ones both need to ask, and both need to know that the wording provided covers their case. The prayer teaches the community how to speak about its own failures without either minimising them or treating them as beyond remedy.

**Prodigality in One's Affairs**

The word rendered as prodigality covers waste and excess and also the act of going beyond limits. The Book uses it for the conduct of people who exceed what is due, whether in the use of property or in the pursuit of their appetites.

The verse's phrase attaches it to the whole range of a person's affairs rather than to one department of life. Such excess is not restricted to the obvious crimes; it includes the abandoning of moderation in ordinary matters, and the Book counts it among the things a community should ask to be forgiven. The prayer therefore acknowledges not only particular offences but a general tendency that needs correcting.

**The Request That Steps Be Made Firm**

The third petition asks God to make the community's steps firm, and the request uses the image of standing and walking on the ground. The Book uses the same image when it promises the believers that if they support God's cause, He will support them and steady their footing.

The request has a particular fitness at this point in the sūrah. The community's failure at Uḥud was a failure of footing, and the men whose prayer is recorded here ask for the remedy in the words that describe the problem. The Book presents such prayers as the place where people take the weakness they have seen in themselves and ask for what would correct it.

**Help Against a Named Opponent**

The prayer's final request is for help against the people who deny, and it names the opposition rather than asking for deliverance in general terms. The community that prays it is in a contest, and it asks for support in the contest.

The Book's prayers frequently name the matter at hand, and this one does so without bitterness or the request for anything beyond success in the struggle. The request is for help and not for revenge, and the difference marks the attitude the sūrah commends to a community under attack.

**Prayer in the Middle of Trial**

The verse places the prayer in the middle of the fighting rather than after it. The men described in the previous verse were in the situation the prayer addresses, and their response was to ask rather than to complain.

The Book presents this as the natural conduct of people whose trust is in God. It does not describe hours of preparation or a particular ritual; it records a short supplication made while events were unfolding. The community that recites the passage learns that the correct use of a person's speech in a crisis is to ask God for what he needs.

**Other Prayers Recorded in the Book**

The Book records prayers of this shape in many places, and the community has used them in its own devotions ever since. One passage reports the address of believers who say that they have believed and ask for forgiveness and protection from the punishment of the fire. Another reports the prayer attributed to Adam and his wife in the sūrah of the Heights, where they acknowledge that they have wronged themselves and that without forgiveness and mercy they will be among the losers. A third, longer prayer asks God not to hold the community to account for forgetfulness and error and to grant victory over those who deny.

These passages together form the Book's instruction on how to pray about one's own faults. The verse before the reader is one of the shortest of them, and its compactness has made it one of the most used.

**What the Verse Teaches About Supplication**

The order of the petitions is instructive. Forgiveness comes first, then steadiness, and only then the outcome of the struggle. A community whose own faults have been addressed can stand; a community that is standing can be helped.

The sequence also describes the priorities the Book assumes its people will have. What is asked for at the start is the repairing of the relation with God, and the request for victory comes last. The order teaches the community that its own condition is the first thing to set right when its affairs have gone wrong, and the verse records the teaching in the form of a prayer rather than a list of instructions.
