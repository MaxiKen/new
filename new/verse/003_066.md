## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:66

> Here you are! You disputed about what you have ˹little˺ knowledge of, but why do you now argue about what you have no knowledge of? Allah knows and you do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rebuke in Two Stages**

The verse takes the form of an observation about the disputants' own record: you argued about things you had some knowledge of, and now you argue about something you have no knowledge of at all. The sentence sets two disagreements beside each other and marks the difference in what the parties could bring to them.

The comparison is severe and it is made gently, in the manner of a person pointing out an inconsistency rather than delivering a verdict. The first dispute concerned matters within the community's competence — the interpretation of its own laws, the content of its own tradition, the points on which its own scholars differed. The second concerns Abraham, who lived long before the books they possessed and about whom their traditions carried no direct information. The verse's charge is that the habit of arguing has outlived the basis for it.

**What the Two Disputes Were**

Commentators offered several accounts of the disagreements in view. The matters the disputants had knowledge of were variously identified as their own scripture and the points at which they differed over it, the disputation over what their laws permitted and forbade, and the question of whether the new revelation agreed with or differed from the earlier books. The matter they had no knowledge of was their competing claims about Abraham, concerning which, as the sūrah has just pointed out, no direct report was available to them.

The distinction is one that any community with a tradition can understand. There is a difference between arguing from evidence within a field one has studied and arguing about a figure from a remote past on the basis of inherited pride. The verse does not dispute the community's competence in the first category. It points out that the second category has been treated as though it were the first.

**"Allah Knows and You Do Not Know"**

The closing clause states the ground of the difference. Knowledge belongs to God in the matter under dispute, and the disputants do not possess it; the verse had already told its readers as much when it described the unseen news being recited to the Prophet.

The formula recurs in the Book where a dispute has reached the point at which the parties are asserting what they cannot establish. Its function is to close the argument by naming the only available authority, and it leaves the hearer with a choice that the sūrah has been building toward: to accept the account revealed by the one who knows, or to continue maintaining a position for which no evidence exists. The verse does not position itself as one opinion among the disputants. It states the fact that makes the dispute decidable.

**Why Abraham Was the Right Subject for This Charge**

The choice of Abraham as the matter of the argument is what makes the rebuke stick. He is the shared ancestor whose story both communities prized, and about whose religion they had no detailed record other than the traditions that had grown around his name.

The Book's own narrative about him is presented as revelation, which is precisely the point: knowledge of Abraham must come from God, because the period is closed and no witness remains. A community that had received a book must have known this about a figure who preceded its book by centuries. The verse's charge is therefore about method as much as about content. What the disputants were doing with Abraham was what the Book regards as the characteristic error of a community that has stopped treating revelation as its source of knowledge and begun treating its own position as the measure.

**The Standing of Argument in This Sūrah**

The sūrah is not opposed to reasoning; it has been reasoning throughout, and this verse is itself an argument. What is being corrected is argument conducted without a basis, and the distinction matters for how the reader understands the Book's repeated appeals to intellect.

The Book asks its hearers to reflect, consider, and understand, and its arguments move from premises its hearers accept to conclusions they can verify. Where the evidence is available, the Book presses for the inference; where it is not, the Book supplies revelation and expects it to be accepted as such. The verse above illustrates the second case. A dispute about a man who lived before the scriptures is a dispute that cannot be resolved by the parties to it, and the Book's answer is that the account has come from the only source that was present.

**A Question of What Can Be Known**

The sentence leaves the reader with a plain intellectual distinction: the disputants had knowledge of one thing and no knowledge of another, and they argued about both in the same tone.

That habit is not confined to the communities the verse addresses, and the sūrah's placement of the observation in the middle of its argument with them makes it available to any reader.

The Book's own answer to the difficulty is on the page in front of the sentence. A dispute about a figure who lived before the surviving records is settled in this sūrah not by weighing the traditions about him but by revelation from the one who knows, and the same principle governs everything the Book reports about the earlier prophets. The next verse gives the positive account of Abraham, and it does so in the form of a series of negations followed by a description, which is how the Book usually corrects a claim it cannot accept.
