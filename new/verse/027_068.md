## Sūrah an-Naml 27:68

> We have already been promised this, as well as our forefathers earlier. This is nothing but ancient fables!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Objection**

Their speech continues, and the argument shifts from the physical to the historical. The first verse said resurrection is incredible; this one says the claim is old. We were promised this, and our fathers before us, and nothing came of it.

The move is a familiar one and it is not stupid. A prediction that has been repeated across generations without arriving loses force with each repetition, and the Meccans are pointing at a track record. Prophets before Muḥammad ﷺ had warned of the same accounting; the warnings are in the stories the chapter has just told. Nobody's grandfather rose. What the objection does not consider is whether a promise with no stated date can be falsified by the passage of time, which is the flaw the chapter will exploit shortly.

**"Ancient Fables"**

The dismissal is *asāṭīr al-awwalīn*, the tales of the ancients. The phrase appears repeatedly in the Qur'an as the standard Meccan verdict on the recitation, and it is worth taking seriously as a classification rather than an insult.

Every culture has a category for stories that are told, valued, and not believed — legends, old wives' tales, the things people say. Filing the Qur'an in that category solves the problem of what to do with a text that is manifestly impressive and inconveniently demanding. It concedes the artistry and removes the obligation. The move is identical in structure to the one Pharaoh's court made in verse 13 of this chapter, when they called the signs *pure magic*: assign the phenomenon to a known and harmless category, and the question of its source disappears. Two peoples, separated by a thousand years, reaching for the same manoeuvre. Neither of them examined the classification before applying it, which is what makes the two cases parallel rather than merely similar. A label adopted because it settles a problem is not a judgment about evidence.

**The Charge Is Self-Undermining**

Calling the material ancient fables sits badly with the objection in the previous verse. There they argued from observation that bodies do not come back. Here they argue that the claim is merely a repeated story. These are different kinds of complaint, and the second weakens the first.

If the teaching is only old storytelling, why is it being repeated by a man they have known for forty years, in a form they cannot match, with content about peoples whose ruins they pass on the trade road? And if the objection is genuinely empirical, the age of the claim is irrelevant — a true statement does not become false by being made often. The Qur'an lets both halves of their speech stand next to each other without comment. Its reply, in the next verse, addresses neither the metaphysics nor the literary classification, and instead sends them to look at something.

**Inherited Objections as Well as Inherited Religion**

Notice what they are actually reporting: their fathers heard this and dismissed it, and they are dismissing it too. The continuity they claim is a continuity of refusal.

That is the same appeal the Qur'an records elsewhere when it has them say they found their fathers on a way and are following their footsteps. What this verse adds is that the inheritance includes the counter-argument. A community passes down not only its practices but its ready answers, and a ready answer that has survived several generations feels like wisdom rather than habit. The chapter has already shown the alternative in Sheba, where a queen inherited sun worship and, when a claim reached her, tested it rather than reaching for what her court already said. The difference between her and this audience is not intelligence or evidence. It is whether the inherited reply gets examined.

**Why the Qur'an Preserves the Insult**

The Book could have summarised this objection. Instead it records the mockery verbatim, including the dismissive tone, and it does so in a text its own community would recite for centuries.

There is a confidence in that decision worth naming. A movement anxious about its standing suppresses the jibes made against it; this one preserves them in scripture, in the mouths of named opponents, with the rhetorical force intact. The same chapter recorded a prophet fleeing from a staff and a king threatening a bird. What the practice produces is a text that cannot be accused of hiding the case against it, and the answer it then gives is correspondingly plain. The Prophet is told to respond with a single instruction, and it is not an argument.

**What "Promised" Concedes**

Their complaint contains an admission. They say they *were promised* this, and their fathers before them — which acknowledges that the warning has a history, was delivered to earlier generations, and has been consistent.

That is a considerable thing to grant while dismissing the content. A teaching invented recently by one man in Mecca would not have reached their forefathers, and they are not claiming it did. They are claiming that a long-standing, repeated promise has not been kept. The Qur'an's reply to that complaint is distributed across the chapter rather than stated here: the promise has no date attached, the peoples who treated the delay as a refutation are the ones whose ruins now stand on the trade roads, and the delay itself is described elsewhere as a space given for return. Their own phrasing establishes the continuity of the message, which is one of the things the chapter has been arguing. An objection that concedes its opponent's main historical claim is not a strong objection. The objection is given in full before it is answered, which is how this chapter consistently handles the positions it rejects.
