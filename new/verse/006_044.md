## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:44

> When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word "Became Oblivious" and What It Covers**

The verse describes a people who forgot what they had been reminded of, and the reminder in view is the lesson written into their own suffering. Commentators read the phrase in two ways: either they forgot the message the prophet had brought them, or they forgot the lesson their own hardship should have taught them about their complete dependence on God. Both readings point to the same failure, which is that an experience of need was not converted into knowledge.

The Book states the same charge elsewhere when it describes people who call on God while lying on their side, sitting, or standing, and who then return to their old ways as soon as the hardship is lifted, as if they had never cried for help at all (10:12). The forgetting is not an accident of memory. It is the disposal of a lesson that has become inconvenient.

**The Gifts That Arrive Instead of the Punishment**

What comes next is the hardest part of the verse: We showered them with everything they desired. Adversity had failed to produce humility, and the response was not a heavier punishment but an opening of the gates. The Book calls this drawing a person on gradually, and it states the mechanism plainly when it says that those who reject the message will be drawn to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend, and that their end is delayed while God's planning remains flawless (68:44–45).

The interweaving of hardship and ease is described by commentators as the work of a wise guardian who uses both, and the clause in this verse shows how easily the second stage is misread. A people who had failed the test of poverty were given a test of wealth, and wealth is the harder of the two because it arrives dressed as approval.

**The Hadith About Prosperity Without Obedience**

A report quoted in the tafsīr literature on this passage states that when you see that God has given much to one of His servants in this world despite his disobedience, it is only a temptation. The Book makes the same point in its own voice when it tells the disbelievers not to imagine that living longer is good for them, since they are only given more time in which to increase in sin (3:178).

The principle is not that prosperity is bad. It is that prosperity is not a certificate, and the Book returns to that point repeatedly because it is the error most likely to be made by successful people. The Makkans were successful, and their success was the argument they used for the truth of their religion.

**Seized at the Moment of Exultation**

Then the seizure comes: just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise. The timing is precise. It is not during the hardship, when the people were still asking, but at the peak of the recovered confidence, when the lesson had been fully forgotten and the prosperity had been fully credited to their own merit.

The Book uses the same sequence in several accounts of destroyed nations. The people of the Heights argued that their forefathers had seen adversity and prosperity, and then they were seized while they were unaware (7:95); earlier societies were destroyed while they were settled in their comfortable living (28:58). The seizure by surprise is the standard ending, and it is chosen at the point where the warning would have had the least chance of being believed.

**Despair as the Final Condition**

The verse ends with the people in a state the translation renders as despair. The Arabic word describes someone dumbfounded, unable to produce an argument, and it is connected by commentators to the name of Iblīs, the being cast out. What the people lose in that moment is not hope alone but the ability to account for themselves.

The word also measures the distance travelled. This is a people who were confident, comfortable, and certain of their position, and they arrive at a condition in which nothing can be said in their defence. The Book ends them there, and the next verse declares what has happened to them in a single sentence.

**The Test That Runs in Both Directions**

The overall shape of the passage is a test that ran twice, once through adversity and once through ease, and the people failed both halves. The Book names that double testing as the standing condition of humanity, since they are tried with good and evil alike and then returned to their Lord (21:35). What makes the passage so uncomfortable is the observation that the second half of the test is the more dangerous, because it feels like a reward.

Read against the Makkan situation, the verse is a warning aimed at the city's most confident men. They had the wealth, the standing, and the inherited prestige, and they were being told exactly what their comfort was measuring. The verse does not tell them to give it up; it tells them what it is.

The Book gives this procedure its own name and repeats it as a settled pattern. Those who deny the signs are drawn gradually to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend, and their end is delayed while God's planning remains flawless (7:182–183). The Quranic term for the drawing-on carries the sense of a person being led forward step by step, each stage feeling like an ordinary advance, until the moment when he discovers what the path was for. The verse in front of us is that process described from its conclusion, which is why the seizure comes at the point of greatest confidence rather than at the point of greatest need.
