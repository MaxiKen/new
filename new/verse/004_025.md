## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:25

> But if any of you cannot afford to marry a free believing woman, then ˹let him marry˺ a believing bondwoman possessed by one of you. Allah knows best ˹the state of˺ your faith ˹and theirs˺. You are from one another. So marry them with the permission of their owners, giving them their dowry in fairness, if they are chaste, neither promiscuous nor having secret affairs. If they commit indecency after marriage, they receive half the punishment of free women. This is for those of you who fear falling into sin. But if you are patient, it is better for you. And Allah is AllForgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Lawful Path When Means Are Limited**

This verse opens a second door for those who cannot reach the first. *Wa man lam yastaṭiʿ minkum ṭawlan an yankiḥa al-muḥṣanāt al-muʾmināt fa mimmā malakat aymānukum min fatayātikumu al-muʾmināt* — but if any of you cannot afford to marry a free believing woman, then let him marry a believing bondwoman possessed by one of you. *Ṭawl* — means, capacity — was read as wealth to pay the dowry and provide maintenance for a free wife. The verse does not command such a marriage; it permits it — *fa mim mā malakat* — as a mercy for those who lack means and fear falling into unlawful intercourse. The sūrah has already limited polygamy to four and tied it to justice (4:3), listed the prohibited degrees (4:22–23), and described lawful seeking as spending wealth in chastity (4:24). Here it acknowledges that lawful spending may be beyond some, and it provides a path that still meets the sūrah’s core requirements: faith, permission, dowry, and chastity. The Qur’an elsewhere encourages freeing slaves as an act of piety and as expiation, and this permission sits inside that larger movement toward liberty, where the bondwoman is not a casual liaison but a wife entered through covenant, with a named owner’s permission and a known gift.

**You Are From One Another**

The phrase *wa Allāhu aʿlamu bi īmānikum baʿḍukum min baʿḍ* — Allah knows best the state of your faith, and yours, you are from one another — addresses the social barrier that might have kept such marriages from happening. In a society where free and enslaved carried different rank, the Qur’an equalises by faith and by origin. *Baʿḍukum min baʿḍ* — you are from one another — recalls the single soul from which all were spread (4:1) and tells the suitor that the bondwoman who believes shares his humanity and his covenant. Faith is the measure God knows best — *wa Allāhu aʿlamu bi īmānikum* — not the outward rank that people see. That line also explains why the verse specifies *believing* bondwomen as the permitted choice when means fail for a free believing woman. The common faith is presented as the tie that makes marriage meaningful as a shelter — *muḥṣin* — rather than as a mere arrangement. Elsewhere the Qur’an says *“a believing bondwoman is better than a polytheist woman even if she pleases you”* (2:221), and the same ranking operates here: where a free polytheist might be sought for status, the believing bondwoman is offered as the lawful, faithful alternative for the man of limited means.

**With Permission and Fair Dowry in Chastity**

The conditions *fa ankiḥūhunna bi idhni ahlihinna wa ātūhunna ujūrahunna bi al-maʿrūf* — so marry them with permission of their owners, giving them their dowry in fairness — restore full marriage procedure to a relationship that could otherwise have been casual. Permission from the owner — *ahl* — protects the existing tie and prevents secret union, while *maʿrūf* — fairness, what is recognized as good — for dowry ensures the woman receives a gift that is hers, as every free woman receives hers graciously (4:4). The character required — *muḥṣanāt ghayra musāfiḥāt wa lā muttakhidhāti akhdān* — chaste, neither promiscuous nor having secret affairs — repeats the sūrah’s definition of lawful seeking as chaste, not fornicating (4:24), and it applies equally to the bondwoman as bride. That equality was emphasized because a bondwoman might have been assumed to have less claim to chastity protections; the verse denies that. She is to be sought as a chaste wife, not as a hidden liaison, and her dowry is to be given fairly, not as a token. The owner’s permission does not make her property to be passed; it makes the marriage open and witnessed, as every marriage under the sūrah’s law should be.

**Half the Punishment and the Meaning of That Halving**

The clause *fa idhā uḥṣinna fa in atayna bi fāḥishatin fa ʿalayhinna niṣfu mā ʿalā al-muḥṣanāti min al-ʿadhāb* — if they commit indecency after marriage, they receive half the punishment of free women — was understood as half the fixed penalty for unlawful intercourse where the offender is unmarried, since the married free person’s penalty was clarified through Prophetic practice beyond lashing. The halving reflects both the bondwoman’s prior vulnerability and the law’s calibration of responsibility to situation. It does not mean the act is trivial; it means the recompense accounts for the difference in protection and agency she had before marriage. Jurists detailed how halving applies to lashing rather than to penalties whose nature cannot be halved, showing again that the Qur’an measures even punishment with precision, as it measured inheritance with halves, thirds, sixths, and eighths. The same precision that gave the wife a quarter or eighth (4:12) now gives proportion in penalty, keeping justice from becoming severity. The sūrah elsewhere promises to multiply a good deed many times over and to give a great reward out of grace (4:40), and here it shows the other side of that mercy — where penalty must fall, it is measured and halved.

**For Those Who Fear Falling Into Sin**

The restriction *dhālika li man khashiya al-ʿanata minkum* — this is for those of you who fear falling into sin — frames the permission as tied to need and to fear of transgression, not as an ideal to be preferred over marrying a free woman where means exist. *ʿAnat* — hardship, falling into sin — was read as the distress and risk that comes from unmet desire that may push toward *fāḥishah*. The sūrah therefore keeps its earlier advice — if you fear you cannot be just, then one (4:3) — and adds here that if you cannot afford a free wife and you fear sin, the believing bondwoman is lawful, but patience may still be better. That restraint — *wa an taṣbirū khayrun lakum* — but if you are patient, it is better for you — acknowledges that marriage to a bondwoman brings complexities: the owner’s ongoing role, the child’s status, and the limits on full cohabitation that free marriage does not face. The sūrah does not decide for the individual whether patience is possible; it offers both — permission where fear is real, and the counsel that forbearance, if it can be borne without falling, is the more excellent path. That counsel fits the sūrah’s repeated wish to lighten burdens (4:28) while keeping moral shelter intact.

**All-Forgiving, Most Merciful Over Means and Restraint**

The closing *wa Allāhu ghafūrun raḥīm* — and Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful — gathers this detailed law into divine forbearance. The forgiveness covers both the difficulty that led to limited means and the imperfections in choosing the lesser path. The mercy covers the provision of a lawful way where otherwise only hardship or sin would remain. The sūrah began by spreading humanity from a single soul (4:1), guarded the orphan’s wealth with witnessing and reckoning (4:6), fixed shares for heirs (4:11–12), and guarded marriage with lists and dowries (4:22–24). Here it shows that guarding marriage also means guarding those who cannot afford its usual form, by giving them a form they can afford that still requires permission, dowry, chastity, and accountability, and by reminding the free community *you are from one another*. A household entered through that door, with half penalty where sin occurs and with patience still named as better, is not a second-class contract in faith, even if it is a second choice in means. The One who knows best the state of faith is also the One who forgives what is lacking in provision and who shows mercy by keeping the lawful way open.
