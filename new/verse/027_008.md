## Sūrah an-Naml 27:8

> But when he came to it, he was called ˹by Allah˺, “Blessed is the one at the fire, and whoever is around it! Glory be to Allah, the Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Voice Where a Campfire Should Have Been**

Mūsā crosses the dark ground expecting to meet travellers or shepherds. What meets him is a call. The Arabic passive, *nūdiya*, states that he was called without naming the caller, and this indirection is kept deliberately for one more verse; only afterwards does the speaker identify Himself. The audience of the sūrah knows who is speaking. Mūsā, at this instant, does not.

The effect of the passive is to reproduce his experience rather than the narrator's knowledge. A man walks toward a light and hears his situation described back to him in words of blessing. No preamble is given, no explanation of the fire, no answer to the question he came to ask. This is the Qur'an's usual method with theophany: it reports what was said and refuses to describe what was seen. Nothing here tells us the shape of the fire, its colour, its size, or whether it moved. The account is entirely auditory, which is consistent with how the Qur'an treats every encounter of this kind, and consistent with what it says of Mūsā specifically, that God spoke to him directly.

**Who Is Blessed — a Question the Grammar Leaves Open**

The sentence *būrika man fī al-nār wa man ḥawlahā* has occupied commentators for centuries, because Arabic permits several readings and the text supplies no object for the verb. Normally *bāraka* takes something to bless. Here it stands alone, in the passive, and the reader must decide who is meant.

Al-Ṭabarī records the main positions with their grounds. One reading takes *the one at the fire* as God Himself, whose light was present in it, so the blessing falls on the manifestation. A second takes it as Mūsā, the one who had come to the fire, with the angels around him — so the blessing falls on both. A third notes that the Arabic *man*, ordinarily *who*, can carry the wider sense of *what*, giving *blessed is what is in the fire*, meaning the fire itself. A fourth, reported by al-Thaʿlabī, supplies an elided word: blessed is the one *seeking* the fire. Al-Zamakhsharī connects the whole clause to the parallel in al-Qaṣaṣ, where the location is called *the sacred ground*, and concludes the blessing attaches to the place. None of these can be eliminated on grammatical grounds, and the honest summary is that the sentence blesses the whole scene — fire, ground, angels and prophet — without separating them.

**Fire or Light?**

A second problem is what Mūsā actually saw. He called it a fire, and the narrative accepts his word for it in the previous verse. Yet fire burns, and nothing in any account of this night is burned. Several commentators concluded that what he saw was light, *nūr*, and that he named it fire, *nār*, because that is what a light at night looks like from a distance to a man who needs one.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Thaʿlabī note that the Arabs sometimes used *nār* where *nūr* was meant, the two words differing by a single vowel and sharing the image of brightness. Ibn Kathīr transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās that it was not fire but a flickering light, and elsewhere that he called it the light of the Lord of the worlds. This is not an attempt to explain a miracle away. It sharpens the episode: Mūsā went out to fetch a burning brand and found something that gives light without consuming, which is the exact inversion of what he wanted it for. His request was for fire; what he received was illumination, and the two Arabic words sit one vowel apart.

**The Veil of Light**

Discussion of this verse regularly draws in a report about God's relation to light, and it is worth stating accurately. Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī reported that the Prophet ﷺ stood among them and told them five things: that God does not sleep and it does not befit Him to sleep; that He lowers the scale and raises it; that the deeds of the night are taken up to Him before the deeds of the day, and the deeds of the day before those of the night; and that His veil is light — and were He to remove it, the splendour of His face would burn away everything of His creation that His sight reached (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 179a). One transmitter of the same report gives *fire* in place of *light*.

The relevance is direct. If the veil is light and its removal would consume, then a prophet summoned to speak with God must be met by something screened. A light in a fire on a mountainside is exactly such a screening — enough to draw a man across the dark, not enough to destroy him. The report also explains why the accounts stay auditory. What Mūsā received was a voice, and the visible element was a covering rather than a disclosure.

**A Glorification Placed in the Middle of an Encounter**

The call ends with *subḥān Allāh rabb al-ʿālamīn* — glory be to God, Lord of all worlds. Declaring God free of every defect at precisely this moment is not a pious flourish; it is a correction issued before an error can be made. A man standing before a burning bush that speaks is in danger of concluding that God is in the bush, contained by it, identical with the flame. The Qur'an closes that door in the same breath that opens the encounter.

The title chosen reinforces the point. Not *your Lord*, not *the Lord of Israel*, but *Lord of all worlds* — the widest formulation available, asserted at the narrowest possible location, a single fire on a single slope. Al-Rāzī reads the glorification as marking the difference between the place being blessed and the place containing what blessed it. The distinction holds for the whole of the chapter that follows. Solomon will command jinn and understand birds, the queen of Sheba will be shown a floor she misreads, and in every case the power on display belongs to the Lord of all worlds rather than to the object through which it appears. The self-identification in the next verse follows this disclaimer, not the other way round.
