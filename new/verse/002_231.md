## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:231

> When you divorce women and they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or let them go honourably. But do not retain them ˹only˺ to harm them ˹or˺ to take advantage ˹of them˺. Whoever does that surely wrongs his own soul. Do not take Allah’s revelations lightly. Remember Allah’s favours upon you as well as the Book and wisdom He has sent down for your guidance. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instruction at the End of the Period**

The verse instructs the community to keep the women honourably or to release them honourably when the waiting period has almost reached its end, and it forbids the retention of the wife for the purpose of harming her. The sentence follows the rules about the number of declarations and returns to the practical conduct of the couple.

The Book's concern is the decision that must be taken once the period has run, and the sentence allows neither of the two courses to be conducted in a manner that injures the other party. Readers are shown that the legislation requires a definite outcome, and the instruction supplies the standard by which the two options are carried out.

**The Harm Forbidden**

The commentators explained the practice that the sentence prohibits, in which a man would take his wife back just before the waiting period ended in order to keep her in the marriage without providing for her, and he would repeat the arrangement as often as he wished. The sentence names the intention as the offence.

The community's teachers read the clause as the measure that closes the possibility the previous verses had left open, and they noted that the Book describes the harm as a wrong done to the man's own soul. Readers are shown that the legislation treats the injury of a spouse as an injury to the one who inflicts it, and the sentence's statement supplies the standard that governs the conclusion of the marriage.

**The Wrong Done to the Self**

The sentence states that whoever acts in that manner wrongs his own soul, and the community's scholars read the phrase as a statement about the condition of the person who inflicts the harm. The Book's use of the language of self-injury appears in its treatment of other offences, and the reader is shown that the offender is not the only party affected.

The wording also removes the possibility that the man might regard the wife as the only injured party, and the community's teachers used the clause when they spoke about the conduct of the household. The account describes a rule whose purpose is the protection of the weaker party and whose warning is addressed to the one who holds the power.

**The Prohibition of Mockery**

The sentence forbids the community to treat God's signs as a jest, and the commentators explained the phrase as a reference to the attitude that treats the rules of marriage and divorce as matters of play. The reports describe the expressions that people used to dismiss a declaration they had made.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere warns against the mocking of its revelations, and the community's scholars connected the passages when they set out the seriousness of the rules. The passage shows that the passage's concern is the weight that the community gives to the arrangements of its households, and the sentence supplies the warning that accompanies the instruction.

**The Reminder of the Blessing**

The sentence instructs the community to remember the blessing of God and the Book and the wisdom that were sent down for its guidance, and the community's teachers read the clause as the reminder that accompanies the legislation. The Book's practice of joining its rulings to the recollection of what the community has received appears throughout the sūrah.

The mention of the Book and the wisdom connects the passage with the sūrah's earlier treatment of what the community was given, and the reader is shown that the rules about the household belong to the guidance that the community has received. The passage closes with the instruction and with the reminder that gives it its setting.

**The Closing Names**

The sentence closes with the command to be mindful of God and the statement that God knows all things, and the community's scholars read the pair as the ground of the whole passage. The attributes accompany the rules about the household as they accompany the sūrah's other instructions.

The Book's practice of ending a legal passage with a description of God gives the reader the disposition in which the rules are received, and the sentence here follows the pattern. The passage shows that the legislation on marriage and divorce belongs to the same body of guidance as the commands about worship and conflict, and the passage continues with the rules about the woman's remarriage.

**The Reckoning of the Household**

The decision that the sentence requires belongs at the end of the waiting period, and the community's jurists built the rules of the return upon it. A man who takes his wife back must do so in a manner that provides for her and treats her as a wife, and the one who releases her leaves her free to marry elsewhere.

The wrong that the sentence forbids has been described by the teachers as the worst of the offences the passage treats, because it is committed by the person who holds the power in the household. The Book names the harm as an injury to the one who inflicts it, so that the offender's own account carries the deed.

The reminder that follows joins the instruction to the recollection of the Book and the wisdom that the community received. The teachers read the clause as the ground of the whole passage, since the rules of the household belong to the guidance that the community had accepted, and the closing attributes place the conduct of its members before the One who knows all things.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the two honourable courses at the end of the waiting period, the prohibition of retaining the wife in order to harm her, the warning about wronging oneself, the prohibition of treating the signs as a jest, and the reminder of what the community has received. The community's teachers used the verse when they set out the law, and the reader is shown that the Book's legislation on the household joins the protection of the parties with the recollection of the guidance that governs them.

The verse supplied the community with the standard against which a return to the marriage was measured, and the teachers used it when they explained the difference between the reconciliation the Book commends and the retention that it forbids. The passage ends with the mention of the meeting with God, so that the private decisions of the household are placed within the account that every person will meet.
