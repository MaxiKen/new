## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:231

> When you divorce women and they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or let them go honourably. But do not retain them ˹only˺ to harm them ˹or˺ to take advantage ˹of them˺. Whoever does that surely wrongs his own soul. Do not take Allah’s revelations lightly. Remember Allah’s favours upon you as well as the Book and wisdom He has sent down for your guidance. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Retention-Abuse Named**

The verse legislates against the divorce-code's most specific cruelty, and al-Ṭabarī's description of the practice is exact: a man declares divorce, then takes his wife back just before the waiting period ends — revoking the declaration at the last permissible hour — then divorces her again when a new waiting period begins, and repeats the action in order to harm her. The cycling keeps the wife permanently inside ʿiddah: never married in fact, never free in law, her life organized around a waiting that renews itself at the husband's pleasure. The count-legislation of 2:229 barred the cycle's infinity — twice, then finality — and this verse bars its spirit: even within the lawful count, the revocation-right may not be exercised *as harm*. The ruling's wording covers both edges of the waiting period — when they have fulfilled their term, keep them honorably or release them honorably — and the double honorably makes the manner obligatory at both poles: the reconciliation near the term's end must be a real reconciliation, not a trap re-set; and the release at the term's end must be a real release, not a last-minute retraction. The harm the verse abolishes is thus not only the cycling but every use of the waiting-period's structure against the woman it was designed to protect.

**Do Not Take the Signs in Mockery**

The verse's second prohibition goes to the heart's posture: do not take God's signs in play — in mockery, as sport. Al-Ṭabarī supplies the case: the man who declares divorce and then says, I was only playing — the sacred formula treated as a game's move, the marriage's fate as amusement, the law's instruments as toys. The prohibition names the disposition that makes the cycling possible: only a heart that holds the divorce-declaration lightly can declare it serially, and only a man for whom the waiting-period is a lever rather than a person's season can work the lever for harm. The Qur'an's warning against taking the signs in mockery runs as a theme — the sūrah's own portrait of those who took God's signs in jest, the mockery of the scripture's recitation, the play of those whose religion became amusement — and the verse imports the whole theme into the household's law: the divorce-word is a sign, its legislation is revelation, and the marriage it moves is held under God's serious gaze. The sūrah of the Table will legislate the same gravity against those who mock the call to prayer (5:57–58 in substance), and the verse here establishes it where the mockery is most costly — in the chamber where a woman's freedom waits on a man's seriousness.

**Wronging Oneself**

The verdict on the abuser is the sūrah's standing arithmetic: whoever does that has wronged himself. The clause is not a softening of the harm — the cycling husband has wronged his wife in fact, and the code's other provisions answer her — but a statement about the wrong's full account. The harm he engineers is harm he houses: the soul that learns to play with sacred signs, to use a person as a lever, to convert law into cruelty, is a soul being deformed by its own devices, and the deformation is registered on the self that the deeds return to. The sūrah has run this accounting throughout — the concealers' fire eaten into their own bellies (2:174), the traders' purchase of error at guidance's price (2:175), the transgressor after settlement carrying his painful punishment (2:178) — and here the formula enters the domestic law: the husband who harms through the waiting-period is not merely liable but self-damaged, the wrong returning to its author as the author's own diminished soul. The clause's function is deterrent and diagnostic at once: it tells the would-be cycler that his cleverness has a cost booked to his own account, and it tells the reader that every abuse of the household's law is, in the scripture's vision, first a mutilation of the abuser.

**Remember the Favor, the Book, and the Wisdom**

The verse's third movement turns from prohibition to remembrance: remember God's favor upon you, and what He sent down to you of the Book and the Wisdom, exhorting you thereby. The favor is comprehensive — the guidance itself, the community's rescue from the pre-Islamic household's injustices, the legislation that gives the wife a calendar and the marriage a law — and the remembering is the antidote to the mockery: the heart that holds its inheritance in memory does not play with its instruments. The Book-and-Wisdom pairing is the verse's notable formula, recurring where the revelation's wholeness is named — the Prophet's prayer for the community to be given the Book and the Wisdom (2:129), the gifts bestowed on the prophets and the believers across the sūrah of the Women and the Table and the Assembly (3:48, 4:113, 5:110, 45:16, 62:2) — and its presence here elevates the divorce-legislation from case-law to bestowed wisdom: the counts, the waitings, the honors of retention and release are not juristic inventions but the sent-down Wisdom itself, exegeting the community through its households. The exhorting clause names the sending's mode — the Book and Wisdom arrive as admonition, as counsel addressed to hearts — and the verse thereby answers its own abuse-case: the man who plays with the signs has forgotten that the signs were sent to exhort him, and the remedy prescribed is memory.

**The Knower of All Things**

The verse closes with the surveillance that makes its prohibitions enforceable: be mindful of God, and know that God knows all things. The cycling-husband's crime is structurally hidden — each individual revocation is lawful in form, and only the pattern reveals the harm; each "I was playing" is deniable; each last-hour retention looks, to outside audit, like reconciliation. The verse stations the audit where the crime cannot hide: the Knower of all things knows the pattern behind the lawful forms, the mockery behind the words, the harm intended inside the technically valid act. The knowledge-clause completes the code's enforcement architecture: the courts can count declarations and calendar waiting periods, but the intention that converts lawful instruments into weapons is legible only to the all-knowing audit — and the verse legislates precisely at that depth. The mindfulness commanded is the clause's human side: the husband who knows he is known holds the signs seriously, releases honorably when release is due, and reconciles only where reconciliation is true. The domestic legislation's whole method stands in the closing: law made clear, harm made forbidden, memory made the heart's guard, and the Knower of all things made the law's final jurisdiction.

**The Code's Conscience**

Within the divorce-legislation this verse holds the office of conscience. The count (2:229) gave the code its arithmetic; the bar (2:230) gave the arithmetic its finality; this verse gives both their manner — the honor that must govern every exercise of every right the code confers. Its three movements form the conscience's full address: to the hands (do not retain to harm, do not cycle, release at the term), to the tongue (do not play with the signs, do not joke with the divorce-word), and to the heart (remember the favor and the Wisdom, be mindful before the Knower). The wife the legislation protects receives her protection through all three: her waiting-period secured against the trap, her freedom secured against the jest, her case secured before the audit that reads the pattern no court can see. And the husband is addressed not as a defendant but as a beneficiary — the favor upon him includes the law that bounds him, the Wisdom sent down includes the code he administers, and the honor commanded at both edges of the waiting period is his dignity as much as hers: the man who releases honorably is the man the legislation exists to form, and the verse leaves him the choice the whole code has left at every count — retention in maʿrūf, release in iḥsān, and never, under the all-knowing gaze, the harm that wrongs the doer first.
