## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:130

> And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool! We certainly chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question of Comparison**

The verse opens with a rhetorical question that measures the refusal of Abraham's faith against the judgment of the person doing the refusing. The Arabic term rendered as fool describes a person whose understanding has been lost or whose judgment is impaired, and the sentence treats the rejection of Abraham's religion as evidence of exactly that condition.

The question form is one the sūrah has used repeatedly in this passage, and it has the effect of removing the dispute from the plane of neutral disagreement. A person who turns away from the faith of Abraham is not being told that he has made a different choice; he is being told what his choice reveals about him.

**The Faith of Abraham**

The religion referred to here is the submission described in the previous verses, and the sūrah will name it in the coming passage as the upright faith of a man who was not a polytheist. The Book uses the same term for the tradition traced back to Abraham when it tells the Prophet ﷺ and his community to follow it.

The phrase therefore has a specific content in the sūrah's argument. It is not a general reverence for the patriarch but a defined position: the worship of one God, the purity of the sanctuary established at His command, and the submission that the Book states Abraham practised before the later scriptures existed. The phrase also serves the sūrah's argument about the direction of prayer, since the community's rites are presented as the practice of Abraham's religion rather than as an innovation. The coming verses connect the Kaʿbah, the pilgrimage, and the community's own observances to that tradition.

**Who Would Turn Away**

Commentators identified those who reject this faith with the communities that traced their lineage to Abraham while declining the religion the sūrah describes. The Book had already begun that argument by describing people who claimed him as their own, and the question here states the criterion by which the claim is measured.

The sentence also addresses the reader of the Book in any generation. What is being rejected in the verse is a way of worship whose elements the Book sets out, and the refusal of an ancestor's religion in favour of inherited assumptions is treated as a failure of understanding rather than as an alternative tradition. The question also shifts the discussion from tradition to judgment. A person who prefers an inherited position to the religion described in the passage is not being accused of belonging to the wrong community but of failing to weigh what he has been shown.

**Chosen in This Life**

The middle of the sentence states that God chose Abraham in this world, and the Book uses the same language of chosenness for Mary, for Moses, for Adam, and for the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and other prophets. The term indicates a selection for a purpose which the Book then explains through the life of the person selected.

For Abraham, the selection is documented in the sūrah's narrative: the test and the fulfilment, the promise of leadership, the founding and purification of the House, and the prayers for a community and a messenger. What the Book presents as his distinction is the conduct that followed the choice rather than a status that operated apart from it. The Book's use of chosenness for several figures also shows that the term describes a purpose rather than a privilege. Each of the chosen figures is described in the Book by what he was given to do, and Abraham's case is set out in the narrative of this passage.

**Among the Righteous in the Hereafter**

The closing clause states his standing in the next world, and the Book uses the same phrase to describe Abraham in another chapter where it also mentions the blessings granted to him in this world. The phrase combines the two horizons, so that what the community claimed for him is presented as the Book's own confirmation.

The Book's descriptions of the righteous elsewhere in this sūrah include the believers who submit and do good, which means the title is attached to a way of living rather than to a lineage. The reader is therefore shown that Abraham's position in the Hereafter is stated in the same terms the Book uses for anyone who follows his path.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence functions as the conclusion of the passage about Abraham and as the premise for the argument that follows. If his faith is the standard, then the community that follows it is following the religion of the man both earlier communities venerate, and the verse states that to turn from it is to fail in judgment rather than to preserve a tradition.

The next verses present the content of that faith in the words of the prophet himself, when he answered the command to submit, and then record the advice he and Jacob gave to their children. The sūrah is assembling its case from the words of the figures whose authority both communities acknowledge, and the question in this verse is the hinge that turns the narrative into an argument.

**What the Question Is Really Asking**

The rhetorical question does more than criticise those who decline the faith of Abraham; it asks the reader to consider what would have to be true for the refusal to be reasonable. If the religion described in the passage has the content the sūrah has set out, then turning from it would require a reason that outweighs the evidence assembled in the surrounding verses. The Book's method here is to let the reader perform that calculation. The passage has presented Abraham's testing, his fulfilment, the founding of the House, his prayers, and the bequest he left his children, and the question follows immediately. A reader who has accepted that material is being asked whether any consideration could justify setting it aside, and the wording of the sentence supplies the Book's own answer in advance. The verse therefore states the conclusion of the narrative in the form of a question and prepares the reader for the confession of Jacob's household that follows.
