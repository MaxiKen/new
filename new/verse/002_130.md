## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:130

> And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool! We certainly chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Answers Itself**

The verse turns from the patriarch's prayer to the reader's position with a rhetorical question of the recital's most compressed kind: *who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool?* The question expects no candidate, and its expecting is the argument: after the file just presented — the test fulfilled (124), the imāmah appointed and its lineage-condition set (124), the House raised and purified (125), the city's security and fruit prayed (126), the building accepted (127), the nation and its messenger requested (128–129) — the rejection of this faith is not a position with reasons but a failure of the organ that weighs reasons. The word for the failure is *safaha*, and its sense is specific: foolishness as lightness, the mind that has lost its ballast, the self squandered by its own holder — the Qur'an's idiom *the fool in himself* describes a deficiency located in the person's own substance rather than in his information. The verse thus completes the passage's polemic by diagnosis. The recital has met rejection with analysis at every station — the distorters' understanding, the traders' arithmetic, the demanders' hearts — and here, at the patriarch's station, the analysis collapses into a single term: whatever else the rejection of Abraham's faith can be called, it cannot be called judgment. The question-form leaves the reader holding the examination: the file is open, the evidence is the preceding six verses, and the only question remaining is about the examiner's own organ.

**Millah: The Faith Named**

The object of the possible rejection — *the faith of Abraham* — translates *millah*, the term the recital defined at verse 120 as a community's whole way: not a creed held but a pattern lived, the total order of belief, practice, and direction. Abraham's millah is therefore everything the file just documented: the submission asked as gift, the commands fulfilled under test, the House and its rites, the prayers for nation and messenger, the covenant held on righteousness rather than blood. The Qur'an's contest over this millah is its historical occasion: the communities of Madina each claimed the patriarch — the Jews through the Israelite line, the Christians through their scripture's genealogy, the Arabs through Ishmael and the House — and each claim implied that Abraham's faith was that community's property. The verse answers every claim at once by naming the millah independently of all of them, and Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the independence as doctrine: *Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian; he submitted in all uprightness, and was not a polytheist* (3:67). The patriarch's faith precedes the denominations that claim it — the uprightness, the ḥanīf-posture, the submission — and the verse's question thereby acquires its full edge: the communities rejecting the Messenger who carries Abraham's millah forward are rejecting, by the recital's account, the faith of their own claimed ancestor, and the rejection is filed not as theological difference but as the fool's lightness.

**Chosen in Both Worlds**

The verse's second sentence states the patriarch's standing in the two worlds, and the double statement is the rejection-question's warrant. *We certainly chose him in this life* — the iṣṭifāʾ, the selection the Qur'an records for a short list of its figures: Mary chosen above the women of the worlds (3:42), Moses chosen for the message (7:144), Adam (20:122), the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ in the sūrah of the Angels (35:32), the prophets collectively (6:87) — and Abraham, chosen with the emphatic *certainly*, his selection spanning the worldly life in office, honor, and function: imām, builder, answered supplicant, the direction of a community's prayer. *And in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous* — the second-world term of the selection: not merely saved but enrolled, placed among the ṣāliḥūn, the company the patriarch's own prayer-work kept naming. The two clauses together answer the millah-contest from the standing side: the faith whose rejection is foolishness is held by a man doubly certified — chosen here, righteous there — and the certification is divine speech, not communal claim. The verse also quietly settles the lineage-question of verse 124 from the patriarch's own side: the covenant did not extend to the wrongdoers among his offspring, and the patriarch himself stands at both worlds' head — the office's first holder, whose holding is the pattern every claimant of his name is measured against. Chosen, fulfilled, enrolled: the three verbs of the patriarch's file leave the rejection-question with no respectable answer, which is exactly what the question asserts.

**The Continuity of the Two Laws**

The exegetes read the verse as the recital's statement of continuity between the patriarch's law and the Messenger's, and the reading is the verse's structural function in the sūrah. Al-Qushayrī's note states the principle: the Law of Muhammad ﷺ retains the Law of Abraham except where it abrogates it — the continuity qualified by the naskh-doctrine the sūrah legislated at verse 106, so that the two mills stand in the relation the recital's own jurisprudence defines: one religion across its dispensations, its forms adjusted by the Owner of the kingdom (107), its substance unchanged. The concrete evidence runs through the passage just filed: the ḥajj-rites held in Islam as Abrahamic institution — the circling, the standing-place, the showing requested at verse 128 — and the tradition's tracing of the bodily purities, the grooming and cleanliness disciplines, back to the patriarch's fulfilled commands (124's first exegetical stream). The continuity-reading gives the verse's question its second edge. The millah is not a museum-piece the communities honor while practicing otherwise; it is a living law, carried forward in the rites the Muslims of Madina were performing and the Messenger they were following — so rejecting the Messenger is not preserving Abraham's faith but abandoning its transmission, the chain the verse's own six-station file documented link by link. The fools the question names are, in the recital's Madinan moment, precisely the claimants who honored the patriarch's name while rejecting his millah's arrival — and the verse files the contradiction as the definition of safah: lightness toward the heavy thing one claims to hold.

**The Fool and the File**

The verse's transfer runs to every reader who meets the patriarch's file, because the rejection-question is permanent in its form. Abraham's millah reaches each generation through its transmission — the rites, the prayers, the submitted orientation, the covenant-on-righteousness law — and each generation faces the question in its own terms: the rejection is rarely announced as rejection; it wears the forms the recital has documented — the millah honored in name and rationed in practice, the patriarch claimed while his tested pattern is declined, the House revered while its Owner's commands behind the back are cast. The verse's diagnosis covers all of it: safah is not the dramatic apostasy but the lightness — the loss of weight that lets a person hold a claim and a conduct that contradict, the organ failing while the vocabulary remains. Against the lightness the verse sets the file's heaviness: commands fulfilled under fire and knife, foundations raised with prayer mid-labor, a nation and a messenger asked for across centuries of unseen future, a city's fruit requested for people not yet born. The patriarch's faith is the weightiest thing in the recital's archive — every petition paid for in obedience — and the question's force is proportional: only lightness rejects weight. The recital leaves the reader with the examination running: whichever community's child one is, whichever patriarch's name one carries, the verse asks the single question that sorts the claimants — does the millah one claims have weight in the hands, in the rites, in the fulfilled commands? The fool is not who rejects Abraham; the fool is who claims him and lives light.
