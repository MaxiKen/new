## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:83

> Do they desire a way other than Allah’s—knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question About Desire**

The verse asks whether the people just described are looking for a way other than God's, and the question is phrased in terms of desire. What is at issue is not only what they do but what they want, and the Book's habit of naming desire as the engine of doctrinal position is on display here.

The question also exposes the impossibility of what is wanted. The sentence continues by stating that everyone in the heavens and the earth submits to God's will, willingly or unwillingly, and that all of them will be returned to Him. A way other than God's is therefore not an alternative route but a contradiction of the only order there is, and the verse's question is designed to be felt as unanswerable rather than answered.

**Submission Willing and Unwilling**

The phrase distinguishes two ways in which submission occurs, and the commentators found it a difficult and fruitful expression. Some read the willing submission as the devout obedience of believers and the angels, and the unwilling submission as the condition of those who must submit to God's decrees in creation, death, and the outcome of events despite refusing His commands.

The Book uses the distinction in more than one place. The sūrah of the Thunder declares that everything in the heavens and the earth prostrates to God, willingly or unwillingly, as do their shadows morning and evening (13:15). In the sūrah of Fussilat, when God addresses the heaven and the earth and calls them to submit, they answer together that they submit willingly (41:11). The sūrah here gives the general case; elsewhere the Book gives the same subject in its particular instances, and the verses about the prostrating sun, moon, mountains, and trees belong to the same theme.

**The Persuasion of an Unavoidable Order**

The verse's argument is not that everyone obeys God's commands, which would be false. Its claim is that nobody escapes God's will, which is a statement about power rather than about piety.

The distinction between being subject to a power and agreeing with it is precisely what the two adverbs are for. The Book elsewhere describes people whose faith appears at the moment of punishment when it can no longer help them, and it states that belief at that point profits no one; the readiness to submit much earlier, while it still counted, is the thing that distinguishes one kind of submission from the other. What this verse establishes is that the universe is not divided between those under God's rule and those outside it. It is divided between those who accept His rule and those who are governed by it against their preference.

**What the Argument Does in This Passage**

The verse stands between the covenant and the passage about the community's inheritance of Abraham, and its placement keeps the sūrah's controversy within a single frame. The disputants had been arguing about which community held the correct position, and the verse reminds them that the question of belonging to God's order is not settled by a community's self-assessment.

The Book's method here is to widen the frame when the discussion has become factional. An argument about who has the correct revelation is set inside a statement about the submission of everything that exists, and the effect is to make the parties' claims look small. The next verse returns to the specifics of the controversy and states the content of the faith that the believers are to profess, and it does so in a form that includes the prophets both sides honoured.

**The Return at the End of the Sentence**

The verse closes with the return to God, which is a favourite ending of the Book either at the end or the beginning of a passage about people who are confident about their standing.

The Book's use of the theme here has a particular force. A person who is looking for a way other than God's will find himself at the end of his road facing the one whose way he was avoiding, and the sentence says this without any elaboration. The verse does not describe punishment, and it does not pronounce a verdict. It states a fact about the destination of all things, and the question with which the verse begins is left lying against it: what does a person who seeks an alternative way imagine that he is seeking, given that everything returns to the same Lord?

**A Note on the Two Adverbs' Comfort**

The passage has a further dimension that the commentators discussed. To submit willingly is the believer's condition, and to submit unwillingly is the condition of the rest of creation; but the two share the same Lord, and the Book states the matter in one sentence so that the difference between them is never obscured by any claim of independence.

A reader who takes the verse seriously learns something about how to think about the world's apparent disorder. The Book's picture is of a creation whose every part is subject to God's will, with human beings given the choice of submitting in the manner of the willing or being carried along in the manner of the unwilling. What distinguishes the two is not the fact of the relationship but the posture of the person within it, which is exactly the distinction the sūrah's next verses set out to describe. The Book returns to the same idea wherever it wants to state the limits of human rebellion, and it states it in the same terms each time: the refusal of a decree does not prevent the decree. What the sūrah's readers are being offered in this verse is not a description of the world as it should be but of the world as it already is, inside which their own decisions take place and by which their weight is measured.
