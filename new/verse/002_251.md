## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:251

> So they defeated them by Allah's Will, and David killed Goliath. And Allah blessed David with kingship and wisdom and taught him what He willed. Had Allah not repelled a group of people by ˹the might of˺ another, corruption would have dominated the earth, but Allah is Gracious to all.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rout by Leave**

The engagement is dispatched in three words: *they defeated them* — and the words that follow carry the verse's entire weight: *by Allah's Will*, bi-idhn Allāh. The rout's attribution is fixed before its description: the remnant won because the winning was permitted, the same leave the maxim on the far bank had invoked — the small-over-large record whose every entry carries the same signature. The narrative then names the stroke that decided it: *David killed Goliath*. The Qur'an assumes the story its hearers knew — the young shepherd, unarmored, declining the king's sword, taking five stones from the brook and one of them from the sling; the giant felled in the forehead, the host's collapse at its champion's fall (1 Samuel 17). The commentary tradition adds the detail the verse's economy implies: David, the youngest present, was the army's champion against Goliath, and the duel — one boy against one giant — condensed the whole campaign's disparity into a single exchange decided, like the campaign, by leave.

**The Boy and the Two Inheritances**

The verse's second movement turns from the battle to the victor: *And Allah blessed David with kingship and wisdom*. The dual gift is read by al-Ṭabarī as a double inheritance: wisdom means the prophethood — David inherits the prophetic office of Samuel, the story's announcing prophet; kingship means the throne — he inherits the royal office of Saul, whose line the narrative will not continue. The shepherd thus gathers into one person the two authorities the tribes had held apart, and the gathering is the verse's quiet answer to the chiefs' old objection about pedigree: the man who ends up holding both offices came from neither house, chosen the way Saul was chosen, for what God knew. The Qur'an's David-portrait continues elsewhere — the sūrah Ṣād's account of him as authority in the land, commanded to judge with truth and warned against desire's misdirection (38:26) — and the kingship-plus-wisdom pairing here is that portrait's origin-story: the office and the character to carry it, granted together, at the moment of the sling.

**Taught Him What He Willed**

The third clause widens the gift: *and taught him what He willed*. The teaching's content is unnamed, and the naming is withheld deliberately — the tradition associates David with the Zabūr, the Psalter, with the mountains and birds joining his praise, with the craft of mail taught by divine instruction (21:80); the verse subsumes all of it under a single principle: what God teaches, He teaches by will, and the recipient's qualification is election rather than curriculum. The commentators connect the clause to the story's leadership-theology: Saul was increased in knowledge and stature (2:247), David is taught what He willed — both men carry endowments whose measure and timing belong to the Giver. The clause also frames the coming verses' subject, the messengers and their preferments: before the sūrah generalizes the principle — some messengers raised above others — it exhibits it in one career, a boy given a kingdom, a prophethood, and an education no school administered. The will that chose the unlikely king chooses the unlikely champion, and teaches him out of the same freedom.

**The Repelling Principle**

The verse's fourth movement steps back from the story to a law of history: *Had Allah not repelled a group of people by ˹the might of˺ another, corruption would have dominated the earth*. The principle — God repels some people by means of others — is the Qur'an's answer to the question every holy war narrative raises: why does the divine administration run through human conflict at all? The verse's answer is counterfactual and total: without the repelling, the earth's corruption would not merely occur but prevail — the fa-sada that consumes societies would go unchecked, and the checking is done, in God's arrangement, by human forces set against one another. The parallel statement in the sūrah of the Pilgrimage specifies the repelling's stakes in the register of worship: *Had Allah not repelled some people by means of others, destruction would have surely claimed monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which Allah's Name is often mentioned* (22:40). The two verses together state the principle's scope: the repelling protects the earth's order and the earth's sanctuaries, and it protects them through exactly the kind of event the story just narrated — a remnant set against a host.

**Who Repels Whom**

The commentators press the principle for its mechanics, and the readings they record span the visible and the hidden. Some say the repelling runs through the spiritual hierarchy: the abdāl, the Substitutes — forty souls, al-Qurṭubī records, an inward order through whom, the exegetes hold, God's grace and shielding reach the world, each vacancy at a death silently filled by another. Others read it socially: the prayerful repel the punishment due to the unprayerful, the pious hold back the consequences of the impious, the obedient check the evil of the disobedient — al-Ṭabarī's version of the same economy. A ḥadīth the exegetes cite states the exchange as a list: God turns away the punishment owed by the prayer-less through the one who prays, and likewise through the almsgiver, the faster, the pilgrim, and the striver for those who leave each act undone. The readings agree on the structure: the earth's habitability is a function of the righteous operating inside it, and their operations — visible battles, invisible prayers — are the repelling by which corruption is denied its dominance.

**Gracious to All**

The verse closes by naming the repelling's source: *but Allah is Gracious to all* — dhū faḍlin ʿalā'l-ʿālamīn, the Possessor of bounty toward the worlds. The name answers the counterfactual's darkness: the sentence begins with the corruption that would have dominated and ends with the generosity that does not let it, and the bounty's range — the worlds, not the believers, not the remnant — includes the repelled and the repellers, the defeated host and the boy with the sling. The echo with the fleeing-thousands scene is deliberate: that story closed on God's bounty toward mankind and mankind's ingratitude (2:243); this one closes on the same bounty, extended through a war, over all worlds. The story-unit that began with a multitude dying for fear of death ends with the principle that keeps the earth alive through the few who did not fear it — and the principle's engine, the verse's final word insists, is not necessity but faḍl. David will reappear in the Qur'an as judge, psalmist, and king; the verse that installs him installs, around his career, the standing explanation of why history has heroes at all: the earth would corrupt, and God is gracious, so He sends the few against the many, by His leave, until the leave runs out.
