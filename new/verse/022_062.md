## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:62

> That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Most High, All-Great.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Ground, and What It Adds**

*Dhālika bi-anna Allāha huwa al-ḥaqqu* — that is because God, He is the Truth.

The sūrah has said this before, at verse 6, in almost the same words. What is added here is the second half: *wa-anna mā yadʿūna min dūnihi huwa al-bāṭil* — and what they call upon apart from Him is falsehood.

The addition changes the sentence's function. Verse 6 was establishing a premise for the Resurrection: God is real, He gives life, He is capable. Verse 62 is drawing a conclusion about worship, and it does so by putting the true and the false in the same sentence with the same predicate structure. God *is* the Truth; what is invoked beside Him *is* the falsehood. Both are declared with the emphatic pronoun, and the parallelism is the argument.

**What "Bāṭil" Means**

The word translated as falsehood is *bāṭil*, from a root meaning to be void, to come to nothing, to fail.

The sense is not primarily that the idols do not exist — a carved stone exists — but that they are weightless in the way that matters. A thing is *bāṭil* when it cannot deliver what is claimed for it, when it dissolves under examination, when it produces nothing. The Qur'an's charge against the objects of pagan worship has never been that they are hallucinations; it is that they are null.

That is why the sūrah's argument has been functional throughout. Verse 12's test was whether the thing can harm or benefit. Verse 73, near the end of the sūrah, will press the same point with a fly. The criterion never changes, and it never concerns whether the object can be seen.

**The Objection This Answers**

The verse is addressing a specific and reasonable objection: that the idols are real forces with real effects, or, more modestly, that they are conduits to a power that is real.

The response is not to deny the conduit's existence but to deny its validity. What is invoked apart from God is *bāṭil* even when the invocation is sincere, even when the worshipper feels something, even when the cult has been practised for centuries. Sincerity does not confer on an object the capacity it lacks.

Sūrah Luqmān states the entire verse almost identically, which confirms that this is a fixed formula rather than a remark tailored to one audience: *“That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and ˹because˺ Allah ˹alone˺ is the Most High, All-Great”* (31:30).

**Two Predicates, One Shape**

The Arabic sets the two claims in exactly parallel form: *anna Allāha huwa al-ḥaqqu wa-anna mā yadʿūna min dūnihi huwa al-bāṭil*. Each clause has the particle, the subject, the emphatic pronoun, and the predicate. Neither is subordinate to the other.

The parallelism is the argument's whole structure, and it is worth slowing down over why it matters. A sentence that said "God is true, so your idols are false" would be making an inference that a listener could dispute at the level of the inference — why should one follow from the other? A sentence that simply states both, with the same grammar and the same emphasis, is not inferring. It is describing two things at once and letting the reader see that they cannot both be held.

The emphatic pronoun *huwa* in each clause does the rest. God *is* the Truth: not a truth among truths, but that which truly is. What is called upon besides Him *is* the false: not weaker, not lesser, but the thing that comes to nothing.

**"The Most High, the Great"**

*Wa-anna Allāha huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-kabīr* — and God is the Most High, the Great.

The two names are chosen for their fit with what precedes. If what is worshipped besides God is null, the reason is not that it is weak in a competition among powers. It is that the distance between it and God is not a distance at all: God is *al-ʿaliyy*, the High, above comparison, and *al-kabīr*, the Great, beside whom greatness is not a measurable property.

That is why the sūrah does not argue that God is greater than the idols. Greatness of that comparative kind would concede that the idols have some. The names are absolute: high without reference to what is beneath, great without reference to what is smaller.

**Why the Pairing Recurs**

The combination of *al-ʿaliyy* and *al-kabīr* appears together at the close of the Āyat al-Kursī in the same emphatic form, and the recurrence tells us what the pairing is for: it is the standard Qur'anic way of ending an argument about God's uniqueness.

Having established that everything worshipped besides Him is void, the verse closes by naming the two attributes that make the voidness necessary rather than accidental. The idols are null because the One they are set beside is high and great in a way that leaves no room.

**The Verse's Place in the Chain**

This is the second of the four causal verses. The first grounded the promise of help in God's management of night and day; this one grounds it in what God is and what everything else is not.

The movement is instructive. The sūrah is not arguing that the believers will win because their cause is just or their number sufficient. It is arguing that help is available because the power being invoked against them is void and the power being invoked for them is absolute. A community facing an enemy's gods is being told what those gods are.
