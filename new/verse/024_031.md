## Sūrah an-Nūr 24:31

> And tell the believing women to lower their gaze and guard their chastity, and not to reveal their adornments except what normally appears. Let them draw their veils over their chests, and not reveal their ˹hidden˺ adornments except to their husbands, their fathers, their fathers-in-law, their sons, their stepsons, their brothers, their brothers’ sons or sisters’ sons, their fellow women, those ˹bondwomen˺ in their possession, male attendants with no desire, or children who are still unaware of women’s nakedness. Let them not stomp their feet, drawing attention to their hidden adornments. Turn to Allah in repentance all together, O believers, so that you may be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Female Mirror of Modesty: Lowering Gaze and Guarding Chastity**

The divine legislation addresses the female community with the exact moral imperatives previously directed toward men: *wa-qul lil-muʾmināti yaghḍuḍna min abṣārihinna wa-yaḥfaẓna furūjahunn*. Visual discipline and sexual chastity are universal ethical duties that bind both genders equally. Women are affirmed as fully autonomous moral agents endowed with spiritual discernment, called upon to master their own sensory intake and protect their personal boundaries.

Modesty in the Islamic paradigm is not a unilateral burden placed upon women to manage the uncontrolled impulses of men; it is a reciprocal social contract. Men must lower their gaze, and women must lower their gaze; men must guard their chastity, and women must guard their chastity. By mirroring the commands across adjacent verses, the Qur'an establishes a shared culture of mutual respect and spiritual dignity, ensuring that public and domestic life is grounded in reverent restraint rather than visual predation.

**The Apparent and Concealed Adornment: mā ẓahara minhā**

The verse establishes a foundational rule governing female dress: *wa-lā yubdīna zīnatahunna illā mā ẓahara minhā*, and not to reveal their adornment except that which normally appears. The term *zīnah* encompasses both natural bodily beauty and acquired embellishments such as clothing, jewelry, henna, and cosmetics. The precise scope of the exception *illā mā ẓahara minhā* generated rich and extensive jurisprudential discourse among early authorities.

The prominent Companion ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd held that the phrase refers strictly to external outer garments—such as the cloak (*jilbāb*) or abaya—which cannot practically be concealed while walking in public. In contrast, the majority of early authorities, including ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbbās, ʿĀʾishah, and prominent Successors, maintained that the exception permits the uncovering of the face, hands, and the customary adornments upon them (such as kohl and rings). This majority view recognized that women participate actively in public life—transacting in markets, giving legal testimony, and managing property—which requires practical visual identification and manual dexterity without undue hardship.

**The Architectural Decree: Khumur over the Juyūb**

The text delivers a specific, practical instruction regarding the arrangement of head coverings: *wa-lyadrīna bi-khumurihinna ʿalā juyūbihinn*. In pre-Islamic Arabia, women commonly wore the *khimār* (head veil), but customary fashion involved draping the fabric backward over the shoulders, leaving the throat, neck, ear ornaments, and the upper chest opening (*al-jayb*) entirely exposed to the public gaze.

The Qur'anic command intervened with architectural precision, instructing believing women to draw the fabric of their head veils forward, fastening them securely over their chest openings. In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4758, ʿĀʾishah praised the Anṣārī women who, the very moment this verse descended, tore pieces from their thick outer garments to cover their chests and heads immediately. This historic response demonstrated how the early community embraced divine modesty not as an oppressive imposition, but as a dignified expression of sacred identity.

**The Sanctuary of the Mahārim: Circles of Domestic Exemption**

The verse delineates an exhaustive catalogue of exempted individuals before whom a woman may relax her external covering: husbands, fathers, fathers-in-law, sons, stepsons, brothers, nephews, fellow believing women, domestic servants, male attendants free of sexual desire (*ghayri ulī al-irbah*), and young children unaware of female sexuality.

The domestic residence is conceived as an intimate sanctuary where a woman is not required to live in perpetual covering. Within these defined circles of unbreachable trust—where sexual tension is permanently dismantled through sacred incest prohibitions (*al-maḥārim*) or biological incapacity—a woman may comfortably display her natural beauty and domestic adornments. The law carefully balances external public modesty with domestic comfort, ensuring that family homes remain spaces of relaxed affection, warmth, and natural ease.

**The Auditory Dimension of Modesty: Striking the Feet**

The divine guidance extends beyond visual fabrics to govern auditory cues and physical movement: *wa-lā yaḍribna bi-arjulihinna li-yuʿlama mā yukhfīna min zīnatihinn*. In ancient Arabia, women frequently wore heavy anklets (*al-khalākhil*) embellished with internal bells or resonant metal beneath their flowing skirts, deliberately striking their feet against the pavement to produce jingling sounds that drew male attention to their hidden ornaments.

The Qur'an strictly prohibits this behavior, establishing that true modesty encompasses body language, sound, and intention. Covering the body visually while broadcasting sensuality acoustically subverts the spiritual objective of the law. The prohibition cultivates a holistic aesthetic of dignity: a believer does not seek cheap public validation through sensory exhibitionism. Modesty requires that one's presence in the public sphere be defined by quiet purpose, moral gravitas, and unpretentious grace.

**Collective Repentance as the Gateway to Success: tūbū ilā Allāh**

The verse culminates in an extraordinary universal call to repentance: *wa-tūbū ilā Allāhi jamīʿan ayyuhā al-muʾminūna laʿallakum tufliḥūn*, and turn to Allah in repentance all together, O believers, so that you may prosper. Concluding an exhaustive legislative passage on female dress with an all-inclusive command for both men and women to repent together reveals the spiritual essence of Islamic law.

Divine legislation recognizes that despite all physical boundaries, veils, and social rules, human beings are frail creatures who inevitably stumble in their thoughts, stolen glances, and subtle intentions. External compliance with dress codes is meaningless without internal spiritual humility. The entire community—men and women alike—is commanded to turn perpetually to God, seeking pardon for shortcomings and renewing their hearts. Ultimate prosperity (*falāḥ*) is achieved not through legalistic self-righteousness, but through collective contrition beneath the boundless mercy of the Creator.
