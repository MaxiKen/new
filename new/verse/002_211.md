## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:211

> Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We have given them. And whoever trades Allah’s favour—after receiving it—˹for disbelief˺ should know that Allah is indeed severe in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put to History**

The verse turns from the cloud-scene to a standing witness: ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We have given them. The command to question is the verse's method — history summoned as testimony in the case the previous verses opened. The community that might slip after its proofs (2:209) is directed to the people who lived the slip at the largest scale the world has seen: a nation fed with signs, delivered by miracles, covenanted with God, and still the scripture's standing example of favor altered. The interrogative kam — how many — expects no number because the number is beyond counting; the question asks the witness to display its record, and the record itself is the argument. Al-Ṭabarī fixes the signs' identity: the miracles of Moses — the staff cast down and become a serpent, the hand drawn out white, the sea parted, the enemy drowned — the sign-cycle the sūrah of the Heights narrates at length (7:105–136). Each sign was a bayyinah, a clear proof in the exact sense 2:209 used for the proofs given this community: evidence so plain that rejection after it is not ignorance but choice. The verse thus establishes the comparison the passage needs — the proofs of this ummah against the signs of that — and asks the question whose answer is the warning.

**The Favor, and Its Alteration**

The verse's second movement names the crime: whoever alters the favor of God after it has come to him. The favor — niʿmah — was interpreted by al-Ṭabarī as the trust and covenant God made with the Children of Israel: the whole endowment of signs, scripture, prophecy, and election that constituted their national blessing. To alter it means, in the exegetes' primary sense, to deny it, to disbelieve in it — the favor received and then re-defined by its receivers as something other than what the Giver made it. The verb baddala carries the substitution-sense the Qur'an uses for the gravest exchange: not merely neglecting the favor but replacing it — and some commentators connect the alteration to the accusation of taḥrīf, the distortion of scripture, recorded at 2:75, where a group hears the word of God and then knowingly corrupts it after understanding it. The alteration is thus comprehensive: the covenant's terms renegotiated, the signs reinterpreted away, the Book's words adjusted toward the readers' desire. And the temporal clause is the crime's aggravator: after it has come to him. The favor was not altered in prospect or in ambiguity — it arrived, was received, was held, and was then changed in the holder's hands, which is the precise anatomy of the slip 2:209 warned this community against, drawn at national scale.

**The Severe Retribution**

The verdict clause repeats the formula the hajj-legislation closed on: God is severe in retribution — shadīd al-ʿiqāb. The same phrase ended the pilgrimage's concessions-verse (2:196), where it guarded the rite's interior against exploitation; here it guards the favor against alteration, and the repetition across the passage is deliberate — the two domains where the law has no human auditor, the pilgrim's intention and the covenant-keeper's fidelity, are the two domains the severe-retribution formula oversees. The severity answers the crime's shape: an alteration performed after full receipt is not weakness but transaction — the favor traded, the verse says in the translation's gloss, for disbelief — and a transaction is answered by a price. The sūrah has been building this economics since its opening: the traders who purchased error at guidance's price (2:16), the concealers who sold revelation for a fleeting gain and ate fire (2:174–175), the covenant-sellers of Āl ʿImrān whose trade earns them no share in the Hereafter and no divine speech on the Day (3:77). The Israelite alteration is the pattern's first national instance, and the severe retribution is the pattern's fixed consequence: the favor exchanged is not merely lost but avenged, because the exchange itself is the insult — the Giver's gift re-valued by the receiver as less than disbelief.

**Why the Question Is Commanded**

The command to ask has a purpose beyond polemic, and the verse's context supplies it. The passage has moved from the community's internal legislation — the hajj, the complete entry, the slip-warning, the cloud-scene — to its external orientation, and the question to the Children of Israel installs the new ummah inside an older story. The believers are not the first people given proofs; they are the latest, and the verse makes them inherit the whole archive: the signs of Moses are their inheritance-warning, the altered favor is their case-study, the severe retribution is their horizon. This is the same method the sūrah has used throughout — the Israelite narratives of the early verses (the calf, the covenant, the prophets killed) read as instruction for the new community rather than as history alone — and here the method becomes explicit: ask, it says; make the inquiry; hold the answer. The question also disciplines the community's self-understanding: the ummah addressed has just completed the hajj-legislation, the most elaborate rite-code in the sūrah, and might read its legislation as evidence of a secured standing; the verse reminds it that Israel had signs beyond counting and a covenant from God, and that neither the count nor the covenant prevented the alteration. Standing is kept, not inherited — and the asking is part of the keeping.

**The Signs and the Scripture's Own Case**

The verse's signs have a specific edge for a community whose central sign is a book. The miracles of Moses were public and physical — the staff, the hand, the sea — signs seen by multitudes and survived by no disputer; and the Children of Israel altered the favor built on them. The new community's clear proofs include a sign of a different order — the Qur'an itself, a bayyinah of speech rather than spectacle — and the verse's warning transfers without loss: the alteration Israel performed on signs it saw, a later generation can perform on a sign it reads, adjusting the Book's words toward desire, re-interpreting the covenant's terms, substituting for the favor a version of the favor that costs nothing. The sūrah has already legislated against exactly this on its own pages: the editors who write the book with their hands and say this is from God (2:79), the concealers who hide what was revealed (2:174), the far-schismatics who differ regarding the Book in truth (2:176). The question to Israel is therefore also a mirror: the clear signs given them and the clear proofs given this community stand under one law — received favor is kept by fidelity and lost by substitution — and the severe retribution named at the verse's end is pronounced over both archives, the sea parted and the Book revealed, against every hand that alters what it was given.

**Between the Clouds and the One Community**

Within the movement's architecture the verse is the bridge between the horizon and the history. The cloud-scene (2:210) showed the waiting its terminus; this verse shows the slipping its precedent; and the verses that follow will show the whole human story its shape — the world made fair to the mockers (2:212), humanity one community then divided, the Book sent down to judge the division (2:213). The Israelite question is the middle term: the case-history that proves the terminus is not theoretical. A people asked, given signs, altered the favor, met the severe retribution — the sequence in completed form, available for the asking. The community reading these verses between the hajj's end and the coming trials-verse stands at the sequence's second step: proofs received, favor held. The verse tells it what the third step can be and what the fourth is — and the command to ask is the instruction for staying at the second: keep inquiring of the archive, keep the case-history present, keep the alteration visible in the witness's own record. The signs given Israel are counted; the favor they altered is named; the retribution's severity is published — and the whole testimony is left standing at the door of every community the proofs afterward reach.
