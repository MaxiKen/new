## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:86

> Forgive my father, for he is certainly one of the misguided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham's Prayer Remembers His Father**

Abraham includes his father in the prayer for forgiveness. The request shows that rejection of idolatry does not erase family feeling. Abraham has challenged his father's worship, but he has not become indifferent to his father's fate. The person who stands against a parent's error may still hope that the parent will receive mercy and leave the error behind.

That hope is not sentimental denial. Abraham calls his father misguided. He does not rename false worship as an acceptable difference in order to preserve emotional comfort. Love and moral clarity remain together. The father is dear enough to be prayed for, yet the practice is wrong enough to be named plainly.

**Misguidance Describes a Path, Not a Worthless Person**

The verse identifies the father as one of those who are astray, a description of direction rather than a claim that he has no human value. To be misguided is to have lost the right path and to need return. Abraham's wording leaves room for the seriousness of error without turning the person into an object of contempt.

This distinction matters in religious speech. A doctrine can be false, and a practice can lead people away from God, while the people involved remain capable of repentance and deserving of honest concern. Abraham's prayer models a firm opposition to idolatry that does not become pleasure in another person's ruin.

**Forgiveness Is Sought From God Alone**

Abraham does not ask his father, his ancestors, or the idols to repair the spiritual damage. He asks God to forgive. The prayer confirms the earlier declaration that the Lord alone governs the servant's final relation to Him. Family loyalty cannot forgive sin, and inherited custom cannot make a person safe before God.

The request also recognizes a limit on Abraham's own power. He can question, warn, and pray, but he cannot force faith into another heart. Forgiveness belongs to the Lord, whose knowledge includes the hidden motives and final choices of the person being prayed for. Abraham's responsibility is compassion and truth; the verdict remains God's.

**A Promise Shapes the Earlier Prayer**

The exegetical tradition connects this request with Abraham's earlier promise to seek forgiveness for his father. The Qur'an later explains that his continued plea was tied to that promise, but that he withdrew from it when the father's enmity toward God became clear. The sequence shows that prayer for a misguided relative is not a permanent endorsement of stubborn opposition to truth.

A promise made in hope can be honored within the limits of obedience to God. Once the moral reality becomes clear, affection cannot require a believer to treat enmity toward God as harmless. Abraham's example therefore balances keeping one's word with recognizing when a spiritual boundary has been reached.

**Filial Care Does Not Mean Religious Surrender**

Abraham's prayer protects family care from being confused with submission to family belief. He does not worship with his father in order to prove love, and he does not soften the truth to avoid conflict. He prays for the father because the father is misguided, not because the guidance is unnecessary.

This is a difficult balance for anyone whose family follows a different path. Respect, service, patience, and sincere concern can remain duties, while worship belongs to God alone. Abraham shows that family affection should become a reason to seek someone's good, not a reason to call falsehood true.

To ask forgiveness for the father is to refuse the assumption that his present condition is his final condition. Abraham sees the error, but he does not claim knowledge of the future that belongs only to God. Prayer keeps open the possibility that a person can turn, even after years of inherited worship.

Hope for repentance should not become naïveté. Abraham's prayer does not deny the father's words or the harm of idolatry. It simply refuses to make despair the only response to error. A warning is most humane when it is spoken while return remains possible.

**A Parallel Prayer Broadens the Family Concern**

In 14:41, Abraham asks forgiveness for himself, his parents, and the believers on the day judgment is established. That parallel shows that his concern is not limited to private emotion. He places family within a wider community of people who need God's mercy. The individual household is important, but it is not the whole horizon of prayer.

The parallel also confirms that asking God to forgive parents belongs within Abraham's devotional language. The request is made to the Lord who hears prayer, not to an ancestor or household power. Its proper end is mercy from God and a community oriented toward Him.

Abraham's concern for his father must not be used to defend false worship or to suggest that love makes every belief acceptable. The verse contains both the request and the diagnosis: forgive my father, for he is misguided. Mercy is sought precisely because the condition is wrong. A prayer that hides the wrong would not be Abraham's prayer.

The passage therefore gives a mature form of compassion. Speak truthfully about misguidance, hope for forgiveness, honor the bond of family, and leave the final judgment to God. Abraham's love is not passive approval. It is the painful concern of someone who wants a beloved person to be rescued from an error that cannot save him. Such concern can remain patient without becoming silent, and firm without becoming cruel. It seeks the person's rescue rather than the satisfaction of proving that the speaker was right.

A family member may therefore keep making sincere prayers and offering honest counsel while refusing to join the error. Abraham's example does not demand emotional distance; it demands that affection serve truth.
