## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:88

> They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condition Described as Lasting**

The verse states two things about the people whose conduct the passage has been tracing: their condition will last, and the punishment will neither be eased nor postponed. The two clauses cover duration and intensity, and together they remove the two hopes that a person naturally entertains about a penalty.

The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter often pair a duration with a detail about the experience within it, and the pairing here is unusually bare. Nothing is said about the nature of the punishment, and nothing is said about a possible change in the arrangement. What the reader is given is a sentence about what will not happen: no lightening, no delay. The negative form is the Book's way of stating finality without describing the machinery.

**Duration and the Question It Raises**

The Book uses the language of permanence for the punishment of those who die in rejection, and this verse belongs to that group of statements. A reader who has followed the passage closely will notice that the persons described here are people who had believed and then turned away, and the severity of an unending condition is correspondingly greater.

The sūrah does not leave the difficulty unaddressed, and the verses after this one supply a provision for those who return in this life. What the passage as a whole does is to distinguish between the person whose turning away is final, because his life has ended in that state, and the person whose turning away is a stage he may still leave. The verse above describes the first case, and its absoluteness is what makes the offer that follows it meaningful rather than decorative.

**"Their Punishment Will Not Be Lightened"**

The clause rules out the hope of a sentence that gradually becomes bearable, and the Book states the same negation for the final condition of the rejecters in other passages. Some traditions describe degrees within the punishment and intervals in its intensity, and the Book itself speaks of a Day whose length varies and of punishments that are described in a range of images.

The specific clause in this verse concerns the people whose earlier history included faith and knowledge, and its function is to close off the expectation of relief. A person who expects that a severe penalty will eventually become lighter has a reason to postpone his repentance; the Book removes that reason for the case it is describing, and it does so in the flat language of a statement rather than the ornate language of a scene. What the reader is being told is not how the punishment will feel but that its terms will not be renegotiated.

**"Nor Will They Be Delayed"**

The second negation concerns time, and it removes the other common expectation. A person might accept that a penalty awaits him and still hope that it can be postponed, that some intercession, excuse, or interval might put off the moment.

The Book is consistent in denying this across the descriptions of the Day. It states that no soul can be of help to another, that no ransom will be accepted, and that intercessors will not be heard in the cases it describes. The verse above gives the same denial in two words. The effect is to make the accounting instantaneous in a certain sense: the person arrives and the matter is settled, with no interval between the arrival and the consequence. The Book offers no schedule for the Day, and this verse tells the reader that the arrangements he may imagine for deferring its outcome do not exist.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Here**

The placement of the verse follows the pattern of the Book's treatments of final rejection, where a description of the settled condition is stated before the offer of a way out. The sūrah states the permanence first, and the provision for repentance comes after it.

That order makes the offer of return the reader's destination rather than an interruption. A person reading through the passage meets the sentence about what cannot be lightened, and then the verse that begins with those who change course and mend what they spoiled. The sequence is not a contradiction but a description of two different moments in a life, and the Book's readers are being shown which of the two they are in a position to choose. What the sūrah has done throughout this passage is to state the truth about both outcomes and leave the reader facing the choice, and this verse is the last statement of the first outcome before the door is described.

**A Statement That Stops Rather Than Argues**

The verse contains no argument and no appeal. It does not urge the reader to avoid the condition it describes, and it does not explain why the condition is just. It states what will be and stops.

The Book uses this form where it has already made the case, and the passage has made it at length. The reader has been shown a covenant accepted in the presence of witnesses, a community that turned away after believing and after acknowledging the truth of the messenger, and the guidance that could no longer reach them as before. What remains for the Book to say is what the outcome looks like, and a description of the outcome stated without commentary is what the verse provides. The following verses then describe what happens to those who return.

**The Last Word of the Sentence**

The two negations at the end of the verse leave the reader with nothing to negotiate. This is the Book's characteristic closing move in passages about the final state of the rejecters, and its effect is to concentrate attention on the one variable that remains, which is the conduct of a person still alive.

The verse is the last of the passage's statements about the consequences of abandonment, and the sentences that come after it turn to the offer that the Book makes to everyone in that position. Nothing in the sūrah's treatment suggests that the door remains open after death; what the verse suppresses is any hope within the framework it describes. What the reader takes away is a clear picture of the seriousness of the matter, and the sūrah then supplies the remedy.
