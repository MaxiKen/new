## Sūrah an-Naml 27:91

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I have only been commanded to worship the Lord of this city ˹of Mecca˺, Who has made it sacred, and to Him belongs everything. And I am commanded to be one of those who ˹fully˺ submit ˹to Him˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Closes With a Job Description**

After the judgment, the mountains and the weighing of deeds, the sūrah's last movement is a statement of what the Prophet was told to do. Not what he achieved, not what he was promised — what he was commanded.

The construction is restrictive: *innamā umirtu*, I have only been commanded. The particle limits the claim. He is not announcing a programme, a mission or a set of demands on his audience; he is reporting the contents of an instruction he received, and the instruction contains two items, worship and submission, with a third added in the next verse. Everything in the chapter's eighty-nine preceding verses is presented, at the end, as flowing from an order given to one man.

**"The Lord of This City"**

God could have been named in any number of ways here, and the chapter has used many — the Almighty, the All-Knowing, the Lord of the Mighty Throne, the Lord of the worlds. The closing statement chooses a local title: the Lord of this city.

Al-Ṭabarī notes that the reference is to Mecca, while adding the necessary qualification that God is Lord of all lands. The specificity is chosen for the audience. This is the home of the idolaters being addressed, and the phrase reminds them of what they have by virtue of living there — a place where they are safe from attack in a peninsula where safety was scarce. The argument is the same one al-Quraysh makes at greater length elsewhere, pointing at the security and provision of the sanctuary and asking its beneficiaries to worship its Lord.

**"Who Has Made It Sacred"**

*Ḥarramahā* — He made it forbidden, inviolable. The word is the root behind *ḥaram*, the sacred precinct, and it names a status the Meccans already recognised and enforced.

The commentators describe what the sanctity involved. Certain acts were prohibited during the pilgrimage; the place served as a refuge for those seeking sanctuary; its trees were not to be cut and its game not hunted. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both note that the Arabs of the time observed these prohibitions, out of a persisting recognition of the sanctity of the Kaʿbah as the House built by Ibrāhīm. That is the crucial point for the verse's rhetoric. The Prophet is not asking them to accept a new claim about their city. He is pointing at an institution they already maintained and naming the one who established it.

**The Argument the Naming Makes**

Put the pieces together and a compressed argument emerges. You observe this place as inviolable. Its inviolability is not something you invented and not something you can revoke. The one who gave it that status is the one I have been commanded to worship.

This is the same method the chapter used in its six questions at verses 60 to 64. Each of those started from something the audience already accepted — that rain falls, that rivers run, that the desperate are sometimes answered — and asked who was responsible. None of them required the audience to concede a disputed premise first. The closing statement applies the technique to the most local fact available, the ground under their feet, and it is the last argument the sūrah makes.

**"To Him Belongs Everything"**

Immediately after the local title comes a universal one. He is the Lord of this city, *wa lahu kullu shay'* — and to Him belongs everything.

The pairing prevents exactly the misunderstanding the first phrase invites. Meccan religion was territorial in the ordinary ancient way, with deities attached to places and tribes holding particular gods. A claim about the Lord of this city, standing alone, would have been intelligible within that system and would have changed nothing. The second clause removes it from the system. The one whose sanctuary this is owns everything else too, which means the city is not His domain but an instance of it. The chapter made the same move with Sheba's throne, where the hoopoe's report of a mighty throne was answered by the mention of a greater one.

**Submission Named as a Command**

The second item in the instruction is that he be among the *muslimīn*, those who submit. It is put as a command rather than as a description, which is worth attending to.

The word has appeared twice before in this chapter, both times at decisive moments. Sulaymān's letter to Sheba ended by summoning her to come as submitters. Her declaration at the palace floor ended with her saying she submitted, with Sulaymān, to the Lord of the worlds. Now at the chapter's close the Prophet is commanded to be one of the same company. The term links the three figures across the sūrah — a king, a foreign queen and the man reciting — and puts them in the same category rather than in a hierarchy. He is not commanded to lead the submitters. He is commanded to be among them.

**Ending With Obedience Rather Than Authority**

There is a shape to this that the chapter has earned. An-Naml is full of commanding figures: a king with armies of jinn and men, a queen with a council, a court that sentenced a prophet. Its closing verse gives its own protagonist no command to issue.

What he is given is an account of orders received, and both of them concern his own conduct rather than anyone else's. He must worship; he must be among those who submit. The audience is told this rather than told to do anything themselves. That indirection is deliberate and it is consistent with what the previous verses established — he cannot make the deaf hear, the judgment is not his to deliver, and grieving over the refusal is to be set aside. A messenger whose responsibility has been narrowed that far has, at the end, only his own obedience to report.
