## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:78

> Do they not know that Allah ˹fully˺ knows their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks, and that Allah is the Knower of all unseen?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Private Speech Is Still Within Allah's Knowledge**

The verse asks whether the hypocrites know that Allah knows their thoughts, secret conversations, and all unseen things. Their public oaths are not the full record. The private discussion in which they mock, plan, or encourage one another is also known. No locked room, whispered message, or hidden motive lies outside Allah's awareness.

This knowledge is a warning to the dishonest and a comfort to the sincere. The hypocrite cannot protect himself by controlling the public version of events. The sincere person who is falsely accused can trust that Allah knows what happened even when no human witness is available. Divine knowledge is not selective attention; it includes the words people conceal and the intentions beneath the words they reveal.

**The Unseen Includes What Has Not Yet Appeared**

The Qur'anic idea of the unseen includes realities beyond present sense, future events, and hidden aspects of existing things. Allah's knowledge is therefore not limited to what has already become public. He knows what a private plan will produce, what a concealed resentment is preparing, and what a person's heart would choose if circumstances changed.

This does not mean that human beings possess the unseen. They must not claim to know another person's final state or invent hidden motives without evidence. The verse belongs to Allah's knowledge. The believer acts on what is clear and leaves the unseen judgment to Him. That humility prevents suspicion from becoming a religious virtue.

**Secret Conversation Can Be Worship or Corruption**

A private conversation is not automatically blameworthy. People need counsel, comfort, planning, and confidential care. The moral question is what the privacy serves. A secret used to protect a vulnerable person can be mercy. A secret used to mock the Prophet, spread falsehood, or coordinate harm becomes evidence of corruption.

The source tradition contrasts the hypocrites' private talk with the private remembrance of virtuous people. This contrast is important. Privacy removes the pressure of an audience, so it reveals what a person values when applause is unavailable. The believer should cultivate hidden worship, honest prayer, and confidential acts of care. The private self should not be a place where public faith goes to die.

**Knowing Allah Sees Changes the Use of Silence**

The servant may choose silence for wisdom, but he cannot use silence to create a realm beyond accountability. A person who says nothing publicly may still be responsible for the plan he supports privately. Conversely, a person who lacks the power to speak publicly may still be faithful through private refusal, prayer, and truthful counsel.

Awareness of Allah's sight makes privacy cleaner. It prevents the believer from using anonymity as permission to insult, deceive, or exploit. It also gives him courage to do good when nobody will praise it. The hidden act has an audience: Allah, who knows the secret before it becomes an action.

**Thoughts Need Discipline Before They Become Speech**

The verse mentions thoughts as well as conversations. Not every passing thought is a chosen belief, but cherished thoughts shape speech and action. Envy may appear uninvited; the servant becomes responsible when he feeds it, justifies it, and turns it into a plan against another person.

The believer disciplines the inner life through remembrance, truthful self-examination, and refusal to rehearse contempt. He asks Allah to replace suspicion with evidence and resentment with justice. This work may never be visible to people, but Allah sees it. Hidden obedience is as real as hidden corruption, and the unseen Judge knows which one the heart is choosing.

**Divine Knowledge Makes Repentance Urgent**

Because Allah already knows, repentance does not require informing Him of a secret. It requires ending the secret's power over the servant. The person stops the harmful conversation, admits the lie to the people affected, and returns to obedience. He does not wait for public exposure before changing.

The verse's question is therefore an invitation to wakefulness. Do they not know? The believer answers with conduct. He lives as someone whose messages, private rooms, and unspoken motives are within Allah's knowledge. That awareness produces neither despair nor performance. It produces sincerity: worship when unseen, truth when inconvenient, and humility before the One who knows everything hidden.

The knowledge of the unseen also prevents the servant from worshipping secrecy. Privacy can protect sincerity, but secrecy cannot make an unlawful act safe. A hidden kindness may be more valuable because it is free from applause; a hidden insult may be more dangerous because it is rehearsed without correction. The believer gives his private life to Allah through prayer, restraint, and honest thought. He does not demand that people know every struggle, but he also does not use their ignorance as permission to continue a wrong. Allah knows the secret, and that knowledge is enough to make the secret morally real.

The believer can use this awareness to build a private life that is better than his public image. He prays when nobody is watching, refuses a hidden wrong when it would be easy to commit, and speaks well of people who cannot reward him. These acts are not escapes from society. They are the roots of trustworthy public conduct. A person whose private speech is worship is less likely to use public religion as a mask. Allah knows every unseen thing, so the servant asks that what Allah knows of him be better than what people suppose.

No private room is beyond the sight of the One who knows the unseen.

That awareness makes hidden worship meaningful and hidden wrongdoing frightening.
