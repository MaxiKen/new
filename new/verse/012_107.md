## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:107

> Do they feel secure that an overwhelming torment from Allah will not overtake them, or that the Hour will not take them by surprise when they least expect ˹it˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Questions About a Sense of Safety**

The verse does not ask whether people are right or wrong in what they believe. It asks whether they feel safe. The rhetorical form is characteristic of the Qur'an's closing arguments, and the two questions cover two different kinds of ending: a punishment that overtakes a people within this world, and an Hour that arrives without warning and finds them unprepared. The verse's target is not ignorance but composure — the settled assumption that nothing will interrupt the present arrangement. After diagnosing inattention in verse 105 and mixed belief in verse 106, the chapter identifies the emotional condition that sustains both, and it identifies that condition as a feeling rather than as a position. That is what makes the questions unanswerable in the affirmative. Nobody can guarantee that a punishment will not come from above or from below, and nobody can guarantee the reverse either; what people actually hold is an unexamined expectation of continuity. The verse addresses the expectation directly, which is why it is framed as a pair of questions rather than as a warning.

**"An Overwhelming Torment"**

The commentators gloss the word in two directions. Some take it as a thunderbolt, a sudden destructive stroke from above. Others read it more generally as an enveloping punishment, something that covers a people entirely so that no part of them escapes and no one is left outside it. Both readings preserve the same essential feature: the punishment is not proportionate to human defence. It is not an event that can be prepared for, negotiated with, insured against or survived by resources. The chapter's own narrative contains an instance of a different kind — a famine that no household could survive without central planning across fifteen years (12:48) — and verse 107 describes something that no planning addresses at all.

**"The Hour Suddenly, While They Are Unaware"**

The second question concerns timing and awareness together. The Qur'an states the same feature repeatedly: the Hour comes suddenly, and it finds people in a state of heedlessness. The verse specifies that the unawareness is present at the moment of arrival rather than only beforehand — they are inattentive when it happens, which means the inattention described in verse 105 is not corrected by the very event it should have prepared them for. The chapter's closing argument is thus circular in a deliberate way: signs are passed by, belief remains mixed, security is assumed, and the end arrives during the same inattention that produced the first three conditions. Nothing in the sequence interrupts the sequence. The uninterrupted sequence is the substance of the threat. A punishment arriving during a period of anxiety could be braced against; one arriving inside an ordinary day, without interruption or warning, cannot be prepared for at all. The verse describes a structure the chapter's own history keeps repeating — a boy taken from a well-digging party, a brother detained after a fair search.

**The Rhetorical Question as a Standing Form**

The Qur'an uses this form of address in several places, asking whether people feel secure from a punishment descending on them or from the earth swallowing them, or whether they feel secure that the Hour will not come upon them suddenly. The repetition establishes the question as a permanent feature of the message rather than a local threat addressed to one town in one decade. Verse 107 belongs to that series, and its placement at the end of the longest continuous narrative in the Qur'an gives it particular weight: after a hundred verses of a story in which every human plan was overtaken by an arrangement nobody designed, the reader is asked whether he feels safe inside his own plans.

**Security as the Verse's Central Word**

The chapter has used the same noun twice in the immediately preceding verses, and both uses point in the opposite direction. Joseph invited his family to enter Egypt in security, with the divine exception attached to the promise (12:99), and the security he offered was given rather than assumed. Verse 107 asks whether an audience that has received no such invitation nevertheless feels secure. The contrast is between a safety conferred by God on a family that had lived for decades in fear and under a false report, and a safety claimed for itself by people who have passed by the signs. The chapter does not draw the comparison explicitly; it places the two uses of the word eleven verses apart.

**Why the Question Follows the Story**

The narrative has just demonstrated, over a hundred verses, that nothing in it went as its participants planned. The brothers intended an ending and got another. Zulaykhā intended a seduction and produced a confession before her sovereign. A cupbearer forgot a message and remembered it years later. A king's dream produced a government. Joseph asked to die submitting rather than to keep any of what he had been given (12:101). Verse 107 turns that demonstration outward and asks whether the reader has drawn any conclusion about his own arrangements. The question is not supported by argument inside the verse; it is supported by the preceding story, which is the chapter's chosen method of making it. The support is empirical on the chapter's own terms. Every reversal in this narrative reached the people affected without warning: the brothers did not expect Joseph in Egypt, the cupbearer did not expect to remember him, Jacob did not expect the caravan, and the household did not expect the shirt to work. The verse asks whether its readers are safe from the same pattern.

**The Verse Leads Into an Invitation**

Verse 108 answers the questions with a command to declare a way publicly. The sequence is therefore not threat followed by silence but threat followed by an offer. The chapter's closing movement runs: signs passed by, belief mixed, security assumed — and then, say, this is my way, I invite to God with insight. The invitation comes after the questions rather than instead of them, which means the reader is asked to consider his own position before being told what is on offer. The arrangement is the sūrah's method throughout: establish the condition, then supply the response, and let the response be heard by someone who has already recognised himself in the condition.

Neither question is answered inside the verse, and the silence is deliberate. Whether an overwhelming punishment will overtake a people and whether the Hour will arrive without warning are matters the text does not settle for its audience; it asks them to consider what their composure assumes. The chapter has just narrated a famine that arrived on a predicted date and was survived only because one man had been told about it in advance. That is the nearest thing in the sūrah to an answer, and it points in the direction the questions imply: warnings are given, the warnings are specific, and the difference between the saved and the lost in this story was whether anyone listened.
