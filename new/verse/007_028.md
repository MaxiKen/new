## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:28

> Whenever they commit a shameful deed, they say, “We found our forefathers doing it and Allah has commanded us to do it.” Say, “No! Allah never commands what is shameful. How can you attribute to Allah what you do not know?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Excuse of the Forefathers**

The verse reports the answer that the audience gives when it is confronted with its shameful practices: that it found its forefathers following them and that God has commanded them. The two claims are joined in a single sentence, and the sūrah's answer addresses both. The Book records the same excuse in several passages, and the sūrah of the Cow, the sūrah of the Table, the sūrah of the Prophets, and the sūrah of Luqmān all report the disbelievers answering the call to what God has sent down with the claim that they follow what they found their fathers upon (2:170; 5:104; 21:52–53; 31:21).

The excuse is offered as a justification rather than as an explanation, and the sūrah treats it as the central obstacle to the audience's acceptance of the message. The practice of the ancestors is treated as carrying the authority of custom and precedent, and the verse in front of us shows the audience placing that authority above the revelation. The Book's answer to the claim appears in the same verses that report it, and the answer is that the Book is a better guide than the practices that were inherited.

**The Shameful Deed in Question**

The practice the verse names is described as a shameful deed, and the term covers the sins that are particularly abominable as well as sexual transgression. The commentary tradition connects the verse with the pre-Islamic practice of circumambulating the sanctuary without clothing, and it treats the passage as one of several in the sūrah that address the rituals of the pilgrimage. The sūrah's instruction to the children of Adam to wear their adornment at every place of worship follows a few verses later, and the two passages belong together.

The Book's lists of the shameful deeds include the same term in the sūrah of the Cattle, where the forbidden things are named in sequence, and in the sūrah of the Night Journey, where the list includes the prohibition of approaching indecency. The verse in front of us reports a practice that the audience defended as sacred, and the answer states that God does not command what is shameful.

**The Claim That God Commanded It**

The second half of the excuse attributes the practice to God, and the sūrah's answer rejects the attribution outright. The sūrah of the Cattle reports the same claim from the audience, that if God had willed they would not have associated partners with Him, and it answers the claim in its own terms. The Book's warnings against fabricating lies about God appear throughout, and the sūrah of the Heights names those who do so among the greatest wrongdoers in its later verses.

The claim in front of us is a version of the reasoning that the sūrah's narrative of the adversary has already illustrated, since the adversary also asserted something about God's intention to support his own case. The Book's answer in both instances is that God has not commanded the thing, and the sūrah's following verse supplies the positive statement of what God has commanded instead.

**The Two Questions in the Answer**

The answer given in the verse contains two parts, and the second is a question about how the audience can attribute to God what it does not know. The question exposes the absence of any evidence for the claim, and it places the burden of proof on those who make it. The Book uses the same form of challenge in its arguments about the idols, and the sūrah of the Cattle asks whether the audience has any knowledge to produce.

The sūrah's treatment of knowledge and its absence runs through the chapter's arguments, and the verse in front of us applies the distinction to the practice of attributing commands to God. The audience claims to know what God has commanded, and the sūrah states that it does not know.

**The Authority of the Ancestors**

The excuse of the ancestors has a social dimension, and the Book's narratives of the prophets show the same excuse being offered by the communities that were destroyed. The people of Noah, ʿĀd, and Thamūd each defended their practice by reference to what their fathers had done, and the sūrah's accounts describe the destruction that followed their refusal. The verse in front of us places the Makkan audience in the same position as those communities.

The Book's answer to the authority of custom rests on the revelation that comes to each community, and the sūrah's third verse has already commanded the audience to follow what has been sent down and not to take protectors besides God. The verse in front of us shows the audience preferring the authority of its fathers to the revelation, and the sūrah's next verses state what the revelation commands.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands twenty-eighth in the chapter, and it follows the warning about the deception of the enemy and precedes the statement of what God has commanded. The passage as a whole addresses the children of Adam through the practices of their society, and the sequence of the verses moves from the account of the fall to the correction of the audience's customs.

The reader who has been told about the enemy's methods is now shown the methods at work in the arguments of his own community. The practices defended in front of us are answered by the commands that the next verses state, and the sūrah's argument proceeds from the exposure of the excuse to the statement of the obligation.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse gives the Prophet the command to say that his Lord has commanded justice, that the people should set their faces toward Him at every place of worship, and that they should call upon Him with sincere devotion, and it adds that just as they were first brought into being they will be brought back (7:29). The false attribution in front of us is answered by a positive statement of what God has commanded.

The two verses together present the exchange that the sūrah conducts with its audience: the excuse is given, the attribution is rejected, and the command is stated. The reader is left with the list of what God has actually required.
