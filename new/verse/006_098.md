## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:98

> And He is the One Who originated you all from a single soul, then assigned you a place to live and another to ˹be laid to˺ rest. We have already made the signs clear for people who comprehend.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Single Soul as the Origin of Humanity**

And He is the One Who originated you all from a single soul. The sentence introduces a new sign after the catalogue of the sky, and it turns from the heavens to the origin of human beings themselves. The Book states the creation from a single soul in several sūrahs: the sūrah of the Women opens with the command to be mindful of the Lord who created humanity from one soul and made its mate from it (4:1); the sūrah of the Heights gives the same account with the detail that the mate was made so that he might find comfort in her (7:189); and the sūrah of the Troops states it as the first item in a passage about the formation of human beings in the wombs (39:6).

The claim serves two arguments at once. It states God's power to originate a species from one beginning, which the sūrah uses as evidence for the power to raise the dead, and it states the common origin of all people, which the Book uses to deny the claims of superior descent that the sūrah has been dismantling throughout this chapter. The sūrah of the Chambers draws the conclusion explicitly when it says that God made people into nations and tribes so that they might know one another, and that the most noble among them is the most mindful (49:13).

**The Two Places Assigned**

Then assigned you a place to live and another to be laid to rest. The Book's commentators offered several readings of the two terms. Some took them as the womb and the grave, which are the two stations a person passes through; others took them as the settled dwelling and the place of rest in the earth; others applied the pair to the living and the dead among human beings; and some gave them a broader sense of the stations of the present life and the Hereafter.

What the readings share is a picture of human life as movement between two stations appointed by God. The sūrah has just described the soul taken at night and returned by day (6:60) and the angels taking the soul at death (6:61), and the pair of places in this verse continues the same account of a life that is measured out from beginning to end. The audience is being told that neither the arrival nor the departure is in its own hands.

**The Third Closing Formula**

We have already made the signs clear for people who comprehend. The passage that began with the seed, the dawn, the night, the sun, the moon, and the stars now carries a third qualification of its audience, and the word used here describes a kind of understanding that grasps relationships and draws conclusions. The previous verses addressed people who know, and this one addresses people who comprehend, while the verse that follows addresses people who believe.

The progression is worth noticing because it shows what the Book expects of its readers. The signs are not merely to be observed; they are to be understood in their connections, and the understanding is meant to produce belief. The sūrah's method in this section has been to present one ordinary phenomenon after another and to let the accumulation do the work, and the closing formulas mark the stages of reception.

**The Argument for Resurrection in the Origin of Life**

The sūrah has used several arguments for the resurrection in the course of its first hundred verses, and this verse's contribution comes from the beginning of human life. The sūrah of the Romans states the same case in a compact form, saying that He who originates the creation will repeat it, and that this is easy for Him. The sūrah also uses the revived earth, the seed that splits, and the daily return of consciousness, and each of these arguments works by pointing to a repetition the audience already accepted.

The argument from the single soul is the deepest of the series because it concerns the origin of the reader himself. The Book asks a person to consider that he was brought into being from a beginning he did not manage, and it treats the second bringing-forth as a smaller matter than the first, since the power that performed the first has already been demonstrated.

**What the Verse Says About Human Equality**

The sentence also states a fact about the human family that the sūrah's audience needed to hear. The Makkans divided their society by clan, wealth, and lineage, and the sūrah had already told the Prophet not to dismiss the poor believers at the request of the notables (6:52) and had described the differences of rank as a test (6:53). The claim that all people come from one soul cuts across every division the city maintained.

That claim is the ground on which the Book builds its repeated rejection of ancestral pride, and the sūrah has applied the same reasoning to the prophets in the catalogue of the previous verses, where the guidance was a gift given to men from different families and lands. The verse in front of us supplies the principle: one origin, one Lord, and a standing determined by mindfulness rather than by descent.

**The Series Continues**

The next verse adds the rain and the orchards to the catalogue of signs, and it does so with a wealth of detail about what the rain produces. The sūrah's method in this passage is to move from what the audience could observe in the sky to what they could handle in the field, and the closing formula shifts once more, so that the signs are described as clear for people who believe.

The whole series moves toward that last description. The knowledge of the sky, the comprehension of the human origin, and the observation of the harvest are all steps on a road whose destination is the community that accepts what the signs say about their giver.
