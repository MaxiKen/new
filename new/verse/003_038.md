## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:38

> Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord, saying, “My Lord! Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring. You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Then and There" — What Prompted the Prayer**

The verse's opening words tie the supplication to what the previous scene contained. Zachariah has just seen a girl supplied without a supplier, and his response is not a remark about provision in general. He asks for a child of his own, which means the scene in Mary's room acted on him as evidence that the ordinary rules were not the only rules in operation.

The sequence is worth naming because it is the sūrah's method. A person sees an act of God, and the act produces a request. Nothing in the text suggests that Zachariah reasoned his way to the conclusion that a barren marriage could be repaired; what he saw gave him the ground to ask, and the Book records the asking as the natural response to the sight.

**A Supplication Made in Old Age**

The fuller account of Zachariah's situation appears elsewhere in the Book, and it includes his own admission of age and the barrenness of his wife, along with his fear about what would become of the people after him. The prayer here is the brief form of a long anxiety: a man who has served the sanctuary his whole life and whose service will end with him unless God intervenes.

That the Book gives the story its full length in one place and its short form here is a lesson in how revelation uses its material. The compressed version keeps the essential posture — a servant asking — and the expanded version supplies the circumstances. The reader who knows both hears the fuller history behind the three words of the petition, and the reader who knows only this passage still receives the whole shape of the request.

**"Righteous Offspring" — the Adjective That Makes the Request Precise**

Zachariah does not ask for a son. He asks for a *good* offspring, using a word that covers soundness, purity, and pleasantness. The precision is deliberate, since a man in his position could have asked for an heir to continue a line, and an heir is not the same thing as a good heir.

The Book's other requests for children carry similar qualifiers: the servants of the Merciful ask for spouses and offspring who will be a comfort to them, and Abraham's prayer for a righteous son is answered with the announcement of a forbearing boy. Across these passages a pattern emerges in which the quality of the child is the substance of the prayer and the child's existence is the mechanism. A man who asked for an heir and received a tyrant would have received what he asked for and lost what he wanted.

**"By Your Grace" — the Word From His Presence**

The petition carries the phrase *min ladunka*, from Your presence, the same construction used by the youths of the cave asking for mercy from their Lord and by Moses finding a servant taught knowledge from God's own presence (18:10; 18:65). The word describes something given from inside the giver rather than passed along a chain.

Asking for a child from God's own presence is the language of a man who has stopped considering the available means. He is not asking for the ordinary process to be improved or for his wife's condition to change; he is asking for the gift as a gift, without a mechanism, which is what the phrase conveys. The Book later speaks of a knowledge given from God's presence, and the same idiom has continued in Islamic devotional language for what comes to a servant without an intermediary.

**"The Hearer of All Prayers" — a Name Chosen for the Moment**

The prayer closes with a name that fits a supplication like no other: the Hearer of prayer. The Book uses it where the act of asking is the subject, and the choice is exact, since a man asking for something that cannot happen by ordinary means has nothing to rely on but the fact that the request has been heard.

The name also supplies the ground of the request's answer. The confidence in the prayer is not in Zachariah's standing as a priest, nor in the merit accumulated over years of service, and the text does not mention either. What he brings is a request made to one who hears, and the name is the whole of his argument.

**Why the Vivid Story Comes Before the Bare Petition**

Read as a passage, the sequence has been building to this point without announcing it. A mother made a vow before the birth; her daughter was accepted; the daughter was given provision that had no explanation; and the priest who cared for her then asked for a child from the same source.

The order matters for the reader's understanding of prayer. The Book does not begin with instruction about how to ask; it shows one household's requests being answered, and then gives the reader a prayer in the same setting. Faith in this sūrah is taught by narrated experience rather than by definition, which is why the whole passage works as a single movement from one woman's vow to one man's petition.

**The Answer That Belongs to the Next Scene**

The petition will be answered from the sanctuary itself, in an announcement that the following verse reports. What this verse establishes is the posture in which the answer arrived: an old man standing at prayer, asking for something that had no available cause, and addressing the one who hears.

The prayer is preserved for the reader as a model, and the sūrah leaves it in that position on purpose. A request made in a situation that appears closed, by a person with no means, is not a failure of realism in this Book; it is the normal form of supplication, and the narrative around it has already shown the reader what happens to such requests.
