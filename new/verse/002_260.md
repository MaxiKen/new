## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:260

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead.” Allah responded, “Do you not believe?” Abraham replied, “Yes I do, but just so my heart can be reassured.” Allah said, “Then bring four birds, train them to come to you, ˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops. Then call them back, they will fly to you in haste. And ˹so you will˺ know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet's Request**

"And when Abraham said, 'My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead.'" The request follows the scene of the ruined city, and the sūrah pairs the two so that the reader compares the question of the passerby with the petition of the prophet. The answer opens with a question of its own: "Do you not believe?" — which the commentators read as an affirmation rather than a rebuke, on the pattern of a teacher confirming what a student already holds.

Al-Ṭabarī collects the occasions the early authorities offered: that Abraham had seen carrion contested by creatures of sea, land, and air and wondered how the parts could be reassembled; that he wanted a sign to strengthen his argument with the ruler of v. 258; that the question belonged to his exchange about the sacrifice of his son. The reports do not fix the occasion, and the verse itself supplies none.

**"So That My Heart May Be at Peace"**

The crucial clause is Abraham's answer: "Yes, but so that my heart may be at peace (li-yaṭmaʾinna qalbī)." The Qur'an uses the same root in 13:28 — "surely in the remembrance of God hearts find rest" — and in 89:27 for the serene soul. The word describes a settled state, not the absence of proof.

The scholars of the community took care with this. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, in the ḥadīth of Abū Hurayrah, "We have more right to doubt than Abraham" — a statement the commentators explain as a rhetorical argument: if doubt were possible for a prophet, the Prophet ﷺ would have more occasion for it than Abraham, and since he has none, neither had Abraham. The ḥadīth's purpose is to defend Abraham's rank against a misreading of the verse, not to report a doubt.

Al-Rāzī adds that knowledge has degrees: assent of the mind, and the tranquillity of the heart that comes with witnessing. The request asks for the second while the first is already present.

**The Four Birds and the Command**

"Take four birds and incline them to you" — the command begins with training, since the sign will end with a summons the birds must obey. The commentators explain that Abraham was to make them familiar with him, then cut them into pieces, scatter the parts on the hills, and call them back.

The detail matters to the argument. Resurrection here is presented as a gathering (jamʿ) rather than a raw creation: the parts exist, and they are assembled. The Qur'an makes the same distinction in 17:49–51 and 75:3–4, where the question is whether scattered bones can be collected. The verse answers in the mode of demonstration.

Al-Ṭabarī records that the birds were made so familiar with Abraham that they came at his word before the demonstration, and the commentators read the training as part of the sign: the summons at the end of the sequence is answered because the summons was the same voice that had called them from the first. The scattering on separate hills removes any natural explanation, since no ordinary cause gathers cut flesh from distant slopes; what answers is the call itself.

**The Call Answered**

The command's conclusion is stated in advance: "they will come to you in haste." The obedience of the created order to the call of the One who gave the command runs through the sūrah — the same motif appears in 41:11 with the heavens and the earth answering "we come willingly." The sign is not finally about birds; it is about the relation between the summons and the response.

The scholars also noted that the demonstration used Abraham's own hands and voice. The prophet is not a spectator of his own proof; he trains the birds, scatters them, calls, and watches them return.

The Qur'an describes the response of the created order to the divine command in the same terms elsewhere, as when the heavens and the earth answer that they come willingly (41:11) and when the angels do what they are commanded without hesitation (16:50). The scholars of the community read the birds' return in this verse as a recorded event rather than as a figure of speech, and the conclusion of the sequence — Abraham calling and the parts reassembling and flying to him — is the demonstration that his petition had asked to see.

**"Mighty, Wise"**

The verse closes with ʿazīz and ḥakīm — Mighty and Wise. The first name answers the doubt about power, the second the question about how: the arrangement of the demonstration was not random but ordered, a visible sign in which the pieces are cut, separated, and then rejoined. The whole narrative of the verse is designed to show that the "how" is precisely what the viewer is allowed to see.

**Knowledge That Grows**

The Book commands the Prophet ﷺ to pray for increase of knowledge — "My Lord, increase me in knowledge" (20:114) — a request that would be meaningless if the knowledge of a prophet were a fixed quantity. Al-Rāzī reads the clause as the key to Abraham's petition: what the friend of God asked for was not the belief he already held but a further degree of it, which is why the answer began with "Do you not believe?"

The ḥadīth transmitted by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, in which the Prophet ﷺ says, "We have more right to doubt than Abraham," has been read by the commentators as a defence of Abraham's rank rather than an admission of doubt in either man: if any prophet could have wavered, the Prophet ﷺ would be the more likely, and since he did not, Abraham's request is shown to be obedience seeking evidence. The Sufi reading recorded by al-Rāzī follows the same line — the request was for witnessing, not for proof — and 13:28, where hearts find rest in the remembrance of God, shows what the desired tranquillity is. The verses that follow turn to the seed and the harvest, where the same God who restores birds to life multiplies a grain.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse is the sūrah's most detailed account of the resurrection, and its teaching is delivered through a request made by a prophet and answered by a demonstration he performed. The next verses take up wealth and the multiplying of reward, so that the reader moves from the multiplication of broken birds into life to the multiplication of a grain into a harvest — the same God, the same arithmetic of restoration.
