## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:260

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, "My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead." Allah responded, "Do you not believe?" Abraham replied, "Yes I do, but just so my heart can be reassured." Allah said, "Then bring four birds, train them to come to you, ˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops. Then call them back, they will fly to you in haste. And ˹so you will˺ know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question of the Friend of God**

The third life-and-death scene is a request: Abraham — the patriarch the Qur'an will call God's friend, the man who broke the idols and walked out of the fire — asks to be shown how God gives life to the dead. The verse supplies no occasion, and the commentators record the possibilities the tradition gathered: he had seen, on a seashore, the creatures of sea and land and air feeding on one another, and wondered how resurrection would reassemble pieces scattered through so many bodies; or the request grew from his debate with the king, the demonstration lending his message force; or it connects to the sacrifice of his son; or it seeks confirmation of his own station. The plurality is the verse's first teaching: the how-question is legitimate wherever it arises, and the Qur'an preserves it in the mouth of the last person whose faith needed propping. The divine response treats the question accordingly — not as an accusation to answer but as a faith to confirm: *Do you not believe?*

**The Answered Answer**

*I do — but just so my heart can be reassured.* The commentators hear the exchange as an implicit affirmation wearing the form of a question: you believe, then — so the asking is not doubt's asking. What Abraham names is the heart's dimension: faith held in the mind seeking settlement in the breast. The tradition's vocabulary for the sought state is rich and the verse's word — yaṭmaʾinna, be at peace, be settled — is the same root as the sūrah of the Dawn's invitation, *O tranquil soul, return to your Lord* (89:27), and the sūrah of Thunder's axiom, *in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort* (13:28). Al-Rāzī's reconciliation of the patriarch's asking with the patriarch's peace became the standard reading: spiritual knowledge has degrees, and Abraham is describing passage from one depth of understanding to the next — not hesitation about God's existence or power but the desire, no believer ever finishes having, to witness more of God's self-disclosure. The Prophetic verdict on the whole scene is the famous sentence in the ḥadīth collections — we are more entitled to doubt than Abraham — which defends the patriarch by relocating the question: if this asking counts as doubt, the doubt belongs to everyone before it belongs to him; and in truth it was not doubt but an entreaty for more knowledge. The Qur'an commands the same entreaty of its own Prophet: *My Lord, increase me in knowledge* (20:114) — a prayer that highlights not his ignorance, the commentators note, but God's limitlessness.

**The Four Birds**

The demonstration is ordered in a sequence of imperatives, and the sequence's second step is the tradition's great grammatical debate. *Take four birds and make them inclined to you* — the verb, in the majority reading, means to cut them up, though al-Rāzī records the difficulty the prepositional phrase then carries (cut them *to you*?). On the majority reading the rite runs: four birds — the commentaries list peacock, eagle, raven, and rooster, with other lists preserved — are cut into pieces, the pieces distributed on four mountains, read as the four cardinal directions, and then called. What is called reassembles: the scattered parts return into their forms and come flying, in haste, to the caller. On the second reading, recorded as the translation's alternative, no killing occurs at all: Abraham first draws each bird to himself — tames them, habituates them — then stations them alive on the mountains, then calls them home. The reading transforms the demonstration from reassembly into return: the resurrection pictured as the reunification of souls and bodies with the ease of a man calling birds that come running to the voice they know. Both readings end at the same verse-word — *in haste* — and both make the call, not the cutting, the demonstration's center.

**The Birds of the Self**

The allegorical reading of the four is one of the commentary's most transmitted: the birds are the soul's four — the eagle, lust for food; the rooster, lust for sex; the raven, excessive curiosity; the peacock, vanity — and the rite is their scattering and recall, the self's appetites dispersed on the mountains and summoned back under the caller's command. Al-Rāzī preserves the reading while noting that the commentaries carry many different bird-lists, each generation assigning its own quartet of vices. The spiritual interpretation follows the verse's own invitation: resurrection demonstrated on birds becomes resurrection demonstrated on the heart — the revival of dead hearts through the Spirit, as the gloss has it — and the haste of the returning birds becomes the appetite's speed when the purified will calls it. The four-mountains geography serves the reading: the cardinal directions are the self's four quarters, and the caller standing at the center is the heart obeyed at last by what it feeds. The literal demonstration and the allegorical one are not rival accounts but the same sign at two scales — the bodies reassembling on the hills, the soul's birds reassembling in the breast.

**The Most Hopeful Verse**

The scene's closing gift is not in the demonstration but in its manner. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said of Abraham's question and God's gentle response to it: there is no more hopeful verse in the Qur'an than this one. The hope is grammatical: the patriarch asks to see, and God does not rebuke the asking — He answers it, supplies the birds, the mountains, the call, and ends the instruction with the knowledge it was meant to produce: *and know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise*. The conversation partner of Ibn ʿAbbās offered his own candidate for the most hopeful verse — the sūrah of the Throngs' proclamation: *O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins* (39:53) — and the tradition keeps both nominations, each marking a different frontier of hope: the forgiveness that exceeds the sin, and the answering that exceeds the question. Between them the verse's own promise is the quieter one — that the heart's need for reassurance is not a defect of faith but one of its appetites, and that God's response to the appetite, in the case of His friend, was not patience but a demonstration.

**Almighty, All-Wise**

The two closing names seal the rite's double character. ʿAzīz — the Almighty — is the power the demonstration displays: the pieces recalled, the dead reassembled, the mountains obeyed by what flies between them; the might is shown not as force but as summons, the omnipotence whose instrument here is a voice calling birds. Ḥakīm — the All-Wise — is the demonstration's design: the four and the mountains and the haste, the choice to answer a how-question with a how-answer, the pedagogy that lets the seeker perform the resurrection he asked about with his own hands and his own call. The pair also answers the verse's opening question by refusing it: *do you not believe?* was not asked because the belief was doubtful but because the wisdom wanted the belief to have a body — a rite, a scene, a memory of birds in haste. Abraham asked to see how God gives life to the dead; he was shown how by being made the one who calls. The verse's final word to its own reader is the same instruction in the same names: the how is knowable, the knowing is given, and what is asked of the knower is the call — and the certainty that follows it, that God is Almighty, All-Wise.
