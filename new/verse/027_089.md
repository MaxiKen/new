## Sūrah an-Naml 27:89

> Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded with what is better, and they will be secure from the horror on that Day.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Arriving With Something**

The verb is *jā'a bi* — to come bringing. The image is of a person arriving at the judgment carrying what he has, and the previous verses have already established the setting: a summons, a terror, and a gathering at which everyone comes humbled.

That framing tells us what a deed is in this passage. It is not a mark on a record maintained elsewhere but something the person brings with him, the way a traveller brings goods. The chapter has used arrival imagery repeatedly — envoys arriving with a gift, a queen arriving at a palace, contingents brought before their Lord. Here the whole of a life is compressed into what a person is holding when he gets there.

**What the Good Deed Is**

Al-Qurṭubī identifies the *ḥasanah* specifically: testifying that there is no god but God, and believing in Him. He supports it with a report that this testimony is the best of good things.

The identification narrows the verse considerably and it fits the chapter. An-Naml's entire argument has been about the object of worship — the hoopoe's report concerned a nation prostrating to the sun, the six questions each ended by asking whether there is a god besides God, and the queen's turning point was the sentence in which she submitted with Sulaymān. If the sūrah has been driving at one deed, it is this one. The reading is not the only available one, since *ḥasanah* in Arabic covers any good act, but al-Qurṭubī's is well anchored in the chapter's own concerns.

**"Better Than It"**

The reward is described only comparatively: *khayrun minhā*, better than it. Nothing is specified, and the commentators have offered several candidates for what the improvement consists of.

Al-Rāzī names knowledge of God in this world and the vision of God in the Hereafter. Al-Qurṭubī mentions the divine contentment, and also the arithmetic reading, in which what is better refers to the multiplication the Qur'an describes elsewhere — a good deed returned tenfold. Al-Ṭabarī offers the simplest and most contextual answer: what is better is precisely the security from terror named in the second half of this verse. That last reading has the advantage of not requiring anything from outside the sentence.

**Security as the Named Reward**

Whatever else is included, the verse states one benefit outright: *hum min fazaʿin yawma'idhin āminūn* — they are secure from the terror of that Day. The word for terror is the one used two verses earlier for the reaction to the trumpet.

The connection is direct. Verse 87 said everyone in the heavens and the earth would be terrified except whom God wills, and left the exception unidentified. This verse supplies a criterion. The commentators who guessed at martyrs or named angels were filling a gap that the chapter closes three verses later with a general rule rather than a list of persons. Whoever comes bringing a good deed is secure — which is a more useful answer than a roster, because it is a category a reader can join.

**Security Rather Than Reward**

Consider what kind of benefit is named first. Not a garden, not rivers, not companions — the imagery the Qur'an uses elsewhere at length. The first thing said about the outcome is the absence of fear.

That choice suits the passage it sits in. The preceding verses have described a sound that terrifies everyone alive, mountains dissolving, and a crowd arriving abased. Against that, the most valuable thing available is not an addition but a subtraction. The chapter made the same move with Lūṭ's household, whose deliverance was described simply as being brought out before the rain. An-Naml's picture of salvation is consistently extraction from a situation rather than elevation above one.

**A Hint About Punishment's Limits**

Al-Rāzī draws a further inference from the verse, and it touches a long-running theological dispute. If whoever brings a good deed receives what is better, then people of faith, even if punished in the Hereafter, will not be punished forever.

The reasoning runs through the comparative. A permanent penalty could not be described as receiving something better than the good one brought, so the good deed must retain some effect. Others resisted the inference, holding that the verse describes those whose record qualifies them outright. The argument is a genuine one and it is not settled by this verse alone; what an-Naml supplies is a premise that the more merciful reading uses. [UNVERIFIED: al-Rāzī's inference that believers' punishment is temporary is a theological argument drawn from this verse, not a statement the verse makes.]

**The Verse Pairs With the Next**

This sentence and the one after it are a matched set, and neither is complete alone. One deed brought, one outcome; another deed brought, another outcome. The symmetry is exact in structure and deliberately unbalanced in tone.

The good case is stated in seventeen Arabic words with a named benefit and an unspecified improvement. The evil case, in the next verse, is stated more briefly and ends with a question rather than a description. That asymmetry is consistent throughout the Qur'an's treatment of the two outcomes, where the return for good is described as multiplied and the return for evil as equivalent. The chapter's own statement of the principle arrives in the following verse's closing question.

**Whoever — With No Qualifier**

The pronoun is open: *man jā'a bi'l-ḥasanah*, whoever comes with a good deed. No nation is named, no lineage, no prior standing. The rule is stated in the most general terms Arabic allows.

That generality matters at this point in the chapter. The judgment scene of verses 83 and 84 emphasised that contingents are drawn from every community, which removed the protection of belonging. This verse removes the same barrier from the other direction: if membership does not condemn, neither does it save, and what counts is what a person arrives holding. Sheba's queen is the chapter's proof that the category is open. She came from a nation that worshipped the sun, ruled it, and ended by declaring her submission to the Lord of the worlds along with Sulaymān.
