## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:146

> For those who are Jewish, We forbade every animal with undivided hoofs and the fat of oxen and sheep except what is joined to their backs or intestines or mixed with bone. In this way We rewarded them for their violations. And We are certainly truthful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Restrictions Placed on the Jewish Communities**

For those who are Jewish, We forbade every animal with undivided hoofs and the fat of oxen and sheep except what is joined to their backs or intestines or mixed with bone. The verse turns from the law of the community addressed in the Qur'an to the restrictions that had been imposed on the Jewish communities, and it states them in detail. The animals with undivided hoofs were forbidden, and the fat of the oxen and the sheep was forbidden with the exception of the fat attached to the back, the intestines, and the bone.

The Book records the same restrictions elsewhere. In the sūrah of the Women, God states that He forbade the Jews certain foods that had been lawful to them because of their wrongdoing and because they hindered many from the way of God (4:160), and the sūrah of the Bee says that to the Jews God forbade what had been related before and that they did not wrong God but wronged themselves (16:118). What those passages describe is set out in detail here.

**The Reason Given in the Sentence**

In this way We rewarded them for their violations. The sentence states the reason for the restriction, and the Book's account of the matter is that the prohibitions were imposed as a consequence of the community's conduct. The same connection appears in that sūrah's passage about the wrongdoing of the Jews, their taking of interest, and their consuming of people's wealth unjustly (4:160–161).

The Book's treatment of the earlier laws does not present them as burdens imposed without cause. The sūrah of the Family of Imran states that all food was lawful for the children of Israel except what Israel made unlawful for himself before the Torah was revealed, and it invites the audience to bring the Torah and recite it if they are truthful (3:93). What the restriction in this verse represents is a penalty attached to a community's violations, and the Book states it as a fact of the history it recounts.

**The Testimony Attached to the Verse**

And We are certainly truthful. The sentence closes the verse with the Book's testimony about its own account, and the Book uses this form of statement when it reports matters that the audience could dispute. The sūrah has been answering the audience's claims about the dietary rules of the earlier communities, and the sentence in front of us states that the account given in the revelation is accurate.

The Book's practice of testifying to its own truthfulness appears at the end of passages that correct the audience's understanding of the earlier scriptures. The sūrah of the Cow uses the formula in its account of the covenant, and the sūrah of the Cattle itself uses a comparable statement earlier in the chapter. This declaration closes the passage on the Jewish restrictions, and the reader is left with the revelation's account rather than with the one the audience had inherited.

**What the Verse Says About the Two Communities' Laws**

The passage that includes this verse sets the community's law beside the law given to the Jewish communities, and the comparison is deliberate. The four prohibitions stated in the previous verse are few and simple, while the restrictions described here are detailed and attached to a particular history. The sūrah's audience is being shown that the burden it was being asked to carry was lighter than what had been imposed on those who came before.

The Book states the same comparison in other passages. The sūrah of the Cow describes the covenant with the children of Israel and the severity of its terms, and the sūrah of the Table describes the obligations that were laid upon them. What this verse adds is the dietary portion of that history, and its statement that the restrictions were a recompense for violations explains the difference between the two arrangements.

**The Relation of the Verse to the Audience's Practice**

The audience had built a system of its own restrictions, and the sūrah has just answered the system with the four prohibitions stated in the previous verse. Its function in the passage is to show that restrictions attached to a community's wrongdoing are not a model for other communities to copy, and it states the reason the earlier community was given a heavier law.

The Book's treatment of the people who impose restrictions that God has not imposed is direct elsewhere in the sūrah. The verse that follows the passage about the Jewish restrictions states that if the audience denies the Prophet, he should say that the Lord is infinite in mercy and that His punishment will not be averted from the wicked (6:147), and the passage after that records the polytheists' argument that God's will lay behind their practices (6:148).

**The Place of the Verse in the Sūrah**

The verse completes the sūrah's treatment of food, which has run from the command to eat of what has been slaughtered in the Name of God (6:118) through the examination of the audience's system (6:136–144) to the declaration of what is actually forbidden (6:145). The passage in front of us adds the account of the earlier community's law, and the section is complete once the comparison has been made.

The sūrah's argument in this section has followed a consistent method. The audience's practices are stated in their own terms, the revelation's ruling is given, the earlier communities' laws are explained, and the responsibility for the practices is left with the people who adopted them. The last of those elements is supplied here, and the passage that follows returns to the audience's own arguments about God's will.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse tells the Prophet that if they deny him, he should say that the Lord is infinite in mercy but that His punishment will not be averted from the wicked people (6:147). The sentence follows the account of the restrictions and the testimony attached to it, and it states the two attributes that frame the audience's position: a mercy that gives time and a punishment that is not withdrawn from those who persist.

The passage that follows records the polytheists' argument that God's will lay behind their association of partners with Him and their prohibitions, an argument the sūrah answers by asking whether they have any knowledge to produce (6:148). This sentence stands between the two, and it states the position of the community that has received the revelation and the law in its plain form.
