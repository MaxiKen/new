## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:72

> establish prayer, and be mindful of Him. To Him you will all be gathered together.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Completes the Verse Before It**

This line finishes the declaration begun in the previous verse, and the connection is grammatical as well as thematic: the sentence started there does not end until here. That is why the passage has to be read as a unit. The Prophet announces submission to the Lord of all worlds, and then states what the submission consists of: the prayer and mindfulness of God, and the gathering that awaits everyone at the end.

The practice named first is the prayer, which the Book presents as the standing obligation of every community that received a messenger. The sūrah of Ibrāhīm records Abraham asking his Lord to make him and his descendants people who maintain the prayer, and the Book's descriptions of the righteous regularly include the establishing of it. The order in this verse places the ritual act before the interior state, which is the Book's ordinary way of describing religious life: the visible practice carries the inward one with it.

**Why Prayer Comes First**

The Book treats the prayer as the marker of a community's identity and the ordinary channel of its connection with God. The sūrah of Ṭā-Hā gives the command in the first person on God's behalf, telling Moses that He is God and that there is no deity but Him, and then instructing him to worship and to establish the prayer for the remembrance of his Lord (20:14). The sūrah of the Spider states that the prayer restrains people from indecency and wrongdoing (29:45), and the sūrah of the Cow tells the believers to seek help through patience and prayer (2:45).

Those verses show what the prayer was understood to do. It is not presented as an obligation discharged for God's benefit, since the Book states that God has no need of any of His creatures. It is presented as a discipline that builds the person who performs it, which is why the sūrah of the Spider attaches moral effects to it and why this verse attaches it to submission.

**Mindfulness as the Ground of Conduct**

The second item is to be mindful of Him, and the Book's word for mindfulness is one of its central concepts. It describes a person who orders his conduct around the awareness that God is present, sees what he does, and will hold him to account. The sūrah of the Cow opens by describing the Book as guidance for those who are mindful (2:2), and the Book commands mindfulness repeatedly in connection with specific acts rather than as a general sentiment.

Al-Rāzī arranged the three items of this passage into a scheme that shows how the Book's religion fits together: submission is the best of the acts of the heart, prayer is the best of the acts of the limbs, and mindfulness is the best of the acts of restraint, since it is the quality that keeps a person from what he should avoid. The arrangement is a commentator's, but it captures the verse's balance. The passage has given the audience an allegiance, a practice, and a disposition.

**The Gathering at the End**

The sentence closes with the statement that it is unto Him that you shall be gathered, which returns to the theme the sūrah has been developing since its account of the keys of the unseen. The Book states the gathering in dozens of places, and the sūrah has already described the return of every soul to its true Master (6:62). Here the statement comes at the end of a declaration of worship, which gives it a different weight: the person who prays and lives mindfully is not performing an isolated act but travelling toward an assembly.

That is why the Book joins the command to worship with the announcement of the gathering so consistently, and why the sūrah of the Cave reports the same linkage in the words of the Prophet's declaration. The end of the journey is a fact about the future that shapes the present, and the verse states it in the same breath as the obligations that fill the present.

**The Turn the Passage Makes**

Read together, the two verses mark a turn in the sūrah. The long argument with the Makkans about idols, signs, punishment, and mockery ends with the Prophet stating what he has been commanded to do, and the statement is a declaration of commitment rather than a rebuttal. The Book uses that movement repeatedly: after a section of contention comes a statement of the worship that distinguishes the community.

The next verse continues the declaration by describing the God to whom this worship is directed, and the description is drawn from the creation itself. The heavens and the earth, the command that brings things into being, the sovereignty that belongs to God on the day the trumpet sounds, and the knowledge that covers the unseen and the seen: those are the terms in which the sūrah now describes the One the Prophet has been commanded to serve.

**The Book's Instructions About the Prayer and Mindfulness**

The Book repeats the two obligations of this verse in forms that show what it expected of the community. The believers are told to observe the prayers, especially the middle prayer, and to stand in true devotion to God (2:238); they are described among the successful as those who humble themselves in their prayer (23:2); and they are commanded to be mindful of God in the way He deserves and not to die except in a state of submission to Him (3:102). The sūrah of the Mutual Disillusion adds the concession that the obligation is measured to a person's capacity, telling the believers to be mindful of God to the best of their ability (64:16).

Those verses place the passage in the middle of the Book's practical teaching rather than at its edge. What the Prophet declares about himself in this verse is the same programme the Book lays on the community, stated in the first person before it is imposed on anyone else.
