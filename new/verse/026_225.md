## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225

> Do you not see how they rant in every field,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ranting in Every Field Means Wandering Without a Moral Center**

The verse describes poets as wandering through every valley or field. The image is not mainly about physical travel. It portrays speech that moves from claim to claim, praise to insult, certainty to reversal, without a stable commitment to truth. The speaker follows the response he wants rather than a principle he is willing to obey.

A valley can also suggest a separate direction or subject. The poet enters one field to praise a patron, another to attack an enemy, and another to entertain a crowd. The problem is not variety by itself. A truthful writer can address many subjects. The problem is the absence of a moral center that would restrain the speech in every subject.

**Imagination Without Accountability Becomes Drift**

Words can create many worlds. They can make a coward appear brave, a tyrant appear generous, or a false accusation appear heroic. If the speaker is not answerable to Allah, imagination becomes a tool for rearranging moral reality. The audience may enjoy the movement while forgetting to ask whether the movement has any destination.

The verse's question, Do you not see, asks the listener to notice the pattern rather than be captured by each performance. A new image may distract from yesterday's contradiction. A fresh target may make the audience forget that the same poet praised the opposite position when a different patron paid or a different crowd applauded.

**A Wide Range of Subjects Can Hide a Narrow Motive**

The speaker may appear versatile because he can move through politics, love, tribal memory, religion, and satire. Yet the motive can remain the same: gain attention, protect status, or defeat an enemy. Variety of subject does not prove depth of thought. It can be a way of keeping the audience from testing the speaker's consistency.

Quran 18:103–104 describes people whose efforts go astray while they think they are doing good. That parallel warns that confidence and productivity do not guarantee a sound direction. A person can produce much speech, win admiration, and still be moving away from truth if his work is detached from obedience and honest self-examination.

**The Listener Can Be Swept Along by Verbal Motion**

An audience may follow a speaker because every new valley brings a new emotion. Anger rises, pride rises, pity rises, and then the next turn prevents reflection on what came before. This is why the Quran exposes the movement itself. The listener must slow down enough to ask whether the words agree with one another and whether the life behind them supports the claims.

The warning applies to modern speech without making every modern genre equivalent to pre-Islamic poetry. Any medium can become a field in which a person wanders for applause. The responsibility remains the same: do not let fluency replace truth, and do not let a changing performance become the measure of guidance.

**A Moral Center Gives Speech Direction**

Speech guided by Allah can address many subjects without losing its center. It can praise what is true, criticize what is wrong, and use imagination without making falsehood beautiful. Its variety serves a stable purpose: remembrance, justice, and preparation for the return to Allah.

The verse therefore asks the listener to look past verbal motion. Do not be impressed simply because a speaker can occupy every field. Ask where the speech leads, whom it serves, and whether the speaker will obey the truth when obedience costs him. Movement becomes guidance only when it has a right direction.

The image of valleys also suggests exaggeration without rootedness. A speaker can describe every emotional landscape but never stand on a principle when standing becomes costly. He praises peace when the crowd wants peace and praises conflict when conflict brings applause. The words move quickly, but the person has not moved toward truth.

This instability harms the audience because it rewards attention rather than integrity. Listeners learn to follow the strongest mood in the room. One day they despise an enemy, the next day they praise him, and the day after they forget the harm caused by both reactions. The speaker's range becomes a substitute for a stable moral account.

A truthful communicator can change his judgment when new evidence appears. That is not the wandering condemned here. Correction is a sign of humility when it follows truth. The problem is changing positions to follow appetite while pretending that each new position was always held. Honest revision has reasons; verbal wandering has only momentum.

Quran 18:103–104 warns about people whose efforts go astray while they think they are doing good. The passage helps distinguish sincere energy from sound direction. Effort, productivity, and applause cannot prove that a speaker's field is right. A person may work hard for a false cause and become more attached to it because of the work already spent.

The listener therefore needs a pause between performance and response. Ask what claim is being made, what evidence supports it, and what conduct it would produce if accepted. A field of speech becomes a field of guidance when the words can survive those questions. If they require constant excitement to remain persuasive, they may be carrying the audience away from reflection.

The verse does not demand that every speaker use one tone or one subject. It demands a center that remains true across subjects. A writer can be tender and forceful, descriptive and direct, as long as Allah's truth governs the movement. The valley is not the danger; losing the road is.

A community that values this discipline will enjoy language without becoming captive to it. It will welcome imagination that illuminates justice and reject cleverness that disguises falsehood. Speech can travel widely while the heart remains anchored to Allah, and that anchored movement is different from the wandering exposed here.
