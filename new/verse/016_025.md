## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:25

> **Let them bear their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. Evil indeed is what they will bear!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bearing Burdens Fully And Partially**

Li-yaḥmilū awzārahum kāmilatan yawma al-qiyāmati wa-min awzāri al-ladhīna yuḍillūnahum bi-ghayri ʿilm — so that they may bear their burdens in full on Day of Resurrection and some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. Awzār is plural of wizr, heavy burden, sin, load that weighs down. Kāmilatan means complete, full, without reduction. Yawma al-qiyāmah is Day of Resurrection, when all stand. The verse says those who called revelation fables will bear their own sins fully, plus some of sins of those they misled without knowledge. Al-Ṭabarī explains that leaders of disbelief who called people to shirk and to calling Qur'an fables will bear their own burden of disbelief plus a share of burden of followers who followed them in ignorance. This does not mean followers' burden is reduced; followers bear their own full burden as well, because they chose to follow. Rather, misleaders bear additional burden for being cause of others' misguidance. The same principle appears in 29:13: *And they will surely bear their burdens and other burdens with their burdens* (29:13) and in prophetic saying: *Whoever calls to guidance has reward like those who follow, and whoever calls to misguidance bears sin like those who follow, without reducing their sins* (Muslim 2674).

**Without Knowledge**

Bi-ghayri ʿilm — without knowledge — describes both misleaders and misled. Misleaders mislead without knowledge, meaning their claim that revelation is fables is based on ignorance, not knowledge. They do not know that it is revelation; they assert without evidence. Those misled are also without knowledge, meaning they follow without knowledge, blind imitation. Al-Rāzī notes that bi-ghayri ʿilm is crucial: if misguidance were with knowledge, it would be even worse, but even without knowledge, responsibility remains because knowledge was available via signs, blessings, and messengers. The phrase also indicates that misleaders deceive others by presenting ignorance as knowledge: they say fables with confidence, as if they know, while they do not know. Their burden includes the sin of speaking without knowledge, which the Qur'an condemns in 16:116: *Do not say what your tongues falsely describe, this is lawful and this is forbidden, inventing lie against Allah* (16:116).

**Evil Is What They Bear**

Alā sā'a mā yazirūn — evil indeed is what they bear, what a wretched load they carry. Sā'a means evil, bad, wretched. Yazirūn from w-z-r means to bear burden, to carry load. The exclamation alā is for attention: behold, how evil is what they bear. Al-Zamakhsharī explains that this is a statement of censure: their burden is not only heavy but evil in nature, because it includes shirk, denial, mockery, and leading others astray. The burden metaphor is powerful: sins are like loads carried on back, weighing down, making journey to Hereafter difficult. On Day of Judgment, everyone carries own deeds as load. Misleaders carry extra because they added others' misguidance to their own. The image of bearing burdens also connects to earlier verse about cattle bearing athqāl, heavy loads, to distant lands without hardship. Cattle bear physical loads as mercy; misleaders bear sinful loads as punishment. One bearing is gift, other is consequence.

**Why Misleaders Bear Others' Burdens**

The principle that one bears burden of those he misleads may seem unjust at first: why should one bear another's sin? Al-Qurṭubī clarifies that it is not that sin is transferred, but that misleader earns additional sin for being cause. When a person calls to misguidance, he initiates a chain of wrongdoing that continues after him. Each person who follows commits his own sin, but the initiator also earns sin for initiating. This is similar to reward for calling to guidance: caller earns reward for each follower's good deed without reducing followers' reward. The justice is in causality: you are responsible for what you cause, not only what you do directly. The Qur'an says *That they may bear their burdens completely and some of burdens of those they mislead* — min, some, not all, indicating that misleaders bear a portion of followers' burden equal to their share in causing misguidance, not entire burden.

**The Connection To Fables Accusation**

Verse 24 said they call revelation ancient fables. Verse 25 says consequence: they will bear burdens fully and some of those they mislead without knowledge. The link is that calling revelation fables is not a private opinion; it is public misguidance that leads others astray. When a respected Makkan says Qur'an is fables, others who trust him may also dismiss it without investigating. Thus his words become cause for others' denial. The verse warns that speech has consequences beyond speaker. To label revelation as fable may seem like a small remark, but on Day of Judgment it will be a heavy load, plus loads of those who were influenced. This is why the Qur'an takes speech seriously: *Not a word does he utter but there is a watcher ready* (50:18).

**Linguistic Note On Wizr And Ḥaml**

Wizr from w-z-r means heavy burden, sin, from same root as wazīr, minister, one who bears burden of king, and wizr, mountain refuge, because it bears weight. Ḥaml from ḥ-m-l means to carry, bear, as in cattle bearing loads in 16:7. The same verb is used for both physical and sinful loads, creating deliberate parallel: cattle bear your physical burdens as mercy, you will bear sinful burdens as justice. Kāmilatan, complete, from k-m-l, to be complete, indicates full, not partial, bearing. The phrase alā sā'a mā yazirūn is an exclamatory condemnation, common in Qur'an to express horror at evil deed. The verse's structure — purpose clause li-yaḥmilū, then description of burdens, then exclamation — moves from cause to consequence to evaluation, guiding listener to feel weight of misguidance.
