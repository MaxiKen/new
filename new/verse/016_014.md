## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:14

> **And He is the One Who has subjected the sea, so from it you may eat tender seafood and extract ornaments to wear. And you see the ships ploughing their way through it, so you may seek His bounty and give thanks ˹to Him˺.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sea As A Subjected Servant**

Wa-huwa al-ladhī sakhkhara al-baḥra — and He is the One who has subjected the sea. The sea, baḥr, in Qur'anic usage includes both salt seas and large bodies of water. Taskhīr of the sea means making it navigable, productive, and traversable despite its power and danger. The sea could have been created as an impassable barrier, a deadly expanse that drowns all who enter. Instead, it is subjected: it carries ships, yields food, and produces ornaments. Al-Ṭabarī explains sakhkhara al-baḥra as making it subservient so that ships can sail upon it and people can benefit from what is in it. Al-Rāzī adds that subjection includes the physical properties that make sailing possible: buoyancy, currents, winds that push sails, and the fact that water, though fluid, can support heavy vessels. These properties are not inevitable; they are designed. The emphatic huwa al-ladhī again excludes partners. No idol subjected the sea; no star governs its tides independent of Allah.

**Tender Meat From A Salty Source**

Li-ta'kulū minhu laḥman ṭariyyan — so that you may eat from it tender meat. Laḥm ṭarī is fresh, tender seafood, primarily fish. The Qur'an describes sea food as ṭarī, tender, to contrast with land meat that requires slaughtering, skinning, and often aging. Sea food is ready, soft, easily digestible. Al-Qurṭubī notes the legal implication: sea food is lawful without ritual slaughter, and can be eaten even in iḥrām, the state of pilgrimage where land hunting is forbidden, as in 5:96: *Lawful to you is game of the sea and its food as provision for you and travelers* (5:96). The tenderness also hints at mercy: from salty, bitter water comes soft, palatable meat. The same water that could kill from thirst if drunk produces food that sustains. Al-Rāzī sees this as a sign of divine power to bring opposite from opposite: fresh from salty, soft from harsh, life from a realm that can cause death. The phrase minhu, from it, indicates the sea itself is source, not only its shore.

**Ornaments Extracted From Depths**

Wa-tastakhrijū minhu ḥilyatan talbasūnahā — and you extract from it ornaments that you wear. Ḥilyah means ornament, jewelry, adornment, specifically pearls and coral, as clarified in 55:22: *From both seas come forth pearl and coral* (55:22). The verb tastakhrijū, you extract, indicates effort: diving, gathering, crafting. Yet the material is created by Allah, not by humans. Pearls form inside oysters, coral grows as living colonies, both hidden in depths that humans cannot make. Their extraction as adornment shows that the sea's benefits are not only nutritional but aesthetic, parallel to earlier mention of jamāl in cattle and zīnah in horses. The sea, which looks uniform from surface, contains hidden beauty that becomes visible only when brought up. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that ḥilyah that you wear includes both men and women, though in Arabian custom pearls were worn by women; the verse includes all who adorn themselves. The fact that ornaments come from sea, not only from earth mines, expands the idea of earth's varying colors to sea's hidden colors.

**Ships Ploughing Through Water**

Wa-tarā al-fulka mawākhira fīhi — and you see the ships ploughing through it. Fulk is a collective noun for ships, large vessels. Mawākhir from m-kh-r means ploughing, cleaving, cutting through water, with a sense of pushing and parting. The image is vivid: ships cut through waves as a plough cuts earth, leaving a furrow that quickly closes. Al-Ṭabarī explains mawākhir as ships that go and come, cutting water with their prows, driven by wind. The phrase wa-tarā, and you see, invites the listener to visualize a harbor or coast where ships are visible, a common sight for coastal Arabs and for Quraysh who traded via Red Sea ports. The subjection of sea is thus made visible: you see with your own eyes that heavy wooden structures do not sink but plough through. This visible proof supports the unseen claim that sea is subjected by Allah. The verse does not say you hear about ships; it says you see them, appealing to direct observation.

**Seeking Bounty And Giving Thanks**

Wa-li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa-laʿallakum tashkurūn — and so that you may seek His bounty and so that you may give thanks. Two purposes are given for sea subjection. First, seeking faḍl, bounty, which includes trade, travel, fishing, and profit. The Qur'an uses faḍl for provision sought through effort, especially via sea trade, as in 17:66: *Your Lord is He who drives the ship for you through the sea that you may seek of His bounty* (17:66). Second, gratitude: that you may give thanks. The sequence is important: bounty first, then thanks. Allah provides means to seek provision, then invites thanks for that means. Al-Rāzī notes that seeking bounty is not condemned; it is presented as a legitimate purpose of sea travel, provided it leads to shukr, not to arrogance. The sūrah's repeated phrase laʿallakum tashkurūn, so that you may give thanks, appears in verses 14, 78, and elsewhere, making gratitude the intended outcome of all blessings. The sea, which could be feared as a god, is instead a field for seeking bounty and a reason for thanks to the One who subjected it.

**A Verse That Completes The Tour Of Provision**

Verses 10-13 covered rain, crops, celestial bodies, and earth colors. Verse 14 adds sea, completing the major environments: sky, land, sea. After this will come mountains, rivers, and landmarks, completing the traveler's world. The verse's structure — He subjected sea, so you eat, extract, see ships, seek bounty, give thanks — moves from divine act to human benefit to human response. It models how a believer should process any blessing: recognize who subjected it, enjoy its specific gifts, observe its visible signs, use it to seek lawful provision, and end with thanks. The bracketed ˹to Him˺ in translation clarifies that thanks is due to Allah, not to sea or ships. The Arabic tashkurūn is absolute, but context makes object clear. In Sūrat al-Niʿam, thanks is never generic; it is always directed to the Creator of the blessing.
