## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:180

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shuʿayb Removes the Suspicion of Profit**

Shuʿayb has challenged the economic conduct of the residents of the Forest. They could claim that he is seeking money or influence through the warning. He answers that he asks no reward from them. His message is not a business arrangement in which they pay for moral advice or purchase a favorable judgment.

The declaration is especially fitting for a messenger addressing a people whose life is connected to exchange. Shuʿayb does not enter their world as another trader seeking a better bargain. He stands outside the transaction and calls them to a Lord whose command cannot be bought or adjusted according to wealth.


Shuʿayb locates his reward with the Lord of all worlds. His hope therefore does not depend on the residents accepting him, praising him, or financing his work. God knows the labor of warning, the pain of rejection, and the sincerity hidden beneath every word.

This independence allows the messenger to speak against practices that benefit powerful people. If his reward came from them, he might soften the call whenever the market, chiefs, or wealthy families objected. By expecting reward from God, he can remain truthful without turning his opposition into personal resentment.


**No Payment Makes the Message Public**

A message that requires payment can become a privilege of the wealthy. Shuʿayb's refusal keeps the call open to the entire community. The poor do not need to buy access to guidance, and the prosperous cannot purchase a private moral standard.

This does not mean every human teacher or worker must refuse lawful support. Ordinary labor may be compensated. The messenger's declaration concerns the source of his authority and the purity of his mission. God's guidance is not a commodity that a speaker may sell to the highest bidder.


The title Lord of all worlds expands the call beyond the Forest's local interests. Shuʿayb is not saying that one clan's deity supports his personal program. He speaks of the Lord who owns every people, land, market, and generation. The community cannot limit God's authority to its own customs.

The title also explains why the reward is secure. The residents may withhold money and status, but they cannot withhold God's recompense. A messenger's work is held by the One whose knowledge reaches beyond the immediate audience and whose judgment is not controlled by human fashion.

**The Messenger Cannot Be Bribed Into Silence**

A leader who depends on wealthy supporters may avoid naming corruption. Shuʿayb's refusal of payment denies the community that leverage. He cannot be bought into approving dishonest exchange, nor can he be punished financially into abandoning the call.

This does not make him indifferent to their livelihoods. He wants their trade to become just and their society to avoid punishment. But concern for their welfare is not the same as dependence on their approval. A trustworthy messenger seeks their reform, not their money.


Noah, Hūd, and Ṣāliḥ all make the same kind of declaration in this sūrah. The repetition shows that prophets do not create personal religious empires. They deliver the message and direct reward back to God. Shuʿayb's mission belongs to that continuous pattern.

The similarity also gives the residents an opportunity to compare their response with earlier communities. Powerful peoples have rejected messengers who sought no payment. Shuʿayb is warning them not to repeat the same mistake by treating a free moral summons as a threat to their independence.

**The Lack of Payment Does Not Mean the Mission Is Costless**

Shuʿayb receives no worldly fee, but his work still costs him patience, social standing, and safety. A servant can sacrifice greatly without asking the audience to compensate him. The absence of a price makes his motive clear; it does not make his service easy.

This distinction also helps those who serve good causes today. The deepest value of a task may not appear in wages or praise. Work should be honest and lawful, but sincerity keeps a person from believing that only visible compensation makes service worthwhile. God sees what the audience overlooks.


Once Shuʿayb denies personal gain, the residents must face the message itself. They cannot fairly say that he wants their money. They must decide whether worship of God and just dealing are true obligations, even when obedience may reduce an easy profit.

A clear choice is a mercy. It prevents the people from hiding behind suspicion and identifies the real issue: will they submit to the Lord of all worlds? Their answer will reveal whether commerce is their master or whether their commerce can become an act of accountable service.


A messenger who expects reward from God can continue speaking when the audience responds with hostility. He does not measure the truth of his message by immediate success. He mourns rejection because he cares for the people, but he does not abandon the call because they refuse to honor him.

That patience should not become passivity. Shuʿayb continues to warn and identify injustice. He simply leaves the final recompense with the Lord. The servant's duty is faithfulness; the Lord decides how the audience responds and what reward the faithful effort deserves.


This statement completes the familiar opening shared by the prophetic accounts: a messenger from the people, a call to fear God, a declaration of trustworthiness, and a refusal of payment. Shuʿayb has made the source and motive of his mission clear before addressing the community's specific corruption.

For the reader, the verse teaches how to hear moral counsel. Do not dismiss truth because the speaker lacks wealth, and do not trust a message merely because the speaker is successful. Examine whether the call returns worship to God, seeks justice, and remains independent of personal gain. Shuʿayb's reward lies with the Lord of all worlds.
