## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:21

> Their Lord gives them good news of His mercy, pleasure, and Gardens with everlasting bliss,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lord Himself Announces the Reward**

Verse 21 presents the reward of those who believe, emigrate, and strive, and it begins with an intimate action: their Lord gives them good news. The title Lord, rabb, carries the sense of the One who owns, nurtures, and brings a thing to completion. The people who spent their wealth and lives are not abandoned to guess whether the sacrifice mattered. Their Lord personally announces what awaits them.

Good news is not an ornament added after the command. It sustains the person who must act before seeing the result. A community asked to leave homes, bear hardship, and risk life needs more than a bare order. It needs a true account of what its obedience is moving toward. The verse supplies that account in three linked gifts: mercy, pleasure, and Gardens of lasting bliss. The order teaches that the reward is not merely a collection of delights. It is a relationship with Allah that gives every delight its meaning.

**Mercy Comes Before the Garden**

The first gift is mercy from Him. Mercy is the covering of failure, the acceptance that makes a servant fit for nearness, and the care by which Allah brings a person through the dangers of the journey. The believer's striving is never a price that forces Paradise from Allah. It is itself made possible by mercy, and its defects are received through mercy. The verse therefore prevents the worshipper from turning sacrifice into a claim of ownership.

A person may leave home sincerely and still carry fear, weakness, or an imperfect intention. Allah's mercy does not make obedience unnecessary; it makes obedience fruitful despite human limitation. The Qur'an repeatedly joins forgiveness and reward because a servant needs both. He needs the wrong covered and the good accepted. Without mercy, the believer would examine his effort and despair at what is missing. With mercy, he works seriously while depending on the generosity of the Lord who knows his effort better than he knows it himself.

**Divine Pleasure Is Greater Than Possession**

The second gift is pleasure, riḍwān. This word points to Allah's approval and contentment with the servant. It is more personal than a reward placed before a person. A person can receive a gift from someone while remaining distant from the giver. Riḍwān means that the giver's relationship with the servant has itself become a source of joy.

The Qur'an later says that Allah's pleasure is greater than the Gardens and their pleasures (9:72). Verse 21 introduces that hierarchy here. The believer wants Paradise because it is Allah's promised home, but the highest desire is not the landscape without the Lord. The heart that has learned to sacrifice for Allah wants to be received by Him. The Garden is blissful because Allah's pleasure surrounds it. Without that pleasure, abundance would still leave the deepest hunger untouched.

**Naʿīm Names a Life Without Corrosion**

The final gift is Gardens with everlasting bliss. The word naʿīm suggests ease, flourishing, and enjoyment free from the strain that marks earthly pleasure. Worldly joy can be real, but it is exposed to loss, guilt, interruption, boredom, illness, and death. The Qur'anic promise does not merely increase the intensity of pleasure. It removes the defects that make created pleasure unstable.

The plural Gardens suggests abundance and variety, but the verse's emphasis rests on lasting quality rather than on a catalogue of objects. A gift that ends cannot provide complete rest to a heart that knows it will end. Everlasting bliss answers that weakness. The emigrant who left a house, the striver who spent wealth, and the believer who accepted danger are promised a dwelling where no faithful sacrifice becomes a source of regret.

**Good News Makes Sacrifice Intelligible**

The verse also shows how revelation speaks to human motivation. Some people object to reward as though a believer should act without hope of benefit. The Qur'an does not share that assumption. It knows that human beings are moved by love, fear, and hope, and it purifies those motives rather than pretending they do not exist. Mercy answers the fear of rejection. Pleasure answers the desire to be loved by Allah. Gardens answer the longing for secure joy.

Mature faith does not despise these gifts. It learns to love the Giver through the gifts. A child may first desire a reward because it is pleasant; a mature servant desires it because it is from Allah and because it represents His promise. The order of verse 21 allows that growth. The believer receives mercy, seeks divine pleasure, and rejoices in the Garden as the home prepared by the Lord.

**A Reward That Reverses the World’s Valuation**

The people of verse 20 were not necessarily honored by the world when they made their sacrifices. Emigration can look like loss, and striving can look like waste to people who measure life by property and safety. Verse 21 reverses that judgment. What appeared to be abandonment was movement toward mercy. What appeared to be social defeat was a path to divine pleasure. What appeared to be the loss of home was preparation for a lasting home.

For believers today, the verse gives a way to understand costly obedience without romanticizing pain. Hardship is not automatically proof of righteousness. The action must be connected to belief and Allah's cause. But when a person loses a worldly benefit because he refuses what Allah forbids, the loss is not the final measure. The Lord who sees the sacrifice gives good news that the world could not give: mercy, pleasure, and a bliss that does not expire.
The believer should therefore welcome the transformation of fear into hope. Allah's mercy does not erase the seriousness of the journey; it gives the traveler strength to continue toward Him.
