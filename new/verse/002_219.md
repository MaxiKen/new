## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:219

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about intoxicants and gambling. Say, “There is great evil in both, as well as some benefit for people—but the evil outweighs the benefit.” They ˹also˺ ask you what they should donate. Say, “Whatever you can spare.” This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you ˹believers˺, so perhaps you may reflect

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Questions, One Method**

The verse continues the sūrah's question-legislation with two inquiries answered in one descent: what about wine and gambling? and what should we spend? The first answer is a weighing rather than a prohibition — in them is great sin, and benefits for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit — and the method of the weighing is the verse's first lesson. The revelation does not begin by declaring the two evils pure and simple; it concedes that benefit exists, places it on the scale, and then out-weighs it. The concession is rhetorically and morally load-bearing: a community that knows wine loosens the miser's purse and gambling feeds the poor (the benefits the exegetes will enumerate) is not asked to pretend otherwise — it is asked to hold both columns of the ledger and accept the verdict of the total. The second answer is equally a method: what to spend? *The spared* — the ʿafw, what can be given without strain. Both answers train the same faculty: judgment under complexity, the weighing of real goods against real harms, the refusal of both the zeal that denies facts and the indulgence that ignores totals.

**Maysir: The Gambling of the Arrows**

The verse's second term — maysir — denotes a specific Arabian practice the commentators describe in detail. The maysir was a game of arrows over meat: the players drew lots, and the loser was obliged to pay for the whole animal while receiving no portion of it — and was ribbed and chided in the bargain — while the winners distributed their shares to the poor as a public display of generosity. The institution is thus gambling clothed in charity: a mechanism of random extraction whose losses funded a spectacle of giving, ruinous to the loser and flattering to the winner's reputation. The exegetes note the broader terminology — gambling in general is qimār, of which maysir is one form — and the legal tradition's careful distinctions: wagering is forbidden outright, while games such as chess and backgammon, as idle pastimes, are frowned upon by many as spiritual distraction without being technically prohibited. The historical footnote the commentators add is telling: both games traveled from the Islamic world to the West, and — not being forbidden in the legal sense — both remained popular among Muslims, including devout ones. The verse's target is the wager's structure: wealth transferred by chance rather than by work, enmity manufactured by loss, and the poor made props in the winners' theater of generosity.

**The Benefits and Harms of Wine, Enumerated**

Al-Qurṭubī's enumeration of wine's benefits is remarkably candid: it aids digestion, makes the miserly magnanimous, gives boldness to the timid, brings color to the face, assists sexual capacity, and produces a short-lived feeling of well-being. The list reads like an honest inventory of what the drink actually offered its culture — medicinal, social, and psychological goods, none of them invented. Against it the same tradition sets the harms: intoxication's loss of the rational faculties, quarreling, violence, promiscuity, impaired judgment, and — the harm the Qur'an's final prohibition-verses will name first — the forgetting of God's remembrance. The verdict of the weighing follows the columns: the sin is greater than the benefit. The commentators note the extension the law drew from the term: though the verse names wine (khamr) specifically, Islamic law understands the ruling to cover all intoxicating drinks, following the prophetic definition that khamr is whatever clouds the mind. The enumeration's candor is the verse's enduring method-lesson: the ruling is not built on a pretense that the forbidden thing has no goods, but on a comparative judgment that its evils — especially the evil against the intellect and the remembrance — outweigh them.

**ʿUmar's Prayer and the Staircase of Prohibition**

The verse is the first of three steps in one of the Qur'an's clearest cases of gradual legislation, and the reports preserve the sequence with ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb at its center. Before the prohibition was declared, ʿUmar used to pray: O Allah, give us a clear, satisfying ruling about wine. This verse was revealed — in them is great sin and benefits — and was recited to him; and he prayed again: O Allah, give us a clear ruling about wine. Then came the sūrah of the Women's clause — do not approach the prayer while intoxicated (4:43) — and was recited to him; and he prayed a third time. Then came the sūrah of the Table's decisive verses — Satan only seeks to cast enmity and hatred between you through intoxicants and gambling, and to turn you from the remembrance of God and from the prayer; so will you not desist? (5:90–91) — and when they were recited, ʿUmar said: we have desisted. The sequence, preserved in the Sunan collections, is the legislation's own pedagogy: a community weaned off a deeply rooted habit not by a single command but by staged revelation — first the weighing that educated judgment, then the prayer-time restriction that built the habit of abstention, then the final question that asked for the community's own consent. The verse of the weighing is the staircase's first step, and its method — benefit conceded, sin out-weighing — prepared minds the flat prohibition would have met with resistance.

**The Objectives of the Law**

The weighing's rationale entered Islamic legal theory as one of its foundational cases. The harms named for intoxication — beginning with the loss of the rational faculties — made the ruling on wine a central proof for the maqāṣid al-sharīʿah, the objectives-of-the-law doctrine, in which the protection of the intellect stands as one of the essential ends the Law exists to secure, beside the protection of religion, life, lineage, and property. Gambling's harms were analyzed in the same framework: al-Rāzī notes that it creates enmity and despair, produces ill-gotten gains, and diverts from the healthy pursuit of profit — and the risk-structure of the wager entered Islamic financial theory directly, where certain transactions are invalidated for entailing excessive risk, a doctrine connected by financial theorists to the prohibition of ribā, itself discussed by some as carrying a severe form of risk — the usury-legislation this very sūrah will deliver (2:275–280). The verse thus seeded two entire disciplines: a theory of law's purposes built on the intellect's protection, and a theory of finance built on the wager's distinction — gain by chance versus gain by work — with the maysir of the arrows as its founding case.

**The Spared, and the Sentence Left Open**

The second answer names the measure of giving: *the ʿafw* — what can be spared, what comes easily, given without difficulty; the same word elsewhere carries the sense of pardon, and al-Rāzī notes both. The exegetes debated whether the directive concerns voluntary giving or a required duty — some holding it abrogated by the later institution of the zakāh, others seeing no contradiction between a fixed alms-tax and a standing command of charity beyond it. The practical ruling they draw is a middle-path economics: be neither miserly nor improvident; never impoverish yourself through giving — the same balance the sūrah of the Night-Walk states as an image: let not your hand be chained to your neck, nor let it be fully open (17:29). The answer expands the earlier spending-verse — which named the recipients (2:215) — by addressing the amount, completing the curriculum of giving with its two coordinates: to whom, and how much. And the verse's closing opens into the next: thus God makes the signs clear to you, that haply you may reflect — the sentence continuing across the verse-division into the two worlds the reflection is to span. The clarity and the reflection are the whole passage's method in one clause: the signs made clear, the ledger set out, the totals stated — and the mind left to do what clear signs exist for: to think, across this world and the next, about what out-weighs what.
