## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:219

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about intoxicants and gambling. Say, “There is great evil in both, as well as some benefit for people—but the evil outweighs the benefit.” They ˹also˺ ask you what they should donate. Say, “Whatever you can spare.” This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you ˹believers˺, so perhaps you may reflect

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question About Wine and Gambling**

The verse reports a question about intoxicants and gambling and answers that both contain great evil and some benefit for people, with the evil outweighing the benefit. The Book's answer does not forbid the practices outright, and the community's scholars noted the stages by which the prohibition was established.

The wording states a proportion, and the community's teachers read the sentence as an instruction about how the two matters are to be weighed. Readers are shown that the Book acknowledges the advantages that people find in the practices, and the sentence then supplies the measure by which they are to be judged.

**The Nature of the Two Practices**

The commentators explained the term for gambling as referring to a particular practice in which participants drew lots over the division of meat, with the loser bearing the cost and the winners distributing their portions. The description shows the social setting in which the practice was conducted, and the community's scholars noted that the prohibition was later extended to wagers of every kind.

The term for intoxicants covers the drinks that remove a person's control of his faculties, and the community's jurists applied the ruling to all substances of that kind. Readers are given the account of two practices that belonged to the ordinary life of the society addressed, and the sentence's assessment of them is stated in terms of the harm they do.

**The Benefits Acknowledged**

The commentaries preserve the observations of the community's teachers about the advantages people found in these practices, including the sociability and the boldness that the drink produced and the wealth that could be acquired without effort through the wager. The list shows what the sentence means when it acknowledges a benefit.

The Book's recognition of those advantages gives the sentence its force, since the answer does not deny what the practitioners experienced. The community's teachers noted that the passage treats the matter as a comparison rather than as a simple prohibition, and the reader is shown that the Book's method here is to state the balance and leave the conclusion to the hearer.

**The Harm Named**

The sentence states that the evil of the two practices outweighs their benefit, and the commentators listed the consequences of intoxication, including the loss of judgement, quarrels, violence, and the neglect of worship. The harms of the wager include the loss of property and the resentment it produces.

The community's scholars noted that the protection of the intellect came to be counted among the essential objectives of the law, and they connected that principle with the sentence. The passage shows that the Book's assessment rests on the effects the practices have on the person and on the community, and the sentence supplies the criterion by which later rulings were drawn.

**The Second Question**

The sentence then reports a question about what the community should spend and answers that they should give what can be spared, and the two questions are joined in a single verse. The community's teachers noted the pairing of the questions about harmful practices with the question about generosity.

The instruction about giving has already appeared in the sūrah, and the answer given here is repeated in a different form. The reader is shown that the Book's replies to the community's inquiries address both what is to be avoided and what is to be given, and the sentence supplies the second of the two answers.

**The Closing Formula**

The sentence closes with the statement that God makes His revelations clear so that the community may reflect, and the phrase introduces the continuation in the following verse, which names the two worlds as the subject of reflection. The community's teachers read the formula as the introduction to the verses that follow.

The Book's habit of ending an instruction with an invitation to think appears throughout the sūrah, and the sentence here supplies the transition to the account of this world and the Hereafter. The passage closes with the two answers and with the invitation to consider what the comparison means for their conduct, and the passage continues with the treatment of the orphans.

**The Gradation of the Prohibition**

The community's teachers read the sentence as one stage in a sequence that the legislation followed, and the reports describe the steps by which the practice was removed from the life of the believers. The Book first forbade the prayer while a person was intoxicated, then named the harm of the drink and its excess over the benefit, and finally forbade it outright with the strongest description.

The sequence has been studied as an example of the manner in which the legislation trained the community, since a practice that was woven into its hospitality and its poetry was not removed in a single word. The report records the supplication of one of the companions who asked God to make the matter clear, and the passage that followed supplied the decisive instruction.

The word the sentence uses for the practice is the one that the later verse takes up, so that the instruction here and the prohibition that followed are joined by their vocabulary as well as by their subject. The jurists applied the rule to every substance that removes the control of the faculties, and they built the law on the combination of the passages.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes that the two practices contain both harm and benefit, that the harm exceeds the benefit, that the community is to give what it can spare, and that the Book's instruction is given so that the community may reflect. The reader is given the assessment and its measure, and the community's teachers used the verse when they explained the manner in which the legislation on intoxicants was completed.

The comparison the sentence draws has been used by the community's teachers as a principle for weighing what is permitted, so that the measure of a thing is taken from its harm rather than from its immediate appeal. The verse also supplies the framework for the law of intoxicants, since the passages that followed completed what the sentence began. Commentators gathered the benefits the sentence acknowledges and the harms it names into lists that the community studied when it taught the subject.
