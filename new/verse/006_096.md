## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:96

> He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, AllKnowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dawn as a Sign**

He causes the dawn to break. The Book's word for the breaking of the dawn is the same root used for splitting something open, which links this sentence to the previous verse about the seed and the fruit stone. The night is rent by the light in the same way that the seed is opened by the shoot, and the sūrah places the two images side by side so that the audience reads them together.

The Book uses the same root for the daybreak in other places, including the sūrah that opens with the seeking of refuge in the Lord of the daybreak. The ordinary event of the morning is thus treated as an act of God rather than as a mechanical occurrence, and the sūrah's chain of signs gains a second item that the audience had watched from childhood.

**The Night Made for Rest**

And has made the night for rest. The verse names a purpose for the darkness, and the Book states the same arrangement in the sūrah of the News, where it says that God made sleep for rest, the night as a covering, and the day for livelihood (78:9–11). The sūrah of the Discernment gives the same set of functions in slightly different words, saying that God made the night a covering, sleep a repose, and the day a rising (25:47).

The sūrah used this particular gift at length in the passage about God calling back the souls of people at night and reviving them by day (6:60), and it treated the alternation as a daily demonstration of the resurrection. Here the same gift is listed among the signs of the created order, which shows how firmly the sūrah's argument ties the ordinary rhythm of human life to its claims about the Hereafter.

**The Sun and the Moon With Precision**

And made the sun and the moon to travel with precision. The Book attaches the same description to the two luminaries in several places: the sūrah of the Most Compassionate says that the sun and the moon travel with precision (55:5); the sūrah of Yūnus says that God made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases so that people might know the number of years and the calculation of time (10:5); and the sūrah of the Troops says that the sun and the moon each orbit for an appointed term (39:5).

The precision in view is the order that makes calendars possible, and the audience, whose religious year depended on the moon, understood what it meant. The sūrah of the Cow records the question about the phases of the moon and answers that they are a means for people to determine time and the pilgrimage (2:189). The verse in front of us states the arrangement as a design rather than as a convenience, and the design is credited to the One whose signs the sūrah has been presenting.

**The Two Names That Close the Sentence**

That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing. The two names state the attributes that the created order displays: the power that holds the arrangement in place and the knowledge that measured it out. The Book attaches the same pair to the same subject in the sūrah of Yā-Sīn, where it says that the sun travels for its fixed term and that this is the design of the Almighty, the All-Knowing (36:38).

The consistency of the Book's language is worth noticing. Wherever it describes the regular motions of the heavens, it uses the vocabulary of measuring and decreeing, and wherever it names the attributes of God in those passages it names power together with knowledge. The sūrah's audience lived in a culture attentive to the stars, and the verses answer the attention by pointing it to the arrangement rather than to the objects.

**What the Sign Does Not Do**

The sūrah's description of the dawn, the night, the sun, and the moon is followed by the statement that God has made the signs clear for people who know (6:97). The sequence leaves open the outcome the sūrah has been documenting from its opening: the signs are clear, and clear signs do not compel a response. The sūrah has described people who saw the same sky and drew the conclusions the verses are correcting, and it has described people who read the signs and were led by them.

The difference between the two responses is the subject of the sūrah's constant attention, and this passage states the difference in terms of knowledge. The signs are made clear for people who know, and the sūrah's next verses supply the next sign in the series: the stars that guide travellers across land and sea.

**The Order of the Signs**

The order in this verse moves from the small to the vast. The seed and the fruit stone open the series, then the daybreak, then the night, then the two great luminaries whose motion measures the years. The sequence gives the audience a ladder of evidence from the field outside their walls to the sky above them, and each rung is something they had handled or watched.

The next verse adds the stars, which are the complement of the sun and moon in the sūrah's argument. The two luminaries mark time, and the stars give direction, so the Book's catalogue covers both the calendar and the road. The audience that used both was being asked to acknowledge the One who made both, and the verse states the acknowledgement as a matter of knowledge rather than of ritual.
