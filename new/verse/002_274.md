## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:274

> Those who spend their wealth in charity day and night, secretly and openly—their reward is with their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Four Quadrants of Giving**

The charity-section that has run from the grain-parable onward closes by completing its coordinate system: *day and night, secretly and openly*. The two pairs cross to make four quadrants, and the verse's claim is that the givers it praises have filled all four — every hour and every mode. The commentators read the doubling deliberately: night and day cover the axis of time, secret and open cover the axis of audience, and between them the verse exhausts the circumstances in which a gift can be given. Nothing is left outside: the urgent need at midnight and the assessed due at noon, the hand nobody saw and the hand the market watched. The section has legislated each quadrant separately — the ranking of the secret over the public (2:271), the open giving that remains good, the gift to the unseen recipient (2:272), the search for the silent poor (2:273) — and this verse gathers the legislation into a portrait of the person who has absorbed all of it: a giver for whom no hour is wrong and no audience disqualifying.

**The Quartet of ʿAlī and Abū Bakr**

Beyond the general import, the commentators record specific identifications. Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī preserve reports that the verse describes particular givers — ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq among those named — who are said to have distributed a sum in exactly the fourfold pattern the verse describes: a quarter by night, a quarter by day, a quarter in secret, a quarter in open. The reports function less as historical claims to verify than as the tradition's way of reading the verse's quadrants as a practiced discipline: giving as a deliberately diversified portfolio of occasions, so that no single mode dominates and the soul's attachment to any one audience — the night's privacy, the day's visibility — is broken by rotation. The specific-occasion reading and the general reading coexist in the commentaries without conflict, the verse's grammar allowing both: these are people who gave thus, and this is what all such giving earns.

**Their Reward With Their Lord**

The reward-clause repeats the section's custody-formula with precision: *their reward is with their Lord* — ajruhum ʿinda rabbihim, the same prepositional deposit promised the un-reminding givers (2:262). The repetition at the section's close is its final answer to the visibility-problem the whole passage has worked through: the four quadrants include two where no human ledger records anything — the night, the secret — and the verse assures both that the record exists, kept *with* the Lord, in a custody the giver's anonymity does not weaken and the recipient's silence does not erase. The commentators' earlier ruling that the hidden gift is better, and the open gift good, converges here in a single reward-location: whatever the quadrant, the payment stands in the same place, with the same Keeper — which is why the quadrants can be filled indifferently, the giver choosing between them by need's occasion rather than by reward's arithmetic.

**No Fear, No Grief**

The closing formula is the sūrah's language of arrived security, and its appearance here marks the charity-section's believers as having reached the state the formula names: *there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve*. The pair covers both directions of the heart's disturbance — the future's fear, the past's grief — and the section has already explained why its givers hold both: no future to fear, because the reward is deposited where nothing reaches it; no past to grieve, because nothing they gave has been taken back in reminders or injury. The same formula sealed the sūrah's early portrait of the faithful across the revelations — the believers, the Jews, the Christians, and the Sabians who believe and do good (2:62, 5:69) — and its assignment here places the complete giver in that same company of the secured. The verses ahead will name the opposite fate in its own formula — residents of the Fire, abiding — but this verse ends the section on the security-clause, and the givers it describes are the last people the charity-legislation leaves standing before the sūrah changes subject.

**The Total Giver**

Read as a portrait rather than a rule, the verse describes a disposition the section's fifteen verses have been constructing piece by piece. The good-things command (2:267) determined what he gives; the annulment-prohibitions (2:262–264) determined what he never says afterward; the garden-parable (2:265) determined why — God's pleasure and confirmed certainty; the burnt-garden parable (2:266) determined what he fears, and the fear corrected him; the ranking of the secret (2:271) trained his audience down to zero; the guidance-verse (2:272) widened his recipients past every boundary of creed; the mark-verse (2:273) sharpened his eye for the need that does not ask. The four quadrants are the resulting life: giving at every hour, in every mode, to every kind of need, from the good of what he has. The verse needs no new command to describe him because the commands have all been issued — it only states his schedule, his secrecy-range, and his deposit, and adds the two negations that define his interior: nothing ahead frightens him, nothing behind grieves him.

**The Section's Seal**

The verse's position does structural work the commentators mark: it is the last of the charity-passages, the closing seal on the sūrah's longest continuous ethical legislation between the family-code and the ribā-passages that follow. The seal's form — a portrait plus a reward-formula — matches the section's opening parable, the grain that multiplies (2:261): the section began with the deed's growth and ends with the doer's security, the agricultural promise translated into the eschatological one. Between the two, every verse has been a condition or a protection of the deed, and the final verse is what remains when all conditions are met: the givers named, their quadrants filled, their wage with their Lord, their hearts emptied of the two things the section has been emptying them of all along — the fear of want that closes hands (the poverty-threat of 2:268) and the grief of the gift spoiled (the reminders and injuries of 2:262). The charity-section's last word is therefore its quietest: no drama, no parable, no command — only the four adverbs of a completed practice and the two negations of a completed peace.
