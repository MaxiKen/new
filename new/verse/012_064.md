## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:64

> He responded, “Should I trust you with him as I once trusted you with his brother ˹Joseph˺? But ˹only˺ Allah is the best Protector, and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Rather Than a Refusal**

Jacob's answer opens with an interrogative: shall I entrust him to you except as I entrusted his brother to you before? The form is rhetorical and it does the work of a refusal without stating one. Nothing in the question asserts that Benjamin will be lost; it asks whether the precedent permits confidence. The chapter's method here is precise. A father who has been through this once does not need to argue, because the argument has already been conducted and settled by events. He needs only to name the precedent, and the naming is enough to make the sons' assurance in verse 63 sound like the assurance they gave in verse 12. The question also refuses the premise the plea rests on. His sons have presented themselves as changed men offering a new guarantee; by asking whether he should trust them as he trusted them before, Jacob declines to treat that guarantee as new. He files it with the previous one and lets the filing do the arguing for him.

**"His Brother" — Joseph Is Never Named Here**

The reference is relational throughout. Jacob does not say Joseph. He says *his brother*, meaning Benjamin's brother, and the phrasing carries a particular force: the son you once asked me to entrust to you was this boy's brother, and the outcome is known to both of us. The avoidance of the name is consistent with the chapter's practice — Benjamin is never named either — and it has an emotional register the commentators note. Jacob's speech about Joseph elsewhere is equally oblique: he will say *my grief* rather than my son's name in verse 84, and his eyes will go white with sorrow (12:84). A man who has not accepted a death does not use the dead person's name in an argument about a living child.

**"God Is the Best of Keepers"**

The second clause changes direction entirely. *Fa-Llāhu khayrun ḥāfiẓan* — God is the best of those who guard. The same noun was used of Joseph's self-description in verse 55, where he asked to be put over the storehouses as a *ḥafīẓ*, a careful keeper. Here the word is transferred to God, and the superlative form does the work: of all who keep a thing, God keeps it best. The clause is Jacob's way of releasing the matter. He cannot guard Benjamin himself, his sons have proved unreliable guardians, and the only remaining custody is divine. The commentators read this as trust in God combined with resignation of the whole affair to Him, which is what the wording supports. The superlative is doing quiet work. By naming God the best of keepers rather than a keeper among others, Jacob implicitly ranks his sons' undertaking beneath it, and that ranking is the whole of his objection. He never says they will fail; he says that if anyone is to guard the boy there is a better candidate, already present in his sentence.

**"The Most Merciful of the Merciful"**

The verse closes with *arḥam al-rāḥimīn*, a superlative of mercy that the Qur'an places in the mouths of prophets at moments of loss. Moses uses it after the golden calf, asking forgiveness for himself and his brother: *“You are the Most Merciful of the merciful”* (7:151). Joseph will use it at the end of this chapter, forgiving the brothers who sold him: *“May Allah forgive you! He is the Most Merciful of the merciful!”* (12:92). Three speakers, three occasions, one formula, and in each case the formula is uttered by someone who has been wronged or bereaved and has no remedy left. Jacob's use of it here places him inside that pattern before he has agreed to anything. The placement matters more than the assent it precedes. The name is offered not as a reason to let the boy go but as a description of the One he is trusting with the outcome, which allows Jacob to consent without ever claiming the journey is safe. He transfers the risk instead of minimising it, and states the transfer in a single phrase.

**Two Clauses That Pull Apart**

The verse contains reproach and surrender in immediate succession. The first half refuses confidence in the sons; the second half places the son in God's keeping. A reader might expect an intervening negotiation, a set of terms, or a refusal followed by persuasion. The chapter gives none. Jacob moves from an accusation to an act of trust in a single sentence, and the movement is what defines his faith in this story. He does not cease to know what his sons are capable of. He ceases to rely on his own capacity to prevent it. Verse 66 will show him imposing an oath anyway, which means the trust and the precaution are held together rather than resolved. The tension is never resolved and the chapter makes no attempt to resolve it. Jacob's reliance is total in his wording and partial in his conduct, and both halves are recorded without comment. The reader is given a believer who prays for protection and then requires an oath as security, with no explanation of why he needs both.

**Grief That Has Not Diminished Faith**

Between this verse and verse 84 Jacob will lose a second son, weep until his eyes turn white, and be told by his remaining family that he is going to die of grief. His answer there is the chapter's clearest statement of his position: he complains of his grief and sorrow to God alone and knows from God what they do not know (12:86). Verse 64 anticipates that stance. A man who says God is the best of keepers and the most merciful of the merciful, while refusing to trust his own children with a son, is holding two convictions that most people would find incompatible. The chapter presents the combination as faith rather than as contradiction, and the sequel — Joseph and Benjamin both alive in Egypt — vindicates it.

**The Reply Answers the Assurance, Not the Argument**

Jacob responds to *we will definitely watch over him* and to nothing else in verse 63. He does not address the shortage of grain, the need for the eleventh load, or the famine's duration. The omission is deliberate: the material case is not in dispute, and Jacob is not refusing because Egypt's demand is unreasonable. He is refusing because the guarantee offered is the same guarantee that failed before. When the brothers produce new evidence in verse 65 — the money returned, the family's need, an extra load easily obtained — Jacob still does not withdraw the objection. He converts it into an oath, which is a way of accepting the journey while recording his distrust of the travellers. The asymmetry is deliberate. Jacob has no answer to the economic case his sons have made, because the case is sound: without grain the household starves, and the minister has attached a condition to the grain. What he can answer is their reliability, and he answers it by consenting in the same breath as he records that he does not trust them.

**The Verse Names the Only Custody Jacob Accepts**

Between the two clauses sits an unstated judgement about human reliability. Jacob does not say his sons are liars, does not accuse them of the well, and does not repeat the bloodied shirt. He says that he once trusted them with a brother and asks whether the present case is any different. The question leaves the earlier event undefined, which is more severe than defining it would have been: the sons know exactly what is meant, and the reader knows too. What Jacob then does is transfer the custody rather than negotiate it. God is the best of keepers. The formulation concedes that no human guardian in this family has proved adequate, and it is the only ground on which he will eventually let Benjamin go.
