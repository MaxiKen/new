## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:165

> Why is it when you suffered casualties ˹at Uḥud˺—although you had made your enemy suffer twice as much ˹at Badr˺—you protested, “How could this be?”? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “It is because of your disobedience.” Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Asked After the Defeat**

The verse addresses the community about the affliction that had befallen it and notes that the community had already inflicted a wound twice as large on its opponents. The question that follows is the one people were asking: where had this come from?

The Book reports the question and then supplies the answer in its own words. The members of the community who asked it were trying to reconcile their losses with the promise of help they had received, and the verse takes up their difficulty directly rather than leaving it unanswered. The answer it gives is addressed to the whole community and not to a faction within it.

**The Comparison Between Badr and Uḥud**

The commentators worked out the arithmetic behind the reference to a double wound. Al-Ṭabarī reports that the believers lost seventy men at Uḥud, while at Badr they killed about seventy of the idolaters and took seventy more as prisoners, which amounts to twice the number in the sense that the prisoners could have been executed.

Other explanations were offered as well: that the community had routed its opponents twice, once at Badr and once in the early part of the fighting at Uḥud, while it had been routed only once. The differences among these readings concern how the accounting is done, and the verse's point survives all of them: the community's losses were smaller than those it had inflicted.

**The Answer Given in the Verse**

The answer to the question is that the affliction came from the community itself, and the sentence is stated as a command to the Prophet to say it. The Book repeats the principle in another passage, where it states that whatever good befalls a person is from God and whatever evil befalls him is from himself.

The sentence is one of the most direct statements of responsibility in the Book, and it follows a passage in which the community's failures have been described in detail. The verse does not attribute the defeat to the enemy's strength or to fate; it attributes it to the conduct of the people who suffered it.

**The Conduct That Brought the Loss**

The commentators identify the conduct in various ways that all point to the same events. Some held that the community was afflicted because it had ransomed the prisoners of Badr rather than putting them to death, obtaining worldly wealth in exchange for a military advantage. Others held that the cause was the disobedience of the archers, whether by leaving their positions, pursuing the spoils, or fleeing later in the day.

Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī record these views without settling on one. What the explanations share is that the loss followed from decisions the community had made, and the verse's answer holds all of them: the affliction came from the community's own side.

**The Question as It Was Actually Asked**

The reports explain the question of whence this came as arising from the rout during the battle, and the commentators note that it was sometimes phrased in a way that carried an implication: if this is a true prophet, why did the defeat take place?

The verse treats the question as a serious difficulty and answers it without impugning the questioner. The Book's manner in such passages is to give the community the explanation it needs, and the sentence here does so in four words before going on to state the underlying truth about God's power.

**Why the Answer Was Necessary for the Community**

A community that had been promised help and had then been defeated had two ways of explaining what had happened. It could conclude that the promise had failed, or it could conclude that the community had failed, and the verse states the second.

The first conclusion would have destroyed the community's confidence in its message. The second preserved the message and directed the community's attention to its own conduct, which is the only one of the two factors it could do something about. The sūrah's whole treatment of the battle follows this line, and this verse states it in the plainest available terms.


The verse closes by stating that God is powerful over all things, and the sentence places the community's conduct and its consequences within the scope of that power. The Book uses the declaration at the end of passages about both mercy and punishment.

The phrase prepares for the verses that follow, which will describe what happened to those who stood firm and what happened to those who fell. The community's losses and its victories alike are held inside the same power, and the declaration that closes this verse prevents either of them from being read as an accident.

**The Lesson the Community Drew**

The verse has been read by later generations as a statement of a general principle: that a community's setbacks cannot be blamed on its enemies alone, and that the Book directs it to examine its own conduct first.

That reading is supported by the Book's treatment of the matter elsewhere and by the uses to which this verse has been put in the community's teaching. The question the community asked after the battle is one that every generation asks after its own losses, and the answer supplied in this verse is the one the sūrah has been preparing from the beginning of its account, with its descriptions of the quarrel, the pursuit of spoils, and the failure to hold the position that had been assigned.

**The Arithmetic Behind the Claim of Twice Over**

The comparison in the sentence was given numbers by the commentators. The believers killed at Uḥud are counted at seventy, and at Badr the community killed about seventy of the idolaters and took about seventy prisoners (al-Ṭabarī). Taken together, the two figures give the community double what it suffered a year later, which is how the sentence's claim has been read.

Al-Qurṭūbī added the argument that the prisoners belong in the total because their lives were at the community's disposal; had they been executed, the number reached would have been a hundred and forty. The point is not a statistic the verse states but an explanation of how the comparison holds.

The commentators also connected the verse with two explanations offered for why the loss came when it did. Some held that the community was afflicted at Uḥud because it ransomed the prisoners of Badr instead of settling the matter there, and others because of the disobedience described in the preceding passages. The verse itself states the comparison and leaves those reports as reports.
