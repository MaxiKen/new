## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:77

> O believers! Bow down, prostrate yourselves, worship your Lord, and do ˹what is˺ good so that you may be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Changes**

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* — O you who believe.

This is the first time in the sūrah that the believers are addressed by this formula. For seventy-six verses the sūrah has said *O humanity* (22:1, 22:49, 22:73) or spoken about the believers in the third person. Now, at the very end, it turns and speaks to them directly.

The shift is the sūrah's structure in miniature. The chapter has been addressed to everyone: the Hour announced to all people, the argument offered to doubters, the evidence set out for anyone who will look. Only after the argument is complete does the sūrah turn to those who accepted it and tell them what is now required.

The order matters. Worship commanded before the case is made would be a demand; worship commanded after it is an answer.

**Four Imperatives, in Ascending Order**

*Irkaʿū wa-sjudū wa-ʿbudū rabbakum wa-ifʿalū al-khayra* — bow, prostrate, worship your Lord, and do good.

The four commands are not a list of equivalent items, and the sequence is not arbitrary.

The first two are specific physical acts: *rukūʿ*, the bowing, and *sujūd*, the prostration. Both are named separately rather than being folded into the third, which is *ʿibādah*, worship in general. The specification is what gives the command its shape — the religion being described has a body, and the body does particular things.

The fourth command is the widest: *do the good*. It is not limited to ritual, and its placement last is significant. The three acts directed at God come first; the act directed at the world comes after, and cannot replace them.

**The Second Prostration of This Sūrah**

This verse is one of the two places in Sūrat al-Ḥajj at which the reciter bows, and the practice is attested in the ḥadīth literature.

ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir reported that he asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ whether there are two prostrations in Sūrat al-Ḥajj, and was told: *“Yes, and whoever does not perform them should not recite them”* — recorded in *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* (1402), where al-Albānī graded the chain weak, though others have accepted it. A related report from ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, also in Abū Dāwūd (1401), counts fifteen prostrations in the Qur'an, among them two in this sūrah. The first is at verse 18.

The fit between verse and practice is exact, and the first prostration makes the point better than the second does. At verse 18 the sūrah described everything in creation bowing down — the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees and the animals — and the human being as the only creature that can stand. The reciter who bows there is joining the list.

**What the Bowing Means**

The prostration is the posture in which the highest part of a person — his face, the seat of his dignity and his recognition — is placed on the ground.

That is why the Qur'an treats it as the summary act of worship rather than as one gesture among many. Everything in the sūrah's argument leads to it: the creature made from dust, raised from the grave, given a record, and told that sovereignty on the final day belongs to another, has one fitting posture available to him.

The sūrah has also been insistent that the inward state is what matters (22:32, 22:37). Placing that insistence beside a command to perform a physical act resolves a question readers often force into a contradiction: the inward state is what the act is for, and the act is how the inward state is expressed. Neither is the other's rival.

**"That You May Prosper"**

*Laʿallakum tufliḥūna* — so that you may prosper, perhaps you will succeed.

The verb *aflaḥa* is from a root meaning to split open, used for a seed breaking and for a field yielding. Success in this vocabulary is not the acquisition of something but the fruition of something.

The particle *laʿalla* carries hope or purpose, and its presence at the end of a command is worth noting. The verse does not say that doing these four things mechanically produces prosperity. It says that this is the path on which prosperity is to be hoped for, which leaves the outcome with God.

**"Do Good" Is the Command That Has No Limit**

The fourth imperative is the one the sūrah leaves open, and the openness is deliberate.

Bowing and prostrating have forms; worship has its rites, which this sūrah legislated at length. Doing *al-khayr*, the good, has no specified form, no quota and no closing date. The sūrah has used the same word at verse 72, where the Fire was described as something worse than what enrages them, and the two uses frame the same idea: the good is the undetermined remainder of what a person is asked to do.

That is also why the command is placed last. A religion of specified acts alone would be completable, and a person could finish it. Appending an open obligation means the religion cannot be discharged by performance — which is consistent with a sūrah that has located the value of every rite in the state of the person performing it (22:32).

**Why This Comes Before the Final Verse**

The sūrah's last verse will command striving — a word that, in the sūrah's own context, has just been used for fighting (22:39), for emigration (22:58) and for the campaign against the signs (22:51).

Putting the command to bow and prostrate immediately before it is the sūrah's final ordering principle. The striving is real and it is costly, and the person who does it is first of all a person who bows. A community that reverses that order has misread the sūrah.
