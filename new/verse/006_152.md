## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:152

> And do not come near the wealth of the orphan—unless intending to enhance it—until they attain maturity. Give full measure and weigh with justice. We never require of any soul more than what it can afford. Whenever you speak, maintain justice—even regarding a close relative. And fulfil your covenant with Allah. This is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will be mindful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wealth of the Orphan**

And do not come near the wealth of the orphan, unless intending to enhance it, until they attain maturity. The command continues the recitation, and it protects the property of children who have no one to defend them. The exception is precise: a guardian may handle the property if he intends to improve it, and he must hand it over when the child reaches the age at which he can manage it.

The Book returns to the same subject in several passages. In the sūrah of the Women, the guardians are instructed to test the orphans until they reach marriageable age and then to release their property to them (4:6), and it warns against consuming it. The same command appears in the sūrah of the Night Journey in words close to those of this verse, with the addition of the command to fulfil every covenant (17:34). The rule appears in several parts of the Book, and the passage in front of us states it as one of the terms of the covenant that the recitation lays down.

**The Giving of Full Measure**

Give full measure and weigh with justice. The command governs the conduct of trade, which was the foundation of the audience's economy, and the Book attaches the same requirement to its accounts of the earlier communities. In the sūrah of the Night Journey, the believer is commanded to give in full when he measures and to weigh with an even balance, which is described as fairest and best in the end (17:35); the sūrah of the Most Compassionate commands the community to weigh with justice and not to give short measure (55:9); and the sūrah of the Defrauders opens with a condemnation of those who take full measure for themselves and give short measure to others (83:1–3).

The Book's placement of the command within the covenant shows how the sūrah treats commercial conduct. The requirement of honesty in the market is stated alongside the prohibitions of murder and indecency, and the two are presented as parts of the same obligation. The dishonest traders are threatened in the sūrah of the Defrauders with a day when the people will stand before the Lord of the worlds, which is the same reckoning that the sūrah in front of us has been describing.

**The Limit of Obligation**

We never require of any soul more than what it can afford. The sentence follows the commands and states the measure of the obligation, and the Book uses it at the end of passages that lay down requirements. The long passage of legislation in the sūrah of the Cow closes with the declaration that God does not require of any soul more than it can afford, in a verse that also records the community's prayer for forgiveness and mercy (2:286); the sūrah of the Divorce tells the person of limited resources to provide according to what God has given him (65:7).

The sentence answers a fear that the audience might have had about the demands being made. The rules are firm and their scope is wide, and the clause that follows them states that the obligation is measured to the person's capacity. The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere states the same principle in the context of spending and of worship, and the sentence in front of us makes it part of the covenant itself.

**Justice in Speech**

Whenever you speak, maintain justice, even regarding a close relative. The command governs testimony and speech, and the Book attaches the same requirement to the community's dealings with one another and with their opponents. In the sūrah of the Women, the believers are told to stand firm for justice as witnesses for God even if it is against themselves, their parents, or their close relatives (4:135), and the sūrah of the Table commands them to bear true testimony and not to let hatred of a people drive them into injustice (5:8).

The phrase even regarding a close relative addresses the strongest pressure against justice in a society organised around kinship. The audience belonged to clans whose loyalty was absolute, and the command tells them that the ties of family do not change the requirement to speak truthfully. The Book's treatment of the matter elsewhere states that justice is to be maintained even where the interest of a relative is at stake.

**The Fulfilment of the Covenant**

And fulfil your covenant with Allah. The sentence states the general obligation that the various commands belong to, and the Book presents the requirements of the passage as the terms of an agreement between God and the community. The same command stands in the same position in the list of the sūrah of the Night Journey (17:34), and the sūrah of the Family of Imran states that God loves those who honour their trusts and are mindful of Him (3:76).

The Book's account of the covenant with the children of Israel runs through the sūrah of the Cow and the sūrah of the Table, and the commands listed in those passages cover the same ground as the list in front of us. The verse in front of us states the obligation in its own terms, and the closing formula identifies the whole as what God has commanded.

**The Closing Formula**

This is what He has commanded you, so perhaps you will be mindful. The sentence repeats the closing of the previous verse with a change in the final word, and the two closings address the community as people who can understand and remember. The triple formula that ends the passage in the sūrah of the Night Journey uses the same structure, and the sūrah of the Cattle's version distributes the appeals across its two verses.

The appeal to mindfulness is the second of the three that close the sūrah's recitation, and the verse that follows adds the third with the appeal to consciousness of God (6:153). The progression states what the passage requires of the reader and what it hopes for from him, and the arrangement gives the law a hearing rather than a mere statement.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse identifies the whole recitation as the path of God, describing it as perfectly straight, and commands the community to follow it and not the paths that branch off and lead away from His way, closing with the appeal that they may be conscious of Him (6:153). The sentence gathers the commands into a single road, and the sūrah's final passages turn to the Book, the reckoning, and the testimony that closes the chapter.

The verse in front of us completes the list of what the covenant requires, and the reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening verses now has both halves of the message: the argument against the audience's religion and the conduct the revelation asks of those who accept it.
