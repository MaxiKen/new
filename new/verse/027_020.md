## Sūrah an-Naml 27:20

> ˹One day˺ he inspected the birds, and wondered, “Why is it that I cannot see the hoopoe? Or could he be absent?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A King Who Counts**

Sulaymān reviews his birds and finds one missing. That is the whole event, and its force lies entirely in the scale. His forces include jinn, humans and every kind of bird; the previous verses have described them as a marshalled host filling a valley. Out of that assembly he identifies the absence of a single individual.

Al-Qurṭubī draws the governing lesson from exactly this: a great leader knows the condition of those he leads, down to one bird. The observation is not sentimental, because the alternative is a real failure mode. A ruler of a large organisation who knows only aggregates cannot notice anything that happens to a single member of it, and a system in which individuals can vanish unremarked is precisely the system the ant feared two verses earlier — one where creatures are crushed *unknowingly*. Sulaymān's inspection is the answer to that fear. The same man who heard one ant in a valley notices one bird missing from a parade.

**The Verb Is About Careful Looking**

*Tafaqqada* is the word, and it is stronger than *looked at*. The root *f-q-d* concerns loss and missing, and this verbal form means to seek out what is absent — to inspect with the specific intention of finding what is not there. English has no single word for it; the idea is closest to taking a roll call.

That the Qur'an uses this verb rather than a general verb of seeing tells us the absence was found rather than stumbled upon. Sulaymān was checking. This turns the episode from a lucky noticing into a routine of administration, which is a more demanding claim about his kingship. Anyone might spot a gap; maintaining a practice that reliably surfaces gaps is the work of governance. The verb also explains the tone of his question. He is not startled. He asks what has happened in the measured manner of someone who has just found a discrepancy in an inspection and wants it accounted for.

**Why This Bird Was Missed**

The commentators asked what made a hoopoe's absence noticeable, and gave two answers with different grounds. Al-Ṭabarī reports that the hoopoe's function was locating water — it could detect where water lay beneath the ground — so that when the army needed water and Sulaymān called for the bird, the gap appeared. Al-Rāzī transmits a different account, that the hoopoe's role was to shade the king, and the absence was felt directly.

Neither account can be sustained as report. Both are exegetical conjecture rather than transmitted fact, offered to answer a question the verse raises and does not settle, and the verse itself says only that he inspected and found the bird gone. They should be read as what they are — two commentators reasoning about an omission, in the way a modern reader reasons about a gap in a narrative. What both attempts have in common is an assumption worth stating: each bird in the host had a function, and the absence registered because a function went unperformed. That is a coherent reading of an army described as perfectly organised. In such a body, nobody is decorative, and the disappearance of any member shows up as work not done.

**What the Hoopoe Actually Is**

The *hudhud* is a real and identifiable bird, not a legendary one, and the Qur'an's choice of species repays a look. The hoopoe is a medium-sized bird with a long, thin, downcurved bill and a striking crest it raises and lowers, barred black and white wings, and an undulating flight. It is found across North Africa, the Levant and Arabia, and it was known to everyone in the Qur'an's first audience.

Two features make it apt for the role the chapter gives it. Its bill is built for probing — it feeds by driving that thin beak into soil to extract insects, which is the natural basis for the tradition about detecting what lies underground. And it is a migratory bird over much of its range, covering long distances between continents, which makes a journey to a distant kingdom and back a plausible assignment rather than a fantastic one. The Qur'an did not select a mythical creature for this errand. It selected a common bird whose actual habits — long-range travel and probing for what is hidden — match the two things it is about to do.

**The Second Question Corrects the First**

Sulaymān's speech has two parts, and the movement between them is the most carefully observed thing in the verse. He begins *mā liya lā arā al-hudhud* — what is it with me that I do not see the hoopoe. Then he adds *am kāna min al-ghāʾibīn* — or is he among the absent?

Arabic marks the second clause with *am*, a particle that introduces an alternative to what was just proposed, so the two halves are genuinely offered as competing explanations rather than as a single rhetorical flourish. Either I have failed to see him, or he has failed to be here.

The first question puts the fault on his own seeing. Something is wrong with me, that I am not seeing this bird. Only in the second does he raise the possibility that the bird is genuinely gone. A man reviewing his ranks assumes he has missed something before he assumes his subordinate has failed, and he says so out loud. The order is not accidental; Arabic allows either question to come first. This is a ruler who checks his own perception before charging anyone, which is the same disposition that made him respond to the ant's unflattering warning with a prayer instead of a defence. The tone shifts sharply in the verse that follows, and the shift only registers as sharp because of how mild this one is.
