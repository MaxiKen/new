## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:78

> They appealed, “O Chief Minister! He has a very old father, so take one of us instead. We surely see you as one of the good-doers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Title Joseph Once Served Under**

The brothers address him as *ʿAzīz*. The chapter has used the word before, and its earlier appearances are uncomfortable. The city's women used it of Joseph's master when gossiping about his wife: *“The Chief Minister's wife is trying to seduce her slave-boy”* (12:30). It was that man's household Joseph was sold into, that man's wife who accused him, and that man's residence he was imprisoned after leaving. The tradition adds that when the Chief Minister died, the king gave Joseph his position and with it his title. The brothers are therefore addressing, by his official style, the person who once stood in that office as a slave — and they do not know it. The address reverses a relationship the reader has already seen from the inside. These men petition the minister of Egypt in the language of petition, and the minister is the boy their father's household lost and their own hands delivered to a caravan. Every formula of respect they use is accurate, and every one of them is spoken to a brother, which is what gives the scene its weight.

**An Appeal to a Father's Age**

The argument the brothers put forward is humanitarian and specific: he has a father, an old man. The commentators explain the force of the reference. Jacob loves Benjamin above the others, and Benjamin has been his consolation in place of Joseph; losing him would produce a grief the old man could not survive. The brothers are not asking for mercy on abstract grounds. They are describing a household they know intimately and a bereavement they have already caused once, and asking the official in front of them not to cause it again. Everything in the appeal is true. What is missing from it is the identity of the person being appealed to — the son whose loss produced the age they are now citing, and whose absence made Benjamin the consolation they are describing.

**Ten Men Volunteer to Be Taken**

The offer is the verse's most striking element. Ten men volunteer to be substituted for one, in a jurisdiction where the penalty they themselves stated involves being handed over to the person robbed. The offer is not rhetorical; it is made to an official with the power to accept it, and it is refused in the next verse. Whatever else the brothers are, they are not here attempting to escape at Benjamin's expense — they are proposing to pay the cost themselves. The chapter records the proposal without comment and without accepting it, and the refusal is grounded in law rather than in unwillingness.

Note what the offer concedes. By proposing a substitution the brothers accept the finding: the cup was in the bag, the penalty applies, someone will be detained. They do not contest the search, do not question the herald's account and do not claim the object was planted. Their defence in verse 77 was about character, not about fact, and here it collapses into a request about who pays. That concession is what makes the following verses possible, since a party still disputing the discovery could not negotiate its consequences.

**The Verse Is the Brothers' Best Moment**

Compare this appeal with the conduct recorded in verses 8 to 18. The same men planned the disposal of a brother, argued about killing him, agreed on a well, and brought their father a bloodied shirt with a fabricated story. Here, faced with the loss of a second brother, they offer themselves. The sūrah does not announce the change and does not praise them for it. It simply places the offer in the narrative, where a reader can compare it with what came before. The chapter's account of these men is not that they were wicked and became good; it is that they were capable of both, and that a long sequence of events eventually made the second capacity operative.

**"We See You Among the Doers of Good"**

The closing clause praises Joseph with the chapter's own central term. *Muḥsin* — a doer of good — is what the narrator called Joseph when he reached maturity (12:22), what Joseph called himself implicitly when describing his conduct in prison (12:36), and what the reward clause of verse 56 refers to. Joseph will use the same noun in verse 90 when identifying himself to these men. The brothers are therefore describing him accurately in the very sentence in which they misunderstand him completely. The compliment is true, and it is addressed to a man they believe to be an Egyptian official, and its truth is confirmed four verses later by the person receiving it. The confirmation comes four verses later, from the man being addressed, in private, to a brother who cannot repeat it. The compliment is therefore true twice: as the brothers' honest assessment of an official who has dealt with them fairly, and as a statement about a prophet that the sūrah has spent seventy verses establishing. Neither speaker knows that both descriptions apply to one person.

**The Irony of the Compliment**

They praise him as a good-doer while he is executing a plan that has just detained their brother. The reader knows both halves. Joseph has fed them at full measure twice, returned their money unasked, and disclosed himself privately to the boy they are pleading for; he has also planted a cup, conducted a search in a calculated order, and allowed them to state the penalty that would retain him. Whether all of this is the conduct of a doer of good is the question the chapter answers in verse 76, where the plan is attributed to God. The brothers' judgement and the narrator's coincide, from opposite sides of a secret. The coincidence is produced by twenty years of hidden evidence. The reader has watched Joseph refuse a locked door, decline a prison release, feed a famine and forgive a brother in private; the brothers have watched him load their camels and run a search fairly. Both sets of observation reach the same verdict, and the chapter lets the compliment stand as true from either direction.

**The Appeal Fails, and the Failure Is the Point**

Verse 79 refuses the substitution on legal grounds. Verse 80 reports the brothers' despair and the eldest's decision to remain in Egypt. Verse 81 has nine of them return to Canaan with a report Jacob will not accept. The plea in verse 78 therefore produces nothing — except that it is recorded. The chapter keeps it because it matters that these men asked to be taken instead of their brother, and because the asking is part of the evidence that the family has changed. Joseph hears it. The reader hears it. The refusal that follows is a refusal of the mechanism, not of the intention, and verse 92 will show what Joseph eventually does with both.
