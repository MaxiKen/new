## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:263

> Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury. And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comparison Stated**

The sentence states that a word of kindness and an act of forgiveness are better than a charity that is followed by an injury, and the community's teachers read the comparison as the statement of the Book's scale of values. The verse follows the instruction about the reminder and the hurtful word, and it supplies the alternative that the passage recommends.

The comparison has been explained by the commentators in the light of the passage's concern for the poor. A gift that is accompanied by a reproach leaves the recipient with a wound, while a kind word gives him something without cost to the giver, and the sentence rates the second above the first.

The wording does not diminish the duty of charity. It states that the value of a given act is measured by the whole of what the recipient receives, and the community's scholars drew from the sentence the rule that the manner belongs to the act.

**The Kind Word**

The commentators explained the phrase as the speech that soothes the person who is in need, and they included the greeting, the encouragement, and the prayer offered on his behalf. The teachers noted that such a word can come from a person who has nothing to give, so that the sentence opens a door of goodness to everyone.

The Book's treatment of speech elsewhere commends the words that mend a relationship, and the community's scholars gathered the instances when they explained the phrase. The sentence therefore places the tongue beside the hand among the means by which the community serves its members.

The teachers also observed the order of the sentence, since the kind word is named before the forgiveness that follows it. The two belong to the same disposition, and the community's preachers used the phrase when they described the manners that accompany the withholding of a gift that cannot be given.

**The Forgiveness Named**

The second element of the comparison is the forgiveness that a person extends to a request that troubles him, and the commentators explained the phrase as the overlooking of the manner of the one who asks. The teachers described the case of a poor person whose approach is rough or whose asking is importunate, and they read the sentence as the instruction to answer him with pardon rather than with reproach.

The community's scholars connected the phrase with the passages about patience and about the pardon that the Book commends, and they used the material when they taught the conduct of the giver and the one who has nothing to give.

The wording places the forgiveness among the goods that the sentence rates above the spoiled gift. The person who pardons the fault of the needy has given him something, and the community's teachers counted the act among the goods that the Book rewards.

**The Attributes Named**

The verse closes with the description of God as self-sufficient and forbearing, and the community's scholars explained the pair as the ground of the comparison the sentence has drawn. The first name states that God has no need of the property that the community offers, and the second describes the patience with which He bears the faults of His servants.

The teachers drew from the attributes the lesson that the honour of the giver is not increased by the gift itself, so that the reminder of a favour reveals an error about the source of what a person owns. The Book's teaching about property elsewhere describes it as a trust, and the community's scholars gathered the passages when they spoke about the subject.

The two names also answer the state of the giver who fears that the withholding of a gift will be held against him. The One who is self-sufficient does not require the gift, and the person who has nothing to give may still earn the reward by the manner he keeps.

**The Place in the Passage**

The sentence stands between the instruction about the spoiled charity and the comparison of the two givers in the verses that follow, and the community's teachers read the arrangement as the development of a single subject. The passage has treated the reward of the giver, the fault that destroys it, and the modest good that the Book rates above the harmed gift.

The teachers of the community drew the lesson that the passage attends to the condition of the poor as much as to the virtue of the rich. The Book's concern runs through the whole section, and the sentence in this place supplies the statement that a person who has nothing is not left outside the service of his community.

The following verses carry the argument to its illustration, and the community's scholars read the whole passage as a unit when they taught the law of charity. The sentence therefore supplies the principle, and the parables that follow supply the demonstration of it.

**The Teaching Drawn**

The community's preachers used the sentence when they answered those who excused themselves from giving because they owned little. The verse supplied the reminder that the kind word and the pardon are within the reach of every person, and the material shows the formula repeated in the exhortations of the early teachers.

The scholars of the community also noted that the sentence protects the dignity of the poor in the very passage that calls for generosity, since the Book does not treat the recipient as an object of pity. The wording places him among the persons to whom a service is owed and from whom a fault may be pardoned.

The passage's instruction therefore covers the whole of the relationship between the giver and the one who receives. The sentence in this place supplies the standard by which both are measured, and the verses that follow develop the same theme with the figures of the rock and the garden.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the superiority of the kind word and the pardon over the charity that is followed by an injury, the standing of the modest goods within the community's service, and the attributes of God that supply the ground of the comparison. The community's teachers used the verse when they taught the conduct of the giver and the consolation of the one who had nothing to give, and the sūrah's treatment of charity continues with the comparisons that follow in the next verses.
