## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:263

> Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury. And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Better Thing**

The verse performs an act of ranking so counterintuitive that its whole function is to reorder the giver's arithmetic: *kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury*. The comparison is not between giving and not-giving — the verse assumes the charity happened, the transfer completed, the need met — but between a completed gift with a wound attached and two things that cost no dirhams at all: the honorable word and the pardoning of what the asker did. Al-Rāzī supplies the scenario the verse legislates: the poor person asks, and the asked cannot or will not give; what is owed then is not the gift but the word — an honorable reply — plus forgiveness for whatever harm the asking carried, whether the asker's manner was rough or the request itself caused embarrassment. The ranking's shock-value is deliberate: a refused request answered with kindness outranks a granted request poisoned by injury, which means the section's true currency was never the amount transferred. The wounded gift is worth less than nothing given gently — less, the verse says, than two words and a pardon.

**The Honorable Word**

Qawl maʿrūf — the word the translation renders *kind*, and the code's own standard-term: maʿrūf, the recognized-good, the same measure that governed the divorced women's treatment (2:229), the nursing provision (2:233), the compensation scaled by capacity (2:236). The honorable word at the door of the un-given is thus not a softer substitute for the gift but the code's regular instrument applied where the gift cannot be: what the community's decent practice recognizes as the right saying to a person in need. The commentators' examples fill the term — the reply that preserves the asker's dignity, the promise of future help if it is honest, the explanation that does not shame, the greeting that treats the asking as no disgrace. The word is *better* than the wounded charity for a reason the verse's ranking makes structural: the maʿrūf-word continues the relationship — the asker may return, the giver may give later — while the injury inside the gift ends it, converting the recipient into a debtor of dignity who will not ask again. The section's economics reappear inverted: the grain sown multiplies (2:261), the gift with adhā burns (2:262), and the honorable word, sown without a gift, outperforms both when the gift was going to carry its wound.

**Forgiveness of the Asking**

The verse's second no-cost gift is directed at a harm the Qur'an quietly assumes: asking can injure the asked. Al-Rāzī names the two forms — the manner of some askers is rough, and some requests embarrass — and commands the pardon: one should be kind and gentle with the poor, and if their asking causes trouble or discomfort, forgive them for it. The legislation is remarkable for what it refuses to do: it does not criminalize the importunate asker, does not rank the deserving from the undeserving, does not make the giver's patience conditional on the recipient's grace. It asks the person with the power in the exchange — the one who has been asked — to absorb the friction the asking generated. The commentators connect this to the section's coming portrait of the truly poor: the ones too constrained to beg, known by their mark, who do not ask importunately (2:273) — the verse here handles the cases where the constraint failed, where need did make the asking rough or persistent, and assigns the roughness to the forgiveness of the asked rather than to the account of the asker. The dignity the silent poor keep by not asking, the asking poor keep by being pardoned.

**Why the Wounded Gift Loses**

The ranking requires the injury-gift to be worth less than the word-without-gift, and the mechanism is the verse's assumed theology of giving. The charity followed by injury has already failed the preceding verse's condition — the reward that stands is with the Lord, and the adhā collects its payment from the recipient instead — so what remains in the verse's comparison is not a rewarded deed versus an unrewarded one but a completed good against a spoiled good and two intact small ones. The spoiled gift also does active damage the honorable word does not: it teaches the poor that asking costs humiliation, and it teaches the giver that giving buys superiority — two lessons that shrink every future exchange between them. The word-and-forgiveness, by contrast, leave both parties whole: the asker unshamed, the asked unburdened, the relationship open. The verse's better is thus a better in total effect, not merely in intention — the arithmetic of a community where gifts can wound and refusals can heal, summed across every door.

**Self-Sufficient**

The first closing name indicts the wounded gift at its root: Ghaniyy — Self-Sufficient, the One who needs nothing from anyone. The giver who injures has made his gift into an instrument of return — acknowledgment, deference, remembered favor — and the verse answers him with the name of the Giver his gift was supposedly for: God is Ghaniyy, beyond every need, which means the gift given for His sake has no collection mechanism at all; its entire return is with Him (2:262). The commentators tie the name to the section's direction of travel: God does not need the charity, and He does not need the honorable word either — both are legislated for the community's fabric, and the Ghaniyy-name frees the giver from the only temptation that produces adhā: the sense that the recipient, or God, owes something back. Al-Rāzī's cascade in the coming verse (2:268) begins from the same point: the soul that gives expecting return gives badly, then gives less, then gives nothing. Ghaniyy is the name that stops the cascade at its first step.

**Most Forbearing**

The second name is the verse's own subject applied to God: Ḥalīm — the Forbearing, the One who does not answer injury with immediate consequence. The pairing with Ghaniyy is the section's signature move — the same pair closed the oaths-verse (2:225) — and here it does a precise work: the Self-Sufficient One has nothing to gain from the gift, and the Forbearing One does not strike the giver who spoiled it. The ḥilm named over the verse covers both parties in the exchange: the rough asker is pardoned because God Himself forbears with far greater offenses, and the injuring giver is not annihilated for his adhā but given the section's remaining verses — the rock and the garden, the secret and the public, the good things to spend — as the road back. The commentators read the name's placement as the verse's final ranking inverted: the kind word and the forgiveness are better than the wounded charity, and both are imitations of the divine conduct named in the closing pair — the Self-Sufficiency that gives without needing return, the Forbearance that absorbs without retaliating. The giver who cannot manage the gift without injury is asked to manage, first, the word — and to learn from the two names why the word, un-costly and un-collected, is where the section's whole ethic begins.
