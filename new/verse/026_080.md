## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

> And He ˹alone˺ heals me when I am sick.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham Names God as the Healer**

Abraham now speaks about sickness and healing. After mentioning creation, guidance, food, and drink, he identifies the Lord as the One who heals him when he is ill. The statement recognizes a need that can make human beings feel vulnerable and powerless. Illness reveals that the body is not self-sufficient, while healing reveals care that reaches the person in weakness.

The verse does not deny the role of treatment, physicians, rest, or other means. It identifies the ultimate source of healing. A remedy may be used, a caregiver may assist, and the body may recover through processes people study and support, but none of these means becomes a god. Abraham directs gratitude to the Lord who gives means their efficacy and gives the body the capacity to respond.

**Abraham Attributes Healing to God With Deliberate Language**

He says “He heals me,” rather than assigning healing to an idol, to luck, or to his own control. The wording preserves divine agency without pretending that illness is unreal. Abraham expects sickness to occur and speaks of recovery within that reality. Faith is not the denial of the body's limits. It is trust that the body's Lord remains present within them.

The statement also carries personal tenderness. Abraham does not speak only of a general principle that God heals creatures. He says that God heals me. The Lord's care is experienced in the singular life of a servant. This personal recognition can sustain a person through illness without turning every delay in recovery into a verdict about God's absence.

**Illness Does Not Place the Sick Outside Divine Care**

Sickness can tempt a person to think that he has been abandoned or that his weakness makes him less worthy. Abraham's sentence resists that despair. He includes illness within his account of the Lord's care. The God who creates, guides, and provides does not cease to be Lord when the body becomes fragile.

This does not mean that every illness ends in visible recovery during a person's lifetime. Healing can occur through treatment, gradual restoration, relief of suffering, or the completion of a life in God's wisdom. The verse's central point is dependence: the servant does not command the outcome, and sickness does not remove him from God's knowledge. The believer seeks healing while remaining humble before the form and timing of divine decree.

**Acknowledging God as Healer Does Not Reject Medicine**

A false opposition can arise between trusting God and using medical means. Abraham's statement does not require such a choice. If God provides food through farmers and guidance through revelation, He can also provide healing through knowledge, skilled care, medicine, and the body's own processes. Means are not rivals to God when they are understood as means.

The proper attitude avoids two extremes. A person should not worship treatment as though success were guaranteed by human technique alone, nor should he abandon responsible care while claiming that trust makes action unnecessary. He may seek competent help, protect the sick, and pray for recovery, knowing that the result remains with the Lord. Reliance and effort belong together.

**Healing Reveals the Difference Between Power and Appearance**

Idols may be surrounded by requests for recovery, but they cannot heal the people who address them. Abraham has already asked whether such objects can benefit or harm. His declaration now gives a positive answer to the question of where benefit comes from. The Lord is not merely less powerless than the idols. He is the One upon whom life, nourishment, and restoration depend.

This contrast makes the idol's appearance irrelevant. A beautifully carved image cannot mend a wound, restore strength, or command the hidden processes of the body. Human beings may honor it, decorate it, and plead before it, but none of those actions gives it healing power. The Lord heals without needing an image to represent His authority.

**The Healer Is Also the Creator**

Because God created Abraham, healing is not an arbitrary intervention by an outsider. The Creator knows the body from its origin and its limits. He knows what is damaged, what can recover, and what wisdom may lie beyond immediate recovery. This does not make suffering easy, but it gives the sick person a foundation for trust that is deeper than the success of any one remedy.

The order of Abraham's words matters here. Creation came first, provision followed, and healing appears within the same relation. The Lord is not a specialist invoked only in emergencies. He is the continuous Lord of the whole person. Sickness reveals a need that was present all along: dependence on the One who gives and sustains life.

**Healing Calls for Humility From the Healthy**

Those who are well may imagine that health is a permanent possession earned entirely by discipline or strength. Abraham's wording corrects that illusion. Health is a mercy, and its continuation is not guaranteed by human confidence. Remembering God as Healer should make the healthy grateful and compassionate toward those whose bodies are suffering.

Such humility should also prevent judgment of the ill. A person's sickness is not proof that God has rejected him, just as health is not proof of superior virtue. Abraham speaks of being sick within the account of divine care. The condition can be a trial, a means of dependence, or part of a wisdom the person does not yet see. The community's duty is support, not contempt.

**Prayer and Treatment Can Share One Direction**

Prayer does not replace care, and care does not replace prayer. Both can be directed toward the same Lord. The patient asks for healing, the caregiver works with skill, and the family offers patience and protection. Each recognizes that ability and outcome are gifts rather than reasons for self-glorification.

Abraham's statement therefore forms a complete posture toward illness. Seek what helps, accept the limits of control, and keep hope attached to God rather than to an object or technique. The Lord who heals is not reduced to a mechanism, and the means He provides are not turned into idols. That balance guards both faith and responsible action.
