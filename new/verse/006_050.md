## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:50

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I do not say to you that I possess Allah’s treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel. I only follow what is revealed to me.” Say, “Are those blind ˹to the truth˺ equal to those who can see? Will you not then reflect?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Claims the Prophet Is Told to Give Up Before They Are Made**

The verse lists three things the Prophet does not claim: the treasuries of God, knowledge of the unseen, and angelic status. Each one corresponds to a specific demand recorded elsewhere in the Book. The disbelievers asked why a treasure had not been sent down with him (11:12); they asked to see an angel, or for the messenger himself to be an angel (15:7; 17:94–95); and they demanded to know about the Hour (7:187; 43:53). The verse removes all three of the goods they were asking him to supply.

The order is deliberate. The first two would have made him a dispenser of power, and the third would have made him something other than a human being. What remains after the three are removed is a man with a message, which is exactly the position the sūrah has been defending since the demand for an angel was first raised (6:8).

**Noah Said the Same Thing First**

The wording of the denial is not unique to this Prophet, and the Book records it almost identically on the lips of Noah when his people accused him of seeking wealth and status through his message. He told them that he did not ask them for money, that his reward was with God, and that he was not going to drive away the believers (11:29; 11:31). The parallel places the Makkan demand inside a long history in which the same demands were made of the same kind of man.

The comparison also answers a suspicion the Makkans were fond of raising. If the Prophet had claimed control of provisions or foreknowledge, his message would be a claim about himself rather than about God, and the Book's habit of making its messengers renounce such claims is a mark of what kind of mission they carried.

**"I Only Follow What Is Revealed to Me"**

The positive half of the sentence is the important half. After removing the three claims, the Prophet states what he does possess: revelation, and the practice of following it. The Book uses the same formula elsewhere when it has him say that he is nothing new among the messengers and that he does not know what will be done with him or with his people, and that he only follows what is revealed to him (46:9).

The phrase also defines the authority of everything he says about God. He speaks from a text he did not compose, and the criterion of his teaching is its conformity to what arrived rather than his personal judgement. That is the doctrine the sūrah has been pressing on an audience that kept trying to negotiate the terms of the message.

**The Unseen Belongs to God**

The Book states the principle behind the second denial in several places. With God are the keys of the unseen, and no one knows them except Him (6:59); He alone knows the unseen and reveals it to no one except the messengers of His choice (72:26–27); and the unseen is known only to God (10:20; 27:65). The revelation given to the Prophet contains news of the unseen, as when the Book tells him stories he could not have known, such as the account of Mary's guardianship (3:44) and the story of Joseph's brothers (12:102).

The distinction is between possessing knowledge and receiving it. A messenger is informed of what God chooses to disclose, and he is not a source of disclosure in himself. The sūrah's audience was demanding a fortune-teller's knowledge of hidden matters, and the verse refuses the demand on behalf of the man they were asking.

**The Challenge About Blindness and Sight**

The verse ends with a question that draws the line between two kinds of people rather than two kinds of faculty: are those blind equal to those who can see? The Book uses the same question in several places, including where it contrasts the one who stands on clear proof from his Lord with the one whose evil deeds have been made appealing to him (47:14), and where it asks whether the blind and the seeing can be equal, or the darkness and the light (13:16).

The imagery refers to spiritual perception, and the Book makes that explicit when it says that it is not the eyes which go blind but the hearts inside the chests (22:46). The question is therefore not about the faculties a person happens to possess. It is about what he does with them, which is the same argument the sūrah has made in its earlier discussion of veiled hearts and deafened ears (6:25).

**What the Verse Leaves as the Prophet's Position**

After the three denials and the one affirmation, the Prophet's position in the argument is complete. He cannot be asked to produce wealth, to disclose the future, or to appear as something other than a human being, and everything he has said rests on what was revealed to him. The sūrah's audience is left with the message itself, which is where the Book has been trying to place them from the beginning.

The final question asks them to reflect, and the reflection is not difficult to perform. Either the guidance is real and the difference between seeing and blindness is worth attending to, or it is not, and the verse's refusal to argue further is the strongest form of the claim it makes.
