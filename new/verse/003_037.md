## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:37

> So her Lord accepted her graciously and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing—entrusting her to the care of Zachariah. Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions. He exclaimed, “O Mary! Where did this come from?” She replied, “It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Acceptance, and the Answer to a Mother's Request**

The verse opens with the answer to the request made a line earlier. The mother had asked God to accept what she offered; the Book now says that her Lord accepted her graciously. The Arabic uses a form of intensity — accepted her with a beautiful acceptance — and the wording ties the answer to the petition without any interval between them.

The acceptance is of the child rather than of the vow as the mother had framed it. She had offered a servant for the sanctuary and received a daughter; the narrative answers by describing the daughter's acceptance in the same terms, and by making her growth the subject of the sentence. Whatever disappointment the earlier verse recorded is settled here, and it is settled without any explanation of the sex of the child.

**"A Pleasant Upbringing" — the Image Behind the Words**

The phrase rendered "blessed her with a pleasant upbringing" uses the vocabulary of growth. The Arabic noun for upbringing is related to the sprouting of plants, and the Book's other uses of the image connect a good life to a good tree: a good word is like a good tree, its roots firm and its branches in the sky, yielding fruit in every season, while a bad word is like a tree torn out of the ground with nothing to hold it (14:24–26).

Applied to a child, the image does two things. It presents the shaping of a person as a work of cultivation rather than an act of manufacture, and it places the cultivation in God's hands. The verse does not say that Zachariah raised her well, though he did; it says that her Lord caused her to grow well, and the human arrangements that follow are described as the hands that held what God had made.

**Entrusted to Zachariah**

The verse reports that she was placed in Zachariah's care, and the sūrah will explain later that her guardianship was decided by casting lots among the priests of the sanctuary. The reports about the relationship add that Zachariah's wife was related to Mary's mother, which would have made him a natural guardian for an orphaned child.

The narrative leaves the human arrangements visible and the divine arrangement primary. A girl enters a sanctuary through a procedure that a community would have recognised — a claim, a dispute, an allocation of responsibility — and the Book describes the process in the same breath as her acceptance by God. The result is a portrait of providence working through ordinary institutions, which is how the sūrah handles the whole of Mary's story.

**The Sanctuary and the Word miḥrāb**

The place Zachariah visited is called a *miḥrāb*, and in this context the word describes a room or a private chamber set apart for worship rather than the arched niche familiar from mosque architecture. The Book uses the term elsewhere for the place where Zachariah stood in prayer and where he emerged to signal his people.

Later Islamic building practice took the word from these verses, and the passage about provision in the sanctuary is often inscribed above the prayer niche in a mosque, a placement that turns the verse into a reminder that God supplies His servants wherever they are devoted to Him. The reading and the architecture belong to the same instinct: the narrative of a girl's room becomes a sentence written on the wall of every place of prayer.

**Provisions That Had No Carrier**

The heart of the verse is the scene Zachariah keeps walking into. Whenever he came to her in the sanctuary he found her supplied with food, and the reports explain the provision in different ways. Some said the fruits of summer were with her in winter and the fruits of winter in summer, a supply no caravan could have delivered; others said that what she had been given was multiplied beyond its measure, so that a small quantity behaved like a large one.

The two explanations agree on what matters for the narrative. Either way, the provision arrived without a visible chain of supply, and Zachariah's question is the reaction of a man who knows the economy of the city. What he encounters in her room cannot be explained by what he knows, and his question is the natural one for a guardian who has arranged for her maintenance himself.

**"Where Did This Come From?" — the Question and the Answer**

The dialogue is brief and its balance is exact. Zachariah asks where the food came from, which is a question about means; the girl answers with a statement about the provider. She does not describe a visitor, a route, or a mechanism, because the mechanism is not the point of the story.

Her reply — that it is from Allah, and that Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit — is the same phrase that closed the great prayer of verses 26 and 27, and the sūrah is careful to reuse it. The Book takes a formula that was spoken in a petition about all provision and places it in the mouth of a young woman answering a question in a small room. What a supplicant asked for in general terms is demonstrated in the narrative in the most concrete way the text can manage.

**Why the Sūrah Keeps Returning to Provision**

The theme is not incidental to the chapter. A community reading these verses in Madinah was a minority community with economic anxieties, dependent on trade routes and on neighbours with more power than itself, and the passage is repeatedly concerned with where a person's security comes from.

By placing the promise of unlimited provision in Mary's answer, the sūrah makes it a claim about ordinary life. If God supplies a girl in a room without a supply line, then the arrangements a household depends on are means rather than causes, and the people who hold those arrangements are not the ones who guarantee them. The next verse shows what Zachariah did with that realisation.
