## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:46

> Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Assumes the Answer Is No**

*A-fa-lam yasīrū fī al-arḍi* — have they not travelled in the land? The interrogative expects a negative answer only in the rhetorical sense: of course they have travelled. The Quraysh were merchants; their caravans went to Syria in the summer and to Yemen in the winter, and the routes passed directly through the ruins of the communities named in the previous verses.

So the question is not whether they moved about. It is whether the moving about did what moving about is supposed to do. The sūrah has just described abandoned wells and lofty palaces lying on their roofs; this verse asks why passing them produced nothing.

**What Travel Was Supposed to Produce**

*Fa-takūna lahum qulūbun yaʿqilūna bihā aw ādhānun yasmaʿūna bihā* — so that they would have hearts by which to understand, or ears by which to hear.

Two organs are named, and the pairing is unusual. The heart is given the verb of reasoning — *yaʿqilūna*, to use the intellect — which is not the attribution modern readers expect. In the Qur'an's psychology, the heart is the organ of understanding, and the intellect is a function of it rather than a rival to it.

The ear is given hearing. The choice of the ear over the eye, in a verse about travel and ruins, is pointed. Sight had already done its work: they had seen the wells and the palaces. What was missing was the hearing of the accounts — the reports of what those places were and what happened to them — and the reasoning that would connect what was seen with what was told.

**"It Is Not the Eyes That Go Blind"**

*Fa-innahā lā taʿmā al-abṣāru* — for it is not the eyesights that are blind. The word *abṣār* is the plural of *baṣar*, eyesight or insight, and the verse denies the defect there.

The denial is the verse's centre. The problem with these people is not a failure of observation. They saw the ruins, they saw the wells, and they saw that the towns were empty. Their eyes worked perfectly. What failed was the step from seeing to concluding.

That is a diagnosis of a specific and common condition: the evidence is in front of a person and produces nothing, because the person has no framework that would make it mean something. Adding more evidence would not help. What is missing is the organ that weighs it.

**"But the Hearts Which Are in the Breasts"**

*Wa-lākin taʿmā al-qulūbu allatī fī al-ṣudūr* — but blind are the hearts that are in the breasts.

The phrase *allatī fī al-ṣudūr*, which are in the breasts, looks redundant and is not. It is there to insist that the heart in question is the physical organ in a person's chest and not a poetic abstraction. The Qur'an's anthropology locates understanding in a real thing inside a real body, and it says so to prevent the reader from spiritualising the diagnosis away.

The same insistence appears in a well-known ḥadīth. Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: "In the body there is a piece of flesh; if it is sound, the entire body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the entire body is corrupt — it is the heart." It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (52) and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (1599). The report's logic is the verse's logic: the condition of the whole person follows the condition of a particular organ.

**Blindness Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The image of blindness for a failure of understanding is one of the Qur'an's most frequent, and it rarely describes a person who lacks information. It describes a person who has it and cannot use it.

The sūrahs use the same vocabulary for the refusal to accept the message, and they pair it with deafness and dumbness — the senses intact, the reception switched off. What the Qur'an calls blindness is a property of the receiving organ, not of the signal.

**What the Verse Requires of a Traveller**

The purpose clause is explicit: travel *so that* hearts reason and ears hear. The Qur'an is not commending travel for its own sake and is certainly not commending curiosity about old stones.

That qualification matters, because travel of exactly this kind was familiar to the audience. The caravans passed the ruins every year and the guides told stories about them, and nothing about the arrangement produced a change. What is being demanded is not more miles but a different kind of attention: the willingness to let what is seen count as evidence about oneself rather than as entertainment.

The sūrahs that repeat this appeal frame it identically, as a demand to see *the end of those before them* rather than merely their remains: *“Say, ‘Travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers’”* (6:11), and *“Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers”* (3:137). The object of the seeing is always a conclusion, never a sight.

**Why This Verse Ends the Ruins Passage**

Verses 45 and 46 form a pair, and the second is the answer to the difficulty the first creates. Verse 45 said that the evidence of destroyed towns is everywhere. The natural question is why the evidence does not settle anything.

The answer is that evidence is not self-interpreting. The ruins require a heart that reasons and an ear that hears, and where those are absent, the traveller comes home with nothing but a story about old buildings. That is why the sūrah's next move is not to produce more evidence but to address the demand for the punishment — the demand of people who have seen everything and concluded that nothing is coming.
