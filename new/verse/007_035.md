## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:35

> O children of Adam! When messengers from among yourselves come to you reciting My revelations—whoever shuns evil and mends their ways, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messengers From Among the People**

The verse addresses the children of Adam and states that messengers will come to them from among themselves, reciting the revelations of their Lord. The Book's accounts of the messengers present them as human beings drawn from the communities they address, and the sūrah of the Cattle reports the argument of the disbelievers that a messenger should have been an angel. The sūrah of the Heights itself will report the same argument in its account of Noah and his people, where the notables object that the messenger is only a man like themselves.

The phrase from among yourselves states the arrangement that the Book consistently follows, and the commentary tradition notes its significance for the reception of the message. The messenger shares the language, the history, and the circumstances of the people he addresses, and the revelation he recites is therefore accessible to them. The sūrah's later accounts of the prophets show each of them addressing his own people in that manner.

**The Recitation of the Revelations**

The verse describes the messengers as reciting the revelations of their Lord, and the recitation is the form in which the message reaches the community. The Book's use of the term for its own text presents it as something read aloud, and the sūrah of the Heights begins with letters that belong to the same presentation. The recitation in front of us is the activity by which the messenger conveys what has been sent down.

The Book's descriptions of the messengers' work include the delivery of the message and the warning that accompanies it, and the sūrah's opening verses have stated the duty of the Prophet in those terms. The verse in front of us states the same work in the context of the children of Adam, and the two passages together define the mission.

**Those Who Are Mindful and Mend Their Ways**

The verse names the response that carries the promise: those who are mindful of God and who mend their conduct. The phrase describes a turning from the wrong path to the right one, and the commentary tradition explains it as repentance from the former conduct combined with obedience to the commands the messenger brings. The Book's use of the phrase appears in its descriptions of the righteous in several sūrahs, and the sūrah of the Heights uses it again in its account of Shuʿayb's people.

The promise attached to the response is the absence of fear and grief. The Book repeats the formula in several passages, and the sūrah of the Cow states it in its account of those who follow the guidance (2:38), the sūrah of the Family of Imran states it of those who have believed and are mindful, and the sūrah of Yūnus states it of the friends of God (10:62). The formula in front of us is one of the Book's standard descriptions of the state of the saved.

**The Fear and the Grief That Are Removed**

The two words name the anxieties that the Book associates with the reckoning and with loss. Fear concerns what is coming, and grief concerns what has gone. The promise is that neither will afflict those who respond to the messengers, and the sūrah's opening verses on the weighing of deeds have already supplied the picture of the day on which the two afflictions would otherwise appear. The formula in front of us is therefore connected with the chapter's own account of the judgment.

The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter include the removal of grief from the people of Paradise, and the sūrah of the Heights will state in its later verses that God has removed rancour from the hearts of the believers. The promise in front of us states the outcome for those who respond while their term is running.

**The Address to the Children of Adam**

The verse's address to the children of Adam continues the sequence of addresses that the sūrah began after the account of the first pair. The audience is therefore placed in the position of the descendants who receive the messengers, and the terms of the promise are stated for them. The Book's practice of addressing the descendants of Adam in instructions about conduct appears throughout this passage, and the call to listen to the messengers is the natural continuation of the earlier commands.

The sūrah's later passages describe the communities that refused the messengers who came to them, and the contrast with the verse in front of us is deliberate. The children of Adam are told what awaits them if they respond, and the narratives that follow show what happened when the response was refusal.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands thirty-fifth in the chapter, and it follows the statement of the appointed term and precedes the account of those who deny the revelations and treat them with disdain. The passage that follows the deliverance of the messengers presents the two responses, and the verse in front of us states the first of them with its promise.

The reader who has been given the commands, the term, and the messengers now receives the statement of the reward. The sūrah then states the condition of those who refuse, and the two descriptions stand side by side in the chapter's arrangement.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that those who deny the revelations and treat them with disdain are the inhabitants of the Fire, where they will remain (7:36). The promise to those who respond in front of us is followed by the description of those who refuse, and the sūrah's account of the two outcomes is complete.

The two verses together present the division that the passage has been preparing. The messengers come with the revelation, one group accepts it and is promised the removal of fear and grief, and the other treats it with contempt and is assigned the Fire.
