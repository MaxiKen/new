## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:17

> Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after—Allah will pardon them. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repentance That Is Accepted**

This verse answers a question that verses 15 and 16 left open: after an act described as foul and after discipline that ends when repentance and mending appear, whose repentance counts? The answer is precise: *innamā al-tawbatu ʿalā Allāhi lilladhīna yaʿmalūna al-sūʾa bi jahālatin thumma yatūbūna min qarīb* — Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil ignorantly or recklessly then repent soon after. The particle *innamā* restricts; it tells the reader that not every claim of turning back is weighed equally. The condition *bi jahālah* — ignorantly — was explained by the early authorities not as lack of information that the act was forbidden, but as the state of acting under impulse, desire, and heedlessness even while knowing the ruling. Every person who disobeys God is, in that moment, acting ignorantly in this sense, because knowledge that does not restrain has not governed. The Qur’an elsewhere describes wrongdoing as done out of ignorance and then followed by repentance and reform (6:54), showing that the category is broad enough to include many who will later be forgiven. What the verse therefore accepts is not a narrow excuse but a common condition: the wrong was done in the heat of desire, not as a settled denial of the law.

**What Soon After Means**

The phrase *thumma yatūbūna min qarīb* — then repent soon after — was read as repentance that follows relatively quickly, before habit hardens and before death approaches. Some commentators took *qarīb* to mean before the soul reaches the throat at death, as the next verse will state explicitly: when a person is dying and says now I repent, it is not accepted (4:18). Others took it to mean soon in the sense of closeness to the sin, so that the return interrupts the pattern rather than postponing it indefinitely. Both senses converge: the repentance that counts is the one that does not wait until it is too late. The sūrah’s language of hastening to return matches its broader call to *“compete in returning to Allah”* elsewhere and to seek forgiveness promptly, because delay itself becomes a form of persistence. A person who tells himself he will repent later while continuing the act treats repentance as a final hour amulet rather than a present turning. The verse closes that strategy. The accepted turn is the one that happens while life remains an open field for action, not when the field is closing and the return is forced by sight of the consequence.

**Allah Will Pardon Them**

The promise *fa ulāʾika yatūbu Allāhu ʿalayhim* — Allah will pardon them — places the act of turning on God’s side as well. The same root *t-w-b* is used for the servant’s repentance and for God’s acceptance; the servant turns to God, and God turns to the servant with pardon. The doubling shows that forgiveness is not earned by a formula but received as a turn from the other side. The verse does not require the penitent to offer a ransom or to perform an exact counterweight; it requires ignorance acknowledged, speed in returning, and mending where mending is due, as the previous verse added *wa aṣlaḥā* — and they mend their ways (4:16). When those appear, God’s turn follows as a matter of His own commitment, expressed as *ʿalā Allāhi* — upon Allah — a phrasing that signals a promise He has laid upon Himself in mercy. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“Allah loves those who constantly turn to Him”* (2:222), and the same love is implied here where the return is accepted soon. That love is not indiscriminate; it distinguishes the one who returns quickly from the one who persists until the final hour.

**All-Knowing, All-Wise**

The closing *wa kāna Allāhu ʿAlīman Ḥakīmā* — and Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise — gives the measure by which repentance is judged. *ʿAlīm* knows the state of the heart when the sin was committed — whether it was heedlessness, arrogance, or confusion — and knows the sincerity of the return — whether it was immediate or delayed, full or partial. *Ḥakīm* places that knowledge inside wisdom: not every regret is wisdom, and not every delay is pardonable. The pair appears after the inheritance shares (4:11) and will appear again after the clarification of law (4:26) for the same reason. What seems intricate to people — who has repented enough, who has returned soon enough — is known and wisely judged by God. That closing also deters two equal errors. One person may despair, thinking his ignorant act can never be forgiven; the verse says ignorance with a soon return is precisely what is forgiven. Another may presume, thinking any late cry will suffice; the next verse says it will not. Knowledge and wisdom hold the line between despair and presumption, keeping the door open without removing its frame.

**Ignorance and the Broader Pattern of the Sūrah**

The sūrah has been defining ignorance not as lack of intelligence but as acting without the restraint that knowledge should bring. That is why it pairs this verse with earlier pictures of hasty consumption before the orphan grows up (4:6), mixing wealth to make use easier (4:2), and inheriting women against their will (4:19) — all acts where the actor knows the roster of right yet follows appetite. The remedy is not more information alone but a quick return — *min qarīb* — while the heart is still soft. The Qur’an elsewhere describes the believer’s speed: *“those who, when they commit an indecent act or wrong themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness for their sins”* (3:135), and that remembering is presented as immediate. The same immediacy is asked here. A community that learns to turn soon — to stop the act, to amend, to seek forgiveness — builds the reflex that keeps smaller wrongs from becoming the major sins whose avoidance secures forgiveness for lesser ones (4:31). The verse therefore trains a habit more than it defines a threshold. The threshold will be defined by death in the next verse; the habit is defined here as soon.

**A Promise That Invites Speed**

Read with what follows, this verse is an invitation to hurry toward mercy. The next verse will close the window for those who persist until they are dying and only then say now I repent, and for those who die as disbelievers (4:18). This verse opens the window wide for everyone else who acted in ignorance and then turned quickly. That pairing shows the sūrah’s method: it does not leave the definition of repentance vague so that people oscillate between anxiety and complacency; it names the conditions — evil done in heedlessness, repentance soon after — and attaches a clear promise — Allah will pardon them. The promise is not conditional on human witnesses or on the discipline being lifted by the community (4:16); it is a direct turn from God to the servant. For the person who has committed the act named earlier as foul, that directness matters. The same One who set limits around property and family (4:13) and who is ever Watchful (4:1) is the One who is ever Accepting when the return is real and soon. The law that seemed only to threaten thus also welcomes, and it does so while life still allows the return to be chosen, not forced.
