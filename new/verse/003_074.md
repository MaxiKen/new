## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:74

> He chooses whoever He wills to receive His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence That Answers an Envy**

The verse follows the statement about bounty with a sentence about mercy, and it does so in the same key. God chooses whomever He wills to receive His mercy, and He is the lord of an abundant bounty. Read after the reported fear of the previous verse, the sentence explains what the group's objection had misunderstood.

The two nouns are close in meaning and the verse uses both. Bounty describes the generosity of a giver, and mercy describes the relief that reaches the one who receives it. The sūrah's arrangement places them in sequence: the mercy is what is chosen, and the bounty is the source from which the choosing proceeds. What the community had feared as a threat — that someone else might be given revealed knowledge — is thus classified as an act of mercy on God's part, and the classification removes the sense that anything has been taken from anyone.

**Chosen, Not Earned**

The verb describes selection, and the verse repeats the pattern the sūrah has used since its opening verses. Authority is given to whom God wills; honour and disgrace are His to confer; the families named at the beginning of the sūrah were chosen rather than self-made; and here mercy is granted by the same decision.

The recurrence of the theme across the sūrah is a running correction of the assumption behind the disputes. Every party in the argument had a claim resting on something it possessed — a lineage, a book, a history — and each was being told that the standing it relied on had been given rather than achieved. The sentence in this verse is the general form of that correction, and its brevity makes it harder to argue with than a long demonstration.

**Who Is Chosen in This Instance**

The commentators offered several identifications for the recipient of the mercy in this verse. Some read it as a reference to the Prophet of the sūrah, chosen for prophethood despite the objections of those who had expected the position to remain elsewhere. Others referred it to the Qur'an itself, and still others to the religion that had been established through it.

The readings are not far apart. In each case what is being described is the conferral of something that no community could award to itself and that no community could withhold from another, which is the point the verse is making. The sūrah's own account of the objection — that a messenger could not be raised from among the unlettered — fits the first reading, and the surrounding verses about the Book fit the second. What matters for the reader is the act rather than the object: the choice belongs to God, and the object of the choice is a mercy rather than a property.

**The Lord of Infinite Bounty**

The verse's final description uses a word that denotes greatness and abundance, and the commentators noted that it is used in the Book for gifts that are not measured out. The phrase appears in the Book in connection with the giving of knowledge, of provision, and of reward, and it is always a statement about the scale of the source rather than about quantity in a particular case.

The placement of the phrase here answers the fear in the previous verse a second time. If bounty is infinite, then the gift given to one party is not subtracted from another, and the reasoning of those who worried that someone else might receive something like theirs is exposed as a misunderstanding of the economy they were dealing with. A person who fears that another's gain is his loss is thinking in terms of a limited store, and the verse names the giver as the lord of a store without limit. That single word removes the ground on which the whole anxiety in the previous quotation rested.

**What the Verse Says About Religious Envy**

The passage as a whole diagnoses a condition that has recurred in religious communities. It is the conviction that one's own standing depends on the exclusion of others from the same gifts, and it produces the conduct described in the earlier verses: concealment, mixing, and stratagems aimed at the confidence of outsiders.

The Book's answer here does not argue that the gifts are distributed fairly, and it does not promise anything to the envious. It states that the distribution belongs to God, and it describes the distribution as mercy. A reader who accepts that statement has removed from his own religion the assumption that its value depends on anyone else's deprivation, which is a service the verse performs for every community that recites it.

**The Comfort and the Caution in One Sentence**

For the believers who had been warned that a group wished to mislead them, the sentence is a reassurance about the ground they stand on. What they hold was given, and it can neither be earned away from them nor taken by the manoeuvres described a few verses earlier.

For those who had made the plan, the sentence is a caution. The gifts they were guarding belong to someone else, and the choice of recipients has never been theirs. The Book states both readings in one sentence without differentiating its audience, which is how it usually handles a matter that concerns everyone in the argument, and it leaves the reader to work out which side of the sentence he is standing on.

There is one more thing the verse does, and it is easy to overlook because it is small. It describes the mercy as something God chooses someone *for*, rather than something a person qualifies for by rank, learning, or lineage. A community that has spent generations curating its standing will find the sentence uncomfortable for the same reason the disputants of this sūrah found it uncomfortable: it makes the criterion invisible from outside. No one can inspect another person's selection, and no one can certify his own, which is why the Book's readers are left with the practice the sūrah has been describing rather than with a status they can point to.
