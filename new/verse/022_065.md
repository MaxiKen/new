## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:65

> Do you not see that Allah has subjected to you whatever is in the earth as well as the ships ˹that˺ sail through the sea by His command? He keeps the sky from falling down on the earth except by His permission. Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fifth "Do You Not See"**

*A-lam tara anna Allāha sakhkhara lakum mā fī al-arḍi* — do you not see that God has subjected to you whatever is on the earth?

The question is the same one the sūrah has been asking, and the pattern of these questions is worth tracking. They come at the end of the sūrah rather than the beginning, after the argument from the embryo and the field has already been made and accepted or refused. The closing movement is a rapid accumulation of signs, and the effect is of a case being summarised rather than opened.

**What "Subjected" Means**

*Sakhkhara* means to make something subservient, to put it at someone's disposal. The sūrah has used the word before, in verse 36, for the sacrificial camels, and the meaning is the same: a thing stronger than the person is made usable by him.

The word's asymmetry is what makes it a sign rather than a fact about human cleverness. The earth and everything in it is not weaker than a human being; the animals are stronger, the soils and waters are indifferent to him, and the whole arrangement could have been otherwise. That it is arranged for him is the claim, and the sūrah repeats it elsewhere in the same vocabulary: *“Have you not seen that Allah has subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and has lavished His favours upon you, both seen and unseen?”* (31:20).

**The Ships**

*Wa-l-fulka tajrī fī al-baḥri bi-amrih* — and the ships run upon the sea by His command.

Ships are the Qur'an's second most frequent sign after rain, and they are named here alongside the subjection of the earth because they are the exception that proves the rule. The land was subjected by being made habitable; the sea was not. It remains uninhabitable, and human beings cross it only by an arrangement they did not establish.

The phrase *bi-amrih*, by His command, is the operative one. A ship floats by physics and sails by wind, and the Qur'an attributes the whole operation to a command — which is not a denial of the mechanism but a claim about whose mechanism it is.

Sūrah al-Jāthiyah states it as a purpose: *“Allah is the One Who has subjected the sea for you so that ships may sail upon it by His command, and that you may seek His bounty”* (45:12). Sūrah Ibrāhīm pairs it with the rivers: *“He has subjected the ships for your service, sailing through the sea by His command, and has subjected the rivers for you”* (14:32).

**"He Holds the Sky From Falling"**

*Wa-yumsiku al-samāʾa an taqaʿa ʿalā al-arḍi illā bi-idhnih* — and He holds the sky back from falling on the earth except by His permission.

This is the most remarkable clause in the sequence, and it is remarkable because of what it describes: nothing happening.

Every other sign in the closing movement is an event — rain falling, a ship moving, the earth greening. This one is a restraint. The sky is held, and the holding is the thing being pointed at. Al-Rāzī took it as the divine maintenance of the natural order that makes seafaring possible at all; others understood *samāʾ* as a metonym for what comes down from it, the rain.

Both readings converge on the same claim: the stability of the world is an act rather than a given. A person standing on the ground is being kept from a catastrophe that would occur by default if it were not prevented — which is precisely the structure of the sūrah's whole argument about delay. Communities are held, the Hour is held, and the sky is held, and in every case the holding is an act of God rather than a property of matter.

**The Exception**

*Illā bi-idhnih* — except by His permission. The clause keeps the catastrophe available.

That is what stops the sign from being sentimental. The sky does not fail to fall because falling is impossible; it fails to fall because it is permitted not to. The permission is renewed, and the sūrah's audience is being asked to consider what the arrangement would look like if it were withdrawn.

The same structure appears in the sūrah's account of the destroyed towns: the normal state of a settlement is that it stands, and its standing was not a property of the town.

**The Subjection Is Not a Licence**

A reader should be careful here, because *sakhkhara* — made subservient — is a word that has been used to justify a great deal.

The sūrah's own use of the verb confines it. Verse 36 used it for the sacrificial animals and immediately followed it with the command to give the meat to the poor and the destitute. Verse 37 followed it by saying that none of the animal reaches God. The subjection in this sūrah is always accompanied by an obligation toward the people who have less.

The same structure is visible here. The earth is subjected, the ships are made to run, the sky is held up — and the verse closes by describing God as kind and merciful to humanity. The subjection is an act of kindness rather than a grant of dominion, and a person who uses it as the latter has missed the attribute the verse ends on.

**"Kind and Merciful to Humanity"**

*Inna Allāha bi-l-nāsi la-raʾūfun raḥīm* — God is, to humanity, Kind and Merciful.

The object of the mercy is specified: *bi-l-nāsi*, to people as such, not to the believers and not to a particular community. That specification matters in a sūrah that has spent much of its length describing what happens to those who reject.

The two names are close but not identical. *Raḥīm* is the one who acts with mercy; *raʾūf* is the intensive form, the one whose kindness is directed at the specific condition of the person receiving it. Placed at the end of a verse about ships, rain and a held-up sky, the pair describes a God whose general provision is also personal.

The verse is therefore not only an argument from nature. It is the answer to the question that the held sky raises: what is holding it up, and for whom.
