## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:19

> **Still they have labelled the angels, who are servants of the Most Compassionate, as female. Did they witness their creation? Their statement will be recorded, and they will be questioned!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim Stated Plainly and Corrected in Passing**

After three verses of question and description, the verse states the Makkan belief in its own terms: they made the angels female. The verb is the same jaʿala used in verse 15, to make or assign. They did not discover that the angels were female; they made them so, by decision. In the middle of stating the claim, the verse corrects it. The angels are described, in a parenthesis, as ʿibād al-raḥmān, servants of the Most Compassionate. That single phrase answers the whole doctrine of divine daughters. A servant is not a child. The angels' relation to God is one of ownership and obedience, not of kinship, and 21:26 states this exactly: they say the Compassionate has taken a child; glory be to Him; rather, they are honoured servants. The verse therefore does two things in one sentence. It reports what the idolaters said, and it tells them what the angels actually are, so that the rest of the verse can ask on what basis they said otherwise.

**A Variant Reading: "Those Who Are With the Compassionate"**

The word ʿibād, servants, is read by some of the canonical reciters as ʿinda, "with", so that the phrase becomes "the angels, who are with the Most Compassionate". Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both discuss the reading, and both find Qurʾānic support for it. In 7:206 the angels are those who are with your Lord, who do not disdain to worship Him. In 21:19 those who are with Him are not too proud to worship Him nor do they tire. The two readings are not in conflict; each is authentic and each carries a slightly different argument. "Servants of the Compassionate" refutes the idea of daughters by naming the angels' status. "Those with the Compassionate" refutes the idea by naming their location. Al-Rāzī draws out the second: the angels are with God and not with the disbelievers, so the disbelievers have no access to them and no ground for saying what sex they are. Together the readings say that the angels are servants and that they are elsewhere, and both facts make the Makkan claim about them baseless.

**"Did They Witness Their Creation?"**

The verse's central question is a-shahidū khalqahum, were they present at their creation? The question is a demand for evidence. The sex of a being is known by observation, and no Makkan had observed an angel, let alone the making of one. Al-Rāzī sets out the dilemma the question creates. Knowledge of the unseen comes in only two ways: by direct witness or by report from one who knows. The idolaters had no witness, since they were not present at the creation of the angels and admitted as much. And they had refused the only reliable report, since they rejected revelation, which is the sole channel through which such knowledge could reach human beings. They were left with neither, and a claim with neither is not knowledge. The same question is put in 37:150: or did We create the angels female while they were witnesses? The answer both times is silence. The Qurʾān treats this demand for evidence as decisive because the Quraysh themselves accepted it in every other area of life. No Makkan would accept a claim about a caravan's cargo from a man who had not seen it. The verse asks them to apply their own standard to their theology.

**The Statement Is Written Down**

The verse ends with a warning: sa-tuktabu shahādatuhum wa-yusʾalūn, their testimony will be written and they will be asked. The word shahādah, testimony, picks up the verb shahidū, did they witness, from the previous clause. They did not witness the creation of the angels, but they gave testimony about it anyway, and false testimony is a recorded offence. The passive verb tuktabu, will be written, refers to the angelic scribes who record every word, as 50:18 says: not a word does he utter but there is a watcher ready beside him. The idolaters' claim about the angels will be recorded by the angels. The irony is pointed. The beings they called daughters are the beings writing down the slander, and the record will be produced on the Day when they are questioned about it. The future tense of yusʾalūn, they will be asked, is the sūrah's first explicit mention of the accounting, and it lands on the specific sin of speaking about God without knowledge. That sin is named in 7:33 as among the gravest, alongside idolatry itself, and this verse shows why: it is idolatry's foundation.

**Speaking of God Without Knowledge**

The verse's deepest charge is not that the Makkans were wrong about the angels' sex but that they had no right to an opinion on it. They said something about the unseen with no means of knowing it, and the Qurʾān treats this as a moral fault, not merely an intellectual one. Man is told in 17:36 not to follow what he has no knowledge of, because hearing, sight, and heart will all be questioned. The Makkans' theology of angelic daughters was built on nothing: no observation, no revelation, no reasoning, only inherited assertion. The verse's three moves, the correction of the angels' status, the demand for evidence, and the warning of a recorded and questioned testimony, all press on that emptiness. The idolaters would respond, as the next verse records, by shifting the ground from evidence to fate, arguing that God must have willed their worship since He allowed it. But the question of this verse would remain unanswered. They had not witnessed the creation of the angels. They had said what they said anyway. And it was written.
