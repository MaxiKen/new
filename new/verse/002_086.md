## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:86

> These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world. So their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict in Two Lines**

The previous verse reported the community's conduct in detail; this one states in a single sentence what the conduct amounts to. There is no new accusation and no further evidence. The sūrah simply names the transaction, and the naming is the judgment.

That is how the Book often closes a case. The evidence has been laid out clause by clause, and the summary line identifies the nature of the act rather than repeating its details. A reader who has followed the passage from the covenant's terms through the ransoming of captives already knows the facts, and this verse tells him what to call them.

**The Direction of the Trade**

The image is of an exchange, and the wording is precise about what was given and what was taken. The community handed over the Hereafter and accepted this present life in return. The Book uses this commercial language for religious failure throughout, and it always preserves the direction of the trade, so the reader can see that a durable asset has been surrendered for a perishable one.

The buying and selling metaphor recurs in describing those who purchase error at the price of guidance, and the sūrah's own opening used a similar figure when it described people who sold guidance for misguidance and made no profit. The vocabulary is consistent enough that readers of the Book recognize the transaction as soon as it is named, and the trade in this verse is the same one in a different currency.

**What Makes This Trade Possible**

A trade requires a valuation, and the community in this passage had assigned its values before it acted. A shared tribal loyalty was worth more than a covenant; a moment's advantage over a rival outweighed a prohibition its own scripture stated plainly. The verse does not say these people denied the Hereafter; it says they priced it and found it worth less than something closer at hand.

The Book's treatment of this error is not to add another command but to reorder the reader's estimation of what lasts. Its repeated contrast between the passing nature of this world and the permanence of the next is aimed at exactly that faculty, and the sūrah's earlier verses used the same argument when they asked people not to expect the Fire to be brief and then described two permanent residences.

**Punishment Not Reduced**

The sentence's first consequence is that the punishment will not be lightened. The Book uses this formula for those whose position has been settled, and it places it here immediately after the community's claim that its punishment would be limited to a number of days. The answer to that claim is not a different number but the removal of the arithmetic altogether.

The formula also appears in the sūrah's description of the fate of those who persist in hostility, and it recurs in other passages where the Book describes people for whom intercession and compensation will not be accepted. The Book's point in each case is the same: the resources by which a person might ordinarily avert a penalty have been exhausted by the nature of the offence.

**Nor Will They Be Helped**

The second consequence is that no help will arrive. The Book uses this phrase for the removal of every possible source of assistance: allies, intercessors, and substitutes. In the sūrah's account of the Day of Judgment no soul will pay anything on another's behalf, and no plea will be entertained for those whose account has been closed.

That second clause is where the community described here is cut off from the loyalty on which it relied. The tribal system that made their conduct reasonable provided protection, mutual aid, and the expectation that one's people would stand by one, and the verse states that this system does not extend to the court that will hear this case.

**The Warning Reaches the Believers**

The verse is addressed to a community that had been given a scripture and had acted on part of it. The warning is therefore available to any community in the same position, and the sūrah will shortly address its own followers in these terms. Later chapters speak of people trading faith for worldly advantage in exactly the commercial vocabulary used here, and they include the Prophet's companions among the people addressed.

That is the pattern of the whole passage. The Book reports an earlier community's failure in order to establish the principle, and then applies the principle to the people now holding the revelation. A reader who studies the sūrah to learn what happened to other people will find the same standard turned on him before the chapter ends.

**The Verdict Left Unqualified**

The sentence attaches no community to the description and no duration to the penalty. What it fixes is the nature of the choice and the finality of its outcome. The verses that follow return to the history of this people with a different subject, the messengers sent to it, and the account of a community that refused what it recognized continues to build the case the chapter is making.

**Who Pays the Price for a Community's Choices**

A trade is normally made by the people who benefit from it, and this verse states that the community sold its own souls. The leaders who arranged the factions and the ransoms are not named separately from the people who fought in their wars. The Book spreads the responsibility across everyone who accepted the arrangement, which is why the passage has taken such care to describe the community's conduct as a collective act. That refusal to separate the architects from the followers is one reason the sūrah's account reaches across generations. A community's inherited assumptions are held by each of its members individually, and the sentence here treats the wrong choice as the property of whoever makes it, regardless of whose idea it originally was or which ancestor first struck the bargain.
