## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:86

> These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world. So their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name of the Transaction**

Two sentences close the case the previous verse opened, and the first is a diagnosis: *these are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world*. The recital has reached the underlying form of every crime it has filed in this passage. The distortion after understanding, the two rooms' two scripts, the expulsion and the ransom, the covenant rationed — each is now named as one act wearing different clothes: a purchase. The verb is the market's own — buying and selling — and the sūrah has been building this economics of the sacred since its early verses: the signs exchanged for a cheap price (2:41), the souls sold for a price the sūrah elsewhere calls miserable indeed (2:102), the book written by hands and sold for a fleeting gain (2:79). Verse 86 states the general law behind the ledger: every sin against known revelation is a trade in which the Hereafter is the currency and the world's life the merchandise. The trade's structure explains the selectivity the previous verse indicted — the cheap commandments kept, the costly ones broken — because a trader selects by price: what the world pays for is bought, what it does not pay for is left on the shelf, and the shelf's contents are the covenant's expensive terms. The verse's demonstrative — *these are the ones* — points at a defined class rather than a moment of weakness: the people whose governing orientation has become the transaction itself.

**The Terms of the Trade, and Its Arithmetic**

The arithmetic of the purchase is the verse's unspoken exhibit, and the Qur'an files it wherever the trade appears. The merchandise is *the life of this world* — a bounded span whose longest lease ends at the grave, whose gains are consumed in the consuming, whose entire quantity the recital has just watched tribes kill and expel each other to hold. The price is the Hereafter — the unbounded estate the two previous interlude-verses defined: the Fire's forever and the Garden's forever, permanence on both sides of the line (81–82). A finite good purchased with an infinite one is the trade's fixed structure, and its fixed character is why the recital calls the price miserable wherever it appears — the same astonishment running through the sūrah's economics that the sacred is always sold cheap. The traders of verse 86 are not fools in worldly reckoning; the Madinan alliances the previous verse described were shrewd — fortress, position, market, survival, all real goods, pursued with real skill. The verse's indictment is of the reckoning's horizon: a shrewdness that prices the visible estate correctly and the invisible one at zero is not wisdom but a category error with eternal consequences, and the error is the trade. What makes the arithmetic tragic rather than merely wrong is that the Hereafter paid away is not a stranger's claim but the traders' own covenant, ratified and witnessed (84) — they are selling their own inheritance, and the buyers are their appetites.

**“Will Not Be Reduced”**

The sentence's first clause answers the passage's earliest false comfort with total precision: *their punishment will not be reduced*. The numbered-days claim of verse 80 had promised a schedule — the Fire's touch capped at a count; this clause removes not only the schedule but every mitigation below it: no reduction, no lightening, no gradation of the sentence once it begins. The two negations of the verse work as a pair against the two resources a condemned party reaches for — the sentence's intensity and the sentence's isolation — and the first negation binds the intensity. The exegetical discussion of verse 80 had carefully preserved Islam's actual doctrine: release from the Fire is possible for whom Allah wills, and intercession is real (11:107); al-Rāzī's distinction stood between a promise of release, which no one holds, and a possibility of release, which Allah has not closed. Verse 86 addresses the traders specifically, and for them the text is flat: no تخفيف — no lightening. The precision matters for the verse's justice. This is not the general metaphysics of Hell but the sentence of a defined class — those whose orientation became the trade of eternity for the world — and the unmitigated wording marks the class's defining feature: they already had the covenant, the witness, the charter, and the visible costs of every term. A reduction is a mercy applied to weakness; the trade of the Hereafter is not weakness but settled policy, and the sentence matches the policy's permanence with its own.

**“Nor Will They Be Helped”**

The second negation closes the second resource: *nor will they be helped*. No advocate, no rescuer, no ally stepping between the trader and his sentence — and the negation lands with particular force on the verse's historical addressees, because alliances were the exact instrument of their trade. The tribes bought the world's life through pacts — with each other's enemies, against each other's kin — and the verse's judgment unmakes the instrument in the only court that survives: the alliance-network that paid so well in Madina has no standing at the Day, no voice, no hand. The *nuṣrah* denied here is the whole vocabulary of help the Qur'an keeps for the believers: the helper, the protector, the patron — names the traders spent in their purchase, for the world's life cannot pay them back in the court where payment matters. The clause also answers the ransom-paradox at its root. The community of verse 85 helped its captives — ransoming them, funding their release — while refusing the help its covenant owed the expelled; the sentence returns the measure: on the Day, no one funds their release, because the help they rationed in the world is not merely withheld but structurally unavailable — they traded the estate whose currency is help. The two negations together are the sentence's completeness: the punishment undiminished in degree, the condemned unaided in person — a judgment with no interior mitigations and no exterior intercessors, filed against a trade made with full knowledge of both sides of the counter.

**The Trade Is Still Open — Until It Isn't**

The verse names a class and sentences it, and its pastoral edge is that the class has open enrollment in every generation, with the enrollment form the reader recognizes: the small preference of the immediate over the eternal, repeated until it becomes orientation. Every deferred prayer for a meeting, every dishonest word for a contract, every alliance kept against a commandment broken, every expulsion performed and ransom paid — the trade rarely announces itself as a sale of the Hereafter; it presents as management of this week. The recital's remedy for the trader is the recital itself: verse 86 exists to be heard mid-transaction, to name the purchase while the buyer still holds the goods and can put them down. The door the sūrah keeps open stands open here — *do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins* (39:53) — and its width is exactly the width of the trade's reversibility: what was sold can be renounced, the covenant re-signed, the orientation turned, and the class-exit is repentance, the one transaction the recital never caps. But the verse's two negations mark where reversibility ends: at the sentence. *Not reduced, nor helped* is spoken from the far side of the last opportunity, and its finality is the verse's mercy to the living — the trader who hears it while hearing still works is being told, in the plainest terms the Book possesses, that the counter closes, that the merchandise was never worth the price, and that the audit clause beneath every verse of this passage is running while there is still something the audit can receive.
