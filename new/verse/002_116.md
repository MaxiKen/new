## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:116

> They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him! In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Will.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim Reported**

The verse reports a statement made about God and answers it in the same sentence. The Book does not quote the claim as a quotation from a particular community in this place; it states it in general terms, and the commentaries applied it to more than one group.

The objection is not new in the sūrah, which has been addressing statements made about God throughout the passage, and it is not limited to the doctrine of one religion. The Book's answer is stated in three moves: a declaration of God's transcendence, a statement about ownership, and a description of the created order's relation to its Lord. The Book records the claim without naming its authors, which allows the sentence to address the position rather than the community that held it. That choice matches the sūrah's practice elsewhere of stating a doctrine in general form and letting the reader apply it to whatever quarter fits.

**The Several Forms of the Claim**

Commentators did not restrict the sentence to Christian theology. They also connected it to the belief of the pagan Arabs that the angels were daughters of God, a claim the Book refutes in more than one place, and to other traditions that assigned a special standing to particular figures.

The Book responds to all of these together because its argument is about God rather than about the figure being associated with Him. Its objection is to the possibility of the divine nature being described by a relation that belongs to created beings, and the sentence's structure makes that clear by turning immediately to the created order. The Book's answer covers all of them in one sentence because the error being corrected is the same in each case: a created being is assigned a relation to God that belongs only to God. The variety of the claims shows how persistent the mistake has been, and the sūrah treats it as a single position to be answered.

**"Glory Be to Him"**

The Arabic word translated as an exclamation of glory carries the sense of disassociation: God is far above what is being said of Him. The phrase appears throughout the Book where a claim about God needs to be cleared away, and it functions here as a rejection rather than as a protest.

The commentators noted that the word is used this way in connection with claims about God having a son, and that its force is to place the divine nature outside the category the claim assumes. The sentence therefore answers the assertion before it explains why the assertion is impossible, which is the Book's usual order when a statement about God is at issue.

**Al-Rāzī's Arguments**

The classical theologians worked out the implications of the divine attributes in a systematic way, and al-Rāzī's discussion of this verse is representative of the mainstream position. He argued, first, that every being described in the Book as God's offspring is a contingent and created thing, whereas offspring must be begotten. Second, he observed that such an offspring would be either eternal or created: if eternal, nothing would distinguish father from son, and if created, the offspring would be a made thing. Third, a son resembles his father in some respects and differs in others, which would require that each be composed of parts, and composition cannot be affirmed of God. Fourth, he noted that human beings have children out of need, pride, or anxiety about the future, and none of these can be attributed to the One who is free of all needs.

The arguments are the work of later scholars rather than of the verse itself, and they show how the community's theologians defended the position the sentence states. What the Book provides is the principle they reasoned from: God is unlike anything in creation, and every statement about Him must be consistent with that.

**To Him Belongs Whatever Is in the Heavens and the Earth**

The sentence then answers the claim by describing ownership. Everything that exists is God's creation and possession, and the relation between Him and every being is that of maker to made. The claim being refuted treats one of these beings as though it stood in a different relation to Him, and the verse closes that possibility by placing every being in the same category.

The Book uses this kind of argument regularly: a statement about God is corrected by a statement about the created order. The reader is not asked to weigh competing doctrines but to consider whether the relation being proposed matches the relation that actually exists between God and everything that is not God.

**All Are Devoutly Obedient to Him**

The verse ends by describing the created order as obedient, and the word chosen carries a range of meanings that includes compliance, humility, standing, and silence. The commentators explained it as a description of the state in which all beings acknowledge their Lord, whether willingly or under compulsion.

Some read the clause as a statement about the Day of Resurrection, when every being will submit and stand silent before God, and others applied it to the figures whom people had wrongly associated with Him, noting that they themselves are sincere worshippers. Both readings support the verse's argument: whatever standing the claimants were assigning to a created being, that being belongs among the worshippers rather than among those who share divinity.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Here**

The sūrah has been dealing with people who misstated the truths they were given, and it now corrects a claim about God Himself. The placement of the sentence between an account of obstructed worship and a statement about the divine creative command gives the passage a shape: the Book clears away false claims about God, establishes who He is, and then shows what His command achieves. The sentence also prepares the reader for the two verses that follow, which answer the claim by describing God as the originator of everything and as the one whose command brings things into being. The placement is deliberate: the false claim is stated, rejected, and then replaced with a statement of who God is.

**Why the Denial of Sonship Matters to the Book's Argument**

The claim the verse rejects touches the centre of the Book's teaching about God, and that is why it is answered with such severity. If any being could stand in a relation of offspring to God, then the divine nature would be shared, and the distinction between creator and created on which every other claim in the Book rests would be dissolved. The sūrah has been describing communities that received revelation and misused it, and the doctrinal error named here is presented as the deepest form of that misuse. The Book's answer is not a negotiation about titles but a statement about what exists: everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to God, and every being is subject to His will. The verses that follow continue the argument by describing how things come to exist, so that the reader sees the difference between the One who commands and everything that answers to the command.
