## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:116

> They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him! In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Will.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim and All Its Claimants**

The verse quotes the greatest of theological claims in four words — *Allah has offspring* — and its generality is deliberate: the commentators do not restrict it to Christian theology, because the claim-family has more members than one church. The pagan Arabs held the angels to be daughters of Allah, a belief the Qur'an confronts in a running polemic (16:57, 37:149, 43:16, 52:39); Sūrah al-Tawbah files the two scriptural versions side by side: *the Jews say, "Ezra is the son of Allah," while the Christians say, "The Messiah is the son of Allah"* — baseless assertions, in the verse's own phrase, parroting the words of earlier disbelievers (9:30). The disavowals run through the Book wherever the claim appears: Sūrah al-Māʾidah's two answers (5:17, 5:72), Sūrah Maryam's (19:35), this verse's. The gathering of claimants matters for reading the verse correctly: it is not a sectarian broadside but the negation of a structural possibility — divinity with filiation, in any community's version, with any named son or daughter. The *they say* of the verse is therefore plural in reference and singular in target: every speaker of the sentence, across every confession that ever framed it, is answered by the same four-word rebuttal that follows. And the rebuttal's first word is not an argument but an exclamation, which is the verse's first instruction about the claim's standing: some propositions are not weighed but refused.

**Subḥānahu: The Word That Removes**

The exclamation — *glory be to Him* — is the Qur'an's *tasbīḥ*, and the exegetes mark its full force: *subḥāna* carries disassociation along with glorification, so that the phrase means both *Allah is glorified* and *Allah is far beyond such a thing* (al-Qushayrī, al-Ṭabarī). The double sense is the exclamation's theological work. To glorify is not merely to praise but to remove — to declare Allah clear of every deficiency, and the child-claim is filed as a deficiency not because offspring is base in itself but because having offspring imports need, division, and kind into a nature that admits none. Sūrah Maryam pairs the exclamation with the same claim's denial in one breath: *it is not for Allah to take a son! Glory be to Him* (19:35) — the *laysa* of impossibility followed by the *subḥāna* of removal, negation and glorification as one act. The tasbīḥ's placement teaches the recital's audience a reflex: the sentence *Allah has offspring* is not to be entertained, examined for its merits, or held in suspension — it is to be glorified against, instantly, the way the tradition's etiquette trains the tongue to answer every attribution of defect. The exclamation is also the verse's argument in compressed form. Glory belongs to one who is beyond need; the claim describes one who needs an heir, a companion, a second; the two descriptions cannot hold one subject, and the tasbīḥ declares which one the subject bears.

**The Arguments from Divine Oneness**

The tradition's developed case against divine filiation runs directly from tawḥīd — the Oneness, Transcendence, and Absoluteness of Allah — and al-Rāzī's four arguments are its classic statement. First: the beings called Allah's offspring are contingent, created things, and offspring in the proper sense are begotten — begottenness and createdness cannot be the same relation. Second: the alleged offspring is either eternal or not; if eternal, there is nothing to make one party father and the other son, since both stand in the same necessity; if not eternal, it is a created being, and creation is not filiation. Third: an offspring shares its progenitor's kind — similar in some respects, different in others — which requires both to be composed of parts, and composition cannot be said of Allah. Fourth: offspring are taken for needs — companionship, care in age, pride of continuation — and every such need is excluded from the divine nature. Al-Rāzī adds the concession that sharpens his rigor: a purely metaphorical fatherhood — Allah as prior, as responsible, as the cause of a being's existence — can be entertained as language, but it reflects no actual filial relation, because the metaphor's content is exactly what the four arguments remove. The case's foundation is the principle the verse's next clause states: Allah is unlike anything in creation and beyond need of all things, so no relationship that relativizes Him — and fatherhood is the most relativizing of relations, binding the father to the son's nature — can enter His description. Islam's emphasis on the Absolute thus denies filiation not on scriptural fiat alone but on the structure of absoluteness itself.

**Ownership as the Refutation**

The verse's own argument arrives in its third sentence, and it is an argument from property: *in fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth*. The *in fact* — *balā*-force correction — replaces the claimed relation with the actual one. Everything within the two horizons stands to Allah as belonging stands to owner, creature to Creator; the candidate offspring are inside the belonging, and whatever belongs cannot be of the Owner's nature, because belonging is itself the mark of createdness. Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ states the same doctrine in its compressed negations: He has never had offspring, nor was He born (112:3) — neither generating nor generated, the two directions of the filial relation closed together. The ownership-clause also explains why the claim is not merely false but confused: it mistakes a category. A son would be a second of a kind; the verse's universe contains no kind of which Allah is a member — He is not one being among beings but the Owner of all being, and ownership has no offspring, only property. The argument's shape answers the claimants' own logic: the honor they sought for their named figures — Ezra, the Messiah, the angels — is real honor, but it is the honor of property exalted, not of kinship shared; and the verse's final clause states what the honored figures actually are, which is where the refutation becomes a portrait.

**All Subject to His Will**

The closing words describe everything in the two horizons with one participle: *all are subject to His Will* — *qānitūn*, from a root whose range the commentators unfold as compliance, humility, standing, and silence together. The four senses gather into one posture: the creature standing before its Lord, compliant, humble, silent — the position of the qānit in prayer's own vocabulary, the standing that precedes the bow. The exegetes read the participle at three scopes. At the eschatological scope, it describes the Day when every soul is forced into exactly this state — obeying, standing, silent before Allah (al-Qushayrī's reading), the world's rebellion suspended with the world. At the cosmic scope, it parallels the prostration-verse of al-Raʿd: to Allah bow all in the heavens and the earth, willingly or unwillingly, and their shadows at morning and evening (13:15) — the qunūt of everything, including the things that deny it. And at the personal scope — the reading the context most invites — the participle names the very figures the claim tried to promote: Jesus, Ezra, the angels, far from being offspring, are themselves sincere in their worship of Allah, qānit in the same standing as every other servant. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the personal scope in its own sentence: the Messiah would never be too proud to be a servant of Allah, nor the angels nearest to Him (4:172). The verse thus ends by turning the claim against itself: the alleged sons are witnesses for the prosecution, their exalted names enrolled in the register of the devoted — property, servants, qānitūn — and the ownership they adorn is the exact opposite of the kinship that was claimed for them.
