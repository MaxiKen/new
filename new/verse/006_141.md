## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:141

> He is the One Who produces gardens—both cultivated and wild — and palm trees, crops of different flavours, olives, and pomegranates— similar ˹in shape˺, but dissimilar ˹in taste˺. Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gardens and Their Produce**

He is the One Who produces gardens, both cultivated and wild, and palm trees, crops of different flavours, olives, and pomegranates, similar in shape but dissimilar in taste. The verse returns to the theme of provision after the passage on the audience's regulations, and it lists the produce of the region in the detail the sūrah has used before. The gardens appear in the sūrah's earlier account of the rain that produces green stalks, clustered grain, dates hanging within reach, vineyards, olives, and pomegranates (6:99).

The phrase both cultivated and wild covers the two kinds of planting the audience knew, and the description of the fruits as similar in shape and dissimilar in taste repeats the observation the sūrah made in its earlier passage. The Book's method in these verses is to lay the provision before the reader as evidence of the provider, and the variety of the produce is part of the demonstration.

**The Command to Eat and the Dues**

Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest. The verse attaches a command to the description, and the command includes both the permission to eat and the obligation to give. The Book's word for the dues describes what is owed from the produce, and the commentators understood it as the share due at the time of harvesting, which the community was required to set aside.

The Book's treatment of the duties attached to wealth appears throughout its instructions to the community. The sūrah of the Cow describes the believers as those who spend from what God has provided and sets out the kinds of giving the community was to undertake, and the sūrah of the Bee commands the believers to eat of what God has provided and to give thanks. The verse in front of us joins the permission and the obligation in a single sentence, and the arrangement reflects the Book's treatment of provision as a gift held in trust.

**The Prohibition of Waste**

But do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful. The sentence closes the verse with a prohibition, and the Book's term for waste describes spending beyond what is needed or destroying what could be used. The prohibition appears in the Book's instructions about wealth in several places, and the sūrah of the Night Journey includes waste among the practices it condemns, describing the wasteful as brothers of the devils.

The prohibition attaches to the harvest that the verse has just described, and the connection between the two sentences is practical. The produce that God has given is to be eaten, shared, and used, and what is left over is not to be destroyed. The Book's approach to provision is that it is received for use rather than for display, and the sentence states the limit.

**What the Verse Says About the Present Order**

The verse describes the created provision without attaching it to the audience's rules, and the omission is part of the argument. The gardens, the palms, the crops, and the fruits are produced by God, and the command that follows concerns eating and giving rather than the classification of the produce into permitted and forbidden categories. The sūrah has just finished describing the system the audience had built around those categories, and the verse in front of us states the simple arrangement the Book puts in its place.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere makes the same contrast. The sūrah of the Heights asks who has forbidden the adornments and the good provisions that God has brought forth for His servants, and it answers that they belong to the believers in this world and will be theirs alone on the day of resurrection (7:32). The verse in front of us belongs with that passage, and the two together state the Book's position on the goods of the world.

**The Link to the Earlier Passage on Provision**

The verse echoes the sūrah's earlier account of the rain and the produce of the earth (6:99), and the two passages share the same details: the palms, the crops, the olives, the pomegranates, and the observation about the similarity of shape and the difference of taste. The earlier passage closed with the statement that these are signs for people who believe, and the verse in front of us closes with the command to eat, to give, and not to waste.

The repetition gives the sūrah its shape. The provision was presented first as evidence of the provider, and it is presented here as the material of the community's obligations. The movement from sign to duty is the pattern the sūrah follows in its treatment of everything it describes.

**What the Verse Says to the Audience**

The audience had spent its ingenuity on rules governing the produce of its fields and herds, and the verse tells the people who made those rules what the provision is for. What God has given is to be eaten, what is due from it is to be paid, and what remains is not to be squandered. The three instructions stand in place of the elaborate system that the sūrah has just examined and condemned.

The Book's treatment of the community's use of wealth elsewhere supplies the wider framework. The sūrah of the Cow commands the believers to spend from what they have been provided before the day comes when there will be no trade, no friendship, and no intercession, and it presents the giving as a feature of the community's life rather than as an occasional act. The verse in front of us states the same principle at the point where the harvest is gathered.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse returns to the livestock, stating that some cattle are fit for labour while others are too small, and it instructs the community to eat of what God has provided and not to follow the footsteps of Satan, who is an avowed enemy (6:142). The pair of verses covers the produce of the fields and the produce of the herds, and the instruction in both cases is the same.

The passage that follows takes up the argument about the pairs of animals and the claim that God had forbidden certain of them (6:143–144), and it answers the claim by asking the audience for the knowledge on which it rests. The verse in front of us supplies the positive command that the argument presupposes, and the verses that follow remove the false restrictions that had been placed on the provision.
