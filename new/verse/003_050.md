## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:50

> And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you. I have come to you with a sign from your Lord, so be mindful of Allah and obey me.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Confirming the Torah, Once More**

The speech continues and returns to the theme the sūrah opened with. The messenger confirms the Torah revealed before him, which is what the Book says of the Gospel itself: We gave him the Gospel containing guidance and light and confirming what was revealed in the Torah (5:46). The same relation is stated of the Qur'an at the start of this sūrah, which comes confirming what was before it.

The sentence therefore places Jesus in a line rather than at a break. He does not arrive with a new religion that displaces what his people had; he arrives attesting it and correcting what had gone wrong in its handling. That is the office of every messenger in the Book, and it is the model against which the sūrah measures the communities that received scriptures and disputed them.

**"I Will Legalize Some of What Had Been Forbidden"**

The second clause announces a change in legal content, and it says *some*: a portion of what had been forbidden is to be made lawful. Commentators discussed what the forbidden items were, and two explanations were recorded. On one reading they were the restrictions imposed on the Jews as a consequence of their conduct, which the Book describes when it says it forbade them certain foods that had been lawful and names the specific categories of animals and fats (4:160; 6:146). On the other, they were burdens the community had taken upon itself over the generations rather than obligations from God.

The first reading fits the context more closely, since the sūrah is describing what a messenger was sent to do for a people whose laws had become heavy. It is also supported by the Book's account of Jesus describing his mission as coming with wisdom to clarify some of what the people differed about (43:63), which corresponds to a correction of practice rather than the introduction of a new law. The two readings converge on a single fact: what the messenger loosened concerned rules of conduct within Israel's own tradition, and the change was announced as a relief.

**What Was Not Loosened**

The verse's wording guards against a misreading that has followed this text since it was revealed. Nothing in the change touches the moral commandments, which the sūrah's own list of perennial obligations elsewhere treats as the substance of what every community has been told: worship of the one God, truthfulness, justice, kindness to parents, and the prohibitions of murder, theft, and adultery.

The Book states the principle in the sūrah of the Cattle, where those commandments are gathered and described as what God has enjoined, and it treats them as the constant content of revelation across the messengers. A messenger who came to lighten a dietary rule did not come to lighten the prohibition of killing, and the sūrah's later verses on justice and honesty continue to hold those obligations exactly where they were.

**The Sign, Repeated as a Credential**

The verse repeats the claim that he has come with a sign from his Lord, using almost the same words he used a line earlier. The repetition is a device of the speech's own rhetoric: a prophet addressing a suspicious people states his authorisation more than once, since the whole of what follows depends on the audience accepting it.

The Book's narrative elsewhere shows the same audience asking for exactly that: proof, a sign, evidence of the kind that would leave them no choice. The speech in this verse gives them what they asked for in the form of deeds they could witness — a bird, a healing, a resurrection — and then, having supplied the evidence, it moves to the demand that the evidence implies.

**"So Be Mindful of Allah and Obey Me"**

The command couples the two obligations the sūrah has already joined in its instruction to the community: fear God and obey the messenger. The phrase itself is a formula of the prophets, and the Book puts it in the mouth of messenger after messenger — Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, and Shuʿayb each say, in the sūrah that reports their missions, the same words: so fear Allah, and obey me (26:108; 26:126, and the parallel verses throughout that sūrah).

The uniformity is the point. A preacher of a new doctrine with a personal claim to obedience would be a person to distrust; a messenger who says what every messenger has said, and who asks for obedience in the same breath as mindfulness of God, is speaking in the register of revelation. The Book's prophets answer a people's question about authority in advance by making their claim identical across centuries.

**What Obedience to a Messenger Involves**

The sūrah has already stated the formula in its own voice, instructing the community to obey Allah and His Messenger and describing the consequence of a refusal. It will return to the theme when it describes the family of ʿImrān and the disciples who declare their submission.

The logic behind the command is that a messenger's authority is not his own. He is obeyed because he was sent, and his words carry the weight of the sender; a community that disobeys the messenger has not simply disagreed with a man, it has declined a message it can no longer separate from him. That is why the Book consistently joins the two names in one command rather than presenting obedience to the messenger as an additional layer.

**A Reformer Inside a Tradition**

Taken as a whole, the speech in this verse shows a prophet who works within a known tradition: confirming the scripture of his people, adjusting the burdens that had grown heavy, and calling them back to the thing the tradition was always for.

The portrait is important for the sūrah's audience, which had its own scriptures, its own disputes, and its own accumulated practices. What the messenger of this verse does with the tradition he inherited is the sūrah's model for what any community ought to do with a revelation it holds: attest what is genuine, relieve what was never required, and return to the demands that have been there from the beginning.
