## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:4

> As for the polytheists who have honoured every term of their treaty with you and have not supported an enemy against you, honour your treaty with them until the end of its term. Surely Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception Is the Rule’s Moral Center**

This verse prevents the opening proclamation from being read as a command to abandon every treaty with every polytheist. It identifies the protected group by conduct: they have honored the agreement and have not aided an enemy. Their religious identity does not erase the obligation created by their faithful behavior. The passage is therefore not sorting people only by creed. It is also sorting political relationships by trustworthiness.

The first condition is broad: they have not diminished the treaty in any respect. The second adds an indirect form of breach: they have not supported someone acting against the Muslims. A party cannot claim neutrality in its own name while secretly supplying the force that attacks its treaty partner. By naming both direct and indirect betrayal, the verse closes a familiar loophole. Loyalty is measured by what an alliance enables, not only by the words written on its face.

**Complete the Agreement to Its End**

The command to honor the treaty until its term is complete uses the language of bringing something to fulfillment. A covenant is not merely tolerated until a more profitable arrangement appears. Its endpoint is part of its meaning. If the other party has not broken it, the Muslim side is required to carry it through. The promise binds even when the partner is not a believer, because the real witness to the promise is Allah.

This principle echoes the Qur'anic command, Fulfill the covenant of Allah when you have pledged it (16:91). It also accords with the Prophet's public proclamation during the pilgrimage, which preserved the agreements of those who had not violated them. The verse gives a concrete test for piety. A person may appear religious in worship and speech, yet the truth of his God-consciousness is tested when keeping his word costs political advantage. The faithful treaty partner receives protection because fidelity itself has moral weight.

**The Passage Distinguishes Groups Carefully**

The surrounding verses contain strong language about hostile idolaters, but verse 4 refuses collective blame. The chapter does not say that because some tribes helped break a pact, all tribes must be treated as though they did. A sound agreement remains sound until its own conditions change. This is a principle of legal evidence: responsibility follows a demonstrated act, not an inherited label.

Early commentators discussed which tribe or tribes were intended by the exception. Some connected it with members of Banū Bakr who did not join the attack that violated the wider arrangement. Others proposed different historical identifications. Their disagreement does not remove the verse's operative rule. Whatever the precise referent, the text itself establishes the standard: those who stand by their terms are to be met with the same steadiness. The historical question narrows the application; it does not weaken the obligation.

**Taqwā Appears in Diplomacy**

The closing statement, Allah loves those who are mindful of Him, gives treaty law a spiritual foundation. Taqwā is often associated with private worship, but here it appears in public relations. Keeping an agreement is an act of reverence because it requires a person to remember Allah while dealing with people who cannot enforce every detail. If the only reason to keep a promise is fear of retaliation, the promise reveals little about character. Faithfulness when oversight is limited shows that the covenant is being kept for the One who sees.

The verse also corrects a narrow idea of loyalty. Some people reserve honesty for members of their own group and regard outsiders as legitimate targets of manipulation. This verse reverses that instinct. A treaty partner who has kept faith has a claim upon fair treatment precisely because the covenant has made him a partner in an obligation. Religious difference does not make a fulfilled promise disposable.

**Trust Requires Both Mercy and Memory**

Honoring the term does not mean ignoring evidence of breach. The verse is careful about its conditions, and the next verses address what happens when those conditions disappear. Mercy is not blindness. It is disciplined fidelity toward the person who has earned the protection promised. Conversely, suspicion is not wisdom when it treats every partner as already guilty. The believer must remember both sides: investigate conduct honestly, then act according to what the evidence establishes.

This has applications beyond treaties. An employee who has fulfilled a contract, a borrower who is meeting the agreed schedule, a neighbor who has respected a boundary, and a spouse who has kept a promise are all examples of people whose reliability should not be punished because someone else behaved badly. The verse gives a general moral rule: do not make the faithful pay for the faithless. Allah's love is attached to the person who keeps the line when the easier path would be to erase it.

**Faithfulness Is Not Naivety**

The verse does not ask the believers to surrender judgment. It asks them to judge accurately. A treaty that has been honored remains binding; a treaty that has been broken is treated under different provisions. That distinction is the opposite of naivety. Naivety ignores danger. Taqwā refuses to invent danger where the record does not show it.

The verse's final lesson is therefore both political and personal. Public strength must be restrained by a promise. Private piety must become dependable conduct. A believer cannot claim to fear Allah while using a covenant as a temporary tool and discarding it when convenient. The people of this verse are called worthy of protection, and the believers who protect them are called loved by Allah. The chapter's stern opening is thus governed from within by a simple demand: where the agreement has been kept, keep it too.
