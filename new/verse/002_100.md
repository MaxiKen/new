## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:100

> Why is it that every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside? In fact, most of them do not believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Habit Written Into the Grammar**

The verse opens with a question whose particle — *kullamā*, every time — carries the entire indictment. Not once, not in a crisis, not under compulsion: *every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside*. The iteration is the finding. A single breach is an event with causes; a breach repeated at every covenant is a disposition, and the question form — *why is it that every time?* — asks the audience to read the repetition as evidence of what the community has become. The subject of the casting is precise in a way that matters: *a group of them* — farīq, a party, a faction. Not all, each time; a group, every time. The distribution is diagnostic of a community whose covenant-breaking has become institutional — always enough of them, at every station, to make the covenant inoperative, while always fewer than all, so that the exception keeps the accusation from being a blanket. The recital has been filing the stations: the charter of verse 83 turned away from, the witnessed bond of verse 84 violated in Madina's wars, the Torah's testimony to the coming prophet cast aside at his arrival. The verse gathers the stations into the pattern, and the pattern into a character diagnosis delivered in the tone of exhausted recognition — the question a prosecutor asks when the exhibits have run out and only the summary remains.

**Which Covenant? The Occasions and the Pattern**

The exegetes supply three historical referents and then transcend all three. The first: the Jews of Madina pleaded, against the Prophet's ﷺ call, that they had never made any pact or covenant concerning Muhammad specifically — a denial of any obligation toward him — and this verse was revealed against that plea. The second: the covenant in view is the old one, the promise the community had made to follow the prophet who was to come and give them victory over the idolatrous Arabs (the promise verse 89 recorded them praying by). The third: the betrayals of the alliance with the Prophet ﷺ himself — the covenants of mutual defense in Madina broken by siding with the Quraysh, the conduct the recital will address in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ as well (4:51–52's context). Al-Rāzī's note settles the method: the meaning is not restricted to any particular incident. The iterative grammar supports him — *every time* spans occasions, and the three referents the tradition preserves are instances of the law rather than its exhaust. The weighing is worth stating because it decides how the verse reads: taken as incident-report, it belongs to Madina's politics; taken as the tradition finally takes it, it is a standing description of covenant-people's covenant-conduct, with the incidents as its evidentiary base. The recital needs both readings at once — the occasions make the verse historical, the iteration makes it permanent — and the verse's grammar is built to hold them together.

**“Most of Them Do Not Believe”**

The second sentence delivers the cause, and the cause inverts the expected order: *in fact, most of them do not believe*. One might expect the sequence to run the other way — covenants broken, therefore faith lost — but the verse files disbelief as the ground and the casting as the growth. The habit of discarding bonds is not a failure of faith under pressure; it is what a community's life looks like when faith is absent and covenant-language remains in use. The word *most* does its precise work against the earlier exceptions: the few who kept the charter (83), the few excepted at every sealing (4:155's remnant) — most is not all, and the verse's justice needs the margin. But *most* also fixes the community's center of gravity: a people whose majority does not believe will produce, at every covenant, the group that casts it aside, because the casting is simply the majority's actual relation to the bond expressing itself when the bond is renewed. The clause completes the passage's epistemology of refusal: verse 95 located the unspoken wish in the hands' record, verse 99 located denial in rebellion, and verse 100 locates the broken covenant in unbelief — consistently, the recital traces outward behavior to the interior's actual content, and consistently finds that content other than what the profession claims. The covenant-language survives in the community's mouth; the belief that would make it binding does not survive in the community's chest; and the gap between the two is what *every time* measures.

**The Covenant as the Qur'an's Central Bond**

The weight of this verse depends on the weight of covenant in the recital's whole theology, and the sūrah has built that weight deliberately. Covenant in the Qur'an is not a contract between negotiating parties but a bond whose terms descend and whose acceptance is owed — Allah's covenant with Israel at the charter (83), the witnessed bond against blood and expulsion (84), the covenant of every prophet's community, and behind all of them the primordial taking of testimony from the children of Adam at 7:172, the covenant every person ratifies by existing as a creature of Allah. Breaking such a bond is therefore not breach of contract but rupture of relationship — which is why the sūrah's causal chain at verse 83's parallel in al-Māʾidah runs covenant-breaking directly into hardening of hearts: *for breaking their covenant, We condemned them and hardened their hearts* (5:13). The group that casts the covenant aside at every station is the same population whose hearts verse 74 found harder than stone, and the two verses describe one process from its two ends — the will's repeated exit producing the organ's settled stone. The recital's covenant-theology gives verse 100 its tragic register: each casting is not a fresh start but a deposit, and a community that casts at every covenant is, without noticing, completing the hardening its own history reports.

**Every Time: The Renewal Test**

The verse's iteration has a use for its hearing community, and the use is the reason it stands inside a recitation addressed to believers as much as to its historical subjects. Covenants are renewed constantly in the religious life — at every prayer, every Ramaḍān, every moment of clarity in which the heart re-signs its bond — and the verse's *kullamā* applies to the renewals with the same exactness it applied to Madina's treaties. The test it installs is behavioral and available to everyone: watch what happens to the covenant at the next station of cost. The group that casts aside is rarely the whole community and rarely announces itself; it is the party whose interest turns against the bond at each renewal, and its membership shifts, and every member knows the moment he joins. The verse's second sentence is the diagnostic for that moment — the casting is unbelief expressing itself, which means the small abandonment of a renewed bond is not a minor event but a reading of the heart's actual contents. For the community that received this sūrah, the warning was structural rather than tribal: nothing in the verse binds its law to Israel's lineage, and everything in the recital's second-person method applies it to whoever renews and casts. The covenant is made every time; the group forms every time; and most of them — the verse's permanent margin of sorrow — do not believe.
