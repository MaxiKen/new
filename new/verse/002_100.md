## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:100

> Why is it that every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside? In fact, most of them do not believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question About a Pattern**

The verse asks about a habit rather than an incident. The wording every time indicates repetition, and the community's own history had produced several examples of an agreement accepted and then discarded. The question is addressed to the reader as much as to the community, and it invites an explanation that the rest of the sentence supplies.

The sūrah has already narrated one such covenant in detail, the one about bloodshed and expulsion, and it recorded the community's pledge and the fact that they bore witness to it (2:84). That episode is treated here as an instance of a recurring practice rather than as an isolated failure. The wording treats the covenant-breaking as a habit rather than an accident, and the reader who has followed the narrative can count the examples. The question is addressed as much to the reader as to the community, and it invites an explanation the rest of the sentence supplies.

**The Covenants in the Book's Account**

The Book records several agreements with this community: the covenant at the mountain, the undertaking about worship and kindness to parents and the poor, the prohibition of internal violence, and the promise connected with the coming prophet. Each was accepted and each was followed by conduct that contradicted it.

The pattern extends beyond the sūrah. The Book states in another chapter that the community was bound by a covenant to make the scripture known and not to conceal it, and it describes what became of that undertaking. The verse's question is not based on a single case, and the reader who has followed the narrative can count the examples. What the verse treats as evidence is therefore a record rather than a single case. The Book's practice of listing these agreements in a single passage suggests that the reader is meant to see one disposition rather than several lapses, and the final clause of the verse confirms that reading. The later verses continue in the same way, so that the community's history reads as a series of opportunities met with the same response.

**What the Commentators Reported**

The commentators offered several occasions for the verse. One report describes the Jewish community of Madinah saying that they had made no pact concerning Muhammad, which means they did not regard themselves as bound to follow him specifically. Another takes the reference to be the earlier promise that they would follow the prophet who was to come and would be given victory over the idolaters. A third connects the verse with their betrayal of an alliance with the Prophet ﷺ in favour of the Quraysh.

Al-Rāzī noted that the meaning is not restricted to any one incident, and the wording of the verse supports him. The question is phrased generally, so that each occasion becomes an illustration rather than the whole reference. The variety of occasions attached to the verse is itself an indication of the reading the commentators favoured, since a sentence that fits several events is usually telling the broader story. What they agree on is that a pledge was given and treated as though it had never been made.

**A Group Within the Community**

The sentence says that a group casts the covenant aside, and the qualification appears repeatedly in this passage. The sūrah never treats an entire people as a single moral unit, and it has already stated that a few among them kept faith. The charge here falls on those who broke their word, and the rest of the community is left outside the description.

That precision has a function in the argument. A reader who hears a general accusation will either reject it or accept it wholesale, and the Book's practice is to specify so that judgment can follow the evidence. The verses that follow will continue the same way, naming those who cast the Book behind their backs and those who follow what the devils promoted.

**The Root Cause Named**

The sentence closes by stating that most of them do not believe, and the clause explains the pattern rather than adding a second charge. A covenant is an act of trust, and a person who accepts an agreement he does not intend to keep has already settled the question of belief in the God before whom the agreement was made.

The Book uses the same diagnosis elsewhere when it links the breaking of oaths to a weak relation with God, and the sūrah's treatment of the community's history is built on it. The failures it describes are not lapses in an otherwise consistent commitment; they are the visible form of a position already taken. A covenant is an act of trust, and a person who accepts an agreement he does not intend to keep has already settled the question of his belief in the God before whom the agreement was made. The Book links the breaking of oaths to a weak relation with God in other passages as well.

**What the Question Achieves**

By placing the observation in the form of a question, the verse makes the reader work. No explanation is offered for why the pattern repeats, which forces the hearer to supply the conclusion the final clause states. That structure is one of the sūrah's most common, and it appears here at the end of a series of charges.

The question also prepares the next verses, which move from covenants to the revelation itself. The community's habit of discarding undertakings is followed by its habit of discarding the book it had been given, and the two are presented as aspects of one disposition. The sentence about belief at the end of this verse governs both. The sentence gives the community's own record as the only evidence required, and it does not depend on testimony from outside. What the reader is left with is a description of a habit and a single explanation for it, and the verses that follow test the explanation against conduct.

**A Warning Held for the Believers**

The sūrah will turn the same warning on its own community later in the chapter when it instructs the Muslims about keeping their agreements and warns them against making oaths a means of deception. What the sentence describes is not a hazard peculiar to one community. The question in this verse is therefore not only a description of an earlier people, and the sūrah later commands its own community about keeping agreements and warns them against making oaths a means of deception. A people that holds a revelation is exposed to the same hazard the verse describes. The command to keep agreements appears in the chapter's later instruction to the believers, and the warning against using oaths for advantage belongs to the same teaching. A community that has been shown what happened to earlier keepers of covenants is expected to draw the lesson for itself rather than wait to be taught it twice.

**Why the Question Is Asked Repeatedly**

The sūrah returns to this community's covenants more than once because the pattern is the point. A single broken agreement could be explained by circumstance, and the Book's account would carry less weight. A series of agreements accepted and discarded describes something settled in the community's relation to revelation, and the verse's wording reflects that. The same method appears in the earlier account of the calf, where the offence is placed in a remembered history rather than presented as an accusation from outside. What the verse does is ask the community to explain its own record, and the explanation it supplies is the final clause about belief. The verses that follow continue the account in the same register, moving from covenants to the book that was cast aside and to the practices that took its place.
