## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:69

> Those mindful ˹of Allah˺ will not be accountable for those ˹who ridicule it˺ whatsoever—their duty is to advise, so perhaps the ridiculers will abstain.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question the Previous Verse Raises**

Having been told to leave the company of the mockers, a believer might reasonably ask what he owed them afterward. The verse answers that the mindful are not accountable for the mockers in any respect, and the answer is paired with a duty that remains: to advise them. The sentence separates two things that people tend to confuse, which are responsibility for another person's conduct and responsibility for what one says to him.

That separation is stated as a general principle elsewhere in the Book, where it says that no soul burdened with sin will carry the burden of another (6:164), and where the sūrah of the Troops says that the messenger is not a keeper over the people. The verse in front of us applies the principle to the specific case of a man who has walked out of a gathering and may be worrying about what becomes of the people he left behind.

**The Word Translated as Mindful**

The sentence is addressed to those who guard themselves against wrongdoing, and the description serves as a qualification rather than as a formality. The people who receive the exemption are the ones already ordering their lives around God's commands, including the command they have just obeyed by leaving. The verse is not addressed to someone looking for permission to sit comfortably beside those who mock the revelation.

That is how the Book uses the description of mindfulness in other passages, where it is joined to a specific act of obedience or restraint. The effect here is that the exemption from liability comes with the duty of advice attached to it, which means the person who qualifies has already taken on a task.

**What the Duty of Advice Includes**

The word for advising describes a reminder delivered with the aim of correcting conduct, and it does not require a sermon. The Book uses the same idea when it tells the Prophet that his only duty is to remind (88:21), and when it describes the believers as those who call others to good and forbid what is wrong. The duty in this verse is narrower than a general obligation to reform the society; it concerns the mockers the believer has just left.

The Book's model of this advice is visible in the story of Noah, the most mocked of the messengers, who kept warning his people and calling them to their Lord for centuries while they laughed at him. The Book does not present that persistence as a failure, and it does not describe the mockers as beyond the reach of the invitation. The purpose clause attached to this verse carries the same expectation in a single word.

**"So Perhaps They Will Abstain"**

The clause states the aim of the advice rather than a guaranteed result. What the Book hopes for is that the ridicule may cease, and the hope is stated in the form used throughout the Book for outcomes that depend on human choice. The Book's use of this construction keeps the free will of the audience intact, since a certainty would leave no room for the decision the warning is meant to produce.

The clause also explains why advice is worth giving to people who are mocking. The person mocking the revelation may stop, and the Book's approach to the hardened is to keep the possibility open, which is why it tells the Prophet to bear with the mockers and answer them with peace (43:89). The stance the Book recommends is neither contempt nor cooperation but a persistent reminder delivered without hostility.

**The Two Verses Together**

Read as a pair, the last two verses give the community a complete rule for living beside a majority that ridiculed its scripture. They are told where not to sit, how to behave if they forget, and whom they are not answerable for. They are told to keep advising, and they are told that the outcome of the advice is not their possession.

That arrangement is characteristic of the Book's method in the Makkan sūrahs, where the believers are a minority with no power to enforce anything. The instructions they receive are about conduct, speech, and distance, rather than about authority, and the Book's confidence in the message is not built on the expectation of social success.

**What the Passage Establishes About the Community's Boundaries**

The passage also quietly establishes a boundary that later jurisprudence would elaborate. The community is defined by its adherence to the revelation rather than by its kinship, its city, or its class, and the people who mock what it holds sacred are described by their activity rather than by their tribe. The rule about leaving a gathering is the earliest form of a principle that appears in the sūrah of the Women as a general law.

The next verse turns from the conduct of the believers toward the people the passage has been describing, and it gives the Prophet a further instruction about how to deal with them. The Book is still speaking to him about the same group, and the instruction that follows defines the limits of what a believer may do for them and what no one will be able to do for them at all.

**The Duty Stated Elsewhere in the Book**

The Book describes the messenger's obligation in the same terms in several places, and each statement narrows the task to the delivery of the message. The Prophet is told that if people turn away after the invitation, his only duty is to deliver (3:20); he is told that whether he sees the punishment or dies before it, his duty is to deliver and the judgment is God's (13:40); and the sūrah of the Spider states that the messenger's only obligation is clear delivery (29:18). The sūrah of the Women puts the same thought in the form of a promise, saying that whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God, and that whoever turns away has not been placed in the Prophet's keeping (4:80).

Read with the verse in front of us, those statements explain why the believers' duty toward the mockers is limited to advice. The task of the messenger and of the community that carries his message is to make the truth clear, and the Book repeats the boundary so often because the temptation to go beyond it is permanent.
