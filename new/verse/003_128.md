## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:128

> You ˹O Prophet˺ have no say in the matter. It is up to Allah to turn to them in mercy or punish them, for indeed they are wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Limit Placed on the Messenger**

The verse tells the Prophet that he has no share in the decision, and it explains that the matter belongs to God, who may relent toward the people in question or may punish them, since they are wrongdoers. The sentence defines the boundary of the messenger's role at a moment when his feelings were most engaged.

The Book states the same limit elsewhere in different words, telling the Prophet that his duty is to convey the message and that guidance is not his to give. What the verse before the reader adds is the specific situation of a messenger who has been harmed by the people he is calling, and who is told that the outcome of their case is not his to decide.

**The Occasion Reported in the Commentaries**

The commentaries connect the verse with the day of Uḥud, when the Prophet was wounded and one of his teeth was damaged. The reports describe him saying, as he bled, that he did not know how a people could prosper who injured their prophet while he was calling them to their Lord, and they say that this verse was then sent down.

[UNVERIFIED: the report that the Prophet was wounded in the face at Uḥud, that a tooth was damaged, and that he spoke the words attributed to him on that occasion, which the commentaries relate without a chain I have examined.]

Other accounts connect the verse with supplications the Prophet made in prayer against certain opponents, and report that he stopped after it was revealed. The two versions differ in the details of the occasion rather than in the content of the verse.

**Mercy and Punishment Reserved to God**

The sentence names two possibilities and places both in God's hands. The people described had done harm to the community and to the messenger, and the verse allows that the response may be either forgiveness or punishment, with the decision resting where it belongs.

The Book holds this position consistently. One passage states that God's mercy encompasses everything, while the same passage affirms that punishment falls where He wills (7:156). Another passage, addressed to those who have gone to extremes against themselves, commands them not to despair of God's mercy and states that He forgives all sins (39:53). The verse before the reader applies the same framework to a specific group.

**The Prophet's Conduct After the Verse**

The reports about the Prophet's prayers are the practical side of the verse. A messenger who has been told that the matter is not his to decide no longer asks for a particular outcome against individuals, and the tradition records the change in his practice.

The point of the instruction is not to forbid the messenger from feeling anything. The Qur'an records his distress elsewhere and never presents him as indifferent to the harm he suffered. What the verse does is separate the feeling from the authority to settle the case, and the separation allowed him to continue calling the same people to the same message.

**Who the Wrongdoers Are**

The verse ends by naming the people as wrongdoers, and the description is stated as the reason the matter requires a decision rather than an immediate outcome. The Book uses the term for those who have injured a right, whether it belongs to God or to people.

The identification leaves the possibility of return open. A wrongdoer can cease to be one, and the Book's accounts of these very opponents include people who later accepted the message and became part of the community. The verse's placement at the end of a battle passage is a reminder that the account of the conflict is not the account of its ending.

**What the Verse Teaches About Authority**

The instruction to the messenger establishes a principle that applies to everyone who carries his message: the one who conveys is not the one who judges. A community that takes the message seriously will find itself in the same position, calling people whose response it cannot control.

That principle is the answer to a temptation as old as the mission itself. A person who is certain of the truth and has been harmed by those who reject it will want the matter settled in his own favour, and the verse removes the settlement from his hands. What remains for him is the work of conveying, which is the part he was given.

**The Community's Share in the Lesson**

The verse is addressed to the Prophet, and its instruction reaches his followers as the pattern of their own conduct. They too may be wronged and may find the wrongdoers unrepentant, and they are told the same thing about where the case rests.

The Book's teaching on this point runs through its passages about patience, and it is the ground of the community's willingness to live alongside people who oppose it. The verse before the reader states it in the spare language of an address to one man, and the sentence that follows confirms the framework in which the address is to be understood.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The verse brings the promises and purposes of the preceding verses to a close with a statement about the limits of the messenger's role, and it prepares the reader for the account of the battle that follows.

Between the account of the promises and the account of the testing, the Book places this reminder that the outcome of the conflict was never entrusted to the community. The reader is then introduced to the events themselves with the proper frame in view, which is that the community is being examined and not the enemy.
