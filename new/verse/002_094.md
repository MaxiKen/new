## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:94

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively for you ˹Israelites˺ out of all humanity, then wish for death if what you say is true!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Challenge Rather Than an Argument**

The verse instructs the Prophet ﷺ to put a proposal to the community rather than to dispute with it. If the claim it had been making about its exclusive standing in the Hereafter were true, there would be a public way to demonstrate it, and the sūrah names the demonstration: wish for death. The Book's method here is to accept the claim provisionally and then ask what follows from it.

That structure recurs in the sūrah's dealings with this community. The previous verses asked them to produce a pledge from God for their claim about limited punishment and to bring proof for their claim about entry into Paradise (2:111). The challenge in this sentence belongs to the same family. It does not deny the claim outright; it asks what a person who believed it would do.

**The Claim Being Tested**

The sentence presupposes a position the sūrah has already described, in which a community treats the Hereafter as its own possession and everyone else as excluded. That position is not merely an opinion about the afterlife; it is a claim about a private arrangement between God and one people, and the Book treats it as a serious assertion because it attributes to God a commitment He has not announced.

The sūrah will meet the same kind of claim from other quarters later in the chapter, where Jews and Christians each declare that only their own will enter the Garden (2:111). The verse here deals with the same confidence in advance, and it offers the community an opportunity to demonstrate what it had been asserting in public. The chapter states the same position again a few verses later, where the two communities reserve the Garden for themselves, and it answers with a criterion rather than a counter-claim. The challenge here belongs to that sequence, and it treats the community's confidence as a position open to examination.

**Why Death Would Be the Wish**

The reasoning is straightforward once the claim is granted. Death is the door to the felicity that the community claimed as its exclusive property, and a person certain of what lies behind that door would want it opened as soon as possible. The sūrah does not treat the wish for death as a virtue in itself; it treats it as the natural consequence of the certainty the community said it possessed.

The commentators explained the challenge in these terms, noting that the logic of the demand follows from the claim rather than from any general encouragement to seek death. Once the claim has been accepted, there is nothing left to wait for, and the Book lets the community's own premise produce the test. The Book makes the same point about the believers, who are told that death is not to be feared because it opens the way to what God has promised. The difference is that their confidence is paired with effort and repentance, while the community described here wanted the destination without the preparation.

**The Prophet's Teaching on Wishing for Death**

The sūrah's command is addressed to a specific situation, and the Prophet ﷺ taught his own community to avoid the wish for death in ordinary circumstances. The well-known report states that none of you should wish for death because of a harm that has befallen him, and that a person who must say something should ask God to keep him alive while life is good for him and to take him when death is better for him, a tradition recorded in both Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

The two positions are consistent, and the difference lies in the assumption behind the wish. A Muslim who wishes for death in despair has stopped expecting good from God in this life, while the challenge in this verse presupposes a certainty about the Hereafter that the community had claimed and did not hold in practice. One wish reflects hopelessness, and the other would reflect a certainty the Book shows them lacking.

**Certainty and the Knowledge of One's Own Record**

The following verse supplies the reason the challenge was never taken up, and the reason is instructive. People who expect Paradise as a matter of entitlement still know what they have done, and the Book states elsewhere that a person is a witness against himself even when he offers excuses (75:14–15). Certainty about a destination that ignores a person's own record is not certainty at all.

The Book preserves this insight for its own readers as well. The sūrah's commands are addressed to people who hope for the Garden, and the hope it commends is accompanied by effort, repentance, and fear. The community in this verse had detached the two, and the challenge exposes the separation by asking for a public act that only the confidence it claimed could produce.

**The Suggestion of a Mutual Imprecation**

Some commentators understood the challenge as a form of mutual imprecation, in which two parties who disagree about a matter call down destruction on whichever of them is lying, and they noted that the practice was known in the region. Under that reading the verse invites the community to join the Prophet ﷺ in an oath that would settle the dispute by its outcome.

The Book does not develop the procedure here, and the sūrah's interest lies in the community's response rather than in the mechanics. The next verse states what happened, and the answer is that no one came forward. Whether the challenge is read as an imprecation or as a simple test of conviction, the result is the same. The reports differ over whether the community was invited to join such an oath or simply to demonstrate its confidence by the wish itself, and the wording supports the simpler reading. What the passage needs from the sentence is that the offer was made openly, and that is what the verse supplies.

**What the Sentence Measures**

The verse measures the distance between a claim made in public and a conviction held in private. The community had been asserting something about its standing before God, and the sentence offers to test the assertion at no cost to anyone but the person making it. What the offer produces is silence, and the sūrah reports it in the next sentence without adding an accusation of its own. A claim about the Hereafter cannot be checked by argument in this world, and the challenge converts it into something that can be. The offer costs the community nothing except the risk of an answer, and its refusal has been preserved in the Book ever since as the reply the claim received. What the sūrah records is therefore not a debate that ended without a result but a test that was available and not taken.

**Why the Challenge Is Recorded Rather Than Settled**

The sūrah reports the offer and the refusal and moves on without pronouncing on the community's fate. That restraint is characteristic of the passage, which has been describing conduct rather than delivering verdicts, and it leaves the reader with a demonstration rather than a sentence. The purpose of the record is not to humiliate a people but to show what happens when a claim about God is tested in public. A position built on an assumption about one's own standing survives as long as nobody asks for evidence, and the sūrah asks. The Book preserves the exchange because the assumption it describes is not confined to one community. Every group that mistakes its inheritance for a guarantee can be asked the same question, and the challenge reaches the reader's confidence about the Hereafter before it reaches anyone else's.
