## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:140

> If you have suffered injuries ˹at Uḥud˺, they suffered similarly ˹at Badr˺. We alternate these days ˹of victory and defeat˺ among people so that Allah may reveal the ˹true˺ believers, choose martyrs from among you—and Allah does not like the wrongdoers—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comparison Stated Plainly**

The verse opens by setting the community's wound beside the wound of its opponents and observing that they had already suffered something like it. The Book does not soften the fact of the loss or present the day as anything other than a defeat.

The comparison serves a purpose that runs through the whole passage. The believers are in no position to conclude that God had singled them out for punishment, because their enemies had been bloodied too, and recently enough that everyone remembering the day would know it. The sūrah's argument throughout this section is that what happened was a test within a contest that both sides were undergoing.

**Days Handed Out in Turns**

The image used for the sequence of events is that of days distributed or handed over in turns, and the word behind it describes the alternation of fortune between one party and another. A day belongs to one side, then to the other, and neither holds it permanently.

The Book uses the picture to teach how the community should read its own history. The tradition of interpretation understands these turns as something God arranges deliberately, so that the success of one side and the reverse of another are both instruments rather than accidents. The verse's wording places every outcome on that ledger, and it offers the community a way of thinking about a battle it lost which does not require it to believe that God's help had failed.

**Why History Moves in This Way**

The sentence supplies its own reason in the clause that follows: the turns come so that God may know the believers. The purpose is stated in terms of distinguishing rather than in terms of punishment or favour.

The sūrah had already explained, in its treatment of the earlier defeat, that days of this kind serve to expose what people are. The verses about the archers, the wavering, and the grief in the middle of the battle are the working out of that principle in a single afternoon. The verse before the reader states the principle in general terms, so that the community can apply it to whatever comes next.

**The Wording About God Knowing**

The statement that God acts so that He may know those who believe is one of the Book's many expressions of this kind, and the tradition of interpretation explains it as knowledge that becomes evident rather than knowledge newly acquired. God's knowledge of a person's faith does not change, but the moment of trial makes the faith visible to the community and to the person himself.

The Book uses the same language about testing elsewhere, and the commentators consistently read such sentences in this way. The distinction matters for how the verse is understood: it does not present God as learning something at Uḥud, but as bringing out into the open what was there, so that the community could see who among it was prepared to stand.

**Taking Witnesses From Among You**

The next clause states another purpose, that God may take witnesses from among the community. The word carries both the general sense of one who testifies and the special sense the community gave it later, of one who dies in the way of God.

Both senses work in this place. The men who fell at Uḥud had borne testimony by their conduct and their deaths, and the community's tradition came to honour them under that name. The verse presents the loss from the side the community could not see from the field: the people who died were not merely subtracted from the ranks, they were selected.

**The Sentence About the Wrongdoers**

The verse closes with a statement that God does not love the wrongdoers, and the sentence serves the passage as a boundary marker. The turns of fortune are not morally neutral for the people who take part in them.

The clause distinguishes the community's losses from the outcome of those who fight against the truth, and it prepares the reader for the contrast the next verses draw between the two groups. The Book states often that God does not love wrongdoing, and its placement at the end of a verse about the alternation of fortune prevents the reader from concluding that the Book is indifferent to which side a person supports when the days turn either way.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not tell the community that its dead were being punished, and it does not tell the survivors that they had been abandoned. Both conclusions were available to people who had watched the day unfold, and the sentence closes them off.

What it offers instead is a way of holding two facts together in the mind at the same time: the wound was real and the hurt was deserved by no individual in particular, and the loss happened within an arrangement whose purposes the community could not see from its position. That combination is the Book's usual mode of consolation in the sūrah's battle material.

**The Consolation in the Sentence**

Read within the passage, the verse answers the two commands that precede it. The community was told not to weaken and not to grieve, and this sentence supplies the understanding that makes the commands possible.

The believers are told that the days belong to God, that they turn in accordance with His purposes, and that the day of their discomfort is not the end of the matter. The next verses carry the argument further by describing what the testing is for, and the community that reads the passage to its end is given the reasons for the steadiness it has been commanded to maintain. The command and the reason are meant to be recited together, which is why the sūrah places them within a few lines of each other.

**The Days That Alternate**

The sentence about the turns of fortune was explained by al-Ṭabarī through a proverb the Arabs used: one day for you and one day against you. Al-Rāzī drew the general point from it, that the affairs of the world rotate and that no advantage is permanent. The commentators also stated what the alternation means in this case. The enemy had been wounded at Badr a year earlier, and the community had now been wounded at Uḥud, so the loss is described as a turn rather than as a verdict on either side. Al-Quṭūbī and al-Rāzī recorded the further explanation that God's support is given to the believers when they hold to their duty, and that when they fail their enemies gain the advantage over them, so that the reversal is itself a form of instruction. The sentence therefore refuses both conclusions a person might draw from a defeat: that the cause has been abandoned, and that the outcome of a battle settles the standing of the people who fought it.

**What the Refining Produces**

The word rendered as assay names the process of separating impurities from metal, and al-Ṭabarī explained it as the trials through which patience is tested. The word rendered as blight names reduction and shortage, the dwindling of something until nothing of it can be seen, and it appears again in the sūrah of the Cow (2:276). The two words describe the same operation from different angles: what is lost is gone, and what remains has been clarified. The statement that God may know the believers is also stated in the verse that follows, which asks whether the community imagined it would enter the garden without God knowing those among them who strove and those who were patient. The knowledge in question is therefore displayed rather than learned, and the sūrah's discussion of the point, which the commentators connected with the verse about the change of direction in prayer, keeps the reader from reading the phrase as a statement about information God acquires (2:143).

**What the Wording Assumes**

Al-Zamakhsharī noted a grammatical feature of the passage: the clause about God knowing is attached to what precedes it through an implicit phrase, so that the sentence reads as either God acted in order to know, or God will know by acting. The construction appears in the Book when it describes God knowing the state of a people, and the commentators treated it as one of the places where the language of human experience is used to describe what is beyond it. What the verse conveys is that the trial produces something God recognizes in His servants, and the words used for the refining and the losses describe how that recognition is reached. The sūrah follows the sentence with the reminder that being killed or dying in God's cause brings forgiveness and mercy better than anything the survivors accumulate, which is the answer the passage gives to the community's grief.
