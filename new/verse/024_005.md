## Sūrah an-Nūr 24:5

> except those who repent afterwards and mend their ways, then surely Allah is AllForgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opening of Divine Grace to the Defamer**

Immediately following the three severe penalties decreed against false accusers, the divine voice introduces an avenue of redemption: *illā alladhīna tābū min baʿdi dhālika wa-aṣlaḥū*. The juxtaposition of rigorous judicial sanctions with immediate offers of divine mercy reflects the balanced architecture of Qur'anic justice. Law does not exist merely to crush the transgressor, but to restrain harm and rehabilitate the contrite heart.

Repentance (*tawbah*) is not treated as a trivial emotional confession muttered in private seclusion. The text explicitly conjoins repentance with *iṣlāḥ*—active rectification and moral amendment. The wrongdoer who wounded another person's reputation must publicly undo the damage they inflicted, acknowledging their falsehood and striving to restore the social peace their reckless speech shattered. Without tangible restitution and public vindication of the innocent, verbal expressions of sorrow remain spiritually hollow before God and humanity. The path of redemption requires concrete moral repair and an unambiguous public reversal of the lie.

**The Great Legal Debate Over the Scope of the Exception**

The placement of the exception clause *illā* (except) sparked one of the most famous debates in the history of Islamic legal theory. Verse 4 concluded with three distinct punitive measures: eighty lashes, the permanent rejection of legal testimony (*abadā*), and the characterization of the accusers as rebellious wrongdoers (*al-fāsiqūn*). Jurists debated whether the exception modifies all three clauses, only the last two, or solely the final sentence.

All scholars agreed that repentance cannot suspend the physical punishment of eighty lashes once the victim demands justice and the case reaches the magistrate. Physical penalties serve as deterrent rights of society that cannot be dissolved merely by private remorse. The dispute concentrated entirely on whether sincere repentance could restore the slanderer's civil right to give legal testimony in a court of law, establishing whether judicial disqualification is an irreversible sanction or a curable condition under divine law. This debate highlights the delicate tension between retributive deterrence and restorative justice across classical legal schools.

**The Majority Opinion on Civic Rehabilitation**

The majority of classical jurists—including Imām Mālik, Imām al-Shāfiʿī, and Imām Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal—held that repentance rehabilitates the offender both spiritually and civically. In their grammatical and legal analysis, the exception attaches naturally to the preceding clauses as a coherent whole. When a person repents, admits their slander to be false, and demonstrates upright character, they cease to be a *fāsiq* in the eyes of God, and their civil capacity to bear witness is fully restored.

They argued that divine mercy is comprehensive; if God accepts a person's return from the monstrous sin of idolatry or murder and permits them to testify, it is unreasonable to assume that divine law permanently disenfranchises a repentant slanderer. Permanent civil alienation would only foster bitterness and drive the offender into enduring antagonism against the community, whereas reintegration encourages genuine moral transformation and strengthens social cohesion. The community gains far more by restoring a reformed soul than by maintaining a permanent underclass of legal pariahs.

**The Ḥanafī Position on Permanent Disqualification**

In sharp contrast, Imām Abū Ḥanīfah and his school maintained that the exception applies exclusively to the final descriptive clause: *wa-ulāʾika humu al-fāsiqūn*. Under Ḥanafī jurisprudence, the slanderer who repents is forgiven by God and freed from the spiritual stain of rebellion in the Hereafter, but their legal testimony remains permanently inadmissible before earthly judges.

The Ḥanafīs based their ruling upon the word *abadā* (never/forever), which they regarded as an absolute, unqualified temporal barrier that grammatical exceptions cannot pierce. Furthermore, they pointed out the judicial danger of allowing slanderers back into the witness box: if a person once proved capable of inventing a malicious lie against a neighbor's chastity, courtroom prudence demands that their testimony never again determine the life, lineage, or property of another citizen. Civic caution must persist even when spiritual redemption is granted, ensuring that courtrooms maintain absolute credibility and protect future litigants from perjury.

**The Concrete Demonstration of Moral Rectification**

Classical authorities outlined the precise practical steps required to fulfill the condition of *aṣlaḥū* (mending their ways). Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī relate that the early jurists required the slanderer to present themselves publicly before the judicial assembly and openly declare: I lied in what I attributed to such a person, my accusation was false, and I have repented to God. Merely refraining from further slander is insufficient; the defamer must take positive ownership of their error and actively vindicate the victim's honor before the community.

Additionally, many jurists insisted upon a period of moral probation—frequently stipulated as one full year—during which the community could observe the individual's consistent adherence to truthfulness, prayer, and virtuous conduct. This probationary interval ensured that the repentance was sincere and not a calculated maneuver to regain social influence, safeguarding the courtroom from deceptive opportunism and confirming genuine spiritual renewal. Upright behavior over time remains the only reliable proof of internal transformation and true integrity.

**The Theological Harmony of Forgiveness and Wisdom**

The verse seals the legislative exception by invoking two profound divine names: *fa-inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīm*, for surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. This divine signature provides the ultimate theological justification for the law's flexibility. God does not demand perfection from mortal servants; He demands accountability and the willingness to correct one's course after stumbling into sin.

The attribute of forgiveness (*ghafūr*) indicates that God conceals the past sins of the penitent, shielding them from ultimate ruin, while mercy (*raḥīm*) ensures that continuous divine assistance accompanies their moral rehabilitation. By ending the passage with these attributes, the text instructs the human community to reflect divine generosity in their social structures, welcoming back the contrite wrongdoer who has genuinely laboured to heal the wounds they inflicted upon their fellows. Grace remains the final word in divine governance, offering hope where human judgment often falters.
