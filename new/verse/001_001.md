## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence With No Verb**

The sūrah opens with a phrase that is not a full sentence. *Bi-smi Allāh* — "in the Name of Allah" (1:1) — rests on a preposition, and Arabic does not leave a preposition unattached; a word must be supplied. Al-Rāzī gathered what the grammarians proposed. One reading puts the missing verb first, so the sense is *I begin* in the Name of Allah, or *I seek help*. A second puts it last: in the Name of Allah I begin. A third supplies a noun rather than a verb — the beginning of what follows stands in the Name of Allah — and so the phrase is complete without any act being named at all.

The options are not interchangeable. When the verb comes first, the act is the subject of the sentence and the Name qualifies it: a person acts, and acts with God in view. When the verb comes last, the Name is established before anything is done and the act is then carried out inside it. That difference is small in grammar and large in practice, because only the second reading lets the phrase stand at the head of any lawful act — a letter, a journey, a slaughter, a marriage — rather than at the head of a recitation alone. Later jurists built the etiquette of beginning lawful acts with the Name on exactly that elasticity.

**Is This Line Part of the Sūrah?**

Every sūrah but one opens with this formula, and it also occurs once inside the text (27:30). Whether it counts as a numbered verse of the sūrah it opens has been argued since the first generations, and each side has evidence rather than mere preference. Anas ibn Mālik reports that he prayed behind the Prophet, Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, and ʿUthmān, and that each of them began the recitation with *All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds* (1:2), without mentioning the basmalah (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 399c; the shorter form in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 743). Against that, Abū Hurayrah is reported to have recited the basmalah aloud when he led the prayer and then said that among those present he was performing the prayer closest to the prayer of the Messenger of God. Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq's description of the basmalah as the greatest verse in the Book of God is the ground on which Shiʿi scholarship counts it as a verse of every sūrah that begins with it.

Two further considerations settle the weighing. The first is textual: the Quran calls this sūrah *the seven often-repeated verses* (15:87), and it reaches seven only if the basmalah is counted. The second is functional: Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that the Prophet did not know where one sūrah ended and the next began until this line was revealed — which makes the formula a boundary marker. Both claims can be true at once, and that is the strongest resolution available. The line separates sūrahs and is a verse of the one it opens; the schools differ on whether it is recited aloud in the prayer, and the disagreement is about ritual practice, not about the text's standing.

**A Word the Pagans Already Used**

*Allāh* did not enter Arabic with the Quran. Ask the idolaters of Makkah who created them and they answer with this very word (43:87; 29:61; 31:25; 39:38). What they would not grant was its consequence: that the One who creates also commands, and that no lesser being shares His rule. The Quran's argument against them therefore never has to establish that God exists; it presses the admission they already make to its conclusion.

Whether the word has a root was disputed. One view treats it as a proper name with no derivation, unique and unanalysable. The other derives it from *al-ilāh*, "the Divine," contracted by dropping the hamza with the article fused. The derived reading explains something the other does not: why the word was intelligible to pagan hearers at all, since *ilāh* was already in their vocabulary for a deity. The non-derived reading protects what the derived one risks — that a shared root might imply a shared kind. Al-Ghazzālī's observation holds the two together: Allāh is at once the widest of the divine names, gathering every attribute, and the narrowest, because no creature can be called by it, whereas a human being may be called knowing or merciful in a qualified sense. *He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺* (112:1) puts the point beyond argument.

**Three Names in a Fixed Order**

The formula names God three times, and the sequence is never varied. Allāh, then the Most Compassionate, then the Most Merciful. One line of commentary, associated with Kāshānī, reads the descent as a movement through levels: Allāh points to the divine Essence as such, al-Raḥmān to the unity of the attributes, and al-Raḥīm to the unity of the acts. Read that way, the basmalah is a compressed theology — Being, then what Being is like, then what Being does.

The order also carries a rhetorical decision. The name that cannot be shared with any creature is followed at once by mercy, and not once but twice. Nothing in the opening line threatens; the first things the Quran says about God are the things about Him that attract. That the two mercy names are not synonyms is argued in the commentary on their repetition at 1:3; what belongs here is that they arrive in a fixed slot, always after the unshared Name and always before any mention of judgment. The two names are also interchangeable in address: *Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names* (17:110). The formula is therefore not three rival designations but one Name stated at three levels.

**What the Missing Verb Allows**

Because the phrase is incomplete, it can be completed by whatever follows it. Anything a person does may be taken up into it, which is why the formula governs the whole range of permitted acts in Muslim practice and not only the recitation of scripture. The Quran supplies its own precedent at the moment the ark is boarded: *Board it! In the Name of Allah it will sail and cast anchor* (11:41). A vessel's voyage is placed in the Name before it moves.

A report is often quoted for the same rule in the form, "Every important matter not begun with the Name of God is cut off." That exact wording does not appear in the nine canonical collections; the wording that does appear is Sunan Ibn Mājah 1894 — *Every matter of importance that is not begun with the praise of God will be left deficient* — which Ibn Ḥajar graded ḥasan. The legal ruling that one should open acts with the Name does not depend on that single report; the jurists ground it in the general principle that acts belong to God and are to be performed for Him. [UNVERIFIED: the "In the Name of God" wording attributed here to Ṭabarsī could not be located in a canonical collection; only the "praise of God" wording (Ibn Mājah 1894) was verified.]

**Solomon Opens His Letter the Same Way**

The one place the basmalah occurs inside a sūrah rather than at its head is a letter: *It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful’* (27:30). The formula sits at the opening of a written message sent to a queen who does not yet believe, and it is the first thing she is given to read. That placement tells the hearer what the phrase is for. It belongs at the beginning of things — a book, a voyage, a letter — and it says, before any content is stated, whose the undertaking is.
