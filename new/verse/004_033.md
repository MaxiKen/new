## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:33

> And We have appointed heirs to what has been left by parents and next of kin. As for those you have made a pledge to, give them their share. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Heirs Appointed for What Is Left**

The verse states *wa li kullin jaʿalnā mawāliya mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa al-aqrabūn* — and We have appointed heirs to what has been left by parents and next of kin. The pronoun *We* — divine appointment — matches the earlier framing of shares as an obligation from Allah (4:11) and as limits set by Allah (4:13). Inheritance is not a tribal negotiation left to custom; it is an appointment. The word *mawālī* — heirs, close kin, protectors — was read as those who inherit by fixed portions and those who inherit as residuary kin where fractions leave a remainder. The phrase *mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa al-aqrabūn* gathers every estate, whether little or much (4:7), under the same appointment. The sūrah has already detailed children and parents, spouses and siblings after debts and bequests (4:11–12), and has promised Gardens for keeping those limits and warned of Fire for crossing them (4:13–14). This verse steps back and says the whole system is appointed; no heir is an afterthought. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“to Allah belongs the heritage of the heavens and the earth”* (3:180, 57:10), so that appointment is also a redistribution of what already belongs to Him, which explains why the portions are obligatory rather than suggested.

**Give Those You Have Pledged Their Share**

The second clause *wa alladhīna ʿaqadat aymānukum fa ātūhum naṣībahum* — as for those you have made a pledge to, give them their share — addresses a specific practice of the early community. In the pre-Islamic and early Islamic period, men might make a pact of brotherhood or alliance — *ʿaqd* — promising mutual support, protection, and sometimes inheritance between those not related by blood. Commentators relate that such pledges were made between the migrants and the hosts, and between individuals who sought to secure support in a city new to many. The Qur’an does not erase the pledge; it commands fulfilment of its share where it was made, while the fixed inheritance shares now govern kinship. Some understood the pledge as referring to marriage covenants where a wife’s share must be honoured, or to emancipation ties where a freed person has a claim. The common point is fidelity to a contract voluntarily entered. The sūrah elsewhere commands fulfilling oaths and covenants and describes the marital bond as a firm commitment — *mīthāq ghalīẓ* (4:21) — so that giving the pledged share is of a piece with keeping the firm word. The community that was just told not to crave what another was given (4:32) is now told to give what it pledged, which breaks envy at its root by turning promise into payment.

**Between Fixed Law and Voluntary Pledge**

The pairing of appointed heirs and pledged associates displays the sūrah’s balance between what God fixes and what people undertake. Fixed heirs — *mawālī* — are not chosen by the deceased; they are appointed by the verse and quantified in the fractions that follow. Pledged associates are chosen; they are the result of *ʿaqadat aymānukum* — what your right hands have tied. Both generate a *naṣīb* — a share — that must be given. The first share is obligatory by divine appointment; the second is obligatory by personal commitment kept before God. Together they show that property moves by two just means — law and promise — both of which exclude seizing by desire or by *bāṭil* — falsehood — condemned just before (4:29). Trade by mutual consent (4:29) and the dowry given graciously (4:4) already taught that willingness makes transfer lawful; this verse adds that a pledge made willingly also makes transfer obligatory. The sūrah therefore trains the reader to see wealth as moving along clear tracks: appointed shares where kinship is the track, and pledged shares where contract is the track, with neither track to be diverted by envy (4:32) or by devouring (4:29–30).

**Surely Allah Is a Witness Over All Things**

The closing *inna Allāha kāna ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīdā* — surely Allah is a Witness over all things — supplies the watcher for both tracks. *Shahīd* — witness, present observer — matches *Raqīb* — ever Watchful (4:1) — and *Ḥasīb* — sufficient as Reckoner (4:6) — and *ʿAlīm* — All-Knowing (4:11, 4:26, 4:32). The difference is that *Shahīd* stresses presence at the act of transfer. The apportioning of an estate often happens in a family home without a judge; the fulfilment of a pledge often happens between two persons without an audience. The verse says the division and the fulfilment are already witnessed. That matters for the heirs appointed — the parents, children, spouses, siblings — whose shares could be trimmed if no one sees, and for the pledged person whose share could be denied if the pledge is forgotten after death. Elsewhere the Qur’an says *“Allah is Witness over what you do”* (10:46) and requires witnesses at the orphan’s handover (4:6); this verse is the general principle behind that requirement. What is witnessed by God need not be witnessed by many people to be binding, but human witnesses remain the community’s way of aligning its practice with the divine watch.

**Law and Loyalty Together**

Read with verses 7 through 12, this verse finishes the sūrah’s inheritance architecture by adding loyalty to law. Verses 7 and 11–12 gave the law — men and women have obligatory shares, with precise fractions after debts and bequests. Verses 8–10 gave the ethic around the law — provision for bystanders with kind speech, fear for one’s own weak children as a motive, fire for eating orphan wealth. Verse 13–14 gave the sanction — Gardens for keeping limits, humiliation for crossing them. Verse 32 warned against craving another’s portion and invited asking God for bounty instead. This verse adds the pledge: where you have tied your word, give the share. The placement here is deliberate. A person who has learned to ask rather than crave is ready to give what he promised without resenting the gift. The Witness over all things sees both the appointed share handed to a parent or spouse and the pledged share handed to an ally or a freed person, and He counts both as fulfilment. The sūrah therefore does not separate civil law from personal honour; it joins them under the same watch. To keep the fixed shares is obedience; to keep the pledge is fidelity; to know both are witnessed is to handle wealth as one who knows he is seen, and who wishes his handling to be worthy of that sight.
