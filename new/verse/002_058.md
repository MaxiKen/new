## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:58

> And ˹remember˺ when We said, “Enter this city and eat freely from wherever you please; enter the gate with humility, saying, ‘Absolve us.’ We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Entry Into a Given Land**

The verse's command has the shape of a gift with a liturgy. A people fed for a generation in the open desert is told to enter a city and eat freely, wherever they please, from what it holds — and the entry is fenced with two small requirements: a posture and a word. The first half of the command is deliberately unrestrictive — *eat freely from wherever you please* places no boundary on the table — and the restrictions attach not to the eating but to the entering. The structure replays, with a difference, the first entry-command of the sūrah: Adam was placed in the garden with the same open table, *eat as freely as you please* (2:35), and one restriction — the tree. Here the restriction is inverted into a positive rite: instead of one thing withheld, one thing asked — humility at the gate, absolution on the lips. Both scenes are entries into unearned abundance, and both make the entry itself the test rather than the residence. The wilderness generation stands at the city's threshold as Adam stood at the tree's: everything given, one small thing required, and the required thing aimed at the posture of the receiver rather than at the property of the gift.

**Which City the Command Named**

The city is unnamed in this verse, and the exegetes' candidates follow the geography of the conquest: Jerusalem, Jericho, other cities of Syria or Jordan, and — al-Rāzī's minority reading — Egypt itself. The identifications turn on a historical problem the verse leaves open: Moses never entered the Holy Land, so a command spoken under his leadership would either predate the conquest or belong to Joshua's campaign at Jericho. Al-Rāzī's Egypt reading dissolves the problem by relocating the scene: the punishment of the following verse, he reasons, fits the period before any entry into the Holy Land, and Egypt — the land of their torment — would then be re-entered as a land of provision, the reversal complete. The stronger evidence runs the other way. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf files the same command with the city named: *"Enter this city ˹of Jerusalem˺ and eat from wherever you please"* (7:161), and Sūrah al-Nisāʾ joins the gate-entry to the covenant's catalogue: *"We raised the Mount over them ˹as a warning˺ for ˹breaking˺ their covenant and said, 'Enter the gate ˹of Jerusalem˺ with humility'"* (4:154). The identification with Jerusalem — or with a city of the Holy Land entered at the conquest — carries the Qur'an's own explicit glosses, while the Egypt reading rests on inference. The unnamed city of this verse is named twice elsewhere in the Book, and the naming settles it.

**The Gate Entered With the Head Lowered**

The posture required at the gate is *sujjadan* — prostrating, or in the broader lexical range of the word, bowing low. The exegetes note the ambiguity and its reason: in Islamic rite *sujūd* is precisely the forehead to the ground, but the word's root covers any deep bodily lowering, and the command here is read on both registers — entering the gate in a bow, or entering in full prostration. Either way the requirement is the same in substance: the body performs what the heart must hold at the moment of arrival. A people entering a promised city have one natural posture — upright, victorious, claiming — and the command installs the opposite at the threshold itself. The humility is not general piety but entry-specific: it governs the exact moment when possession begins, when the risk of reading the gift as achievement peaks. The sūrah's wider argument explains why the moment needed engineering. This was a community that had demanded to see Allah as an ultimatum, that had taken a calf while its prophet was away, and that was about to eat freely from a city it had not built. The bowed gate is the covenant's prophylactic against the theology of arrival: enter as recipients, not as conquerors, and let the body say it where everyone can see.

**Ḥiṭṭa: One Word for the Whole Liturgy**

The spoken requirement is a single word: *ḥiṭṭah* — rendered here *Absolve us*, literally an unburdening. The grammarians' reading of its force is exact: al-Zamakhsharī takes it as a cognate-accusative invocation, "unburden us an unburdening" — the way Arabic compresses a whole petition into its verbal noun, so that the word is not a description but a request in its shortest legal form. The word's content completes the posture's work. A bowing body could still carry a boasting heart; the prescribed speech forecloses it, because *ḥiṭṭah* names the speaker's condition — burdened, in need of removal — and names nothing about the speaker's merit. There is no room in the word for *because we fought*, *because we kept the law*, *because our fathers were righteous*; the entire entry-theology of the covenant is compressed into a noun meaning take this load off. The verse thereby makes the liturgy a doctrinal instrument: what a people says at its threshold of blessing discloses what it believes about the blessing. Islamic practice keeps the pattern in its own entry-formulas — the duʿāʾ of the mosque's door, the greeting of peace at every threshold — and the ancestor of the whole genre stands here: one word, spoken entering, that assigns the gift to the Giver and the burden to the only Remover.

**Forgiveness and Increase at the Threshold**

The promise answering the word is double: *We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers*. The first half meets the petition — *ḥiṭṭah* asks for removal, and removal is pledged, maghfira for their line of sins: the calf, the demand, the wilderness record. The second half exceeds it, and the exceeding is attached to a named group: the *muḥsinūn*, the good-doers, the people whose worship carries excellence. The promise's structure is thus the sūrah's whole economy of grace in two clauses — the floor is forgiveness for the asking, and the ceiling is multiplication for the excellent — with both opened by a bowed entry and a four-letter word. The multiplication clause also does specific work in this context. The city's table was *eat freely from wherever you please*; the promised increase says that the material gift is the lesser half of what the threshold offers. A community entering in humility receives not only the city but a rising curve inside the covenant — reward that compounds for those who do the asking well. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf's filing carries the same double promise (7:161), which marks it as the standing terms of entry rather than a one-time offer: wherever the gate of a divine gift is opened, forgiveness and increase are posted on it together.

**Every Blessing Has a Gate**

The verse's permanence lies in its form. Blessings, in the Qur'an's moral geography, are not open fields but walled cities with gates, and the gate has requirements: a posture that says *I did not earn this* and a word that says *remove what I brought*. The wilderness generation's file will be read against these two requirements, but the requirements themselves are addressed to every reader standing at a threshold — of provision, of position, of a house, of a season of ease. The command's economy is its mercy: nothing is asked at the gate but humility and absolution-seeking, both of which cost the asker nothing and save him everything. The verse also completes the recital's arc of arrival. The descent from the garden began the human file with an exit; the entry of verse 38 opened the earth as residence; and here a delivered people is brought to the first re-entry — a city given, a table spread, a rite prescribed — the descent's counter-movement staged inside history. What Allah asks at the gates of His gifts has not changed since this verse: come down off the camel of self-sufficiency, bow at the door, say the word that names your need. The city beyond is already paid for; the gate only checks who is entering.
