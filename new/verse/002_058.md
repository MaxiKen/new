## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:58

> And ˹remember˺ when We said, “Enter this city and eat freely from wherever you please; enter the gate with humility, saying, ‘Absolve us.’ We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command With Three Parts**

The instruction given to the community contains a movement, a posture and a sentence. They are to enter a town, to go in through the gate humbly, and to say one word. Each element is specified, and each one concerns the manner of the entry rather than its object; nothing in the command asks them to fight their way in or to claim a right.

The town is identified by the commentators as Jerusalem or Jericho, and the disagreement turns on whether the leader at that moment was Moses or Joshua. The sūrah's own interest in the place is minimal. What matters in the sentence is the contrast between what was being given — a settled land with food in it — and what was being required, which was an act of humility performed in public.

**The Word They Were Told to Say**

What the community was commanded to utter is a single Arabic word, and the Book leaves it untranslated in most versions because its force lies in the sense of a load being lifted. Commentators explained it as a request for the removal of sins, or as an acknowledgement of wrongdoing that requires relief. The word is not a formal confession and not a statement of doctrine; it is the speech of people who have understood that the thing they carry needs to be taken from them.

The Book's choices in these commands are consistently modest in size. A word, a posture, an entry. What makes the command a test is that none of its parts can be performed by a person who is protecting his standing, since each of them places him in the position of a recipient rather than a conqueror. The requirement is small in appearance and total in what it asks.

**Eating Freely, as Adam Was Told**

The command to eat wherever they please echoes the words spoken to Adam in the Garden earlier in the sūrah, and the parallel is not accidental. In both scenes a gift is given together with a limit, and in both scenes the limit concerns conduct within abundance rather than access to it. The Book places the human being in the same relationship to its gifts at the beginning of the story and in the middle of the history of Israel.

What differs between the two scenes is the territory. Adam was given a garden with a single tree excluded; this community is given a land with a gate to pass through and a sentence to say. The Book's use of the same phrasing in both places tells the reader that the pattern of gift and requirement is not a feature of one narrative but the way the relationship is arranged.

**Forgiveness and Increase in the Same Sentence**

The verse closes with a promise of two things, and the second of them is easy to overlook. Forgiveness of sins would have completed the arrangement, and the Book adds that it will multiply the reward of those who do good. The verb describes an increase rather than a payment, so the sentence offers both the removal of a debt and a surplus on top of it.

The same pairing appears in the sūrah's description of the covenant with this people, where the terms promise forgiveness and admittance to gardens (5:12). The Book's habit of adding increase to forgiveness is part of its account of generosity rather than of justice, and it also explains why the command in this verse is framed as a favour rather than as an opportunity to earn anything.

**A Test Placed at the Door of a Gift**

The entry into a land is presented as an occasion for obedience rather than as a reward for it. The community receives a habitable place and is told how to enter, and the whole of the command concerns conduct: how to walk, what to say, and what attitude to bring. The Book's account of the gift is therefore inseparable from the account of the requirement, and neither can be separated from the other without distorting the passage.

The next verse reports what actually happened at the gate, so the reader is not left to wonder how the test turned out. The arrangement of the two verses makes the second one a piece of evidence about the community rather than a continuation of the story, and the sūrah's address continues to build on that evidence.

**What the Command Assumes About Its Audience**

Nothing in the sentence suggests that the community is being asked to do something beyond its capacity. Entering a gate, adopting a posture and saying a word were within the power of every person being addressed, and the Book's choice of such requirements follows its practice elsewhere: the commands it gives are ordinarily within reach, and the difficulty lies in the will rather than in the ability.

That feature of the Book's ethical instruction carries into the obligations it later places on the new community, which are similarly concrete and specific. The verse here establishes the pattern at a moment when the alternative was a military conquest, and it shows that what was asked of a people on the verge of receiving a land was the self-effacement of a servant rather than the confidence of a victor.

**The Gate as a Threshold of a New Arrangement**

The Book presents the entry into the town as a transition in the community's history rather than as an incidental event. What follows it in the passage is a settled life in a land, with a warning about corrupting it, and the command at the gate is the instruction that governs how the people are to begin that life. The posture and the word were to be carried into the arrangement they were entering, which is why the smallest gesture was made the condition of the largest gift.

The sūrah keeps the same pattern in the other instructions it gives this community. The gifts described in the passage all arrive with conduct attached, and the conduct is always concrete: eating what is provided, avoiding damage, keeping a covenant. Nothing in the address is left as sentiment, and the command at the gate is the clearest instance of a requirement that can be performed in a single moment.
