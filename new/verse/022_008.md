## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:8

> ˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Portrait, and Two Words Heavier**

Verse 3 described a man who disputes about God without knowledge and follows every rebellious devil. Verse 8 describes him again — and the second portrait is heavier than the first by two nouns. What was *bighayri ʿilmin* has become *bighayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīr*: without knowledge, and without guidance, and without an illuminating scripture.

The escalation is not padding. Verse 3 established the defect in the man's reasoning; verse 8 establishes that he has no resources of any kind behind the reasoning either. A person can be ignorant and still be well-directed, or be ignorant of the details and still hold a reliable text. The verse removes all three in a single line, and the triple negation is the strongest form available in Arabic for a total absence.

The requirement is stated as a direct prohibition in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ, with the audit attached: *“Do not follow what you have no ˹sure˺ knowledge of. Indeed, all will be called to account for ˹their˺ hearing, sight, and intellect”* (17:36). Hearing, sight, heart — the three channels questioned, matching the three absences of verse 8. The escalation from verse 3 is therefore not rhetorical piling but the full inventory of what the disputant cannot produce when the accounting comes.

**Three Negations Cover the Three Ways of Knowing**

The three terms are not synonyms, and the care with which they are chosen is the substance of the verse.

*ʿIlm* is knowledge arrived at — what a person works out, observes, or is taught and verifies. *Hudā* is guidance received — direction given from outside oneself, the thing a person is granted rather than achieves. *Kitāb munīr* is an illuminating scripture: a written, public, checkable source that gives light and can be consulted by anyone.

Together they exhaust the field. If a person lacks what he can reason out, what he has been given, and what he can look up, there is no fourth category left to appeal to. The verse is therefore not merely an insult; it is a criterion. It tells the reader what a defensible position about God requires, and by negation it defines what legitimate theology is.

Sūrah al-Zumar puts the inequality as a question that answers itself: *“Are those who know equal to those who do not know?”* (39:9). The night worshipper set against the disputant — prostrating and standing on one side, arguing from nothing on the other — and the verse declines to treat them as two opinions. Verse 8's triple negation is that inequality worked out as a criterion: the knower has sources to be checked against, and the man with none of the three has nothing to be weighed.

**Why Guidance Is Listed Separately From Knowledge**

The second negation is the one most easily skimmed past. If a man lacks knowledge, what does it add to say that he also lacks guidance?

It adds a judgement about his state rather than his information. Knowledge in the Qur'an is something a person can hold, lose, or refuse; guidance is something granted, and its absence is not a neutral gap but a privation. A man without knowledge is uninformed. A man without guidance is unreached. The verse says both, which means the disputant's condition is not simply that he has never been taught — it is that the teaching has not landed on him.

The sequence also rules out a comfortable reading. A person might concede that his knowledge is thin while assuming his intentions are sound and that God will supply the rest. Listing *hudā* as a second, separate absence forecloses that. Ignorance plus sincerity would still leave a man with guidance, and this verse describes a man who has neither.

**"Enlightening Scripture": One Word Doing Two Jobs**

*Munīr* is the active participle of *anāra*, to shine or give light. It describes something that is itself luminous and, at the same time, something that illuminates what is around it. Both senses are in play.

The adjective is rare in the Qur'an and appears in a narrow set of contexts, two of which concern earlier revelation. Messengers before Muhammad ﷺ came *“with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures”* (3:184), and the same formula is repeated for the peoples who denied them: *“Their messengers came to them with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures”* (35:25).

The implication for this verse is sharp. The disputant is being measured against a standard that previous communities were given and met, or were given and rejected. Illumination is not withheld for want of a lamp; it is available, in the form of a text that both shines and shows. The man in verse 8 has walked past a working light.

**Why the Sūrah Returns to This Man**

Repetition of this kind is often read as editing, and it is not. The sūrah names this figure twice within six verses because the figure is not one man with a name. The construction is *wa-mina al-nāsi man* — and among people is one who — a partitive that describes a standing type rather than an individual.

The two appearances also do different work, which is why the repetition is not a duplication. In verse 3, the disputant follows devils, and the emphasis falls on where his opinion comes from. In verse 8 the emphasis falls on what he lacks, and the verse that follows — verse 9 — will say what he *does*: he turns aside in pride to lead others away. The sūrah is moving through a sequence, and the second portrait belongs to the middle of it.

The partitive construction opens the Qur'an's portrait of the hypocrites in the same terms: *“And there are some who say, ‘We believe in Allah and the Last Day,’ yet they are not ˹true˺ believers”* (2:8). *Wa-mina al-nāsi man* — the same *among people is one who*, introducing a standing type rather than a named man. Verse 3 and verse 8 belong to that grammar of types: the sūrah returns to the disputant because the disputant keeps recurring, and each portrait adds what the sequence needs — first his company, then his emptiness, then (in verse 9) his purpose.

**The Remedy Implied by the Diagnosis**

A verse that lists three missing things is implicitly a syllabus. Knowledge is to be acquired, guidance is to be sought, and the scripture is to be read — and all three are available.

This is why the classical commentators drew a positive ruling out of a verse of condemnation. If the fault is disputing *without* these, then disputing *with* them is a different act, and one the Qur'an commands elsewhere in the strongest terms: calling to the way of God on the basis of what has actually been revealed, with wisdom and fair argument. The dispute the Qur'an forbids is the one conducted from preference; the dispute it commands is the one conducted from a source the other party can also consult.

The practical test the verse leaves behind is unusually simple. Before arguing about God, a person should be able to say what he knows, what he has been given, and which text he is reading.

Luqmān's Version of the Same Line

The same indictment appears in Sūrah Luqmān, addressed to a man who cannot name what he is relying on: *“Have you not seen that Allah has subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and has lavished His favours upon you, both seen and unseen? ˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, or guidance, or an enlightening scripture”* (31:20). There the dispute is set against the backdrop of visible provision, which makes the ignorance harder to excuse: the evidence is in front of the man, and he argues anyway.
