## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:142

> Some cattle are fit for labour, others are too small. Eat of what Allah has provided for you and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. Certainly, he is your sworn enemy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cattle Given for Use**

Some cattle are fit for labour, others are too small. The verse returns to the livestock after the description of the gardens, and it names the two ways the herds served the audience. The larger animals carried loads and worked the land; the smaller ones were kept for their meat, their milk, and their hides. The Book's other passages describe the same provision, saying that God created the cattle as a source of warmth, food, and other benefits (16:5), and that they carry loads to distant lands that people could not otherwise reach without hardship (16:7).

The verse thus presents the herds as a gift with functions, and the functions are the ones the audience depended on. The sūrah's earlier account of the audience's rules about the animals had examined the categories they had invented for these same creatures (6:138–139), and the sentence in front of us states the plain arrangement that the categories had obscured.

**The Command to Eat and the Warning**

Eat of what Allah has provided for you and do not follow Satan's footsteps. The sentence joins permission to a prohibition, and it uses the formula the Book applies to the food of the community in several places. The sūrah of the Cow instructs humanity to eat from what is lawful and good on the earth and not to follow the footsteps of Satan, describing him as an avowed enemy (2:168); the sūrah of the Table tells the believers to eat of the good things provided and to be mindful of God (5:88); and the sūrah of the Bee gives the same command with the addition that the eater should give thanks (16:114).

The warning about Satan's footsteps is attached to the dietary instruction in each of these passages, and the connection is the same in all of them. The footsteps are the practices that lead away from what God has provided, and the sūrah in front of us has just finished describing the system the audience had built in place of the plain command.

**The Enemy Named at the End**

Certainly, he is your sworn enemy. The clause identifies Satan, and the Book states the same fact in its addresses to humanity in several places. The sūrah of Yā-Sīn records the question put to the children of Adam on the day of judgement, asking whether they were not commanded not to follow Satan, since he is their avowed enemy (36:60), and the sūrah of the Heights describes the enmity declared at the beginning of human life. The sūrah of the Cow's dietary instruction ends with the same description.

The sentence gives the warning its reason. The practices that the sūrah has been describing were not neutral customs; they were the means by which the community was led away from the provision of God, and the Book names the agency behind them. The identification of Satan as an enemy rather than as a tempter emphasises that the guidance being rejected was for the benefit of the people being addressed.

**What the Verse Says About Custom and Its Sources**

The verse sets the provision of God and the footsteps of Satan in opposition, and the arrangement gives the reader a way of assessing the practices the sūrah has examined. The herds were given for labour and for food; the rules that restricted them had no source in that gift; and the sentence that forbids following Satan's footsteps places the rules in the category of things from which the community was being turned away.

The Book's treatment of the audience's customs follows this pattern throughout the sūrah. The practices are stated, their attribution to God is shown to be false, and the guidance that replaces them is presented as the plain use of what God has provided. The verse in front of us states the choice in the form of a command and a warning, and the reader is left with the two options.

**The Provision and the Community's Gratitude**

The command to eat of what God has provided belongs to the Book's teaching about gratitude, and the sūrah has attached gratitude to the created order since its description of the harvest. The verse does not present the herds as a reward or as a possession to be managed by the community's own rules; it presents them as provision, and the response the Book asks for is the recognition of the provider.

The Book's statements about the world's goods elsewhere carry the same emphasis. The sūrah of the Bee tells the believers to eat of the lawful and good things and to be grateful for God's favours if they worship Him, and the sūrah of the Table gives the same command with the addition of mindfulness. The verse in front of us belongs with those instructions, and its warning about Satan's footsteps states what gratitude excludes.

**The Place of the Verse in the Passage**

The verse stands between the description of the gardens and the argument about the pairs of animals, and it functions as the general instruction that the following verses apply. The sūrah has examined the audience's rules about the herds in the preceding passage, and it will now take up their specific claim that God had forbidden certain animals and answer it with a demand for evidence.

The two verses that follow record that answer, asking the audience whether it was the males, the females, or what was in the wombs that God had forbidden, and requiring them to produce the knowledge on which their claim rested (6:143–144). The verse in front of us supplies the command that the argument presupposes, and the challenge that follows removes the ground on which the audience's rules had been built.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse names the pairs of animals and puts the question to the audience about which of them had been forbidden, ending with the demand that they produce knowledge if they are truthful (6:143). The sentence begins the sūrah's final treatment of the dietary system, and it proceeds by requiring the audience to state the basis of rules it had never examined.

The sūrah's method here is characteristic. It does not simply declare the audience's rules void; it asks the people who made them to explain them, and the question is framed so that the explanation cannot be given. The verse in front of us prepares the reader for that argument by stating the provision that the rules had presumed to regulate.
