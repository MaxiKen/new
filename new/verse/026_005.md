## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:5

> Whatever new reminder comes to them from the Most Compassionate, they always turn away from it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Every Fresh Reminder Meets the Same Retreat**

The verse shifts from what God could compel to what the people actually do. A new reminder arrives, and the response is not examination followed by uncertainty. They turn away. The pattern is repeated reception and repeated refusal. New does not imply that the truth itself changes. It refers to revelation arriving in portions, each portion addressing people at a particular moment and renewing their chance to remember what they already ought to know.

The word reminder is important. The Qur'an does not present guidance as a novelty invented to flatter its audience. It recalls the knowledge of God, accountability, and right worship that revelation restores when custom and desire have covered them. Each passage is therefore both fresh and familiar: fresh in its immediate address, familiar in the truth it calls the human being to recover. Their rejection cannot be explained by a message that never reached them. The verse describes a will that keeps stepping away from a repeated invitation.

**Mercy Stands Behind the Warning**

The reminder comes from al-Raḥmān, the Most Compassionate. That divine name changes the emotional color of the verse. The people are not being abandoned to their error; mercy is sending them another warning. Even the announcement of judgment is an act of kindness when it gives a person time to turn before the consequence arrives. Turning from the reminder therefore means turning away from an offered mercy, not merely declining a piece of information.

The name also blocks a false picture of revelation as hostility. A warning may be painful because it exposes guilt, but pain does not make it cruel. A physician's treatment can sting while serving recovery; in a greater and more serious way, a divine reminder can disturb a person in order to rescue him from self-deception. The audience's conduct is tragic for that reason. They are not being chased by an enemy. They are refusing the hand that calls them back to life.

**Gradual Revelation Gives Time to Respond**

The term rendered new can also carry the sense of something recent or newly occurring. Revelation came over time rather than arriving as one complete book at one instant. Qur'an 17:106 connects that mode of delivery with recitation in stages, and 25:32 records the objection that the revelation should have come all at once. The gradual form allowed an answer to events, a strengthening of the community, and repeated opportunities for correction.

That setting makes the people's reaction more revealing. A single encounter could be dismissed as confusion or surprise. A sequence of reminders removes that excuse. Each new passage meets the same world but addresses a new moment within it. The audience can watch the message clarify itself, answer objections, and connect with the lives of earlier prophets. Yet familiarity does not soften their resistance. The more mercy gives them opportunities, the more clearly their turning away becomes a settled posture.

**Turning Away Is an Active Gesture**

The verb does not describe passive failure to notice. It evokes directing the face elsewhere, withholding attention, and declining to let the message govern one's thought. A person can turn away while hearing every word. This is why the verse is about moral attention rather than intellectual capacity. The hearer knows enough to avoid the reminder; avoidance itself reveals that the reminder has reached a sensitive place.

Such refusal has many forms. One may mock, change the subject, appeal to ancestors, or demand a different kind of proof. These actions look different on the surface, but each can serve the same purpose: keeping a clear warning from judging the life that hears it. The verse does not require the reader to label every question dishonest. It asks for an honest test of direction. Is the question moving toward truth, or is it another way to turn the face aside?

**Repeated Mercy Increases Responsibility**

A first warning can be met with confusion. A second may expose negligence. When reminders continue to arrive from the Most Compassionate and the response remains retreat, responsibility grows with the opportunity. This is not because God needs repeated evidence to discover a person's heart. The repetition reveals the heart to the person himself and establishes a record against the claim that he was never given a chance.

The verse also contains hope for anyone who recognizes the pattern in himself. A reminder is called new because return is still possible whenever it arrives. The past turns do not make the next one meaningless. What condemns the deniers is not that they once faltered but that they make refusal their settled answer. The proper response to a fresh reminder is therefore not fascination with novelty. It is to stop, listen, and let mercy change the direction in which the face is turned.

**A Reminder Tests Attention Before It Tests Memory**

The issue is not whether the audience can repeat a teaching. It is whether the teaching is allowed to interrupt an established direction. A person may know the words of a warning and still turn away the instant those words expose a desire. The verse places attention before argument because an argument cannot be weighed by someone who has already decided not to face it.

This gives the word reminder a practical edge. When revelation recalls a duty, the hearer should ask what in his conduct has made the duty easy to forget. When it recalls accountability, he should ask what comfort depends on pretending that no account will come. The answer need not be dramatic; it may be a small act of honesty, repair, or restraint. Mercy arrives in a form that can be heard, and the turning point is the moment a person stops treating that form as an interruption.
