## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:151

> Since We have sent you a messenger from among yourselves—reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom, and teaching you what you never knew—

**Expanded Commentary**

**"As We Have Sent": A Blessing Recalled Mid-Sentence**

The verse opens with *ka-mā* — "just as," "even as" — a comparative particle that leans on what preceded it, and the grammarians located its anchor in the previous verse's completed favour: the sending of the messenger is the blessing whose completion the qiblah serves, or the guidance whose instrument the messenger is. Al-Zamakhsharī's reading treats the clause as continuing the sentence of favour — *so that I may perfect My favour upon you, as We have sent among you a messenger* — which makes the mission itself the content of the favour rather than an addition to it. The effect of the construction is to recall a gift while enumerating its parts: the community is told not that it will receive something new but that it is living inside a bestowal already made, and the verse itemizes the bestowal in four verbs. Each verb names a function of the messenger, and the four together describe the complete economy of what prophethood does to a people — deliver, cleanse, instruct, and open knowledge that had no prior avenue.

**The Prayer of Abraham, Answered**

The four verbs are not new; they are a quotation of a prayer. Two dozen verses earlier the sūrah recorded Abraham and Ishmael raising the House's foundations and pleading for their descendants: *"Our Lord! Raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them"* (2:129). The correspondence is deliberate and near-total — recitation, Book and wisdom, purification — with one addition here that the prayer did not contain: *teaching you what you never knew*. The verse therefore performs, in grammar, what the passage has been arguing in history: the mission of Muhammad is the answer to the petition of Abraham, filed centuries earlier over the foundations of the very House the community has just been turned toward. The qiblah and the messenger are one gift in two installments — the House built by the patriarch, the community of the House now led by the prophet he asked for. Reading 2:151 without 2:129 leaves the verse's opening particle unanchored; reading them together shows the sūrah's Abraham cycle closing on its Medinan fulfillment.

**From Among Yourselves**

The messenger's provenance receives the verse's central phrase: *min anfusikum* — from among yourselves, from your own selves. The quality answers the two objections the sūrah had quoted from the disputants: that revelation should have come through Israel's line, and that a prophet should have been a great man of the two cities (43:31). He came from the disputants' own stock — the Arabs' own kin, the Quraysh's own clan, a man whose genealogy, poverty, and unletteredness were public facts. The same description anchors the mission in other sūrahs: God conferred a great favour on the believers *by raising a messenger from among them* who had found them clearly astray (3:164), and the sūrah of the Congregation names Him the One who sent among the unlettered people a messenger from themselves, reciting, purifying, teaching (62:2). The phrase is not sentimental; it is evidentiary. A messenger from among you can be audited: his character was known before his claim, his truthfulness was tested in decades of ordinary dealings, and his teaching cannot be dismissed as the import of a foreign tradition. The nearness that made him objectionable to the status-seekers is what makes him credible to everyone else.

**The Order of the Four Verbs**

The functions are listed in a sequence worth examining: reciting the revelations, purifying, teaching the Book and wisdom, teaching what was never known. The order in Abraham's prayer ran differently — recitation, then Book and wisdom, then purification (2:129) — and the variation has drawn comment. In the prayer, purification came last, as the fruit of instruction; in the fulfillment, it comes second, ahead of formal teaching. The arrangement here matches the actual history of the mission: the recitation arrived first and began working on hearts — the Qur'an's own claim is that its verses purify as they are heard — and only the purified community could carry the Book and the wisdom without corrupting them. Purification (*tazkiyah*) is the cleansing of the soul from association, vice, and disordered attachment; the Book is the revelation as text and law; wisdom (*ḥikmah*) is the applied depth — in the classical understanding, the Prophet's own Sunnah and the capacity to place each ruling where it belongs. A people recited to, cleansed, then taught, are a people in whom knowledge becomes character rather than mere possession — which is the difference the sūrah has pressed since its portrait of those who carry the Torah without living it.

**Teaching What You Never Knew**

The fourth verb is the verse's own addition to Abraham's prayer, and it is the most comprehensive: *teaching you what you never knew*. The earlier functions could be described as transmission — revelations recited, a Book taught, a wisdom inherited. This one names an absolute increment: knowledge the community had no prior access to, no route toward, no fragment of. Its content is everything the mission brought that Arabia did not possess — the creed's full architecture, the law's detail, the histories of the prophets corrected against both legend and polemic, the eschatology made precise, the very God-knowledge that the idolatrous environment had overlaid. The clause also carries the unlettered prophet's paradox: the man who could not read taught what none of his contemporaries knew, which is the standing argument the Qur'an makes about its own origin — an unlettered reciter producing a book of this scope is itself the sign. The verse ends where its grammar began, mid-flow — the sentence is not finished, and the next verse supplies the response the four verbs demand.

**The Favour Itemized Is the Favour Owed**

Read within the movement of the passage, the verse performs a conversion: the completed favour of the qiblah (2:150) is revealed to be one item in a catalogue whose largest entry is the messenger himself. The community that was just told not to fear its enemies and to let God's blessing be completed in it now sees the blessing's full inventory — direction, House, recitation, purification, Book, wisdom, unprecedented knowledge — and every item is traced to a Sender who chose the messenger from the community's own selves. The inventory creates the obligation the passage is about to voice, because a favour itemized is a favour acknowledged, and acknowledgment in this economy takes the form of remembrance and gratitude. The verse's suspended syntax is thus a held breath: the gifts are listed, the Giver is named, and what the recipient says in response is the whole content of the sentence's completion.
