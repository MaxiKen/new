## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:151

> Since We have sent you a messenger from among yourselves—reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom, and teaching you what you never knew—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger From Among You**

The verse describes the messenger who was sent from within the community itself, and the phrase was understood by the commentators as a statement about the accessibility of the messenger's example. He shared the community's language, its history, and its circumstances, and the Book elsewhere states that an angelic messenger would have been a greater burden for them to follow.

The sūrah has described the Prophet's mission in earlier verses in similar terms, and the phrase appears again in the context of the community's function as witnesses. The reader is shown that the community's standing among the nations rests on what it received rather than on anything it achieved, and the verse opens with that reminder.

The phrase also answers an objection that the Book records elsewhere, when communities asked why a messenger had not been sent from the angels or from among another people. The sentence states that the messenger shares the community’s condition, and the reports about his life describe the same thing: he lived among the people, traded with them, and was known to them before the revelation came. The community’s access to its messenger is therefore part of the gift the verse is describing.

**Recitation of the Verses**

The first action named is the recitation of the Book's verses, and the Book's vocabulary for recitation carries the sense of following a text in sequence. The community's knowledge of the revelation begins with hearing it recited, and the sūrah's own account of the transmission of the message places recitation at the head of the list.

The commentaries connected the term here with the recitation that takes place in the prayer, since the community's practice is to hear the verses recited in the daily prayers. The Book's claim in this sentence is therefore not about a single occasion but about the recurring practice by which the revelation is made present to the community.

**Purification**

The second action named is purification, and the term covers both the cleansing of the self from faults and the cultivation of virtue. The commentators described it as the inward side of the messenger's work, whereas the recitation addresses the ear and the teaching addresses the understanding.

The Book places purification in the middle of the list, between reciting and teaching, and the position reflects the fact that knowledge and conduct are related. The community's scholars read the sequence as a description of the process: the verses are heard, the hearer is purified, and the Book and wisdom are then taught and received by a heart that has been prepared for them.

The word covers both the removal of what is blameworthy and the growth of what is good, and the commentators applied it to conduct as much as to belief. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have described his mission in similar terms when he said that he had been sent to complete noble character, and the community’s scholars read the two together. Purification in this verse is therefore the moral dimension of the messenger’s work, without which the teaching of the Book would remain a matter of information.

**Teaching the Book and Wisdom**

The third action names the teaching of the Book and of wisdom, and the sūrah has used the pair before when it described the community that had received them. The commentators differed on what the second term means in this pairing, with some taking it as the wisdom that the Book itself contains and others identifying it with the practice of the Prophet ﷺ, which shows how the revelation is to be applied.

The two readings are both reflected in the community's study, since Muslims have always treated the Book and the example as the two sources of their practice and doctrine. The verse states that both were taught by the messenger, and that the community's possession of them is the result of his work rather than of its own discovery.

**"What You Did Not Know"**

The sentence ends with teaching what the community did not know, and the phrase is the broadest of the four actions. The commentators explained it as covering the details of law, reports of past communities, and knowledge of God that could not be obtained by reasoning alone, and some extended it to include all of the sciences that the community later developed under the guidance of the revelation.

The clause states a fact about the community's condition before the message and a fact about what it received. The Book's history of this community describes a people without a scripture of their own, and the verse's closing words indicate the magnitude of the change the messenger brought.

**The Messenger's Four Tasks**

Read together, the sentence describes a mission that addresses the community's faculties in sequence. The ear receives the recitation, the self is purified, the mind is taught the Book and its wisdom, and the knowledge that was missing is supplied. The sūrah presents the messenger as the one who performs all four, and the community's role is to receive what is offered.

The order of the four actions also indicates how the Book understands formation. A community is not built by instruction alone; the recitation comes first, the purification of the self follows, and the teaching of the Book and its wisdom is received by a heart that has been prepared for it. The last item, teaching what the community did not know, shows that the process is meant to continue, since the knowledge supplied by the revelation was beyond what the community could have reached on its own.

**The Verse in Its Context**

The sentence stands at the end of the passage about the direction of prayer and the community's identity, and it explains what the community's blessings consist of. The sūrah's former account of the nation of Israel described blessings of provision, of victory, and of scripture; the verse here describes the blessing of a messenger from among the people, and the reader is invited to see the community's position in those terms.

The sentence follows the commands about the direction of prayer and precedes the instruction to remember God and to be grateful. Read in that setting, the description of the messenger’s work explains what the community has been given, and the commands that follow explain how the gift is to be received. The passage forms a single movement: the community is oriented in worship, reminded of what it received, and told how to respond.
