## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:24

> She advanced towards him, and he would have done likewise, had he not seen a sign from his Lord. This is how We kept evil and indecency away from him, for he was truly one of Our chosen servants.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Contested Clause in the Chapter**

*Wa-hammat bihi wa-hamma bihā lawlā an raʾā burhāna rabbih* — she inclined toward him and he would have inclined toward her, had he not seen the proof of his Lord. Generations of readers have stopped at the middle clause, because it appears to attribute to a prophet an inclination toward a forbidden act. The controversy is old and the positions are sharp. Some refused the reading entirely and argued that the clause must be understood as something other than desire. The majority accepted it and defined it narrowly. That the majority position prevailed is itself significant: the exegetical tradition preferred an honest reading of the grammar to a protective one, and then did its interpretive work inside the honest reading rather than by deleting it.

**Hamm Is the Movement of the Self, Not the Act**

The noun behind the verb is *hamm*, the inclination or resolve that arises in the heart before decision. The commentators are precise about its scope: Joseph experienced what a man experiences, and did not act on it; the inclination passed through him and was dismissed the moment the proof appeared. The distinction preserves both facts the verse insists on — that he was fully human and fully tempted, and that he did not transgress. Al-Rāzī supplies the analogy that made the point standard: a fasting person in summer sees iced rosewater and inclines to it naturally, yet does not drink, because the law he holds is stronger than the appetite he feels. The inclination is real; the abstention is also real; neither cancels the other. A prophet who felt nothing would not be resisting anything, and the verse is careful not to make his virtue costless.

**The Proof of His Lord: What the Reports Say**

Two explanations dominate the transmitted material. The first, widely reported, concerns an idol. The woman, who worshipped images, had covered one in the room; asked why, she replied that she was ashamed to sin before it. Joseph's response — that she was ashamed before something that neither sees nor hears, and not before the One who hears and sees — became, on this account, the proof: the sudden awareness of being witnessed by God. The second holds that Gabriel appeared to him. Neither account is supported by a chain of the first rank, and the verse itself declines to specify, saying only that he saw *the proof of his Lord*. [UNVERIFIED: the idol narrative and the appearance of Gabriel are exegetical reports without graded chains; recorded here as the tradition's readings, not as established fact.] The silence is worth respecting. What the text certifies is the arrival of a certainty from God at the moment of decision, and the mechanism is left where the Qur'an left it.

**Two Things Turned Away, Named Separately**

*Li-naṣrifa ʿanhu al-sūʾa wa-l-faḥshāʾ* — that We might turn away from him evil and indecency. The two nouns are not a hendiadys. *Sūʾ* is the broad category of what is bad, glossed here as the disloyalty toward his master that the act would have involved; *faḥshāʾ* is the specific term the Qur'an reserves for gross sexual immorality. Naming both covers the whole offence: the betrayal of a household and the transgression of a prohibition. The verb *naṣrifa*, We turn away, is causative and divine — the turning is performed by God, not achieved by Joseph. That is consistent with the previous verse, where his first word was a request for refuge, and with his own later statement that he does not acquit himself, since the soul inclines to evil except where God shows mercy (12:53). The chapter is scrupulous about the distribution of credit.

**"One of Our Chosen Servants" Fixes the Reason**

The final clause gives the ground: *innahu min ʿibādinā al-mukhlaṣīn*. The root *kh-l-ṣ* means to be pure, unmixed, exclusively devoted, and the passive form describes one who has been purified or singled out by God. Some read the participle actively, as one who is sincere in his devotion; the difference is between a status conferred and a quality achieved. The chapter's own evidence favours the conferred sense. Joseph's escape is attributed throughout to divine action — God turned the evil away, God answered his prayer (12:34), God established him in the land (12:21) — and his own summary of the episode is that he was preserved rather than strong. The phrase also places him in a class. He is *one of* God's chosen servants, a member of a group that includes the prophets named elsewhere in the Book, and the classification is what makes the temptation scene a chapter in a career rather than an isolated test.

**The Verse Explains the Accusation That Follows**

Read in sequence, verse 24 disposes of verse 25 in advance. The woman tears the shirt, meets her husband at the door and demands a punishment for the man who wanted to violate her. The reader already possesses what the household does not: the inclination was resisted, the proof was seen, the evil was turned away by God. Nothing in the coming investigation can therefore surprise the audience, and the trial by torn shirt in verses 26–28 becomes a demonstration for the characters rather than a discovery for the reader. This is the sūrah's method throughout. It gives the audience the inside of events before giving the characters the outside, so that every subsequent claim can be weighed rather than merely followed. The torn shirt, the witness, the household's verdict and the city's gossip all arrive afterwards, and none of them can add anything the reader does not already hold. That arrangement has a moral purpose beyond narrative economy. The chapter is training an audience to distrust a well-presented accusation, having just shown one being constructed by the person with the most to gain from it. The same training was given in the earlier verses, where a bloodied shirt and a set of convincing tears were shown to a father. Two fabricated cases in one sūrah, both involving a garment, both resolved by evidence rather than eloquence.
