## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:19

> But they said, “Our Lord! Make ˹the distances of˺ our journeys longer,” wronging themselves. So We reduced them to ˹cautionary˺ tales, and scattered them utterly. Surely in this are lessons for whoever is steadfast, grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer Asking for a Harder Life**

The strangest thing in this verse is that it records a prayer. The people of Sheba address God correctly — *rabbanā*, our Lord — and then ask Him to lengthen the stages between their way-stations. They are not petitioning an idol and not cursing. They are using the vocative of a servant, and the content of the request is that the road be made worse. Nothing in the Qur'an's other accounts of ruined peoples looks quite like this. Others deny, mock, or demand a miracle; these ask for an inconvenience, politely, from the right address. The form of the sentence is what makes it so damaging a piece of evidence. A community that can still frame a petition to its Lord and use it to ask for the removal of a favour has not lost its religion through ignorance. It has kept the vocabulary and emptied it.

**Why Anyone Would Want the Distances Longer**

The motive is not stated in the verse, and the reports fill it in convincingly: they wanted long stages so that travel would become an achievement again — so that a journey would require provisions, endurance and money, and would be worth boasting about on return. Short hops between visible towns are available to anyone with a donkey. A crossing of empty country separates the serious merchant from the peasant, and produces a class of men whose wealth is visible in the scale of what they can undertake. Read this way, the request is for the restoration of difficulty as a means of restoring distinction. There is a second reading in the same direction: with way-stations close together, the poor could travel too, and the route's benefits spread widely. Lengthening the stages would have closed the road to all but those who could outfit a proper caravan. Either way the request is about status rather than logistics, which is why the verse comments on it in moral rather than practical terms.

**"Wronging Themselves" — The Verse's Own Verdict**

*Wa ẓalamū anfusahum* — and they wronged themselves. The phrase is the Qur'an's standard description of sin, and it is worth taking literally rather than as a pious formula. *Ẓulm* in Arabic means putting a thing somewhere other than its proper place; the wrongdoer is not primarily a rule-breaker but a misplacer. Applied here, the misplacement is precise. They put hardship where ease had been given, and put their own reputation where gratitude belonged. And the injury lands on them: no one else's road was lengthened. This clause also settles the question of whether the request was granted. The verse does not say God refused it, nor that He made the journeys longer. It moves straight from the prayer to the dispersal, which suggests the answer came in a form they had not asked for and could not decline — the road was not lengthened, it was emptied.

**Turned Into Stories**

*Fa-jaʿalnāhum aḥādīth* — We made them into tales. The word *aḥādīth* is the plural of *ḥadīth*, meaning a report, a piece of news, something people say to each other. The punishment named here is not death or defeat but conversion into subject matter. A people who wanted their journeys to be worth talking about were made into something talked about, in a sense they did not intend. This is one of the Qur'an's sharpest ironies and it is entirely contained in a single noun. The phrase also names the mechanism by which the story reached the sūrah's Meccan audience. Sheba was not known to them through scripture; it was known through exactly this — reports carried up the caravan road, ruins pointed out, a name attached to a cautionary tale. A civilisation reduced to conversation is not merely destroyed. It survives in the only form that can still do damage to it.

**Scattered Utterly, With a Verb Heard Before**

*Wa mazzaqnāhum kulla mumazzaq* — We tore them apart with a complete tearing. The reader of this sūrah has met this construction already, and the echo is not accidental. The deniers of resurrection used the identical intensive form to describe a corpse pulled to shreds: *"when you have been utterly disintegrated you will be raised as a new creation"* (34:7). They chose the most violent available image to argue that reassembly was impossible. Twelve verses later the same word describes what happened to a nation that turned away, and the sense is historical rather than hypothetical — the Sabaean population dispersed after the collapse of the dam, its tribes moving north and east across Arabia, some of them ancestral to communities the Prophet's own contemporaries could name. The scattering of a people is presented in the vocabulary that scoffers used for the scattering of a body, by a power they said could not reverse it.

**A Lesson Reserved for Two Kinds of Person**

The verse ends by naming who can learn from this: *li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūr*, for everyone who is steadfast and grateful. Both words are intensive forms — not one who endures occasionally but one whose character is endurance, not one who thanks but one made of thanks. The pairing is exact for the story just told. Sheba failed on the second count by asking for their ease to be taken away, and would have failed on the first had it been. The two dispositions map onto the two halves of any life: patience is what gratitude looks like in hardship, and gratitude is what patience looks like in comfort, and a person who has only one of them will collapse when conditions change. This phrase appears four times in the Qur'an, always after a sign — with the days of God recalled to Moses' people (14:5), with ships running by His grace (31:31), with the wind that could be stilled and leave them motionless (42:33), and here with a ruined kingdom. Three of the four attach it to something still working. Only this one attaches it to wreckage.
