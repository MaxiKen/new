## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:25

> Say, “You will not be accountable for our misdeeds, nor will we be accountable for your deeds.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Verse in the Sūrah, and the Most Carefully Balanced**

This is a single sentence with two clauses, and everything in it depends on how the two are matched. *Lā tusʾalūna ʿammā ajramnā wa lā nusʾalu ʿammā taʿmalūn* — you will not be questioned about what we committed, nor will we be questioned about what you do. The verb in both halves is *suʾila*, to be asked, in the passive, which in Qur'anic usage means interrogation at the reckoning. What is being drawn is a line of accountability, and it runs in both directions. The believers will not answer for the opponents, and the opponents will not answer for the believers. Coming immediately after a verse that said one of the two parties must be in clear error (34:24), this sentence does something surprising with that conclusion: rather than pressing the advantage, it separates the accounts. The dispute is real and one side is wrong, and each side will nevertheless carry only its own file.

**Two Different Words, Deliberately Chosen**

The symmetry of the sentence breaks in one place, and the break is the most interesting thing in the verse. Of our conduct it says *ajramnā*, from *jurm*, crime or sin — a word that concedes wrongdoing outright. Of theirs it says *taʿmalūn*, from *ʿamal*, work or deed — a neutral word that names activity without judging it. The speaker applies the harsh term to his own side and the mild one to his opponents. This is not diplomacy for its own sake; it removes the one thing that would have made the sentence unusable in an argument. Had the wording been "you will not answer for our righteousness, nor we for your crimes," the disclaimer would have been an insult wearing the costume of fairness. As written, it is the opposite: the speaker takes the worse description and hands the better one across. A man who can concede that much about himself in the middle of a dispute is not conceding the dispute.

**A Standing Principle, Not a Local Arrangement**

The claim behind the verse runs throughout the Qur'an and is stated there in a fixed formula: *"No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another"* (6:164), repeated at (17:15), (35:18), (39:7) and (53:38). What that principle rules out is worth spelling out, because pre-Islamic Arabia ran on its opposite. Blood-guilt attached to a tribe, not to a killer; honour and shame were collective; a man's standing was his clan's standing and his offences were his clan's liability. Against that background, a sentence assigning each party its own reckoning is not a truism. It is the dismantling of a social system's deepest assumption, and the Qur'an applies it in both directions — no one inherits another's guilt, and no one is saved by another's merit. The verse's setting in a dispute over religion makes the point sharper still. Neither side gets to be punished as a bloc.

**Separation of Accounts Is Not Agreement**

There is a reading of this verse that would make it a statement of religious indifference — your path, my path, let us not trouble each other. That reading collapses as soon as the verse is put back among its neighbours. One verse earlier the sūrah insisted that one of the two parties is in clear error (34:24); one verse later it says God will gather both and judge between them (34:26). A sentence framed by those two cannot be saying the difference does not matter. What it says is narrower and more durable: the difference will be settled by a competent authority at a fixed time, and until then neither party is the other's judge or the other's hostage. Yūnus gives the Prophet the same instruction in almost the same shape, telling him to say, if they deny him, *"My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours. You are free of what I do, and I am free of what you do"* (10:41).

**What the Prophet Is Being Relieved Of**

Hearing the verse as an instruction to a messenger under pressure gives it a different weight. The Prophet in Mecca was watching people he knew refuse a message he was certain of, and the natural response to that is to take their refusal as a personal failure. This sentence sets a limit on his responsibility. He is answerable for delivery, not for outcome; their deeds are their file, and his obligation ends where their choice begins. The Qur'an says this to him directly elsewhere, reminding him that he is not set over people as a controller. There is real relief in the arrangement, and it is the same relief available to anyone who has argued for something true and been refused. Responsibility for another person's conclusion is a burden no one can discharge, and the verse declines to place it.

**A Sentence Meant to Be Said Out Loud**

Like the verses around it, this one begins with *qul* — say. That framing matters to how the content works. It is not a doctrine stated for the reader's information; it is a line of speech handed to the Prophet for use in a live confrontation, and its effect in that setting is to lower the temperature without conceding anything. Opponents expecting either a threat or a capitulation are given a third thing: a clear statement that the argument has a judge, and that the judge is not either of the parties present. This is the first of a run of four consecutive *say* verses in the sūrah, and reading them in sequence shows a deliberate progression — first the separation of accounts here, then the naming of the judge, then the demand for evidence. The next verse supplies the court that makes this separation more than an agreement to disagree.
