## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:245

> Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Loan Described**

The verse asks who it is that will give God a goodly loan so that He may multiply it many times over, and it states that God withholds and grants abundance and that the community will be returned to Him. The sentence follows the command to strive and supplies the instruction about the wealth that sustains the community's effort.

The Book's use of the image of a loan presents the giving of wealth for the sake of God as a transaction with the One who repays, and the community's teachers read the sentence as the encouragement that accompanies the command. Readers are shown that the passage's concerns are joined, since the effort of the community and the property that supports it belong to the same instruction.

**The Image of the Loan**

The commentators explained the phrase as the giving of wealth in the way of God presented under the figure of a loan, and they noted that the figure is used because the lender expects a return. The wording speaks of a goodly loan, and the community's scholars discussed the qualities that make it good.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere promises the increase of what is spent for the sake of God, and the community's teachers gathered the passages when they spoke about generosity. Readers are given the account of an image that presents the believer's wealth as means entrusted to him, and the sentence supplies the invitation that accompanies it.

**The Multiplication Promised**

The sentence states that God multiplies the loan many times over, and the community's scholars read the promise as the assurance that accompanies the instruction. The commentators described the rewards that the Book attaches to charitable spending, and the teachers used the material when they encouraged the community.

The wording places the increase with God rather than in the hands of those who give, and the community's teachers read the clause as the statement that the return belongs to the life to come as well as to the present. The passage shows that the Book's invitation to spend rests on a promise that accompanies the act, and the sentence supplies the assurance.

**The Two Actions of God**

The sentence states that God withholds and grants abundance, and the commentators explained the clause as the statement that the provision of the community lies in the hand of God. The wording places the increase and the restriction together, and the community's scholars read it as the ground of the trust that the instruction requires.

The Book's treatment of the subject elsewhere connects the provision of the community with the wisdom of the One who distributes it, and the community's teachers used the clause when they spoke about the property that is given away. The account describes an instruction that rests on the knowledge of who provides, and the sentence supplies the statement about the arrangement of the affairs of the community.

**The Return Named**

The sentence states that the community will be returned to God, and the community's scholars read the clause as the statement of the occasion on which the loan is repaid. The Book's practice of closing its instructions with the mention of the return appears throughout the sūrah.

The wording gives the instruction its setting in the account of the last things, and the community's teachers read the sentence with the passages that describe the accounting. The passage shows that the invitation to spend is presented within the framework of the return to God, and the sentence supplies the statement that accompanies the promise.

**The Reports and the Applications**

The commentaries record the occasions on which the verse was connected with the giving of wealth, and they describe the circumstances of the community in which the instruction was received. The reports show how the early believers understood the invitation.

The material also records the discussions of the teachers about the qualities that make a gift good, including the sincerity of the giver and the lawfulness of what is given. The account describes an instruction that the community applied in its daily affairs, and the sentence in this place supplies the invitation from which the applications were drawn.

**The Wealth That Is Lent**

The reports connect the revelation of the sentence with the generosity of the early believers, and they describe the property that was given away for the sake of the community and the reward that the giver was told to expect. The figure of the loan has been retained in the community's teaching because it presents the gift as a transaction with a sure return.

The jurists drew from the sentence and from the passages that accompany it the rules of charity, the maintenance of the family, and the spending that supports the defence of the community. The teachers placed the voluntary gift after the obligations, so that the wealth that is lent to God is the surplus that remains after the rights have been settled.

The clause about the withholding and the granting of abundance has been read as the statement about the provision of every creature. The community's practice followed the invitation, and the closing mention of the return gave the instruction its place in the account of the last things.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the invitation to lend to God, the promise of multiplication, the withholding and the abundance that belong to God, and the return of the community to Him. The community's teachers used the verse when they encouraged generosity, and the reader is shown that the sūrah joins the command to strive with the instruction about the wealth that sustains the community.

The teachers used the sentence when they encouraged the community to give from what it owned, and the verse supplied the assurance that the gift is neither lost nor forgotten. The passage also joins the effort of the field to the wealth that supports it, so that the two commands that stand beside one another describe the whole of what the community is asked to offer.
