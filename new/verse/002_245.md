## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:245

> Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Is an Invitation**

The verse opens with a question that has no addressee's answer in it — *Who will lend to Allah a good loan?* — and the question-form is the invitation's genius. God, who owns the treasuries of the heavens and the earth, asks to borrow; the borrower names Himself and the loan's quality together: qarḍ ḥasan, a goodly loan, given willingly, from the lawful, without reminder or reproach attached. The commentators note the inversion the verse performs on every ordinary loan: in human lending the poor borrow from the rich, and the rich stipulate their terms; here the Rich asks the poor to lend, and the stipulation runs the other way — the loan must be *good*, and the repayment is promised at a multiplication no human creditor could offer. The transaction-language is not accidental; the Qur'an consistently frames the human relation to God as commerce — a bargain, a sale, a loan — and consistently assigns the guaranteed side of the ledger to God.

**One Verse, Two Readings, No Contradiction**

The verse's placement between the fight-command and the king-story gave the exegetes its first question: what is the loan for? One reading continues the preceding line of thought — the goodly loan is the effort expended and the risk undertaken in the struggle just commanded, the self and the wealth lent to God's cause on the campaign. Another reads the verse as beginning anew: the loan is charity, the giving of wealth to the poor and to religious causes, the ordinary economics of generosity. Al-Rāzī records the third position that harmonizes them — there is no contradiction between the two, since both are lendings to God and the verse's wording names neither exclusively. The harmonization has the verse's own support: the multiplication-promise attaches to the loan's quality, not to its use, and the decrease-and-increase clause that follows governs wealth in every deployment. The reader is left with a question whose every generous answer — purse or person, dirham or campaign — the verse accepts.

**Abū al-Daḥdāḥ's Garden**

The tradition's own answer to the question is a story. When this verse was revealed, the narrations collected by Ibn Kathīr relate, Abū al-Daḥdāḥ al-Anṣārī came to the Prophet and asked: does God truly seek a loan from us? The Prophet said yes, and Abū al-Daḥdāḥ asked for his hand, took it, and said: I have lent my garden to my Lord. The garden held six hundred date-palms, and his wife and children were living in it. He went to them and called his wife: Umm al-Daḥdāḥ. She answered: here I am. Leave the garden, he said, for I have lent it to my Lord. Her reply became proverbial: a profitable transaction — or words to that effect — and she moved herself and the children out. The Prophet's response, in the narrations, praised what Abū al-Daḥdāḥ now had: how many a date-cluster, heavy with pearls in Paradise, hangs for Abū al-Daḥdāḥ. The story is the verse performed: the question asked aloud, the loan contracted by handshake, the whole estate lent rather than a portion given, and the multiplication acknowledged before it arrived.

**The Loan Verses as a Network**

This is not the Qur'an's only loan-invitation, and the repetitions define the institution. The covenant with the Children of Israel made the good loan one of its conditions — *lend to Allah a good loan, I will certainly forgive your sins and admit you into Gardens under which rivers flow* (5:12); the sūrah of Iron asks the same question with the same multiplication and adds the honorable reward (57:11); the sūrah of Mutual Disillusion attaches the forgiveness and names God Most Appreciative (64:17); and the wrapped-in-garments sūrah repeats the call (73:20). The exegetes draw the network's conclusion: serving God and doing good is always, in the Qur'an's economics, a profitable transaction — and its inverse is legislated too, in the condemnation of those who sell God's signs for a paltry price (2:41). The two sides of the ledger give the loan-language its seriousness: every person is transacting with God constantly; the only question is the terms, and the verse's terms — a loan repaid many times over — are the generous side of a trade both sides must make.

**The Multiplication's Arithmetic**

*Which Allah will multiply many times over* — adʿāf kathīrah, multiples upon multiples — and the Qur'an elsewhere supplies the arithmetic's floor and ceiling. The floor is the sūrah of the Cattle's rule: *Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold* (6:160), while the evil deed draws one. The ceiling is this sūrah's own grain-parable — the spent-in-God's-way wealth likened to a grain growing seven ears, each with a hundred grains, and God multiplying further for whomever He wills (2:261). Between ten and seven hundred and beyond, the multiplication is undefined — which is precisely the invitation's form: a creditor who fixes the interest invites calculation; a creditor who says *many times over* invites trust. The commentators note that the verse's own second clause removes even the calculation's motive: since increase and decrease belong to God alone, the wealth kept back was never secure and the wealth lent out was never lost — the multiplication is not an addition to the lender's estate but a revelation of where the estate always stood.

**Contraction and Expansion**

*It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases* — yaqbiḍu wa-yabsuṭu, He contracts and He expands — and the Sufi commentators found in the two verbs one of the Qur'an's central spiritual vocabularies. Qabḍ, contraction, and basṭ, expansion, name the soul's two alternating states: the heart's tightening, when provision narrows and worship goes dry and God feels withheld; the heart's opening, when both return. The states follow each other, the Sufis say, as night follows day, and they pair with fear and hope — the two reins that must be held in balance for the soul to travel. Some read the verse as a three-stage map of spiritual realization: purification, in which God withholds and strips; the acquisition of the virtues, in which He expands and gives; and return, in which the soul arrives at Him. Under another reading the contraction exposes the soul to God's Majesty and the expansion lets it taste His Beauty. The material sense remains — wealth decreases and increases by His hand alone — and the spiritual sense rides inside it: the purse and the heart contract and expand on the same divine schedule, and the loan is what a person does with the contraction while trusting the expansion.

**And to Him You Will Be Returned**

The verse's final clause closes the economics with the eschatology: the return. Every loan-language elsewhere in the Qur'an ends the same direction, and the ending is what makes the metaphor literal — a loan implies a meeting at repayment, and the meeting-place named here is the return to God. The commentators connect the clause to the two before it: the multiplication is promised because the repayment happens where God's ledger is the only one, and the decrease-and-increase is His because both are instruments of the same journey. The verse thus moves in three steps from invitation to cosmology — lend, for the terms are generous; do not clutch, for the clutching changes nothing; and prepare, for the settlement is not here. The return-clause also answers the question the verse began with, in the only register that finally matters: *who will lend?* is, at the end of the sentence, a question about who understands where he is going.
