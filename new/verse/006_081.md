## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:81

> And how should I fear your associate-gods, while you have no fear in associating ˹others˺ with Allah—a practice He has never authorized? Which side has more right to security? ˹Tell me˺ if you really know!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comparison of Fears**

And how should I fear your associate-gods, while you have no fear in associating others with Allah? The verse turns the audience's objection back on them, and it does so by comparing two fears. Abraham is being told that he should be afraid of the gods his people serve, and his answer is that they should be far more afraid of what they are doing with those gods. The question is not whether fear is appropriate but where it belongs.

The argument is built on a comparison of magnitudes that the audience would have accepted if they had been willing to state their beliefs openly. Their gods were objects they had made, inherited, or named (12:40); the One they associated with those gods had made the heavens and the earth (6:73). A person who fears a stone and does not fear its maker has made an error about proportions, and the verse lets the error speak for itself.

**Nothing Authorizes the Practice**

The clause about practice He has never authorized is the legal form of the same accusation, and the Book states it repeatedly. It says that the names the idolaters use are inventions that God never authorized (12:40), and it asks in the sūrah of the Romans whether God has sent down any authority that attests to what the people associate with Him (30:35). The sūrah of Yūnus records the claim that God has offspring and answers that the claimants have no proof and are speaking about God what they do not know (10:68).

The Book's point throughout is not that the audience had chosen badly among options but that they had no warrant at all. Nothing in the revelation, and nothing in the created order, gives a human being permission to assign a partner to God, and the verse describes the practice as unauthorized rather than merely mistaken.

**The Question of Security**

Which side has more right to security? The verse poses the question and asks the audience to answer it if they know. The question is a summary of the whole passage, since it asks what a person is entitled to feel safe about, and the Book's answer arrives in the following verse in a single sentence: those who believe and do not tarnish their faith with falsehood are the ones who are guaranteed security and are rightly guided.

The Book's word for security covers the sense of being safe from harm and fear, and the idolaters' religion was built on the pursuit of exactly that condition. They kept the gods on their side in order to be safe, and the question asks them to compare the safety of a person who worships the maker of everything with the safety of one who divides his trust among creatures.

**The Concept of Wrong That Tarnishes Faith**

The word used in this verse for association is rendered by the translation as falsehood and by many commentators as an act of oppression or wrong, and the Book's usage supports the wider sense. The following verse says that those who believe and do not tarnish their faith with wrong are the ones who are secure, and the commentators discussed at length whether the wrong in view is the specific wrong of associating something with God or any serious sin.

The strongest reading, and the one the passage's own argument supports, takes the wrong in question to be the association the previous verse has been describing. The Book uses the word for wrong across a wide range, and it defines the greatest of it elsewhere as the worship of something other than God. The sentence that follows the question therefore supplies the criterion the question asked for: security belongs to the believer whose faith has not been mixed with the assignment of partners to God.

**The Idiom of Right and Entitlement**

The phrase the translation renders as which side has more right, with the instruction to tell if you know, is an invitation to judge between two claims. The Book uses this form of challenge in several places where it asks the audience to arbitrate against itself, as when it asks whether the blind and the seeing can be equal or whether darkness and light are the same (13:16).

Those challenges work because the audience already knows the answer. The Makkans would not have claimed that a stone had as much right to their fear as the Creator of the sky, and the verse in front of us asks them to say so out loud. The final words, if you really know, carry the sting, since the sūrah has been demonstrating for eighty verses what they did not know.

**What the Two Verses Establish**

Read together, the two verses state Abraham's full position before his people: no fear of the idols, no acceptance of an unauthorized practice, and a question about where security is to be found. The next verse gives the answer as a statement of fact rather than as an argument, and the sūrah then names Abraham's discourse as the argument God gave him against his people (6:83).

The Book has thus presented the case for God's oneness in three forms across this sūrah: as a series of questions put to the Makkans, as a declaration commanded to the Prophet, and now as a story from the life of the man their tradition claimed as its founder. The last of the three is the one the Book calls God's own argument, which is why it stands at the centre of the sūrah.
