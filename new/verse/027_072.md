## Sūrah an-Naml 27:72

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close at hand.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Answer That Refuses the Question and Still Answers**

They demanded a date. The reply gives none, and yet it is not an evasion: perhaps some of what you are hurrying toward is right behind you.

Three hedges are built into one short sentence — *ʿasā*, perhaps; *baʿḍu*, some of; and a verb of nearness rather than a time. Nothing is promised, nothing is scheduled, and nothing is denied. What the sentence does is remove the assumption their question was resting on. They asked when, expecting either a falsifiable date or an admission of ignorance; they are given the possibility that the answer is soon, which is the one response that makes the delay uncomfortable instead of reassuring.

**"Close Behind You"**

The verb is *radifa*, and al-Ṭabarī glosses it as having come near. Al-Qurṭubī adds the root's more specific sense: to follow directly in someone's footsteps, the way a second rider sits behind the first on the same mount.

That image is exact and it is unnerving. What follows in your footsteps is not visible from where you stand, is not in front of you where it could be watched, and is closer than anything you can see. The word does not say the punishment is coming; it says it may already be behind them, moving at their own pace. A demand for a date assumes the threatened event is somewhere ahead on a road, at a measurable distance. The reply places it in the one position that cannot be measured at all.

**"Some of What You Seek to Hasten"**

The partitive is doing real work. Not all of it — *some* of what you are hurrying toward. The Qur'an is distinguishing between two things the questioners had merged.

They asked about the resurrection, a single event at the end of everything. The answer speaks of a portion arriving sooner, which implies that the accounting comes in instalments and that the final one is not the only one. This is consistent with the chapter's own histories: Pharaoh's army drowned, Thamūd's houses were emptied, Lūṭ's cities were buried, and none of those was the Day of Judgment. Al-Rāzī and others took the near portion here as the defeat the Meccan leadership suffered at Badr, which came within a few years of this chapter's revelation. That identification is a commentator's inference rather than the verse's claim, and it should be marked as such, though the structure of the sentence plainly allows for it.

**Why "Perhaps" Is the Honest Word**

*ʿAsā* in the Qur'an, spoken by God, is not the hedge of someone uncertain. Classical commentators generally read the particle in divine speech as indicating something realised, softened in expression rather than in content.

But the softening is not empty here, and the reason returns to verse 65. The Prophet has been told, and has publicly said, that nobody knows the unseen. A flat prediction in his mouth would contradict that statement four verses later. *ʿAsā* is therefore the precise word: it lets him convey the nearness without claiming the knowledge, and it keeps the two verses consistent. The Qur'an is careful about this in a way a careless text would not be, and the care is itself evidence of how seriously the limit announced in verse 65 was meant.

**The Interval Is the Point**

Strip the exchange to its logic and the two sides are arguing about the same gap in opposite directions. They treat the time before the promised reckoning as evidence that it is not coming. The reply treats it as the space in which the question stops mattering.

What the chapter has been saying about that interval is consistent. Ṣāliḥ asked his people why they were hastening the bad outcome when forgiveness was available in the meantime. The hoopoe's report led to a letter rather than an army, because there was time for an argument. Sheba's queen used her interval to test, consult and travel. Nothing in the chapter suggests that the delay is meaningless; the delay is where everything the chapter cares about happens. A person demanding a date has already decided the interval is empty, and the answer he receives tells him it may be shorter than he assumes.

**What the Commentators Identified as the Near Portion**

Al-Rāzī and others took the part described as close behind them to be the defeat the Meccan leadership suffered at Badr, which came within a few years of this chapter's revelation. The identification is common in the tafsīr literature, where warnings of impending punishment in the Meccan sūrahs are frequently read this way.

The reading has something in its favour beyond hindsight. The verse specifies *some* of what they were hurrying toward, which invites exactly this kind of partial fulfilment, and the men who pressed the question hardest were largely the men who died at Badr. But it remains a commentator's inference, not the verse's statement, and the Qur'an names nothing. [UNVERIFIED: the identification of the near portion with the Battle of Badr is an interpretation offered by al-Rāzī and others; the verse specifies no event.] What the text commits to is only the structure — a portion, near, unnamed.

**Answering a Taunt Without Adopting Its Terms**

The reply is a model of how the chapter handles hostile questions. It does not accept the premise that a date is owed, does not concede that the absence of a date is a weakness, and does not counter-attack.

What it does is supply a possibility the questioner had not budgeted for. Their challenge assumed two outcomes, a date they could test or an admission they could mock. The answer produces a third, and the third is the one that costs them something to hear. Sulaymān used the same technique with the envoys from Sheba, refusing the gift rather than negotiating over it, and the queen used it herself when she declined both war and surrender and sent a gift instead. An-Naml is full of people who decline the options they are offered and construct a better one, and the reply here is the last instance of it in the chapter.
