## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:20

> melting whatever is in their bellies, along with their skin.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sentence, Two Surfaces**

The verse is a single clause and it does one thing: it says what the scalding water of the previous verse does. *Yuṣharu bihi mā fī buṭūnihim wa-l-julūd* — what is in their bellies is melted by it, and the skins too.

The pairing is total by design. The belly is the inside and the skin is the outside, and between them there is nothing left. The Qur'an does not say "their bodies," which would have allowed the reader to imagine a limit. It names the innermost cavity and the outermost membrane, and the punishment is described as operating on both at once.

Sūrah Ibrāhīm names the same totality from the outside in: *“with garments of tar, and their faces covered with flames”* (14:50). Garment worn, face covered — the surface sealed and the meeting-place with the world wrapped in fire. Verse 20 completes the picture from the inside: what the tar garment covers on the skin, the scalding water reaches in the belly. Between the two verses nothing of the sufferer is left undescribed.

**Why the Skin Is Named Separately**

The skin deserves its own note, because it is not listed as a covering but as an organ. The skin is where a person feels. Damage that stops at the skin is superficial by definition; damage that reaches the skin is damage that cannot be ignored.

This is why the Qur'an's punishments are so often described at the surface of the body, and why the same tradition renders the reward in the same register. Sūrah al-Kahf uses the identical vocabulary for the punishment of those who reject the truth: *“When they cry for aid, they will be aided with water like molten metal, which will burn ˹their˺ faces”* (18:29). The face is named there for the same reason the skin is named here — not to be lurid but to locate the suffering where a person actually lives, at the place where he meets the world.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat gives the skin a voice, and the voice testifies: *“They will ask their skin ˹furiously˺, ‘Why have you testified against us?’”* (41:21). The organ of feeling turned witness — what registered every touch now reports it. The skin is named separately in verse 20 for the same reason it speaks there: it is not the wrapping of the person but one of his organs of accountability, the surface at which the world was met and the surface at which the meeting is recalled.

**The Belly: What Is Inside Is Also Reached**

The inside is named with a relative clause — *mā fī buṭūnihim*, whatever is in their bellies — rather than with an organ. Nothing is specified, and the vagueness is doing what the vagueness of verse 1 did: it refuses to let the imagination find an edge.

Theologically, the inside matters for a specific reason in this sūrah. The disbelievers' offence has been described as a matter of the heart and the interior: disputing without knowledge (22:3), worshipping on a brink (22:11), arguing from preference rather than from a source (22:8). A punishment that reaches only the surface would leave the actual site of the disease untouched. The water goes in.

Sūrah al-Baqarah locates the offence where the water goes: *“There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase”* (2:10). Sickness in the interior, increased rather than treated — the disputing, the brink-worship, the preference argued as principle, all of it a condition of the inside. A punishment reaching only the skin would cauterise the surface and leave the sickness; the water that melts *mā fī buṭūnihim* reaches the ward where the disease has been kept.

**Why the Description Is Given in Public at All**

A reasonable objection to verses of this kind is that they are addressed to the living, who are not in the Fire, and who therefore cannot be being threatened with what is described. If the punishment were meant only as information about the next world, it could have been stated in one line.

The Qur'an's answer is implicit in where these verses sit. They are given to people who still have time, and their function is not to satisfy curiosity about the next world but to change conduct in this one. A warning that cannot be pictured does not restrain anybody; a warning with a garment and a temperature does.

This also explains the sūrah's restraint elsewhere. The descriptions are physically specific about instruments and locations and almost entirely silent about the experience of suffering itself. What is being offered is enough to be feared and not enough to be contemplated — the exact balance a warning needs.

**What Melting Does to the Idea of a Self**

The verb *yuṣharu* describes a dissolution — what is in the bellies liquefies, and the skin with it. Applied to a person, the image attacks the boundary that makes a person a person.

A human being's sense of himself depends on his remaining contained: an inside that is his and a surface that marks where he stops. The Qur'an's punishments are not randomly chosen, and this one strikes at exactly that structure. The sufferer is not destroyed — destruction would end the suffering — but he is unmade and kept, dissolved while still conscious of being dissolved.

That is why the verse pairs the inside and the outside rather than choosing one. A punishment that reached only the interior would leave the person intact as an object; a punishment that reached only the skin would leave him intact within. Melting both leaves nothing in him that holds a shape.

**A Punishment for Bodies, Because the Resurrection Is of Bodies**

The concreteness of this verse is an argument, and it is worth stating as one. The Qur'an's account of the afterlife is not a set of images for a state of consciousness; it is a claim about what happens to the person who was buried.

That claim was argued at length in the opening of this sūrah — from the embryo, from the field, from the raising of those in the graves (22:5–7). Verses 19 to 22 are the pay-off of that argument, and they are as physical as the argument was. A doctrine of resurrection that produced a merely spiritual afterlife would not need garments cut to measure or water that melts what is in a belly.

The sūrah is consistent from end to end: the body that was created from dust and raised from the grave is the body that is punished, and the matching promise in verse 23 will dress a body in silk.

**Nothing in the Description Is Remedial**

The last thing to notice is what the verse does not say. It does not say the water cleanses, or that the melting is temporary, or that the sufferer is being prepared for something better.

The Qur'an elsewhere uses fire-language for purification — the report cited earlier in this sūrah compared Islam to a fire that draws the impurities out of metal — and that makes the contrast sharper here. The same image, in the same sūrah, in two opposite applications: fire that refines a person in this life, and fire that does nothing to him but damage in the next. The difference is not the fire. It is whether there is anything left in the man for the fire to purify.

Sūrah al-Humazah names the fire's destination, and it is the interior: *“˹It is˺ Allah's kindled Fire, which rages over the hearts”* (104:6–7). Over the hearts — *taṭṭaliʿu ʿalā al-afʾidah* — the fire reaching the seat of the offence and finding nothing there to refine. The refining fire of this-life trial draws impurity out of metal; the kindled fire mounts over hearts in which nothing remains but the disease. Same element, opposite results — and the difference, as verse 20's silence implies, was made before the fire arrived.
