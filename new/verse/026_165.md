## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:165

> Why do you ˹men˺ lust after fellow men,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lot Names the Direction of Desire**

Lot's question identifies the conduct he is challenging rather than hiding behind a vague reference to wrongdoing. The people are directing sexual desire toward men, and the prophet asks why they have chosen that direction. A question can expose a practice without pretending that the practice is too difficult to describe.

The wording also gives the community a chance to examine desire before it is defended as destiny. Lot does not ask whether desire exists. He asks what the people do with it and why they have made a forbidden object central to their public way of life. Moral responsibility begins where desire meets choice.


A society may decide that a widespread practice should be accepted because many people participate in it. Lot's question refuses that measure. Popularity cannot settle what God permits. The community must answer to the Creator who made human beings and knows the purposes and limits of their bodies.

This does not give individuals permission to act as investigators of other people's private lives. Moral seriousness must be joined to justice, privacy, and avoidance of slander. Lot is a messenger addressing a public practice and a people who have made it part of their collective conduct. His warning should not be turned into a license for personal cruelty.

**Desire Does Not Become Right Through Intensity**

The force of an appetite can make it feel authoritative. A person may reason that because a desire is strong, fulfilling it must be necessary or good. Lot's question exposes the gap between intensity and moral truth. Feelings can be real without becoming commands that God has authorized.

Taqwa gives the servant a place to stand when desire presses hard. It does not require pretending that temptation is absent. It requires recognizing that the self is not its own lawgiver. A desire must be disciplined by revelation, wisdom, and concern for the consequences imposed on oneself and others.


Quran 29:28–30 records Lot rebuking his people for male-male desire and also mentions their abuse of travelers and misconduct in public gatherings. That parallel shows that the present question belongs to a wider moral disorder. The problem is not presented as an isolated private feeling but as part of a community's public conduct.

The parallel also explains why Lot's warning carries social urgency. When a practice is reinforced by intimidation, public celebration, or harm to outsiders, it affects more than the people who choose it. A community has to examine the culture it creates around desire and power.


The verse condemns a practice without instructing believers to treat every person associated with it as worthless. A sinner remains a human being who can repent and whose basic rights must not be violated. Lot's role is to warn and invite return, not to authorize humiliation or violence.

This distinction is essential because moral correction can be corrupted by hatred. A person who despises a forbidden act may still behave unjustly toward the one who commits it. Fear of Allah requires the critic to obey God's limits while calling others back to them.


Lot speaks to men as a group because the conduct has become a defining part of the society he addresses. A private struggle can be met with counsel and repentance; a public order that pressures people to accept wrongdoing requires a public warning. The prophet challenges the standard the community is building around desire.

A culture can make resistance difficult by mocking those who refuse, excluding families who seek guidance, or teaching the young that restraint is shameful. Lot's question interrupts that formation. It tells the people that a society does not have the right to redefine God's command simply because it has organized itself around disobedience.

**The Question Restores Created Purpose**

The phrase “fellow men” is set against the created order referenced in the following line. Lot's argument is not that men lack dignity or that women are objects. It is that sexual desire has been directed away from the marital purpose God created and toward a practice the revelation condemns.

The question therefore joins bodily creation to moral responsibility. Human beings are not self-invented creatures free to assign every desire its own purpose. They receive life, body, and capacity from God, and they must ask how those gifts are to be used with justice and restraint.

**A Messenger Challenges the Community's Self-Description**

The people may describe themselves as liberated, advanced, or merely following their nature. Lot's question refuses to let a slogan settle the matter. It asks them to compare their chosen practice with God's guidance and with the harms already connected to their public behavior.

A community may become so used to a sin that naming it sounds like hostility. The hostility, however, is not in the clear warning. It lies in the refusal to allow any authority above the community's appetite. Lot speaks because a people needs more than self-description; it needs truthful judgment.


The verse is not a command to search for scandal in strangers. It is a prophetic challenge to a society that has normalized a forbidden sexual practice and connected it with wider corruption. The reader should take the moral claim seriously without inventing permissions for gossip, abuse, or vigilantism.

Lot's question asks whether desire will rule the people or whether the people will fear God. The answer requires repentance, restraint, and protection of human dignity under divine law. A clear moral boundary can be held without abandoning compassion for those who need guidance.

The question also prevents the community from hiding behind silence. If everyone knows what is being practiced and no one may name it, the absence of criticism becomes a false sign of peace. Lot breaks that silence so that desire can be judged by revelation rather than by the comfort of the crowd.
