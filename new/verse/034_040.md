## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:40

> And ˹consider˺ the Day He will gather them all together, and then ask the angels, “Was it you that these ˹polytheists˺ used to worship?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Put to the Wrong Party**

The scene is a courtroom and the first question is not addressed to the accused. God gathers everyone and then turns to the angels: *a-hāʾulāʾi iyyākum kānū yaʿbudūn* — was it you these people were worshipping? The interrogation of a third party in front of the defendants is a recognisable legal technique, and its purpose is not to obtain information. The One asking knows the answer, and so does everyone in the room. What the question does is force a public statement from the only party whose testimony the defendants cannot dispute. A worshipper can argue with his accuser; he cannot argue with the object of his own worship. By putting the question to the angels rather than to the idolaters, the scene removes any possibility of the defence continuing.

**Why the Angels and Not the Idols**

The Qur'an could have staged this with the stone figures of the Kaʿbah, and elsewhere it does describe the summoning of the partners (28:64). Choosing the angels here is deliberate, and the reason is that they are the strongest case for the other side. Stone cannot answer, which makes its silence ambiguous; a being with intelligence, standing and genuine proximity to God can answer, and is the sort of being for whom the intercession argument was actually made. Some among the Arabs held the angels to be daughters of God, a claim the Qur'an rejects sharply elsewhere. If the highest beings ever credited with divinity disown the worship offered to them, nothing below them can retain a claim. The sūrah has been narrowing toward this point since it denied the idols an atom's weight of ownership (34:22) and showed the angels themselves waiting in terror for a decree (34:23).

**The Emphatic Pronoun in the Middle of the Question**

Arabic word order carries the emphasis here in a way English struggles to reproduce. The question places *iyyākum*, you, before the verb — was it **you** they were worshipping? The isolated pronoun throws the weight onto the identity of the object rather than onto the act. This matters because the answer that follows will turn on exactly that point: the angels will not deny that worship occurred, only that they were its true recipient. The question is therefore precisely framed to elicit that distinction. It does not ask whether these people were idolaters, which is not in dispute, but whether the worship reached the addressee it named. A gift sent to the wrong address is still a gift sent, and it is still lost.

**The Verb Tense Describes a Habit**

*Kānū yaʿbudūn* is the continuous past — they **used to** worship, over and over, as a settled practice. The Qur'an consistently describes the conduct that is judged in this tense rather than in the simple past, and the sūrah has done so twice already in this passage, with those who were repaid for what they *used to do* (34:33) and those who denied what they *used to* deny. What is being examined at the reckoning is not an episode but a pattern. This has a practical implication for how the scene should be read. Nobody in it is being convicted of a moment of confusion or a single misdirected prayer. The defendants spent lifetimes in a practice, built institutions around it, and defended it in public, which is why the question can be put so simply and answered so briefly.

**A Parallel Interrogation Elsewhere**

Al-Furqān stages the same courtroom with a sharper question. There God gathers them along with what they worshipped besides Him and asks: *"Was it you who misled these servants of Mine, or did they stray from the Way ˹on their own˺?"* (25:17). That version puts responsibility directly in issue, asking whether the worshipped party recruited the worshippers. Sabaʾ's version asks only about the identity of the recipient, which is the narrower and, for this sūrah's purposes, the more useful question. The chapter has already settled responsibility in the quarrel between leaders and followers, where each was repaid for his own conduct (34:33). What remains to be established is whether the whole apparatus of intermediary worship ever connected to anything, and that is what this question is designed to expose.

**The Silence of the Accused**

One feature of the scene is easy to miss: the idolaters are present and say nothing. They are gathered, they are indicated — *these* — and they are discussed in the third person while standing there. The sūrah opened with a mockery in which the Prophet was talked about in the third person to a crowd, as *a man who claims* (34:7). The arrangement is now reversed, and the men who reduced him to an exhibit are themselves the exhibit, pointed at in a conversation they are not part of. Nothing in the verse comments on this. The reversal is delivered by the grammar and left there, which is how the Qur'an usually handles such symmetries.

**Gathering Before Questioning**

The sequence of the verse puts the assembly first and the interrogation second: *yaḥshuruhum jamīʿan thumma yaqūl* — He gathers them all, **then** He says. The particle *thumma* marks a gap between the two actions rather than a smooth continuation, so the scene includes a pause: everyone is present and nothing has yet been said. This is the third time the sūrah has mentioned the gathering, after the answer to the taunt about timing (34:26) and the Day appointed that cannot be moved (34:30), and each mention adds a detail the others lacked. Here the addition is the presence of every party at once — worshippers and worshipped, accusers and accused, in one place. The efficiency of the arrangement is part of its terror. Disputes that ran for lifetimes across separate communities are settled in a single session because, for the first time, everyone involved is in the room.

The angels' answer comes in the next verse and it is not a defence of the people who worshipped them.
