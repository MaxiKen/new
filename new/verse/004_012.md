## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:12

> You will inherit half of what your wives leave if they are childless. But if they have children, then ˹your share is˺ one-fourth of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless. But if you have children, then your wives will receive one-eighth of your estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but only a brother or a sister ˹from their mother’s side˺, they will each inherit one-sixth, but if they are more than one, they ˹all˺ will share one-third of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm ˹to the heirs˺. ˹This is˺ a commandment from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shares for Spouses After the Children’s Shares**

This verse completes the fixed portions begun in the previous one by turning from children and parents to spouses and siblings. The husband inherits one-half of his wife’s estate when she leaves no child, and one-fourth when she leaves a child; the wife inherits one-fourth of her husband’s estate when he leaves no child, and one-eighth when he leaves a child. The pattern is consistent: the presence of offspring reduces the spouse’s share because the offspring themselves have the larger fixed claims detailed in *“Allah commands you regarding your children”* (4:11). The Qur’an counts grandchildren through a son among children for this purpose, and it directs that where there is more than one wife — the sūrah having limited wives to four (4:3) — the one-fourth or one-eighth is divided equally among them. That last point matters because it shows how the sūrah’s earlier permission is immediately bounded by arithmetic. A man who takes an additional wife does not create an additional estate share; the existing share is partitioned. The fractions are therefore not only about entitlement but about keeping the household’s expectations inside a known economy.

**The Meaning of Child and of Heirlessness**

The phrase *wa lakum niṣfu mā taraka azwājukum in lam yakun lahunna walad* was understood to include sons, daughters, and grandchildren from a son, because all of them are *walad* in inheritance law. The same inclusion applies on the wife’s side. The reduction from half to quarter and from quarter to eighth therefore applies whenever there is a descendant who will inherit as a child. When no such descendant exists, the spouse’s share rises, but it never consumes the whole estate where other fixed heirs exist. The verse’s third case turns to *kalālah* — a person who leaves neither parent nor child — and assigns shares to brothers and sisters from the mother’s side: one-sixth for one, one-third shared equally when there are more. The qualification *from their mother’s side* is supplied by the recitation and consensus of the early authorities and by the contrast with verse 176, where full or paternal siblings receive a different, gender-differentiated share. Here, maternal siblings share equally regardless of gender, one-third divided evenly, which again shows that the Qur’an does not apply a single ratio everywhere.

**After Bequests and Debts — Without Harm**

The refrain *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin yūṣā bihā aw dayn* — after bequests and debts — appears for a third and fourth time, with the addition *ghayra muḍār* — without harm to the heirs — attached to this verse. The repetition teaches sequence and limits. Debts are paid first, then bequests within one-third, then the fixed shares. To attempt to harm heirs by a bequest that favours one, that disguises a gift as a debt, or that exceeds the known limits is forbidden, and Prophetic tradition invalidated any bequest to a fixed heir that was meant to enlarge his portion beyond what God assigned. The clause without harm therefore guards the fixed shares from being undone by testamentary manoeuvring. The sūrah’s earlier command to be mindful of God when handling orphans’ wealth (4:9) and its warning that consuming it is eating fire (4:10) find a legal counterpart here: the estate is not a private hoard to dispose of at whim; it is a measured trust whose portions are already set. Even the bequest that the Qur’an permits is not a tool to rewrite the fractions but a space to remember those without a fixed share, the very group verse 8 asked distributors to notice with provision and kind speech.

**Why the Shares Differ Between Husband and Wife**

The difference between half and quarter, and between quarter and eighth, at times raises the same question as the two-to-one share among children: why not equal? The sūrah’s answer is structural, not evaluative. The husband is charged with maintenance — *“Men are the caretakers of women, as men have been provisioned by Allah over women and tasked with supporting them financially”* (4:34) — and his larger share corresponds to the larger ongoing obligation he bears after the estate is divided. The wife’s share, while smaller, is hers alone, to keep and to dispose of as she wishes, and the sūrah has already secured that autonomy with *“Give women their due dowries graciously”* (4:4) and with the protection of her property in guardianship rules. Moreover, the total economy of the family often balances across generations: a daughter receives a smaller share from her parents but may later receive a dowry, maintenance, and a share from her husband, while a son receives more but spends it on others. Commentators who traced these flows over a single family’s cycle concluded that the system aims at overall sufficiency rather than identical fractions at each node. The closing Names — *“Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing”* (4:12) — frame that structure with knowledge of every household’s needs and forbearance toward those who find the division difficult to accept.

**Maternal Siblings Share Equally**

The kalālah clause for maternal siblings deserves separate attention because it breaks the gendered pattern. *Wa in kāna rajulun yūrathu kalālatan aw imraʾatun wa lahu akhun aw ukhtun fa li-kulli wāḥidin minhumā al-sudus* — if a man or woman leaves neither parent nor child but has a brother or sister, each receives one-sixth — treats brother and sister identically when they are linked only through the mother. When there are more than two, they share one-third equally. That equal sharing was read as reinforcing the principle that where the link is maternal and the deceased leaves no parent or offspring, the siblings represent the mother’s line and are therefore given like portions. The contrast with verse 176, where a full sister may receive one-half and full siblings divide with the male receiving twice the female, is not a contradiction but a distinction between types of siblinghood. Jurists detailed many further cases — grandparents, grandchildren, paternal siblings — building outward from these texts, but the two poles remained: fixed Qur’anic shares where the text speaks, and residuary distribution to the nearest male agnate where it does not. Even there, the sūrah’s earlier refrain — *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6) — keeps the calculator humble.

**A Commandment That Closes With Forbearance**

The verse ends with *waṣiyyatan min Allāh wa Allāhu ʿAlīmun Ḥalīm* — a commandment from Allah, and Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing. To call the fractions a *waṣiyyah* from God places them beside the human bequests just mentioned but above them. A person’s bequest is his wish; God’s *waṣiyyah* is law. Yet the Name that follows is not only All-Knowing but also Forbearing — *Ḥalīm* — the One who does not hasten to punish. That forbearance is directed at recipients who may struggle with the division, especially the intense moment when a spouse or sibling is present and the reduced share feels personal. The sūrah will immediately follow with *“These are the limits set by Allah”* (4:13) and the promise of Gardens for keeping them and Fire for crossing them (4:13–14), showing that forbearance does not remove accountability. It simply marks that the lawgiver knows the difficulty and still asks for compliance. In that light, the precise arithmetic of half, quarter, eighth, sixth, and third becomes more than calculation; it becomes the measured way a community that began from a single soul (4:1) learns to part with wealth at death without parting from one another.
