## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:12

> You will inherit half of what your wives leave if they are childless. But if they have children, then ˹your share is˺ one-fourth of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless. But if you have children, then your wives will receive one-eighth of your estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but only a brother or a sister ˹from their mother’s side˺, they will each inherit one-sixth, but if they are more than one, they ˹all˺ will share one-third of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm ˹to the heirs˺. ˹This is˺ a commandment from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing.

### Expanded Commentary

**Spouses remain heirs across household boundaries**

The ordinance in this verse protects the marital bond without allowing it to erase blood relations. A husband receives half of his wife’s estate when she leaves no child and a quarter when she does; a wife receives a quarter of her husband’s estate when he leaves no child and an eighth when he does. The difference reflects the deceased person’s surviving financial responsibilities, while the basic principle is mutual: marriage creates an enforceable claim in both directions.

If there are several wives, they share the collective portion assigned to wives. The text speaks in fractions because justice here must be calculable. A family’s grief cannot be allowed to become an excuse for uncertainty, and a widow should not have to bargain with her husband’s relatives for what revelation has already made hers.

**The maternal siblings and the meaning of *kalālah***

The brother or sister mentioned in this part is understood by the exegetes to be a maternal sibling, in the case described here, when the deceased leaves neither parents nor children. Each receives a sixth if there is one; several share a third equally. This equal division differs from the ratio for certain other siblings in verse 176 and illustrates why inheritance cannot be reduced to slogans. The identity of the heir and the relationship to the deceased matter.

The classical commentators discussed the scope of *kalālah*—one who dies without direct descendants and, in many interpretations, without a surviving father or grandfather. The Prophetic and early legal traditions clarify cases not apparent from a quick reading. The wise practice is to begin with a complete family tree, not with the portion one hopes to receive.

**No bequest designed to injure**

Both the priority of debts and bequests and the prohibition of harm are repeated. A valid bequest cannot be a device for depriving fixed heirs, favouring one child secretly, or creating a burden that consumes the estate. The Prophet warned that a bequest should not injure the heirs, and the jurists therefore distinguish a lawful bequest within its limits from an abusive arrangement disguised as charity.

The conclusion joins knowledge to forbearance. God knows the needs behind the shares, and He is forbearing with human grief and confusion. The law is firm, but people applying it must not be harsh, hasty, or vindictive. Proper succession begins with obedience and is carried out with patience.

**A complete estate, not a preferred heir**

The surviving spouse should be identified at the beginning of the calculation rather than treated as an outsider to the deceased’s birth family. Likewise, maternal siblings should not be forgotten because their relationship is less visible in patriarchal customs. A careful executor inventories debts and bequests before distributing anything, since generosity with property that belongs to another is not generosity.
