## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:61

> They promised, “We will try to convince his father to let him come. We will certainly do ˹our best˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb They Use Is the Chapter's Verb of Persuasion**

The brothers say *sanurāwidu ʿanhu abāhu* — we will seek to win him from his father. The root is *r-w-d*, and the chapter has already used it twice in a very different setting. The ʿAzīz's wife was described with the same verb when the city's women reported her conduct (12:30), and she used it of herself when she admitted the attempt before them (12:32) and again before the king (12:51). In each case the verb denotes a sustained, skilful effort to move another person toward something they are resisting. Applied here to ten sons addressing an unwilling father, it carries a weight the English rendering only partly conveys: this will not be a request but a campaign. A campaign has a shape, and this one does. The brothers come back with provisions to display, with a minister's wording to repeat, with money found in their own bags to explain, and with a pledge to offer. Each element arrives in a separate verse, and the accumulation is what finally moves a man whom nothing else has moved.

**What the Campaign Will Cost**

The brothers know their father's position before they speak. Verse 64 will show that Jacob answers their request with a memory of the last time they asked for a son to be entrusted to them. They are not addressing a man who needs persuading on a small point; they are addressing a father who has already lost one child to their care and who has spent years refusing consolation (12:84). The promise in this verse is therefore a promise to overcome a settled grief, and the brothers make it in the same room as the man who is being asked to supply the food. They commit to the difficult part of the task before they have begun it, in front of an official who knows exactly how difficult it is.

**Two Clauses, Two Different Kinds of Certainty**

The verse ends with an emphatic construction: *wa-innā la-fāʿilūn* — and we will surely do it. The first clause promises an effort, and the second promises an outcome. The gap between them is where the verse's tension lies. Effort is within their control; the outcome depends on a father whose refusal they cannot rule out, and on a set of circumstances — a famine, a rationed granary, a minister's condition — none of which they understand. The double assurance is characteristic of these brothers. Their earlier promise about Joseph was equally emphatic (12:12–14), and the emphatic form did not prevent the outcome it guaranteed from failing. The emphatic form is therefore doubly loaded. A reader who watched the first guarantee dissolve knows that intensity of wording is not evidence of reliability, and the brothers have chosen the same register regardless. What the verse offers is not a stronger promise but a repeated one, and repetition is the only additional security these men have left to give their father.

**The Echo of Their First Promise**

Compare the wording with the promise made decades earlier. To a father reluctant to send Joseph out with them, the brothers said they were his sincere well-wishers, asked that he be sent with them to graze and play, and undertook to guard him (12:11–14). The structure is identical to this verse: a request that a son accompany them, a claim of good intention, an undertaking of protection. Jacob recognised the structure then and recognised it again in verse 64. The chapter sets the two promises side by side without comment, and lets the reader supply the obvious question — whether a pledge that failed once can be relied on when it is repeated in the same form. That silence is among the chapter's most economical devices. It does not need to recall the well, the shirt or the invented wolf; the echo in the wording brings all three forward unbidden. What was once done by ten men to their father is now being promised again by the same ten men, in almost the same words, and the verse says nothing about it.

**Joseph Hears a Promise Whose Value He Knows**

The verse is spoken in Joseph's presence. He has just heard ten men undertake to persuade their father to release a brother, and he is the person they are speaking about without knowing it. He heard the first promise too, or its equivalent, from the same mouths at the edge of a well. The chapter does not report his reaction, as it does not report his reactions anywhere in this section. What it does is place him in the room while a commitment is made that he has already seen broken, and then have him act in the following verse as though the commitment will be kept — loading their bags with food and returning their money so that they come back.

**A Single Sentence Given Its Own Verse**

The verse is short. In Arabic it is a single utterance of nine words, and the chapter gives it a whole verse rather than attaching it to the end of verse 60 or the beginning of verse 62. That allocation is a signal. The promise is the hinge between the condition Joseph imposes and the negotiation that follows in Canaan, and the sūrah marks it as such by isolating it. Everything from verse 63 to verse 67 is the working out of these nine words: the report to Jacob, the refusal, the discovery of the money, the second plea, the oath. The verse is the smallest in this sequence and the one that generates the rest of it. Its brevity is functional. A longer verse would have explained the pledge; this one simply records it and leaves the terms to be contested in the dialogue that follows. The chapter treats the sentence as a move within an argument rather than as a statement of fact, and gives it exactly the amount of space a move requires.

**The Promise Is Not Yet an Oath**

Note what is absent. The brothers swear nothing here. They state an intention to their employer, and the statement binds them to him but not to God. The oath comes five verses later, extracted by their father and sworn in God's name with God as its witness (12:66). The chapter distinguishes carefully between the two: a promise made in a granary to obtain food, and a covenant made at home under a father's insistence. Only the second carries religious weight, and only the second will be the measure by which the brothers are judged when Benjamin is detained. Verse 61 is the preliminary commitment; verse 66 is the one that counts. The difference is formal and it matters. A promise offered to persuade is an instrument of the persuasion; an oath extracted by a father who has already lost one son is a covenant, with a named witness and a specified release. The chapter measures the distance between the two by placing five verses of negotiation in between them.

**Ten Men Agreeing in One Voice**

The verse reports a collective utterance. Ten brothers, who have just been given food by a foreign official and a condition they cannot meet alone, answer together with a single sentence and no recorded disagreement. The unanimity is worth noticing, because the same group will later divide. In verse 80 the eldest refuses to leave Egypt while the others go home, and in verse 81 they return to Jacob with a report he does not believe. Here they speak as one, and the chapter gives no hint of reluctance from any of them. The agreement is easy to reach at this stage because it costs nothing yet: nobody has spoken to the father, nobody has sworn an oath, and the difficulty of the task is still an abstraction. The sūrah records the moment when the brothers are most united and least tested.
