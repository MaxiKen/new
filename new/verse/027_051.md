## Sūrah an-Naml 27:51

> See then what the consequences of their plan were: We ˹utterly˺ destroyed them and their people all together.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Look, Used a Second Time**

The verse opens with the same imperative the chapter applied to Pharaoh's court in verse 14: *fa-anẓur kayfa kāna* — see then how it was. Thirty-seven verses apart, two nations are handed to the reader with the same instruction.

The formula recurs throughout the Qur'an at the close of destruction narratives, and its function is to convert a story into evidence. What the reader is told to examine is not the wickedness but the *ʿāqibah*, the end, the upshot. A story judged by its ending is being treated as a completed experiment, and experiments can be compared. The repetition within a single chapter turns two separate accounts into a series, and a series with two matching results is the beginning of a pattern. That is the argument an-Naml has been assembling since its opening verses, and here is where it starts to close.

**The Plot Is What Fared Badly**

The grammar is exact about the subject. It is not *see how they fared*; it is *see how the consequence of their plot was*. The plan itself is the thing whose outcome the reader is invited to inspect.

That precision matters because the previous verse established the frame. They plotted, and a plot was made about them, and they did not perceive it. The question now on the table is whose plan produced its intended result. Theirs was designed to remove Ṣāliḥ, protect their standing, and satisfy an heir with a coordinated lie. What it produced was the annihilation of the people it was meant to serve. Nothing in the verse suggests the conspirators were prevented from acting or that their scheme was clumsy. It was a competent plan by powerful men, and its consequence was the opposite of its purpose, which is a stronger claim than saying it failed.

**"Them and Their People All Together"**

The destruction extends past the nine. *Dammarnāhum wa qawmahum ajmaʿīn* — We destroyed them and their people, all of them. A conspiracy of nine ends a nation.

This is the hardest sentence in the passage and it should not be softened. The Qur'an has just taken care to identify the instigators by number and by conduct, distinguishing them from the city at large, and then reports a destruction that covers everyone. The believers are excepted two verses later, which is the text's own qualification and the place where the moral accounting is done. What remains is the claim that a community which allows nine men to run it into this is not a bystander to what they do. The chapter had shown the alternative in Sheba, where a court was consulted, spoke honestly, and was heard. Thamūd's population is not recorded objecting to anything.

**Destroyed Utterly**

The verb is *dammara*, and it is stronger than the general words for punishment or seizure. The root denotes ruin that leaves nothing standing, and the Qur'an reserves it for the most complete destructions it describes.

Its force here is confirmed by the next verse, which points at empty houses still standing in the landscape. The people are gone and the architecture remains, which is the specific form this ruin took. Nothing is said in this chapter about the mechanism — other accounts of Thamūd describe a cry, a quaking, a thunderbolt — and an-Naml withholds all of it. The omission keeps the emphasis on the reversal rather than the spectacle. What the reader is asked to look at is a result, not a disaster.

**A Warning Placed Between Two Rescues**

Sequence is doing work here. Verse 44 ended with a queen declaring faith. Verse 53 will record the deliverance of those who believed. This verse sits between them, and it is the only one of the three that ends badly.

The arrangement prevents either extreme reading. A chapter of rescues would make the warnings decorative; a chapter of destructions would make the invitations empty. An-Naml alternates, and the alternation is the argument: the same message reached Sheba and Thamūd, and the outcomes diverged entirely according to what was done with it. Nothing in the two situations made the results inevitable. A sun-worshipping queen with every political reason to resist submitted; a nation warned by its own brother, with a believing faction already among them, ended as empty stone. The next verse tells the reader where those stones are.

**What the Nine Achieved**

Trace the conspirators' intentions against their result. They wanted Ṣāliḥ silenced, their standing in the city preserved, the she-camel affair closed, and an heir satisfied with a coordinated lie. Every one of those objectives concerned the continuation of normal life in their city.

What the plan produced was the end of the city. Not a setback, not an exposure, not a reversal of fortune for the nine alone — the removal of the entire community whose arrangements they were protecting. That is the specific irony the imperative points at. The plot was rational on its own terms, and its terms were wrong, because they had left out the only factor that would decide the outcome. The chapter had already named that omission in verse 25, where the hoopoe described God as knowing what is concealed and what is disclosed, and again in verse 50, where the plot they could not see was running alongside the one they made. This verse is where the two threads meet and the account is settled.

**Nothing Is Said About How**

An-Naml gives no mechanism. Other chapters describe a cry, a shaking of the earth, a thunderbolt falling on Thamūd; this one reports only that the destruction happened and directs the reader to inspect the consequence.

The silence is consistent with the chapter's method throughout. It withheld the contents of Sheba's gift, the name of her queen, the identity of the man with knowledge of the Book, and the mechanism by which a throne crossed a continent. In each case what is kept is the part that carries the argument and what is dropped is the part that would satisfy curiosity. A reader who wants to know what Thamūd heard in their last hour is asking a question the chapter has decided is not the point. What it wants examined is the gap between a plan and its consequence, and the mechanism of the destruction adds nothing to that.
