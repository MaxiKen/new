## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:177

> when Shu’aib said to them, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shuʿayb Begins With Reverence**

Shuʿayb addresses the residents of the Forest with the same essential question heard by the earlier communities: will they fear Allah? The question places worship and accountability before the particular social problems that will be discussed. A society cannot repair its dealings while treating God as irrelevant to those dealings.

Taqwa is an awareness that guards the heart from crossing limits. It joins fear of judgment to hope in God's mercy and makes a person careful with wealth, promises, measurement, and power. Shuʿayb's question is therefore the root of the practical reform associated with his mission.


The question is not addressed to an abstract idea of corruption. Shuʿayb speaks to people with a shared place, economy, and identity. Their customs may feel natural because they have been repeated by families and protected by leaders. The question interrupts that familiarity and asks whether the community's way of life can stand before God.

Quran 7:85 presents Shuʿayb speaking to Midian as their brother and calling them to worship Allah, use just measure and weight, and avoid corruption. That parallel shows how reverence becomes social conduct. Fear of God cannot remain in the prayer space while transactions and public decisions ignore His limits.

**Taqwa Protects Fair Dealing**

A person who fears Allah knows that a hidden deception is still known. He cannot treat an absent customer, traveler, or weaker partner as an opportunity to take more than is due. Taqwa gives moral weight to actions that worldly courts may never observe.

This is why Shuʿayb begins with God rather than with a technical rule. Rules can be evaded through clever wording, but reverence changes the intention that seeks loopholes. The honest trader and the just leader ask not only what they can get away with, but what they will answer for before the Lord.


Shuʿayb could open with accusation, but he first asks whether the people fear Allah. The question gives them a way to understand later commands as part of a relationship with God rather than as an attack on their livelihood. Correction becomes an invitation to return, not merely a list of penalties.

Mercy does not require avoiding the truth. A society cannot repent from wrongdoing it refuses to name. But naming harm without calling people back to God can produce shame without reform. Shuʿayb's opening holds both together: reverence is the foundation, and reform is the evidence of reverence.

**Fear of Allah Is Not Fear of People**

A dishonest community may fear losing a customer more than displeasing God. A ruler may fear public opinion more than injustice. Shuʿayb's question reverses that hierarchy. People should care about one another's rights, but their deepest fear must be directed to the Lord who sees every exchange and every hidden motive.

This frees a person from the pressure to imitate wrongdoing. If everyone around him cheats, the fear of being called naive can be strong. Taqwa gives him a greater reason to remain honest. He can accept a worldly loss rather than purchase temporary approval through disobedience.


The people live among trees or a wooded setting, yet a blessed landscape cannot make them automatically grateful. The same environment can sustain honest trade or conceal predatory behavior. Shuʿayb's question shows that moral character is not produced by geography alone.

A community may be proud of its land and resources while treating those gifts as private entitlement. Fear of Allah teaches stewardship. The land, its routes, and its provisions belong ultimately to the Lord, so the people must use them in ways that preserve justice rather than enlarge exploitation.

**Taqwa Gives Reform a Higher Reason**

A market can adopt rules because cheating damages reputation, but those rules may collapse when enforcement is weak. Taqwa supplies an unseen reason to remain truthful. It makes the individual accountable even when no inspector, customer, or neighbor can expose him.

This inner accountability does not eliminate public law. It supports law by making compliance more than fear of punishment. A just community needs both fair institutions and hearts willing to honor them. Shuʿayb's question begins where durable reform must begin: with recognition that God is present in every choice.


The people can answer by accepting God's authority or by treating the question as an intrusion. Shuʿayb does not force their hearts. He makes the moral issue plain and leaves them responsible for the response. A question about fear can be the first step toward repentance while the community still has time.

For the reader, the question reaches beyond trade. Do I fear God when money, reputation, desire, or group loyalty pressures me? Taqwa is not a mood reserved for danger. It is the habit of placing God's knowledge and judgment above the advantage that tempts the hand.


The residents of the Forest will be tested not by what they say about God but by whether fear of Him shapes their dealings. A community that worships publicly while defrauding privately has separated religion from life. Shuʿayb's opening question refuses that separation.

The proper response is therefore comprehensive. Worship the Lord, fear His judgment, and let that fear produce honesty, fairness, and repair. The question is short because the principle is clear: no community can call itself secure while its daily conduct ignores the One who sees it.

Taqwa also changes how a community treats repentance. When people fear Allah, they do not defend a wrongdoer merely because his success benefits them. They make room for confession, restitution, and a return to honest practice. A society that only protects its reputation will conceal harm; a society mindful of God will repair it.
