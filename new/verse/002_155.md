## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:155

> We will certainly test you with a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. Give good news to those who patiently endure—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise of Testing**

The verse states that the community will be tested, and the sentence is phrased as an announcement rather than as a warning. The Book does not ask whether the community will face difficulty; it states that difficulty will come, and it names the forms the difficulty will take.

The claim is connected with the preceding verses, where the community was told to seek help through patience and prayer. A community that has been given a direction of prayer, a messenger, and a Book is told that its possession of those gifts does not exempt it from loss, and the verse's list of trials follows directly from the instruction about endurance. What the reader receives is a statement about the normal course of the life of faith.

**The Five Trials Named**

The sentence names fear, hunger, and the loss of property, of lives, and of crops, and the commentators explained each term with a range of possibilities. Loss of lives was read as the deaths of relatives, friends, and companions, and loss of crops was said to include children, who are described in the commentaries as the fruit of the heart.

The list is arranged from what affects the community as a whole to what affects a household, and the reader is shown that the testing reaches every dimension of life. The Book does not restrict the trials to persecution or to war; it includes the difficulties that come with ordinary existence, so that no member of the community is left outside the scope of the verse.

**The Reports on the Meaning of Each Term**

The commentaries preserve readings in which the terms are given spiritual senses as well as literal ones. One report connects the fear with the fear of God, the hunger with the fast of Ramadan, the loss of property with the alms that are given, and the loss of lives and crops with illness and with the death of children.

These readings do not replace the plain sense of the words, and the sūrah's earlier passages have used the terms in their ordinary meanings. What they show is that the community's teachers looked for the ways in which each believer would meet the list, and they treated the verse as a description of experiences that the community would recognise in its own life rather than as a catalogue of remote disasters.

**"Give Good News to Those Who Patiently Endure"**

The sentence closes with a command to give good news to the patient, and the Book uses the language of good news where a reward is attached to a duty. The news that follows in the next verses is that those who endure receive blessings from their Lord, mercy, and guidance.

The command is placed after the list of trials, and the order gives the reader the comfort before he meets the difficulty. The Book's method in this passage is to name what will happen and to state the outcome for those who hold firm, so that the community's expectation is formed in advance rather than assembled under pressure.

**Patience at the First Blow**

The reports describe the quality the verse requires in a way that rules out a late resignation. The Prophet ﷺ stated that patience is at the first stroke of the affliction, and the commentators explained the saying to mean that the steadiness that counts is the one shown while the blow is being felt.

The Book's teaching on patience runs through the sūrah, and the community's scholars drew a distinction between the endurance that belongs to the moment of loss and the acceptance that arrives once the pain has passed. The verse's promise is attached to the first, and the reader is told that the reward is connected to the response that begins immediately.

**The Distance Between Complaint and Endurance**

The commentators discussed what patience forbids, and al-Qurṭubī answered the question by pointing to the prophets. He noted that Job described his affliction before God in strong words, and that the same prophet is described in the Book as one who was found steadfast, which shows that the complaint a servant brings to his Lord does not break his patience.

The distinction the community's scholars drew is between the complaint that is directed to God and the complaint that is directed against Him. What the verse asks for is the endurance that keeps a person from charging his Lord with injustice, and the reports about the prophets supply the standard for a person who has reached the limit of what he can bear.

**The Book's Use of the List Elsewhere**

The elements of the list appear in other passages of the Book, and the reader who has followed the sūrah will recognise them. The Quraysh are reminded that they were protected from hunger and from fear, and an ungrateful town is described as having been clothed in both. The Book also states that a hunger endured in God's way is recorded as a righteous deed, and the verse's list belongs to that body of teaching.

The repetitions show that the Book treats the trials of this life as a subject it returns to rather than one it mentions once. Each passage supplies a slightly different emphasis, and the reading of them together gives the community its understanding of what the difficulty of its life is for.

**What the Verse Establishes for the Community**

The sentence establishes three things at once: that hardship is expected, that it covers the whole of a person's circumstances, and that the response which secures the promise is endurance. The reader is given no guarantee that his life will be easy, and he is given a promise attached to a specific behaviour. The verses that follow describe the words the patient will say and the reward they will receive, and the passage reads as a single instruction for the community's hardest days.
