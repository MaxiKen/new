## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:155

> We will certainly test you with a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. Give good news to those who patiently endure—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Test Announced in Advance**

The verse states its certainty before its content: *We will certainly test you*. The testing is not a possibility to be feared but a promise to be expected, declared with the emphatic constructions Arabic reserves for oaths. This announcement changes the character of every hardship that follows it. An unannounced affliction arrives as an accident and is read as evidence of abandonment; an announced one arrives on schedule and is read as what it was called. The community receiving this verse had just been told that God is with the patient (2:153) and that its slain are alive (2:154); it is now told what patience will be needed for. The sequence is pedagogy: equipment, consolation, then the syllabus. No believer can claim the test caught the covenant by surprise — the syllabus was published in the same revelation that demanded the endurance.

**A Touch, Not a Flood**

The first qualification of the test is quantitative: *with a touch of* — something of, a portion of, fear and famine. The Arabic construction denotes the partial and the measured. Whatever the affliction, it is a sample, not the species in full; the fear is an installment of the fear that exists, the hunger a fraction of the hunger possible. The commentators read this measurement as mercy embedded in the grammar: the test is calibrated to be survivable, because a test that destroyed the tested would test nothing. It also calibrates the response — a measured affliction asks for measured endurance, and the verse's later promise that patience is rewarded infinitely against a finite trial is the arithmetic of grace. The fullness of fear and famine belongs to the Fire and to the punished nations; the community's portion is the touch by which hearts are shown to themselves.

**The Five Losses**

The catalogue runs through the things human beings hold: fear, hunger, wealth, lives, crops. Ibn Kathīr's reading of "lives" is loved ones — friends, relatives, the deaths that come to the survivor as losses of self; and "crops" or fruits extends beyond harvests to children, who are called in the Arabic idiom the fruit of the heart. So the fivefold test covers the body's security (fear), the body's food (famine), the hand's possessions (wealth), the heart's attachments (lives), and the field's yield (crops) — every layer at which a person is anchored to the world. No one is tested in all five at once as a rule; the verse distributes the catalogue across the community and across lifetimes, so that each person meets some items and all persons meet some. The comprehensiveness is the point: there is no region of loss outside the announced syllabus, and therefore no grief that arrives outside the covenant's coverage.

**The Obligations Hidden in the Losses**

One reading reported by the commentators converts the catalogue from calamities into commandments: the fear is the fear of God, the hunger is the fast of Ramadan, the loss of wealth is the alms-tax, the loss of souls is sickness borne, the loss of fruits is the death of children. On this reading the same verse announces what will be taken from the community by affliction and what will be asked of it by law — and the two columns are deliberately parallel, because the fast is a hunger undertaken, the alms a loss undertaken, and undertaken loss trains the heart for inflicted loss. The reading does not compete with the calamity reading; it deepens it. A community that has practiced voluntary hunger and voluntary giving has rehearsed the involuntary kinds, and when the real famine comes it meets hearts that have already learned the gesture of release.

**Hunger and Fear in the Qur'an's Ledger**

The pair of fear and hunger has a history in the Book. The Quraysh of Mecca were defined by their exemption from it — the Lord of this House *"Who has fed them against hunger and made them secure against fear"* (106:4) — security and fullness named as the two standing blessings of settled life. The ungrateful town received the pair as punishment: a society safe and provisioned from every direction, whose people met the favours with ingratitude, so *"Allah made them taste the clutches of hunger and fear for their misdeeds"* (16:112). And in the cause of God the pair is converted into merit: thirst, fatigue, and hunger suffered in that cause *"is written to their credit as a good deed"* (9:120). The same two experiences thus appear as blessing when given, as punishment when given to the ungrateful, and as reward-bearing trial when borne for God — and this verse assigns the community the third category in advance. What the town of 16:112 lost by ingratitude, the tested believers gain by patience; the difference is not in the event but in the bearing.

**Patience at the First Blow**

The verse's closing command — *give good news to those who patiently endure* — was given its sharpest definition in a report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (1283). Anas ibn Mālik narrated: the Prophet passed a woman weeping beside a grave and told her to fear God and be patient. She answered, go away — you have not been afflicted with my calamity — not knowing who he was. When she learned, she came to his door, found no guard, and said: I did not recognize you. And he answered: *patience is at the first blow*. The definition locates patience precisely where grief is strongest — in the heat of the stroke, not in the calm afterward, when, as the commentators observe, anyone can be patient. The glad tidings of this verse are therefore promised to the first minutes and hours: the instant of news, the funeral's edge, the famine's onset. What comes later is endurance's aftermath; what is rewarded is the standing-fast while the blow is still landing.

**Job's Complaint and the Standard of Patience**

That patience is not silence is settled by Job. When affliction reached its limit he called to his Lord: *"Satan has afflicted me with distress and suffering"* (38:41) — a complaint, spoken to God, about his state. And God's verdict on the same man: *"We truly found him patient. What an excellent servant ˹he was˺!"* (38:44). Al-Qurṭubī drew the jurisprudential conclusion from the pair: patience is not the absence of complaint but the direction of the heart — the complaint carried to God is inside patience, because it treats Him as the only relevant audience; the complaint carried against God, to the world, is what patience excludes. This rescues the verse's command from stoicism. The tested believer is not required to feel nothing or say nothing; he is required to keep the account open with the One who announced the test — to grieve in His direction, which is why the very next verse will give grief its exact words.
