## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:155

> We will certainly test you with a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. Give good news to those who patiently endure—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Form of the Announcement**

The verse opens with an oath-heavy future: *la-nabluwannakum*. The doubled construction makes the test a certainty rather than a possibility, and it follows immediately after the command to seek help through patience and prayer (2:153). The community is told in advance what the next years will contain, which is itself a mercy — a person who expects a storm does not read the first dark cloud as the end of the world.

The commentators dwell on the indefinite *bi-shayʾin* — "with *something* of fear" — and their reading is that the trial is named in the diminutive on purpose. Ibn Kathīr and al-Saʿdī both observe that the sentence does not say "with all fear" or "with all hunger"; the trial is a portion and not the whole, and its measure is set by the One who administers it. The same principle appears in the sūrah's later verse: God does not require of a soul more than it can bear (2:286).

**The Trials Named**

Fear of the enemy is placed first because it was the ordinary weather of the community's early years: the city was watched, the tribes were hostile, and the Muslims had left their property behind in Makkah. Hunger follows — the sūrah elsewhere records the siege of the trench and the hunger of the lean years. Then come the losses that are counted in the market rather than in the field: property, then lives, then crops.

The order has been read as a sequence of increasing cost: money can be replaced, a life cannot, and the failure of a harvest threatens the survival of the household. The list omits the things a community would rather have tested by, since victory, wealth, and health are not the subjects of the sentence. What it names is what the early believers were about to meet in fact.

**Glad Tidings Before the Response**

The instruction attached to the list is unexpected: *wa-bashshir al-ṣābirīn* — "give glad tidings to the patient". The good news is announced before the reader is shown who the patient are, and it is announced to the community that will have to deliver it to its own bereaved. The Book makes the announcement in the middle of the calamity rather than after it.

Al-Rāzī observes that the sentence gives the news to the patient rather than merely reporting the reward, which implies that the state described is entered while the trial is still running. The verse that follows defines them by what they say when the disaster lands — a definition that turns on speech, as the previous verse's prohibition turned on speech.

**The Discipline of Patience**

*Ṣabr* is not resignation in the Qur'an; the previous verse pairs it with prayer as a means of seeking help, which makes it a practice rather than a mood. The community's scholars broke it into kinds: patience in performing what is commanded, patience in refraining from what is forbidden, and patience under what cannot be avoided. The third is the one this verse attaches its promise to.

The pairing with verse 153 also sets the boundary between patience and despair. The community is not told to feel nothing; the following verses show the believers speaking — declaring that they belong to God and that they are returning to Him. The Book's model of endurance includes speech, and its words are given to the community.

**The Reports on Testing**

The clearest expansion of the verse in the reports is well known: no fatigue, no illness, no sorrow, no sadness, no harm, and no distress afflicts a Muslim — not even the prick of a thorn — but that some of his faults are removed by it (al-Bukhārī and Muslim). The community's jurists read it with this verse.

A second report, recorded by al-Tirmidhī as ḥadīth 2398, states that a person continues to be tested until he walks upon the earth with no sin left against him. The two reports give the two halves of the doctrine: the trial removes what has accrued, and the removal continues as long as the person holds on. The community's preachers used the pair when they addressed those whose circumstances had collapsed.

The community's teachers drew the measure of the trial from the indefinite noun of the first clause: the test is a portion, and the reports describe the Prophet ﷺ restoring a person's confidence when the weight of it seemed beyond him. The Book states the same rule in the context of struggle, that the community will be tested until God makes evident who has striven and who has held firm (47:31), so that the announcement of verse 155 is not a threat but a description of the road ahead.

**The Fruit of the Trial**

The reports present the trial under two aspects that the community's teachers kept distinct. The first is expiation, since the ḥadīth states that no fatigue, illness, sorrow, sadness, harm, or distress afflicts a Muslim, not even the prick of a thorn, without some of his faults being removed (al-Bukhārī and Muslim). The second is elevation, since al-Tirmidhī records (ḥadīth 2398) that a servant continues to be tested until he walks upon the earth with no sin left to his name, and the Qur'an names the martyrs and the truthful among those whom God has favoured (4:69).

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse establishes that testing is certain, that its measure is a portion rather than a whole, that the standard response is patience, and that the glad tidings belong to the patient before the trial has finished. The passage that follows puts the words of that response in the community's mouth, so that the reader moves from the announcement of the test to the sentence a believer says when it arrives.
