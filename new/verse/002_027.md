## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:27

> those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land. It is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Portrait Attached to the Previous Sentence**

This verse supplies the description that the previous one promised. The rebellious were named there as the people left to stray; here the Book says what they do, and it lists three activities in ascending reach: a covenant broken, a set of ties cut, and damage done across a whole land. The order runs from a private agreement, through relationships between people, to the condition of the community they live in.

That sequence is the sūrah's ordinary way of connecting a person to a place. The Book rarely treats wrongdoing as an isolated transaction between an individual and God; it follows the damage outward until it reaches the ground other people stand on. The three clauses are therefore one charge with three faces, and the sentence ends by naming who pays for it.

**A Covenant Broken After It Was Affirmed**

The Arabic verb describes the pulling down of something that has been bound. What is being violated is a covenant that had been confirmed, which rules out the defence of ignorance and rules out the excuse of an agreement never properly made. The Book uses the same imagery of a bond in the passage that records the original acknowledgement of God's lordship: *“And ˹remember˺ when your Lord brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their descendants and had them testify regarding themselves. ˹Allah asked,˺ ‘Am I not your Lord?’ They replied, ‘Yes, You are! We testify.’”* (7:172).

Commentators applied the phrase to three different covenants, and each has grounds. One reading takes it as the general obligation to acknowledge God and worship Him, witnessed in the primordial testimony of every human being. A second takes it as the solemn oaths the Arabs swore before the Prophet's coming that they would follow a warner if one came. A third takes it as the specific covenant made with the People of the Book. The three are not rivals so much as three settings of the same failure, and the verse's language covers all of them.

**Breaking What God Ordered to Be Joined**

The second clause is the one that turns the verse toward social life. The Book does not specify here what the ties are, and the commentators filled in the answer from elsewhere. Family relationships are the most frequent candidate, and the Qur'an commands them in the earliest address to humanity: *“O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties.”* (4:1). The duty of relatives toward one another is stated elsewhere as a matter of divine ordinance, and the Book warns the very audience under discussion here that turning away would mean severing kinship ties (47:22).

A wider reading takes the clause to cover any bond God has commanded be kept, whether of blood, of neighbourhood, of treaty or of faith. The generality is probably intended, and the strength of the charge comes from that. What is being severed is not one particular connection but the network of obligations that holds a community together, and the Book treats their maintenance as something God has ordered rather than merely recommended.

**Corruption in the Land as the Third Clause**

The third activity repeats a charge the sūrah has already brought against the hypocrites earlier in the same chapter, where they claim to be peacemakers while the Book calls them corruptors. What makes the repetition significant is the wider claim it carries in the Qur'an: *“Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done, so that Allah may cause them to taste ˹the consequences of˺ some of their deeds and perhaps they might return to the ˹Right Path˺.”* (30:41).

The Book's conception of corruption is not confined to moral or political breakdown. It describes damage to the land itself as the fruit of human conduct, and it treats the damage as a form of instruction, sent so that people might turn back. In this verse the corruption is the visible result of the two broken commitments that precede it: a community whose covenants are dissolved and whose ties are cut will produce exactly this kind of wreckage, and the wreckage is described as an activity performed rather than a misfortune suffered.

**The Losers, in the Book's Ledger**

The verdict is brief: it is they who are the losers. The Book uses the same word at the closing of the parable of the man who traded guidance for misguidance, where the trade is said to have produced no profit, and in the promise attached to those who hold to the covenant rather than break it. The word describes a person who has given away something valuable and received nothing in return, and the sūrah has been teaching its readers to keep accounts in exactly those terms.

The Book's usage elsewhere supplies the other side of the ledger, so that the verdict is not a slogan: *“Surely humanity is in ˹grave˺ loss, / except those who have faith, do good, and urge each other to the truth, and urge each other to perseverance.”* (103:2–3). Loss, on this understanding, is the default condition of a life, and what rescues a person from it is a set of practices that are the opposite of what this verse describes. The people in this verse do not merely err; they dismantle the arrangements by which others are held together, and the sentence states the result without further comment.
