## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:27

> those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land. It is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rebellious, Defined in Three Breaches**

The open sentence of verse 26 closes here, and it closes with a definition: the *fāsiqūn* are those who do three things — break a covenant, sever what Allah ordered joined, and corrupt the land. The definition is entirely behavioral. No creed is inspected, no interior is probed; the rebellious are identified by breaches, and the three breaches share a single shape. Each is the cutting of a connection that ought to hold: the vertical bond with Allah, the horizontal bonds among creatures, and the condition of the ground on which both stand. Sin, in this verse's grammar, is not primarily violation of a rule but severance of a relation — and the sequence is causal, running from the highest bond downward. The definition also universalizes the passage: whatever its occasion among the hypocrites and scoffers of Madina, the three breaches are available to every community and most of them are committed daily somewhere by someone. The Qur'an defines its worst human type not by a name but by a list of acts anyone can check against a life.

**Which Covenant? Three Candidates and a Preference**

The exegetes identified the broken covenant variously, and the range is instructive. The widest reading takes it as the primordial covenant of human nature — the scene in al-Aʿrāf where Allah drew the descendants of Adam from his loins and asked *"Am I not your Lord?"* and they answered, *"Yes, You are! We testify"* (7:172) — a pact every person signs before birth and can breach after. A second reading, favored by al-Rāzī, takes it as the sworn expectation of the pre-Islamic Arabs: *"They swore by Allah their most solemn oaths that if a warner were to come to them, they would certainly be better guided than any other community"* (35:42) — an oath broken the moment the warner arrived. A third confines it to the specific covenants made with the People of the Book. Weighed together, the readings are not rivals but concentric rings of the same offense: every human being holds the first covenant, the Arabs of the revelation's audience added the second, and the scriptural communities the third. "After it has been affirmed" — *baʿda mīthāqih* — fits all three: in each case the bond was established, witnessed, and confirmed before the breaking. The verse prosecutes no one for ignorance; it prosecutes the confirmed and betrayed.

**Severing What Was Ordered Joined**

The second breach — *yaqtaʿūna mā amara Llāhu bihī an yūṣala* — severs what Allah commanded to be connected, and the commentators' first application is kinship: the ties of blood whose maintenance the Qur'an legislates from its opening pages — *"Be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties"* (4:1) — and whose severance it pairs, exactly as here, with corruption in the land: *"Now if you ˹hypocrites˺ turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship!"* (47:22). The pairing in both sūrahs is not accidental but analytic: kinship is the first society, the training ground of every bond, and a person who can cut the ties he was born into has rehearsed the cutting of all the rest. Other exegetes widen the referent to everything Allah ordered joined — faith and practice, the prophets as one brotherhood, the community and its teachers, the People of the Book and the confirmation of their own scriptures in this Qur'an. One classical note adds a barb for Madina's Jews: the tie to this Prophet was itself kinship, Isaac and Ishmael being brothers. Every reading keeps the same structure: a commanded connection, deliberately cut.

**Corruption in the Land — the Passage's Third Return**

The third breach is *yufsidūna fī al-ard*, and the phrase is by now a signature of this sūrah's opening: the charge the hypocrites deflected while claiming to be peace-makers (2:11), the verdict that stripped the claim from them (2:12), and now the defining trait of the *fāsiq*. The recurrence completes an identification — the peace-making of verse 11 and the covenant-breaking of verse 27 are the same phenomenon at two depths, and the land's corruption is what both produce. Read with the two breaches before it, the corruption has a precise etiology: when the bond with Allah is cut and the bonds between people are cut, the ground itself inherits the severance, since the land in Quranic thought is not a neutral stage but a participant that registers the moral state of its inhabitants — corruption spreading on land and sea by what human hands have done (30:41). The three breaches form one ecosystem of damage: vertical, horizontal, environmental. A generation that keeps covenants and kinships farms a different earth than one that does not, and the verse states the farming as fact rather than metaphor.

**Al-Khāsirūn: The Ledger Closed at a Loss**

The verdict uses the market vocabulary the passage has been accumulating: *ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūn* — it is they who are the losers, *khāsir* being the merchant who returns from a venture with less than he carried out, bereft, or in the word's other shade, simply *lost* — having mislaid the road or the self. The restriction grammar — *ulāʾika hum* — repeats the sūrah's verdict formula, and the repetition lets the opening's ledgers be compared side by side: the God-conscious who are the successful (2:5), the traders who bought error with guidance and profited nothing (2:16), and now the covenant-breakers who are the losers. The three entries share one economy. Loss here is not deprivation inflicted from outside but the arithmetic of the breaches themselves — a covenant broken spends a bond, kinship cut spends a society, corruption spread spends the land, and the spender arrives at the audit holding nothing, having consumed every asset that was not his to consume. The verse thus needs no added penalty clause; the loser's inventory is the penalty, read aloud.

**The Inverse Portrait of the Opening Five**

Set against the sūrah's first portrait, this verse reveals its construction: it is the God-conscious described in reverse. Where those believed in the unseen, these break faith with a covenant affirmed. Where those maintained prayer — a commanded connection kept on schedule — these sever the connections commanded. Where those spent from what they were provided, enriching the land's circulation, these corrupt the land. Where those were sure of the Hereafter and thereby joined themselves to the largest bond of all, these live as though the vertical bond were optional. The two portraits are one map read in opposite directions, and that is the verse's pastoral instrument: every quality of the *muttaqīn* has its exact negation here, so the reader can locate himself not by feeling but by checking the bonds — which connections he keeps and which he cuts. The losers are not a people apart; they are the same diagram walked backwards, and the direction is chosen bond by bond, daily, by everyone the verse is recited over.
