## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:74

> Joseph’s men asked, “What should be the price for theft, if you are lying?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question About Law, Not About Fact**

The officials do not ask whether the brothers stole the cup. That question has already been answered by the oath in verse 73, and pressing it would produce only a repetition. What they ask is what the penalty should be if the denial is false. The shift from fact to law is the whole of the verse's significance. A search establishes whether an object is present; a rule establishes what happens to the person in whose possession it is found. Joseph's men are constructing the second half before the first has been conducted, which tells the reader that the outcome of the search is not in doubt for the people conducting it.

**A Condition That Is Careful**

*If you are liars* — the question is conditional and the condition leaves the denial formally intact. Nobody in the exchange has accused the brothers of lying to their faces; the officials ask what should follow if the oath turns out to have been false. The formulation is polite, legal and entirely trap-like. It allows the brothers to answer without conceding guilt, and it commits them to a rule that will be applied whether or not they intended the commitment. The chapter's interest in legal procedure shows here: the wording of a question determines what an answer can mean, and this question is drafted so that any answer binds the answerers. The care lies in what the question does not assume. It presupposes no guilt, names no suspect and proposes no penalty; it asks only what the thief's recompense should be, in terms the answerers accept. Any reply therefore comes from them as a voluntary statement of law, and a voluntary statement of law cannot afterwards be disowned by the people who made it.

**Why the Accused Are Asked for the Penalty**

The unusual feature of the verse is who is being consulted. In most jurisdictions the penalty for theft is fixed by law and announced by the authority. Here the authority asks the accused to state it. The commentators explain the reason in verse 76: under the king's law Joseph could not have retained his brother. The Egyptian rule, on one account, required the thief to be beaten and to pay double the value of what was stolen, after which he went free. If that rule had been applied, Benjamin would have returned to Canaan with his brothers. The penalty had to come from somewhere else, and the only available somewhere else was the accused party's own custom. Asking them for it is not a courtesy; it is the only route to a lawful detention, and the question is drafted so that the answer cannot be avoided.

**The Brothers Become the Source of the Rule**

Verse 75 shows the device working. The brothers state their family's law: the one in whose bag the object is found is himself the recompense, handed over to the person he stole from. That is the custom of Jacob's household, not of Egypt, and it produces exactly the result Joseph needs — the detention of Benjamin. The chapter therefore has the accused supply the sanction that will be applied to one of them, in a jurisdiction where the local sanction would not have produced that result. The verse is the hinge of the mechanism, and its brevity conceals how much of the plot turns on it. Their authorship removes every ground of complaint. Had the penalty been announced by an Egyptian official, the brothers could have objected to the jurisdiction, appealed to the king or refused the search. Because it comes from them, in their own terms, as the law of their father's house, objection would mean contradicting themselves before the men they have just sworn an oath in front of.

**The Question Is Neutral in Tone**

Nothing in the wording conveys hostility, suspicion or triumph. It is a procedural enquiry put by officials to travellers, and the travellers answer it in the next verse without protest or hesitation. The neutrality is consistent with Joseph's conduct throughout this section: he does not accuse, does not insult and does not gloat. He asks a question whose answer he needs and whose answerers do not know what it is for. The sūrah's presentation of the device is therefore clinical rather than dramatic, and the drama is supplied by the reader's knowledge of what is in the bag. The neutrality is what supplies the tension. A shouted accusation would have let the brothers react with outrage; a procedural enquiry allows them only to answer. They are being cross-examined by a man who already knows the response, and the form of the question stops them noticing that they are the ones under examination rather than the ones giving evidence.

**The Chapter's Interest in Legal Mechanism**

Verses 70 to 76 form a compact study in how a legal outcome can be produced without breaking a law. A cup is placed, an accusation is announced publicly, a reward is offered, a denial is sworn, a penalty is requested from the accused, the penalty is stated in the accused's own terms, and the search is conducted in an order that produces the object last. Every step is procedurally ordinary. The chapter reports the whole sequence and then says in verse 76 that God inspired the plan and that without it the objective could not have been reached under the king's law. The device is presented as revelation working through jurisprudence rather than around it.

**The Reader Knows Where the Question Leads**

By verse 74 the reader has been told three things the brothers have not: that the cup is in Benjamin's bag (12:70), that Joseph is Benjamin's brother (12:69), and that Joseph wants his brother to remain. The question about the penalty therefore arrives with its answer already visible. The tension in the verse is not what will happen but how these particular men will bring it about, and the answer is that they will do it themselves, in their own words, under oath, in a jurisdiction that would otherwise have released the accused. The chapter is showing a plan executed through the free speech of the people it affects. That is the chapter's most striking claim about providence. Nothing here is coerced; the brothers are not threatened into stating the law, not misled about its content, and not prevented from saying anything else. They speak freely, accurately and at length, and what they freely say is the instrument that detains their brother. The plan needs their words rather than their silence.

**Smallest Verse in the Sequence, Most Consequential**

Four verses carry the exchange: a question, a reward, a denial, a penalty question. The fourth is the shortest and does the most work. Without it the search would establish a fact and leave the consequence to Egyptian law, which would have released Benjamin. With it the consequence is fixed by the brothers' own statement before the search begins, so that when the cup is produced there is no room to negotiate the outcome. The sūrah gives this verse its own line in the sequence rather than folding the question into verse 73 or verse 75, and the isolation is a marker of its function. The isolation also controls the pacing of the disclosure. Because the question stands alone, the reader is held between the accusation and the rule that will settle it, and in that pause the brothers are free to answer without suspicion. A longer verse would have signalled importance; a short one lets the importance arrive unannounced, which is exactly what the plan requires.
