## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:11

> **And ˹He is the One˺ Who sends down rain from the sky in perfect measure, with which We give life to a lifeless land. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rain by Measure, Not by Flood**

The rain is sent bi-qadar, by measure, and the phrase is the centre of the verse's first half. Qadar is a fixed amount, a proportion decided in advance. Ibn Kathīr explains the measure as sufficiency: God sends enough water for people, their animals, and their crops, neither too little to sustain them nor too much to drown them. Al-Jalālayn note that He does not ordinarily send a deluge, and al-Rāzī names the exception, the flood of Nūḥ, to show that the measure is a mercy and not a limit on God's power. The phrase also has a wider theological reach. The Qurʾān says in 25:2 that God created everything and then measured it with a measure, and in 54:49 that He created everything by measure. Rain is the everyday instance of a principle that governs all creation. The Arabs of the Ḥijāz lived on the edge of water. A season of rain meant pasture and a season without it meant hunger, and they knew better than most that water's usefulness depends entirely on its quantity. The verse tells them that the quantity is decided, and asks them to consider by whom.

**The Grammatical Shift from He to We**

The verse begins in the third person, "the One Who sends down", and shifts mid-sentence to the first person plural, "We give life". The device is called iltifāt, a turning, and Arabic rhetoric prizes it. Its effect here is to bring God from the distance of description into the closeness of direct speech. The list of works that began in verse 10 was describing God as the Quraysh admitted Him: the One Who made this and that. At the moment when the dead land is raised, the voice changes and God speaks as Himself. The change lands on the act that is the verse's real subject. Laying out the earth and sending rain are works that the Quraysh could attribute to God without discomfort. Giving life to what was dead is the act they denied Him in the case of human beings, and so it is the act on which God speaks in His own voice. The shift also prepares the final clause, which is addressed to "you", the audience, directly. The verse moves from He to We to you in one breath, and the movement carries the argument from cosmology to confrontation.

**Balda Mayta: The Land That Was Dead**

The rain revives a balda mayta, a dead land, and the word for dead is the same word used for a dead body. The Qurʾān does not say the land was dry or barren. It says it was dead, and it says the rain gave it life, using the verb anshara, which means to spread out and, by extension, to raise the dead, since the day of resurrection is yawm al-nushūr. The choice of vocabulary is deliberate and the exegetes all notice it. The land's revival is being described in the language of resurrection so that the comparison in the final clause will already be half-made. Anyone who has seen the desert after rain knows the phenomenon: ground that showed nothing for months puts out grass in days. The Arabs saw it every year and called the greening after rain ḥayāt, life. The verse takes their own word and their own annual experience and tells them that what they call life in the land is a small model of what they deny for themselves.

**"And So Will You Be Brought Forth"**

The final clause, kadhālika tukhrajūn, thus shall you be brought out, is the argument the whole verse was building. The particle kadhālika, "thus, in that same way", makes the revival of the land the pattern for the raising of the dead. Al-Rāzī reads the verse as one of the Qurʾān's many uses of this analogy, and the parallels are numerous: 7:57, 16:65, 29:63, 30:19, 30:24, 30:50, 35:9, 36:33, 41:39, 45:5, 50:9–11, and 57:17. The Qurʾān returns to the comparison so often because it answers the Quraysh's objection in their own terms. They said, as 36:78 records, who will give life to bones when they have rotted? The answer is: the One who gives life to soil when it has died, which you watch happen every spring. The analogy is not a proof in the strict sense, since a sceptic could say that seeds and bones are different. But it is a demonstration that the category of bringing life from death is not empty, and that the God who does it in one case has both the power and the habit. The objection to resurrection was never that it was logically impossible; it was that it seemed strange. The verse points at the greening desert and asks what exactly is strange.

**The Soul's Spring as Well as the Body's**

The exegetical tradition reads the dead land in a second way, and it is not a departure from the first. The revival of the earth by rain is a picture of the revival of the heart by revelation, and the Qurʾān invites this reading in verses like 57:17, where the giving of life to the dead earth is placed immediately after a call to the believers to let their hearts soften. Rain comes from the sky and lands on ground that cannot make itself green. Revelation comes from the same direction and lands on hearts that cannot make themselves alive. The commentators on 7:58, 22:63, and 30:50 develop the parallel at length, and it belongs here too. The Quraysh were the dead land. The Qurʾān was the measured rain. Whether they would put out grass was not in the rain's power to decide, since the good land responds and the bad land does not (7:58). But the rain had come, in measure, and the land had no excuse of drought. The next verse continues the list with the creation of pairs and the means of travel.
