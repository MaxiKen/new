## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:148

> Everyone turns to their own direction ˹of prayer˺. So compete with one another in doing good. Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together ˹for judgment˺. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement About Directions**

The verse states that every community has a direction to which it turns, and the sentence acknowledges what the preceding passage has been describing. The disagreement about the direction of prayer is not a temporary confusion that could be resolved by discovering the single correct orientation for everyone; the communities have their own orientations, and the Book states the fact without disputing it.

The commentators read the sentence as a statement about the religious communities generally, and some noted that the grammar also allows the reading that God is the one who directs each community to its direction. Under either reading the verse places the practice of facing a direction within the framework of divine appointment rather than of human preference.

**The Readings and Their Significance**

The alternative reading, in which God is the subject who directs each community, was preferred by some commentators because it accords with the sūrah's insistence that the direction of prayer is a command rather than a communal choice. On that reading the sentence says that God has appointed for each people the direction in which it turns, and that the believers' direction is one of those appointments.

The two readings converge on the same practical conclusion. Whether the sentence is understood as a description of what communities do or of what God has decreed for them, the dispute about directions is not the arena in which the communities will be separated, and the verse immediately turns the reader to a different criterion.

The difference among the earliest readers over the subject of the verb shows how closely the sentence was studied. If the people are the subject, the verse describes a situation that God has permitted; if God is the subject, it describes a matter He has appointed. Neither reading allows the community to treat its direction as a possession it won, and both leave the judgment of the other communities to the One who will gather them.

**Competition in Good Deeds**

The command that follows redirects the community's energy. Instead of competing over directions, the believers are told to compete in good deeds, and the sūrah uses the form of the verb that implies rivalry in a race. The image treats good conduct as the field in which a community should be trying to outdo others.

The Book returns to this command in several places, and it uses the same language when it describes the righteous among the People of the Book racing with one another in good, and when it describes the family of Zachariah and the prophets calling on God with hope and fear. The instruction in this verse therefore belongs to the Book's vocabulary for the life of the faithful, and it is applied here to a situation of dispute.

**The Commentators on the Prayer Times**

Several commentators connected the command to compete in good deeds specifically with the timely performance of the canonical prayers, since the verse's context is the direction of prayer and the times at which it is offered. Their reading gives the command a practical edge: the community's rivalry should be in performing the prayer at its proper time rather than in arguing about the bearing of the building it faces.

The application extends beyond the immediate context. The Book's teaching is that a community's energy is best spent on the acts it can perform, and the passage supplies the acts that belong to the present moment while leaving the disputes about the past to be settled elsewhere.

**"Allah Will Bring You All Together"**

The sentence then states that God will gather all people wherever they are, and the phrase reminds the reader of the destination toward which the whole passage has been moving. The sūrah has described a Day on which no soul helps another and no intercession is accepted, and the gathering announced here is the moment at which the disputes of communities are resolved.

The Book pairs the gathering with the competition in good as a comfort and a warning at once. Communities that take different directions will be brought to one place, and the criterion applied there will not be the orientation of the worshipper but the deeds he has done. The sentence therefore gives the reader the horizon within which the present controversy is to be understood.

**"Most Capable of Everything"**

The closing attribute states that God is able to do everything, and the sūrah has used the same ending in the earlier discussion of the replacement of rulings. The attribute answers the practical question the reader may have about the gathering, since the assembling of all generations in one place is not a matter any creature could arrange.

The repetition of the attribute in comparable contexts gives the sūrah's argument a steadying quality. The matters under dispute in the passage concern what God has appointed and what He will bring about, and the sentence closes with a statement that nothing in either is beyond His power.

**The Verse as a Turning Point**

The sentence brings the section on the direction of prayer to its conclusion and shifts the reader's attention to conduct and to the final gathering. The commands to face the Sacred Mosque are repeated in the next verses, and the reminder of the gathering remains in the reader's mind as those commands are received.

The sentence changes the subject of the passage without abandoning it. The dispute about directions is acknowledged and then set aside, and the community is directed to the conduct that will be weighed on the day when all people are gathered. The verses that follow repeat the command to face the Sacred Mosque, and the reader receives that repetition after having been told what the rivalry between communities is worth.

**How the Verse Should Be Read**

The verse does not dismiss the direction of prayer, since the sūrah commands it repeatedly and gives the command as one of God's appointments. What it does is rank the community's concerns. A community that has been given a direction follows it, and it does not make the direction the subject of a rivalry with others; its rivalry belongs to the good it performs, and the settlement of everything else is left to the day when all people will be gathered.
