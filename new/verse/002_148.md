## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:148

> Everyone turns to their own direction ˹of prayer˺. So compete with one another in doing good. Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together ˹for judgment˺. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Every Community Has Its Direction**

The verse opens by generalizing what the qiblah controversy had made particular: *everyone turns to their own direction* — every religious community has a qiblah, a facing it was given or took. The grammarians noted a second possible reading with a different theology inside it: everyone has a direction toward which *He* turns him — meaning that in the cases of the Kaʿbah and Jerusalem alike, it is God who assigns the facing, and the diversity of directions is a diversity of divine assignments rather than of human inventions. Al-Rāzī preserved that reading, and it changes the register of the verse from description to doctrine: the plural of qiblahs across history is not evidence that direction is arbitrary, but evidence that the One who owns east and west has oriented each community in its turn. Under either reading the verse removes the scandal the fools found in the change of direction — a community turning to its assigned facing is the normal condition of religion, and the only real questions are who assigned it and what the community does while facing it.

**From Disputation to Competition**

The pivot of the verse is the imperative that follows: *so compete with one another in doing good*. The word — *fastabiqū* — is from the root of racing, of striving to be first to a finish line; it pictures the communities not as debaters but as runners. The logic connecting the two clauses is the verse's whole argument: since each community faces its own direction and the directions are not negotiable (the previous verses established the impossibility of a shared qiblah), the energy that argument would waste on disputing direction is redirected into the only contest that remains meaningful — the contest of deeds. Some commentators tied the racing specifically to the prayers, since the qiblah is the prayer's orientation and promptness in the canonical prayers is the first good deed the direction governs. The tie is apt but the command is wider: every good deed is inside the race, and the race is the believers' answer to the sectarian deadlock — not a compromise of facing but an outperformance in conduct.

**The Racing Verses**

The command belongs to a family the Qur'an returns to whenever communities or persons are set side by side. The sūrah of the Table states the fullest version: each community has been given its law and way, God could have made humanity one community but willed to test them, *so compete with one another in doing good* — with the return to God appended, who will then inform them of what they differed about (5:48). Āl ʿImrān lists it among the marks of the righteous of the Scripture-people themselves: they *"race with one another in doing good"* (3:114). The sūrah of the Prophets describes the household of Zakariyyā in the same vocabulary: *"they used to race in doing good, and call upon Us with hope and fear"* (21:90). The believers of the sūrah of the Believers are *"those who race to do good deeds, always taking the lead"* (23:61), and the sūrah of the Originator grades the inheritors of the Book into the self-wronging, the middle, and *"those who are foremost in good deeds"* (35:32). The repetition across Meccan and Medinan sūrahs shows the race as the Qur'an's standing replacement for religious quarrel: differences of community are real, the judgment of them is deferred to God, and the interval is to be spent competing in virtue rather than in claims.

**Diversity Under One Test**

The verse's anthropology of plurality deserves precision, because it is neither pluralism nor triumphalism. It does not say all directions are equally true — the previous passage fixed the believers' direction by revelation and named refusal of it as opposition. Nor does it command the eradication of difference — the Table's parallel states that God willed the diversity as a test (5:48). What the verse establishes is the frame within which difference is bearable: each community faces where it faces, the accounting of facings belongs to God, and the observable field of competition is good deeds, which any community can perform and every community is judged on. This is the practical settlement the qiblah section arrives at after exhausting the theological one. The believers are not instructed to stop caring that others face wrongly; they are instructed to run — because in the economy the verse describes, the only thing a community fully controls is its own deeds, and the only contest whose outcome it can affect is the contest of virtue.

**Scattered and Gathered**

The third clause restores the horizon the race runs toward: *wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together ˹for judgment˺*. The gathering answers the scattering twice over. Geographically: the community's directions of prayer had just been universalized — wherever you are, turn your faces toward the House (2:144) — and a community dispersed across the earth facing one point is held together in space only by obedience; the verse adds that the same God who holds it together across space will assemble it across time, the living and the dead whose prayers toward Jerusalem had worried their brothers (2:143). Theologically: the rival communities, which no argument will gather into one facing, are gathered anyway — at the judgment, where the differences the race could not resolve are informed upon (5:48) by the One who assigned the directions. The dispersion of the qiblahs is provisional; the assembly is certain.

**Capable of Everything**

The verse seals with the divine power: *surely Allah is Most Capable of everything*. The name answers the clause before it — the gathering of a scattered species from every coordinate and century into one judgment is precisely the kind of act whose possibility the closing clause guarantees. It also answers the race: a competition whose prizes are invisible and whose finish line is posthumous requires trust in the capacity of the One who pays, and the verse supplies that trust in its final words. The same capability clause closed the abrogation verse earlier in the sūrah — do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything (2:106) — which places the change of qiblah and the gathering of the racers under one attribute: the God who replaces a direction when He wills is the God who assembles all directions when He wills. Between the replacement and the assembly lies the interval the believers are given, and the verse has told them exactly what to do with it: face where you were turned, and run.
