## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:87

> Their reward is that they will be condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Reward That Is a Curse**

The verse states the compensation for the conduct described in the previous lines, and it uses the vocabulary of cursing. The condemned are cursed by God, by the angels, and by all of humanity, and the sentence names the three parties in ascending order of generality.

The Book's language of cursing describes being driven away and removed from mercy rather than the utterance of an insult. The same triple formula appears in the sūrah of the Cow, where the condemned condition of those who disbelieve and die in that state is stated in identical terms. The repetition across sūrahs is a sign that the Book treats the formula as a fixed description of the final condition of a person who has been cut off from mercy, rather than as an expression of anger that varies with circumstance.

**Why Three Parties Are Named**

Naming God, the angels, and humanity as the sources of the curse gives the sentence its scope. The condemnation is not the reaction of one community to another's offence; it comes from the order of things, and the three named parties correspond roughly to the hierarchy the sūrah laid out in its account of the witnesses to God's unity.

The Book uses the same three-tiered structure when it describes the curse attaching to the person who dies in rejection, and it uses a related one when it describes the truth being witnessed by God, the angels, and the people of knowledge. The verse above turns that structure around. What is being described is the whole of creation withdrawing its regard from a person whose conduct has placed him outside the mercy that everything else participates in, and the universality of the statement is the point of the list.

**The Curse in the Book's Wider Usage**

The Book reports curses in several contexts, and its usage clarifies what the term means. Communities that enter the Fire curse one another, and the followers among them blame their leaders; the people of Abraham's community are told that they will disown and curse one another on the Day of Judgment; and the Book describes the curse that rests upon those who conceal the revelations they have been given.

The element common to these passages is a relation that has been severed. Being cursed is being cut off, and the Book's descriptions of the severed relation always include the parties who would otherwise have been connected: allies who blame one another, communities that curse their own predecessors, and those who held a revelation and were cut off from its benefit. The verse above describes the fullest form of that severance, since the parties named on the other side of the cut are God, the angels, and the whole of humanity.

**The Universality of the Statement**

The phrase about all of humanity is the part of the sentence that requires care, since it would make no sense if it meant that every human being individually pronounces a malediction on the person described.

The Book uses this kind of totalizing phrase for the settled judgment of a community, and the commentators explained the verse in terms of what human beings will recognise on the Day of Judgment. The picture is of a verdict that everyone present concurs in, because at that point the truth about what happened is available to all and no interest distorts it. The Book's account of the final gathering repeatedly presents unanimity about facts where unanimity about conduct had been impossible, and this verse's description of universal cursing belongs to that picture.

**What the Verse Achieves in the Passage**

The passage began with a question about how a people who had believed, acknowledged the message, and seen the proofs could be guided after they turned away. This verse states the consequence, and the next verses state that it is permanent and unrelieved.

The Book's arrangement places the cursing before the description of the punishment, which is the reverse of chronology. What the reader receives first is the severance of every relation, and what he receives second is what that severance looks like in the Hereafter. The order keeps the reader's attention on what has been lost rather than on the mechanics of the penalty, and it matches the sūrah's habit of describing the inner reality of a condition before it describes its outward form.

**A Warning That Applies to Whoever Reads**

The formula is stated in absolute terms, and the Book states it in this absolute form at the close of a discussion about a community that possessed a revelation and left it. The reader who recites the verse is included in the process by which the judgment becomes known.

That is the function of such passages in a book that its own community recites. The verses do not describe a class of people foreign to the reader's world; they describe a conduct he is capable of, and the severity of the description is part of what makes the conduct unlikely. The sūrah does not end the passage here, and what follows it, after a statement of the permanence of the condition, is a provision for those who return.

**The Phrase 'A Reward' and the Irony It Carries**

The sentence calls the curse a recompense, using the ordinary word for the return a person receives for his work. The choice is ironic and precise: what is being described is not an arbitrary infliction but the settled outcome of a course of conduct, and the Book insists on describing it as earned.

The same word is used throughout the sūrah for the reward of the believers, and the parallel is deliberate. Both parties are described as receiving what their conduct produced, and the difference lies in what was done rather than in how the accounting was carried out. The reader who has followed the passage from the covenant to this point has been shown the whole sequence — the acceptance, the turning back, the removal of guidance, and now the compensation — and the final word chosen for the outcome is the one that closes the account rather than opening a new charge.
