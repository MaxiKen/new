## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:42

> **˹It is˺ they who have patiently endured, and in their Lord they put their trust.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Patience And Trust As Two Pillars**

Al-ladhīna ṣabarū wa-ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn — those who patiently endured and in their Lord they put trust. Ṣabarū from ṣ-b-r means to be patient, to endure, to hold firm. Yatawakkalūn from w-k-l means to put trust, rely, depend. The verse describes those who emigrated after being wronged in previous verse: they are characterized by patience and trust. Al-Ṭabarī explains that ṣabarū refers to patience in face of persecution, in emigration, in obedience, and in avoiding disobedience. Yatawakkalūn means they rely on Lord for provision, protection, and reward, not on own strength or on people. The two qualities are paired throughout Qur'an as keys to success: patience and trust, as in 29:59: *Those who are patient and upon their Lord rely* (29:59). The order is significant: patience first, then trust, because patience is needed to endure hardship, and trust is needed to look forward to reward.

**Why Patience Is Mentioned**

Patience, ṣabr, in Qur'anic usage includes patience in obedience, patience against disobedience, and patience in face of hardship and persecution. For emigrants, all three were needed: patience to continue obeying Allah despite persecution, patience to avoid retaliating with wrongdoing, patience to endure loss of home and hardship of travel. Al-Rāzī notes that ṣabarū here is general, covering all types, because emigration after being wronged requires comprehensive patience. The verse does not say what they were patient about, leaving it open to include all. This generality makes it applicable beyond historical emigrants to anyone who endures for Allah. The reward for patience is good home in world and greater in Hereafter, as previous verse promised.

**Trust In Lord**

Wa-ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn — and upon their Lord they rely. ʿAlā indicates exclusivity: upon their Lord, not upon others. Rabb is Lord, Nurturer, Master who raises and sustains. Yatawakkalūn is present tense, indicating ongoing, habitual trust, not single act. Al-Qurṭubī explains that trust means to entrust affairs to Allah, to rely on Him for provision and protection, while still taking permissible means. For emigrants, trust meant leaving known homes and going to unknown land, trusting Allah to provide good home as promised. It also meant trusting that Hereafter reward is far better, even though unseen. Trust is therefore both practical and spiritual: practical reliance for worldly settlement, spiritual reliance for otherworldly reward.

**The Connection To Good Home Promise**

Verse 41 promised good home in world and greater reward in Hereafter for those who emigrated after being wronged, if only they knew. Verse 42 explains who they are: those who were patient and trust Lord. The connection is that good home is not given arbitrarily but to those with these qualities. Patience and trust are conditions for reward. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that ṣabarū wa-ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn are two qualities that make emigration successful: without patience, person would return to persecution or compromise; without trust, person would despair about future. Together they enable perseverance. The sūrah's theme of gratitude also appears: patience and trust are forms of gratitude that show recognition that blessings and tests are from Lord.

**A Verse That Applies Beyond Emigration**

Though verse describes emigrants, its description of patience and trust is general and applies to all believers. Al-Rāzī says any believer who is patient in face of hardship and trusts Lord will have good home in world, good life, and greater reward in Hereafter. The verse is therefore encouragement for anyone facing difficulty for faith: be patient and trust Lord. The promise of good home in world may be fulfilled as contentment, honor, provision, or actual settlement, depending on case, but Hereafter reward is certainly greater. The verse's brevity — two qualities in one sentence — makes it memorable and comprehensive.

**Linguistic Note On Ṣabr And Tawakkul**

Ṣabarū from ṣ-b-r means to restrain, to hold, to be patient, from same root as ṣabir, patient, and ṣabr, patience. In Arabic, ṣabr originally means to restrain self from complaint or despair. Yatawakkalūn from w-k-l means to rely, entrust, from same root as wakīl, trustee, agent. Tawakkul means to take Allah as wakīl, trustee, to rely on Him. ʿAlā rabbihim, upon their Lord, indicates that trust is directed exclusively to Lord. The verse's structure — those who were patient and upon Lord they rely — with wa- connecting two qualities, shows that both are needed together, not one alone. Patience without trust could be stoic endurance without hope; trust without patience could be wishful thinking without endurance. Together they form complete attitude of believer who endures hardship while relying on Lord for reward.

**How Patience And Trust Lead To Good Life**

The Qur'an links patience and trust to good life, as 16:97 says *Whoever does righteousness, male or female, while believing, We will surely give them good life* (16:97). Good life includes contentment, guidance, provision, and honor, all part of good home promised in verse 41. Patience and trust cultivate good life because they free heart from anxiety and resentment. Patient person does not despair over past wronging; trusting person does not worry about future provision. Together they allow heart to be present, grateful, and focused on obedience. The verse therefore not only describes emigrants but prescribes path to good life for all: be patient with what befalls, and trust Lord for what is to come.


**A Lesson That Connects To Gratitude And Reflection**

This verse, like all verses in Sūrat al-Niʿam, ultimately calls to gratitude and reflection. Whether it describes emigration, patience, trust, human messengers, clear proofs, earth swallowing, or angels fearing, the purpose is to make reader recognize blessings and respond with thanks and reflection. Al-Qurṭubī notes that every warning in this sūrah is also a blessing because it warns before punishment, giving chance to change. Every promise is also a blessing because it encourages good. The sūrah weaves warning and promise, blessing and accountability, to cultivate heart that is mindful, humble, grateful. The reader who reflects on this verse with that mindset will find both knowledge and motivation to be among those who say goodness when asked what Lord revealed, and who are greeted with peace at death and enter Gardens.

**How This Verse Fits The Sūrah's Argument**

The sūrah moves from blessings to argument against shirk to warnings to examples to guidance. This verse fits in that flow by showing either reward for patience and trust, or principle of human messengers, or modes of punishment, or universal submission. Each verse adds layer to argument that Allah alone creates, sustains, knows, and deserves worship. The verse's placement after previous warnings and before next commands is deliberate: it shows consequence of previous attitude and prepares for next command. The reader is invited to see connection and to respond with gratitude, patience, trust, and asking knowledgeable, rather than with denial, arrogance, and plotting.
