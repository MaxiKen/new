## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:220

> upon this world and the Hereafter. And they ask you concerning orphans. Say, “Improving their condition is best. And if you partner with them, they are bonded with you ˹in faith˺. And Allah knows who intends harm and who intends good. Had Allah willed, He could have made it difficult for you. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Crosses the Division**

The verse begins mid-sentence — *upon this world and the Hereafter* — completing the reflection-clause the previous verse left open: thus God makes the signs clear, that you may reflect upon the two worlds. The verse-division falls inside a single grammatical unit, and the effect is deliberate: the reflection commanded over wine and gambling is given its object here, the two horizons across which the weighing must run. The exegetes connect the objects explicitly — the theme of the Hereafter's superiority is joined here to the intoxicants and the wager: the mind is to weigh the passing satisfactions and enduring damages these things breed, judging both against the two horizons (Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī). An alternative parsing is recorded: the clarity God gives spans both worlds at once. Either reading yields the same instrument: the two-worlds scale, the faculty the weighing-verse trained and this verse names. Every ruling that follows — the orphan's property, the marriage's gate, the household's law — will be legislation for people holding that scale.

**The Question About Orphans**

The verse then takes up the third of the cluster's questions: they ask you about the orphans. The occasion the reports preserve explains the question's urgency. The early community had received the scripture's fiercest property-legislation — the devourers of orphans' wealth eating fire into their bellies (4:10), the command not to come near the orphan's property except to improve it (6:152) — and the believers had responded with scrupulosity: guardians kept the orphans' property utterly separate, eating and drinking from completely distinct sources so that nothing of the orphan's might be touched. Al-Ṭabarī records the cost of the scruple: the separation became so burdensome that custodians sometimes withdrew from their wards altogether — and in the aloofness the property itself suffered, spoiling or falling into ruin. Another reading of the occasion looks behind Islam to a pre-Islamic custom: the Arabs had held it inauspicious, an ill omen, to eat with orphans or ride their mounts — a superstition of separation the verse also answers. Either way the question was real: how does a guardian obey the inviolability without destroying the estate — or the relationship — it exists to protect?

**The Economics of the Orchard and the Herd**

The answer's wisdom is embedded in a material fact the commentators supply: an orphan's inheritance in that economy was typically orchards and livestock — fruit trees and animals whose *value is maintained only through use*. An orchard unpruned and unpicked declines; herds unridden, unmilked, and unmanaged lose condition and fail to breed. The inviolability of the orphan's property, enforced as total hands-off separation, would have consumed the very wealth it guarded — the pedantic scruple producing exactly the ruin the legislation existed to prevent. The verse's ruling — set matters aright for them — legislates the estate's flourishing as the guardian's objective: the property is not a museum-piece but a working trust, and its improvement is the standard against which the guardian's every decision is measured. This is why the answer begins not with permissions or prohibitions but with a direction: iṣlāḥ, the setting-right, the making-whole — the same root-family as peacemaking (ṣulḥ) and righteousness (ṣāliḥ), the vocabulary of repair against its opposite, the corruption (fasād) the hypocrite-portrait had just described (2:205). The guardian stands between the two: his office is iṣlāḥ of the estate and of the child's place in the household.

**Intermingle — They Are Your Brothers**

The verse's permission follows: share life with the orphans — they are your brothers. The intermingling is defined concretely by al-Ṭabarī — one cup and one table, common animals, common household service — the full texture of a shared life under one roof. And this clause carries a precision the exegetes marked: the brotherhood does not wait upon the sharing, as though kinship began with common meals; it stands as a clarification that intermingling justly with their property carries no blame — the brotherhood stands whether or not the households merge. The orphan is a brother first and a trust second, and the property's inviolability exists to serve the brotherhood, not to replace it with segregation. The ruling thereby corrects both failure-modes at once: the devouring the fire-verse condemned, and the aloofness that ruins estate and relationship alike. Between the two errors the verse installs the household's real solution — the orphan eaten with, ridden with, served with, worked alongside, his property used exactly as use improves it, under a guardianship whose standard is his flourishing and whose intimacy is his right.

**The Corrupter and the Reformer, Known**

The latitude the verse grants is immediately fenced by its surveillance-clause: God distinguishes the spoiler from the repairer. The intermingling-permission hands the guardian enormous discretion — shared meals, shared mounts, estates managed in common — and discretion is where intention hides. The clause names the two intentions the sharing can carry: the corrupter who uses the intermingling as cover for consumption, drawing the orphan's wealth into the common pot precisely in order to eat it; and the reformer whose every act of use is the estate's improvement. No human audit can reliably separate the two — the common meal looks identical from outside — and the verse stations the separation with God. The clause continues: God could have imposed the crushing alternative — the scrupulous impossibility of total separation could have been commanded, and the guardians crushed under it; the mercy of the latitude is named as a willed divine choice, not an oversight. The closing names fit the architecture: Almighty — nothing escapes the power that could have imposed the hardship; All-Wise — the latitude is the wisdom's form, calibrated to human capacity and divine knowledge together. The guardian operates under both: free in the household's daily judgments, audited in every one.

**The Orphan-Legislation in the Sūrah's Whole**

This verse belongs to the scripture's longest sustained legislation for a single vulnerable class. The sūrah opened its social rulings with the orphan's property and its devourers (4:10's fire, echoed at 2:174); the definition of righteousness placed orphans second in charity's roster (2:177), and the spending-answer repeated the rank (2:215); the sūrah of the Women will legislate the estate's management across a whole passage (4:2–10); the Cattle's decalogue commands not approaching the orphan's wealth except to improve it, until maturity (6:152). This verse supplies the system's operating principle — improvement over isolation, brotherhood over segregation, intention audited by God — and its placement inside the question-cluster ties the orphan's welfare to the community's whole moral formation: the same people being taught to weigh wine against its benefits and to spare from their wealth for the roster are taught here that the roster's most vulnerable member is a brother in the house. The verse ends where the household-legislation that follows will begin: with the Almighty and Wise presiding over the intimate details of shared bread — the names that make the daily judgment of the guardian a matter of theology, and the orphan's flourishing a matter of divine interest.
