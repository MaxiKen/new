## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:220

> upon this world and the Hereafter. And they ask you concerning orphans. Say, “Improving their condition is best. And if you partner with them, they are bonded with you ˹in faith˺. And Allah knows who intends harm and who intends good. Had Allah willed, He could have made it difficult for you. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Subject of Reflection**

The verse opens with a phrase that completes the previous sentence and names this world and the Hereafter as the subject of the community's reflection, and it then takes up the question about orphans. The connection between the two subjects is deliberate, since the judgment of the two practices in the previous verse depends on weighing present advantage against what follows.

The Book's method here is to supply the framework before the ruling, and the community's teachers read the two verses as a single instruction. Readers are shown that the assessment of wine and gambling belongs to the larger question of what is transitory and what endures, and the sentence then turns to the treatment of those who have no one to protect them.

**The Question About Orphans**

The verse reports a question about the orphans and answers that setting their affairs aright is best, and the community's scholars noted that the question concerned the degree of contact permitted with the property and the households of those in the community's care. The answer's first word concerns improvement rather than separation.

The instruction places the duty of the guardian in the improvement of the orphan's condition, and the community's teachers read the sentence as the standard by which the guardian's conduct is judged. Readers are shown that the Book's concern is the welfare of the child rather than the purity of the guardian's arrangements, and the answer establishes the purpose that governs the whole subject.

**The Reports About the Practice**

The commentators describe the scruples of the early community, whose members kept the property of orphans entirely separate and avoided eating and drinking with them, so that the arrangements became difficult and the children's own interests sometimes suffered. The reports show the situation the answer addressed.

Other reports describe the custom of the Arabs before the revelation, who regarded contact with an orphan's property as an ill omen and kept their distance from the children in their care. The community's teachers read the two reports together, and the sentence's answer corrects both the excessive caution and the customary avoidance.

**The Permission to Mix**

The sentence permits the guardians to associate with the orphans and describes them as brothers, and the community's scholars read the phrase as a statement about the relationship that the guardianship creates. The permission is accompanied by the warning that God knows whose intention is to cause harm.

The Book's treatment of the orphan's property elsewhere contains severe warnings, and the community's teachers read the two passages together and concluded that the permission does not relax the prohibition of consuming what belongs to the child. The passage shows that the sentence's concern is the care of the orphan rather than the convenience of the guardian, and the permission is bounded by the purpose the verse has named.

**The Knowledge of Intentions**

The sentence states that God knows the one who works corruption and the one who sets matters right, and the clause places the assessment of the guardian's conduct with God rather than with the community's observation. The community's teachers read the statement as the ground of the warning that accompanies the permission.

The Book's practice of closing a ruling with a reference to God's knowledge appears throughout the sūrah, and the sentence here supplies the assurance that the intentions behind a person's arrangements are seen. The passage shows that the difficulty of the guardian's position is understood, and the clause removes the excuse that a person's motives cannot be known.

**The Closing Names**

The sentence closes with the statement that God is mighty and wise, and the community's scholars read the pair as the ground of the instruction. The Book's practice of attaching the names to its rulings gives the reader the disposition in which the matter is to be handled, and the sentence here follows the pattern.

The passage's earlier verses have treated the community's questions about giving and about harmful practices, and the sentence in this place completes a series of rulings with a statement about the one who made them. The reader is left with the instruction about the orphans and with the names that accompany it, and the passage continues with the rules about marriage.

**The Guardian and the Child**

The guardianship that the sentence assumes is a trust whose measure is the welfare of the child, and the community's jurists drew the rules of the orphan's property from this verse together with the passages that warn against consuming it unjustly. The permission to associate concerns the ordinary arrangements of the household, so that the guardian may share his table and his service with the child he raises.

The teachers explained the limit of the permission as the point at which the guardian's own interest displaces the child's. What the guardian takes from the property in return for his labour is measured by what the service requires, and whatever exceeds that is a wrong that the closing clause of the sentence reaches.

The community's practice followed the sentence. Reports describe the companions who raised the children of their brothers and who kept accounts of the smallest items, and they describe others who were reassured by the revelation when their scruples had made the arrangements unworkable. Both reactions appear in the material, and the sentence answered the second while never relaxing the warning addressed to the first.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the purpose of the guardianship, the permission to associate with the orphans, the warning about harm, and the knowledge of God that governs the matter. The community's teachers used the verse when they set out the duties of those who care for children, and the reader is shown that the Book's legislation on the subject begins with the welfare of the child.

The community counted the care of orphans among the marks of a believer, and the teachers used the verse when they spoke about the reward that accompanies the duty. The sentence also protects the child's dignity, since the permission it grants is joined to a standard of purpose rather than to the convenience of the guardian, and the warning at its close addresses the intention that the community cannot see.
