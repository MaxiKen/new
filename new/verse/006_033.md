## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:33

> We certainly know that what they say grieves you ˹O Prophet˺. It is not your honesty they question—it is Allah’s signs that the wrongdoers deny.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grief Is Acknowledged Before It Is Answered**

The verse begins by naming a private experience and confirming it from the highest authority. God knows that what they say grieves the Prophet, and the knowledge is stated before any instruction or consolation is offered. The Book does the same elsewhere: We certainly know that your heart is truly distressed by what they say (15:97); do not let their words grieve you, for all honour and power belongs to Allah (10:65); and be patient, for your patience is only with Allah's help, and do not grieve over those who disbelieve (16:127).

The acknowledgement itself carries weight. A man being mocked in the streets of his own city is told that the effect on him is known, and the first thing he receives is not a lesson about patience but the fact that his distress has been seen. The reassurances come after, and they come as a consequence.

**The Distinction That Makes the Verse Famous**

The sentence then draws a line the Prophet could not have drawn for himself: it is not you they are denying, but the signs of Allah that the wrongdoers deny. The denial is real, and it is aimed at the message rather than at the messenger's credibility, because the audience had already settled the question of his credibility years before. The man they were disputing with was the one they had named the trustworthy, and the Book itself points to his record when it has him say that he had lived a whole lifetime among them before the revelation came (10:16).

The distinction appears in other forms where the Book ties the treatment of the messenger to the treatment of the One who sent him. Those who pledge allegiance to you are actually pledging allegiance to Allah (48:10), and the Prophet is told to say that if they sincerely love Allah they should follow him (3:31). The message and its bearer can be separated in analysis, and the Book separates them so that the rejection is correctly located.

**What It Means to Reject a Sign**

The thing being denied is not an idea the audience found improbable. The sūrah uses the word signs for the created order as well as for the recited verses, and the Makkans had been living inside both. Their caravans navigated by the stars the Book describes as guidance through the darkness of land and sea (6:97), and they were addressed in a language they loved by a man they respected. To reject the signs is to refuse the route those things point to, not to dispute that the things exist.

The Book states the consequence of that refusal in a form that fits this passage: when a people is arrogant in the land, God turns them away from the signs, so that even if they see every sign they will not believe in it (7:146). The denial is thus an activity of the will rather than a failure of the mind, which is why the Prophet's honesty is not really in question.

**Why This Comfort Is Unique**

The Prophet receives many consolations in the Qur'an, but this is the only place where he is told that the denial is not directed at him personally. Commentators point out that this makes the verse the strongest of the reassurances, because it removes a burden he had been carrying rather than simply telling him to bear it. The grief of a rejected preacher is partly the fear that he has failed, and the verse settles that fear with a fact.

That fact is also a warning to the audience. If the denial bypasses the messenger, it lands on the signs of God, and the wrongdoers are the ones named as denying them. By telling the Prophet where the rejection actually lands, the verse simultaneously tells the deniers.

**The Wrongdoers Named in the Sentence**

The verse does not say that the disbelievers deny the signs. It says the wrongdoers deny them, using the term that throughout the sūrah describes those whose conduct corrupts what it touches. The substitution fits the reasoning established earlier, where denial of the signs and fabrication against God were paired as the two crimes ranked above all others (6:21). A person who denies a sign that has reached him has not merely declined an argument; he has refused something that was owed to the one who sent it.

That is the sense in which the denial is a wrong rather than a mistake. The evidence available to the Makkans was sufficient, the messenger was trustworthy, and the message was in their own tongue. What remained was a decision, and the verse names the decision where it belongs.

**The Comfort Given to Everyone Who Speaks After Him**

The line has outlived its first application. Any person who carries a message and is mocked for it can be tempted to hear the mockery as a verdict on himself, and the verse keeps that from happening by separating the bearer from the message. The Book goes further in the same direction where it tells the Prophet not to grieve himself to death over their denial (18:6; 26:3), since he is not responsible for an outcome he cannot control.

The sūrah does not leave that statement floating. The verse that follows explains how the pattern of rejection has run its course through every previous messenger, and it supplies the patience the Prophet needed by showing him what patience had already produced. The Book's customary way of handling grief in its messenger is to widen the frame until the personal injury becomes one instance of a pattern with a known ending, and that is exactly the shape of the passage that comes next.
