## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:67

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow.” They replied, “Are you mocking us?” Moses responded, “I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Named for a Cow**

The longest sūrah of the Qur'an takes its title from this episode: al-Baqarah, the Cow. The naming is itself an exegetical act — of the sūrah's hundreds of rulings, narratives, and arguments, the tradition's chosen identifier is a story about a commanded sacrifice and the reception it met. The episode is also, as the commentators note, unusually detailed by Qur'anic standards: where the Book typically compresses a narrative to its moral geometry, this one runs across several verses of dialogue, question and answer, specification and response. The detail is the reason for the naming. The sūrah's central subject is how a covenant community handles what it is given — scripture, provision, commands — and no scene in the recital displays the handling's failure mode as precisely as a command received with a sarcasm-question. The cow-episode is al-Baqarah in miniature: a divine order, minimal in its terms; a human reception that converts the order into an occasion; and a negotiation that costs the receivers everything the order was going to give them cheaply. A sūrah that legislates prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, marriage, debt, and war names itself after this exchange because the exchange determines whether any legislation will be carried at all.

**A Command With No Specifications**

The order arrives in six words: *Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow*. Its remarkable feature is what it lacks. No age, no color, no lineage, no number — an indefinite noun, *baqarah*, any cow. In a law-code whose later sūrahs will specify sacrificial animals down to their condition and season, this command is maximally general, and the generality is the test's design. A specific command tests obedience's precision; a general command tests obedience's willingness. Any cow would have discharged the order, which means the entire difficulty of the episode was manufactured by the receivers: the command's ease was total, and everything hard about it entered through the response. The exegetical tradition preserves the Prophet's ﷺ verdict on the exchange in exactly these terms: had they taken the nearest cow and slaughtered it, it would have sufficed them — the report standing as the tradition's summary of the whole negotiation. The indefinite command also frames what follows as revelation's standing lesson on the cost of interrogation: every question put to a general order has the power to narrow it, and narrowing an easy command converts ease into difficulty. The order said *a cow*; the dialogue will end with a cow so specified that only one in the region answers the description — and the distance between those two points is the verse's moral geography.

**“Are You Mocking Us?”**

The response to the command is a question about the commander's sincerity: *a-tastahziʾu bi-nā* — are you mocking us? The word is *istihzāʾ*, jest, derision — the accusation that a deadly-serious divine order is a joke. The inversion in the response is total and worth stating exactly: the most serious possible speech — *Allah commands you* — is received as the least serious possible speech. Nothing in the exchange yet justifies the reading; the command is plain, its source is named, its prophet is the one who parted the sea before their eyes. The mockery-accusation therefore tells the reader about the accusers rather than the accusation: these are hearts for which a divine command has become unsayable in plain terms, so it must be deflected as humor before it can be either accepted or refused. Deflection is the response's function. To accept the order means to slaughter; to refuse it outright means open rebellion under a covenant just re-signed at the mountain; to call it mockery means neither — the command is suspended in the ambiguity of a joke, and the receivers keep their options. The Qur'an names this exact posture elsewhere as the habit of hearts hardening after long acquaintance with signs, and the recital's placement of the scene — directly after the covenant filings and the turning-away — files the mockery-question as the sound a turning-away heart makes when addressed again.

**The Prophet's Refuge**

Moses's answer is not anger, correction, or argument — it is *istiʿādha*: *I seek refuge in Allah from being among the ignorant*. The prophet treats the mockery-accusation as a contagion to be fled rather than a charge to be answered, and the refuge-formula reveals his diagnosis: to trade jest for jest, to meet derision with heat, is to join the *jāhilūn* — and the word in Qur'anic usage names far more than missing information. *Jahl* in this Book is a disposition: the settled preference for one's own way over known truth, the active state the prophets are warned against joining — *do not be one of those ignorant* is the address to a prophet grieving others' denial (6:35); Noah is warned not to ask *about what you have no knowledge of*, lest he fall into ignorance (11:46); Joseph prays to be kept from yielding *and falling into ignorance* (12:33). In all three the ignorance is moral motion, not cognitive lack — and the same word, in its social form *jāhiliyyah*, will name the entire pre-Islamic order of tribal insolence. Moses's refuge therefore answers the mockery-question at its root: you ask whether I jest; I seek Allah's protection from the condition that would make me jest with a command of Allah, or answer your jest in kind. The prophet models the only safe handling of derision aimed at revelation — neither absorbing its frame nor returning its tone — and the model became Islamic etiquette: the answer to *are you joking?* is not a joke but a refuge.

**Excessive Questioning Destroys**

The scene the verse opens is the Qur'an's case law for a principle the Prophet ﷺ stated as legislation. Asked, when Hajj was made obligatory, whether it applied every year, he declined the question three times and then said: *"Leave me with what I have left to you, for those who were before you were destroyed because of excessive questioning, and their opposition to their apostles. So when I command you to do anything, do it as much as it lies in your power and when I forbid you to do anything, then abandon it"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1337). The ḥadīth names the cow's questioners explicitly as the destroyed-people archetype — *those who were before you* — and states the mechanism: questioning multiplies obligation, and multiplied obligation breaks compliance. The man who asked *every year?* risked making Hajj annual by asking; the people who asked *what cow?* made their one-cow order into a unique-cow hunt. The ḥadīth's second clause gives the standing rule of Islamic legislation in its place: commands are to be fulfilled to capacity, prohibitions abandoned outright — the whole duty stated without interrogation, because interrogation is how duties grow teeth. The verse and the ḥadīth together fix the cow-episode's function in the sūrah: not an anecdote about one reluctant sacrifice but the founding precedent for how a community should receive its law — act on the general, ask only what action requires, and treat the itch to specify as the itch the destroyed peoples died of.

**The Story the Command Conceals**

The command's oddity — why a cow? — is answered by the case behind it, which the recital discloses at its close and the exegetical tradition fills in early: a murder in the camp, a wealthy man killed by his heir, the killer unknown and the community in dispute; the sacrifice ordered as the instrument of disclosure, the slain to be struck with a piece of the slaughtered animal and briefly restored to speech. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators after him preserve the story's details — the inheritance motive, the search for the specified cow, the price it finally commanded — as reports rather than revelation, and the Qur'an's own filing keeps the mechanism until the narrative's end. The disclosure-order is the verse's deepest point about commands. An order whose wisdom is hidden tests precisely the faculty the mockery-question lacks: trust that the Commander sees what the commanded cannot. The people who asked *are you mocking us?* were asking, in effect, for the order's rationale before its execution — and the rationale existed, was concrete, and was being withheld until the obedience was complete. The sūrah files the whole arc as one lesson in reception: a command minimal in its terms, mocked at arrival, interrogated into difficulty, executed at maximum cost — and vindicated at the end by a disclosure no amount of questioning would have produced. Every reader's unexplained orders stand inside this arc: the cow is the standing parable of the command whose wisdom arrives after the knife, not before.
