## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:67

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow.” They replied, “Are you mocking us?” Moses responded, “I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Delivered in the Plainest Terms**

Moses tells his people what God has ordered and names the act without adornment: a cow is to be slaughtered. The Book gives the command at its most basic and lets the community's reaction supply the rest of the picture. What the sūrah will spend the next four verses on is the community's attempt to convert a simple instruction into a complicated one.

The narrative appears in this sūrah at greater length than in any other passage of the Qur'an, and the contrast with the Book's usual compression is worth noticing. The Qur'an ordinarily reports an event in a sentence or two, and here it gives the back-and-forth in full. What is being displayed is not the ritual but the arguing, and the Book has given the argument the space it takes.

**"Are You Mocking Us?"**

The community's first response questions the messenger's seriousness. Their word accuses Moses of making fun of them, which means they heard the command as either a joke or a trick. The Book preserves the accusation because it reveals the position from which it was made: a people who had lived through the sea, the provisions and the pardons reacted to an instruction by suspecting the man who carried it.

The Book's portrait of this community throughout the passage has the same feature. Their difficulties are consistently with the delivery of a command rather than with its content, and the record of their history that the sūrah recites is a record of negotiating terms. The question in this verse is the first of several that the community will put to Moses about the same order.

**Moses' Refusal of the Charge**

The answer given is an appeal for refuge, and it declines to accept the position the community had offered him. Moses does not defend his sincerity, explain the command or offer evidence; he places himself under God's protection from being among the ignorant. The Arabic term for ignorance in the Book describes not a lack of information but a settled preference for one's own way, and the word fits the situation exactly.

The reply also removes the confrontation from the level of personalities. Moses does not treat the accusation as an insult to himself; he treats the whole exchange as a matter of whose understanding governs, and he takes refuge from any share in the attitude being displayed. The Book presents the same posture in other prophets, and it uses the same vocabulary of refuge when the messenger is accused of invention.

**A Ritual Act With a Long History**

The command concerns the slaughter of a cow, and the Book's account of it is followed by the use to which the animal's body is later put. The Book of Numbers describes a ritual involving a red heifer without blemish and without a yoke, and the details given in the following verses of the sūrah overlap with that description while the sūrah's narrative setting is entirely its own.

The sūrah is not explaining the origin of a rite. It is describing a community receiving an order and resisting it, and the specific animal matters because it becomes the subject of the interrogation that follows. What the Book is displaying is the process by which a straightforward requirement was turned into a puzzle, and the process takes four questions to complete.

**Why the Command Was Hard to Receive**

Nothing in the instruction required a journey, a battle or a fortune. It required the slaughter of an animal, and the community's hesitation did not come from an incapacity to perform it. The Book's account of the incident shows a people who had been given a land and a book interrupting a divine command with procedural questions, and the questions have the appearance of diligence.

That appearance is what makes the episode instructive. The community's requests for clarification are not refusals, and the Book reports them without calling them disobedience at this stage. What they produce, however, is a delay that the sūrah will describe at the end as hesitation, and the verse in this passage introduces the pattern that the next four verses complete.

**A Prophet Accused in the Book's Narratives**

The charge of mockery against a messenger recurs in the Book's accounts of earlier peoples, and it is one of the standing accusations the Qur'an answers on behalf of its messengers. The prophets in these narratives are described as having been called liars, magicians and madmen by communities that could not fault their conduct, and the accusation in this verse belongs to that family.

Moses' answer, in each of those accounts, takes the same form: an appeal to God rather than a defence of himself. The Book's messengers are consistently presented as men who do not treat their own standing as the thing to be protected, and the sūrah's account of this exchange shows the pattern in a single sentence.

**What a Messenger Cannot Do**

The verse shows a prophet receiving an accusation and declining to answer it on its own terms. Moses does not produce a credential, cite a precedent or invite the community to test him. He places the matter outside himself entirely, and the Book presents that refusal as the correct response. A messenger in the Qur'an is not the guarantor of his message; he is its carrier, and the Book keeps the difference visible in every exchange it reports.

That distinction has consequences for how the community's questions should be weighed. Asking a messenger for clarification is not in itself a fault, and the sūrah's narrative treats the later questions as requests rather than as rebellion. What the verse records is a different move: an accusation that the messenger is not serious about what he has been told to say. The reply treats the accusation as a matter between the community and God.
