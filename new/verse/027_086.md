## Sūrah an-Naml 27:86

> Do they not see that We made the night for them to rest in and the day bright? Surely in this are signs for those who believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Back to the Ordinary After the Judgment**

Three verses have described a gathering, an interrogation and a silence. This one asks about last night. The descent in scale is deliberate and it is the chapter's habit.

An-Naml keeps returning from the extraordinary to the available. It showed a throne moved in the blink of an eye and then asked who sends the winds. It described the destruction of Thamūd and then pointed at their houses, still standing on the trade road. The pattern here is the same: after the terror of the judgment scene, the evidence offered is the alternation a person has experienced every day of his life and never once had to seek out.

**The Question Form Returns**

*A lam yaraw* — have they not seen? The chapter used a sequence of such questions in verses 60 to 64, each beginning with the same challenge and each ending by asking whether there is a god besides God.

That series stopped at verse 64 and the chapter turned to the Hour, the mockers, the record and the judgment. Now the interrogative returns, which signals that the argument from creation has not been abandoned but resumed. What has changed is the ending. The earlier questions closed by asking whether another god could do this; this one closes by saying there are signs here for people who believe. The rhetorical pressure has shifted from disputing about deities to identifying who actually reads the evidence.

**Night Described by Its Purpose**

The verse does not say God made the night dark. It says He made it *li-yaskunū fīh* — for them to be still in, to rest in. The night is defined by what it is for rather than by what it is.

That framing is characteristic of how the Qur'an handles natural phenomena, and it makes a claim that goes beyond description. Darkness is a physical fact; rest is a fit between the fact and a creature's need. The word *sakan* and its relatives carry the sense of settling, dwelling, and quiet — the Qur'an uses the same root for the tranquillity between spouses and for a dwelling place. What the verse asserts is that the planet's rotation and the human requirement for sleep are matched, and that the match is the sign rather than either element alone.

**Day Described the Same Way**

The second half is parallel: the day *mubṣiran*, which the translation renders as bright. The Arabic is more pointed than that. The form is active — the day is made *seeing*, or more exactly made such that seeing happens in it.

Arabic grammarians treat this as attributing to the time what belongs to the people in it, a recognised figure. The effect is to put the emphasis again on fit rather than on fact. Light is not described as a property of the sky; it is described in terms of the faculty it serves. Set the two halves together and the verse has named a physical alternation and, for each phase, the human capacity it answers. The argument is not that the world is impressive. It is that the world and its inhabitants appear to have been made for each other.

**Why This Evidence Is Hard to Argue With**

There is a reason the Qur'an returns to the day and night so often, in a dozen chapters and in several forms — the passing of one into the other, their alternation, the withdrawal of daylight. The evidence is inexhaustible because nobody can stop receiving it.

A man can avoid a ruined city and decline to travel. He can dismiss a report, refuse a letter, and walk away from a speaker. He cannot avoid the night. Whatever position he takes about the chapter's claims, he will sleep tonight and wake tomorrow, and the sign will have been delivered again without his cooperation. That is why the chapter places this verse after the judgment scene rather than before it: the reader who has just been asked whether he examined the signs is given one he cannot claim not to have encountered.

**"For a People Who Believe"**

The closing restriction is the same one the chapter has used repeatedly. The signs are for those who believe — not because the phenomenon is hidden from anyone else, but because it registers as a sign only for those prepared to read it that way.

This is the third time the chapter has attached a qualifier of this kind to its evidence. Verse 52 said the ruins are a sign for a people who know. Verse 86 says the night and day are signs for a people who believe. The recurring structure carries the chapter's central claim about evidence: the material is public and the reading is not automatic. Every person in Mecca had seen the ruins and every one of them had slept through a night. The chapter has never argued that the deniers were short of data.

**A Sign With No Rarity Value**

One more feature of this choice of evidence deserves attention. It is completely unremarkable, and the chapter has just spent seventy verses on the remarkable — a talking ant, a throne transported, a staff that moved, a nation destroyed overnight.

Choosing the daily alternation immediately after that material makes a point about what miracles are for. The extraordinary events in an-Naml all served to get somebody's attention at a particular moment; none of them is offered to the chapter's readers as evidence they can inspect. What is offered instead is the most ordinary thing there is. A text that thought wonders were the real argument would not have ended its case with the sunset.
