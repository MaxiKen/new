## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:167

> and expose the hypocrites. When it was said to them, “Come fight in the cause of Allah or ˹at least˺ defend yourselves,” they replied, “If we had known there was fighting, we would have definitely gone with you.” They were closer to disbelief than to belief on that day—for saying with their mouths what was not in their hearts. Allah is All-Knowing of what they hide.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Group Named**

The verse continues the purpose stated in the verse before it and names the hypocrites as the other group that the day had exposed. The construction treats the two groups as outcomes of the same event, and the sūrah has been distinguishing them since its account of the march began.

The pairing is the sūrah's way of showing what a trial does in a community. The same hardship that showed who among the believers would stand also showed who among them had never been committed, and the verse states both results in consecutive sentences without softening either.

**The Call to Come and Fight**

The verse reports the appeal made to the group: come, fight in the way of God or defend. The commentators explain the second alternative in several ways. Some read it as a call to fight in self-defence if they would not fight for God's cause, so that they would at least not be counted among the cowards.

Al-Ṭabarī understood it as a call to remain on the field and form ranks, even if no fighting followed, so that the community's numbers would appear greater to the enemy. All of the readings describe the same intention behind the appeal: the community needed bodies in its line, and the group was being asked for the minimum.

**The Excuse They Offered**

The group's answer is recorded in the verse: had they known there would be fighting, they would have followed. The commentators connect the exchange with the withdrawal of a leader of the hypocrites and about three hundred others before the fighting began, which the sūrah mentioned in its account of the army's departure.

The excuse's content is a claim about their own judgement rather than about their loyalty. They presented their departure as a correct assessment of the situation, and the verse records the claim so that the community could see the reasoning for what it was. **How Their Answer Has Been Read**

Al-Rāzī considered two ways of taking the group's statement. On one reading they genuinely believed that no fighting would take place and expected only a standoff. On the other, they expected that if anything happened it would not be a battle but a massacre, and their departure was prompted by the certainty of defeat.

Both readings, al-Rāzī observes, describe an attitude that the verse calls hypocritical rather than sincere. The group had joined the expedition and then left it on the basis of a private calculation, and the sūrah places that calculation among the things the day had brought to light.

**Closer to Disbelief Than to Belief**

The verse states that on that day they were closer to disbelief than to belief. Al-Rāzī draws a careful lesson from the wording: the Book does not call a person who has pronounced the testimony of faith a disbeliever, since even here it describes the group as nearer to one than the other rather than assigning them to disbelief outright.

Other commentators took the sentence to mean that the group's outward conduct was nearer to disbelief while their inward state was disbelief altogether. The two readings differ over how the sentence should be applied, and the discipline the first of them recommends has been influential in the community's treatment of those who profess the faith while falling short of it.

**Saying With Their Mouths What Was Not in Their Hearts**

The verse describes the group as saying with their mouths what was not in their hearts, and the phrase is the Book's standard description of hypocrisy. It appears in this sūrah's warning against taking confidants from outside the community, and it appears in the sūrahs that deal with the groups in Madinah who watched the community's fortunes before committing themselves.

The Book's descriptions of such people are consistent: they speak the language of the community while their loyalties remain available to whoever seems to be winning. The verse before the reader places the conduct of the group at Uḥud in that category, and the report of their answer provides the evidence.

**God Knows What They Conceal**

The verse closes with the statement that God knows best what they conceal, and the sentence returns to the theme that runs through the passage. The community could see the withdrawal and hear the excuse; the verse states that the full contents of the matter were known to God.

The Book attaches this reminder to its descriptions of hypocrites in many places, and the effect is to remove the possibility that a person might manage his appearance while keeping his conduct out of account. The community is told that the assessment of the day's events is not limited to what was visible on the field, and the sentence about concealment ends the verse on that note.

**What the Verse Teaches the Community**

The verse shows a community being asked to look at itself after a crisis, and it shows that the crisis divided its members by what it brought out of them rather than by what they had claimed beforehand.

The sūrah's treatment of the conduct it describes is severe, and it is also explanatory: the group's reasoning is set out so that the community could recognise it in future. The verses that follow turn to the claim they made about the dead, and the sūrah answers it in terms that the whole community could apply to itself.
