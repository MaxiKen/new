## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:28

> And ˹remember˺ when Lot rebuked ˹the men of˺ his people: “You certainly commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before you.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Crime Without Precedent**

Lot's address to his people begins with a claim about history: this deed has not been done by anyone before them. The statement is part of the indictment rather than a rhetorical flourish. The Book presents the offence as an innovation in human conduct, and it treats the novelty as an aggravating factor, since the people have not merely followed a corruption that arrived from earlier generations. Abraham's people defended their idols by appealing to what their forefathers had done; Lot's people have nothing to appeal to, and the Book's shaping of the two accounts places the two arguments side by side. One town justified itself by inheritance, the other by invention, and the chapter shows both routes ending in the same place. Tradition is not a proof, and neither is the absence of it.

The novelty is also the reason the charge takes the form of a question. Lot does not cite a statute or an earlier prophet's ruling; he asks his townspeople whether they would commit an act that nobody before them had committed, and the Book records the question because the answer was already visible in their conduct.

**The Word Behind Shameful Deed**

Arabic has a term for acts that are ugly in themselves, and the Book uses it for serious sexual transgression more than for anything else. The word signals that the deed is repugnant to a person's own nature before any law is applied to it, which is why the Book can appeal to shame in a prophet's rebuke. Commentators describe the act in view as relations between men, and the Book states the charge without ambiguity in several chapters, noting that the men preferred other men over the wives that God had created for them (26:165–166), and describing the practice as transgression and ignorance (7:81; 27:55). The vocabulary of the Book at this point is direct and legal in tone rather than suggestive, and it attaches the offence to a violation of the order God established for the sexes.

The word also places the offence inside a category the audience already recognized. The Book uses the same vocabulary for sexual wrongdoing wherever it appears, and the effect is to remove any suggestion that the town's conduct was a local custom to be assessed on its own terms.

**What the Book Reports and What It Leaves Out**

The Book names the crime, names the preference, and stops. No description of the acts appears anywhere in the passage, and the other accounts of Sodom in the Qur'an follow the same discipline. The narrative supplies only what a reader needs to understand the judgment: what the town did, why it was warned, and how it answered. Commentators discuss whether the passage reproaches the men for coercive assault rather than for the desire itself, and the evidence in the Book leans toward the wider charge, since the other chapters emphasize the desire and the departure from the arrangement God had made rather than the circumstance of consent. What matters for the reading of this verse is that the Book treats the offence as a settled matter of public knowledge in the town. The messengers of the Book rarely have to prove that a thing is wrong before they forbid it; they invoke the shame that the audience already feels, and in Sodom the shame had been spent.

**The Town That Made It Public**

The next verse will specify that the practice was conducted openly in the assemblies of the people, and that fact changes the nature of the crime from a private wrong to a social system. A person who sins in secret still has a conscience to work on; a community that has abolished concealment has replaced the conscience with a consensus. Lot's rebuke is addressed to the whole town, not to individuals, and this is the second time in the chapter that a messenger confronts a collective practice rather than a personal lapse. The Book's treatment of such towns follows a consistent line, and its language here — a deed no one had done before — identifies a society that has stopped measuring itself against anything but its own decisions. Lot's first word to them is a fact about their history, and the fact is offered as a warning rather than as an insult: a people who understand that they are the first to do something are being told that there is no inherited excuse available to them.

**The Difficulty of a Messenger Who Lives There**

Lot's position deserves a moment. He is not an outsider arriving with a warning; he lives in the place, his household is in it, and the men he addresses know him. The Book records his distress when the danger approaches his guests and the humiliation he fears from his own people (11:78), and it records their blunt reply that they have no need of his daughters and that he knows what they want (11:79). A prophet in his own town faces the hardest version of the assignment, because the audience knows his past and the message has to be carried without the authority that distance lends. That is the setting in which he says the sentence under discussion, and the Book presents him as a man doing his duty in a place that had run out of shame long before he spoke.

Living among them also shaped how the warning had to be delivered. A man who eats with his neighbours and trades in their market cannot address them as a stranger would, and the Book's account preserves a tone belonging to someone speaking to people he has known all his life.

**The Book's Category for Sexual Transgression**

The term used here for the shameful deed belongs to the Book's standard vocabulary for sexual offences, and its other uses show how the category is drawn. The Book tells believers not to go near adultery, describing it as truly a shameful deed and an evil way (17:32), and it uses the same word for the acts of Lot's people in the parallel accounts. The choice of term is significant for the argument of the verse. The Book's charge is not that the town broke a local custom or offended its neighbours; it is that the practice belongs to a class of acts that the Book treats as wrong in themselves, and the rebuke opens with the general category before it names the particular offence.

The category also explains why the Book reports the offence before it reports the sentence. What follows in the sūrah is a judgment, and that judgment becomes intelligible only once the reader understands what kind of act the town had adopted as a public practice.

**A Rebuke Repeated in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf**

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf reports the same moment in the same terms: Lot scolds the men of his people and asks whether they commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before, then names the preference for men over women and calls them transgressors (7:80–81). Two chapters preserve two versions of one speech, which is the Book's standard method of establishing that a warning was delivered and that its content is reliable. The repetition also protects the reader from treating the story as a curiosity of the ancient world. What the chapters report is a community that was reached, warned in plain language, and given time, and the rebuke that opens the account is the evidence that the warning was not vague. A messenger who itemizes the practices of his people has removed every possibility of a misunderstanding, and the Book records the itemization in order to close that door permanently.
