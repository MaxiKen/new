## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:171

> The example of the disbelievers ˹not responding to the Messenger’s warning˺ is like a flock not comprehending the calls and cries of the shepherd. ˹They are wilfully˺ deaf, dumb and blind so they have no understanding.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable of a Voice and a Flock**

The verse answers the dialogue of the previous one with an image. The summons was given and refused; this verse explains what the refusal looked like from the caller's side, and the explanation is a parable: the disbelievers' case is that of one who calls to what hears nothing but a call and a cry. The dominant reading, preserved by al-Ṭabarī, pictures the preacher as the shepherd and the summoned as the flock — the voice reaches the animals, who hear the sound and do not hear the meaning; the call lands on ears that register volume but not message. Al-Rāzī records the inverted reading: the parable describes the idolaters' own prayers to their idols, calls addressed to objects that hear only as dead matter hears — the sound arrives, the comprehension never does. The grammar sustains both, and the two readings compose: the disbelievers are the flock when the Prophet calls them, and they are the futile caller when they call their gods. The verse's genius is that one image convicts both directions of the community's speech — hearing without understanding when addressed, speaking without being heard when addressing.

**Hearing That Stops at the Ear**

The flock simile isolates the exact point of failure with anatomical precision. The animals are not deaf in the physical sense — the shepherd's voice provokes movement, attention, the shuffle of the herd toward feed or shelter. They hear. What they lack is the faculty that converts heard sound into understood speech, and the verse locates the disbelievers' failure at the identical joint: revelation reaches them as it reached every listener — audible, intelligible in its language, accompanied by arguments they could repeat. The failure is downstream of the ear. This is why the parable stings rather than insults: it does not say the disbelievers are beasts, but that their reception functions like a beast's reception — complete at the sensory level, absent at the level of meaning. The sound of the Qur'an was heard in Mecca's streets; its claims were debated in the assembly; its words were memorized by enemies who found them inimitable. All of it arrived. None of it was heard in the sense the shepherd needs, and the verse's second sentence names that deficit as the disbelievers' chosen condition.

**Deaf, Dumb, Blind — and Responsible**

The three deprivations are the sūrah's established formula for the closed heart. Its opening portrait used the identical triad: *"They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺"* (2:18) — with the crucial insertion that governs every use: *wilfully*, the deafness they do not wish to cure. The sūrah of the Spoils states the verdict from the divine side: *"the worst of all beings in the sight of Allah are the ˹wilfully˺ deaf and dumb, who do not understand"* (8:22) — and the sūrah of the Night Journey shows the condition's eschatological permanence: dragged on their faces on the Day, *deaf, dumb, and blind*, with Hell as their home (17:97). The pairing of incapacity language with voluntariness is the Qur'an's careful anthropology: the organs named are not the body's but the heart's, and the heart's deafness is acquired by repeated refusal until it becomes a nature. The flock in the parable cannot choose to understand; the disbelievers could have, and did not — which is why the parable describes their reception rather than their guilt, and the triad that follows describes their guilt as a state they own. The similitude's limit is the verse's deepest point: they are *like* the flock in hearing, and worse than the flock in accountability, because the flock was made so and they made themselves.

**The Qur'an's Animal Parables**

This simile belongs to a deliberate register in which the Qur'an figures insensibility to revelation through animals. The sūrah of the Congregation: those entrusted with the Torah who failed to carry it are *"like a donkey carrying books"* (62:5) — the most exact image of contact without comprehension, the load of meaning borne on a back that cannot read it. The sūrah of the Heights, on the man given the signs who clung to the earth: his example is the dog that pants whether chased or left (7:176) — appetite unchanged by every circumstance, the restless pursuit that no outcome instructs. The three parables form a graded series: the flock hears the call and misses the meaning; the donkey carries the book and never opens it; the dog pants identically in pursuit and in rest. Each is a portrait of revelation's reception blocked at a different joint — hearing, carrying, wanting — and each keeps the animal world's innocence as the foil: the donkey is not blamed for its books, but the scholar who resembles it is. The register's restraint matters: the Qur'an never says its opponents *are* animals; it says their case is *as* an animal's case, leaving the difference — the will — as the whole of the indictment.

**Comfort for the Caller**

The parable has a second addressee, and the tradition hears it clearly: the Prophet himself, and every caller after him. The preceding verses had staged the refusal's full social force — the flat "No!", the ancestral fortress, the killing question unanswered. A caller who has watched his summons dissolve against custom can only conclude one of two things: that the message failed, or that he failed. The verse forecloses both. The shepherd's call in the parable is everything a call should be — audible, purposeful, aimed at the flock's good — and the failure sits entirely in the receivers' incapacity to convert sound into meaning. The deficiency is located in the organ of reception, not in the voice or its owner. This is the same consolation the qiblah passage delivered in other terms — Allah will spare you their evil, He is All-Hearing of their taunts (2:137) — and it governs the Qur'an's whole theology of preaching: the messenger's duty is delivery in the clearest available words, the outcome belongs to the hearing God created and the heart God does not force. The caller who understands the parable preaches without needing conversion statistics to validate his faithfulness, because the verse has already validated it: the call was right; some of the flock could not hear; the shepherd keeps calling.

**Sound, Meaning, Response**

The verse's anatomy of hearing yields its practical doctrine in three stages: sound arrives, meaning is comprehended, response follows. The flock fails at the second stage, and the sūrah's earlier triad shows the stages' moral weight — the deaf refuse the sound's meaning, the dumb refuse the response's confession, the blind refuse the sign's direction. Revelation's reception is thus a sequence in which each stage requires the previous one's honesty, and the disbelievers' condition is the sequence arrested at its first link by will. The verse leaves the remedy implicit in its grammar: a parable about hearing invites the hearer to check his own hearing. The reader of this verse is inside the address — the calls and cries reach him too, in the same words — and the difference between him and the flock is not the clarity of the call but what he does at the second stage. The understanding the verse names at its close (*they do not understand*) is the faculty the Qur'an everywhere appeals to and everywhere assumes can be exercised: the ear that receives, the heart that comprehends, the will that answers. The parable's flock has the first organ only. The verse exists so that no reader settles for less than all three.
