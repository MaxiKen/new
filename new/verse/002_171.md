## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:171

> The example of the disbelievers ˹not responding to the Messenger’s warning˺ is like a flock not comprehending the calls and cries of the shepherd. ˹They are wilfully˺ deaf, dumb and blind so they have no understanding.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comparison Introduced**

The verse compares those who disbelieve to a flock that hears the shepherd's calls and cries without comprehending them, and the image is drawn from ordinary experience. What the passage has been describing in words is now presented in a picture, and the reader is shown the condition rather than the argument.

The Book uses comparisons of this kind when a description would be less effective, and the community's scholars noted that the image concerns hearing rather than the message. What the shepherd says is not the subject; what the flock does with the sound is, and the sentence concerns the reception of a summons that is audible but not understood.

**The Shepherd and the Flock**

The picture places the caller in the position of a shepherd and the addressed in the position of animals that respond to the sound of a voice without grasping its meaning. The commentators explained the comparison as describing people who hear the words of the messenger but take from them only the noise.

The image also describes the relation between the caller and the hearers. The shepherd calls because the flock is his responsibility, and the flock's failure to understand does not alter the fact that the call has been made. What the verse establishes is that the fault lies on the side of the hearers, and the picture therefore answers any suggestion that the message was not conveyed.

**Deaf, Mute, and Blind**

The sentence then describes the people in question as deaf, dumb, and blind, and the Book uses the same trio in other passages about those who refuse the revelation. The commentators explained the terms as descriptions of faculties that are present but not used, since the people addressed were able to hear and to speak.

The Book's use of these descriptions elsewhere includes a passage in which the same three words are applied to those whose hearts have been closed to the message. The community's teachers read them as statements about function rather than about the organs, and the reader is shown that the loss described is one that a person brings upon himself by use and habit.

**The Conclusion of the Sentence**

The verse closes by stating that they have no understanding, and the phrase governs the three descriptions that precede it. The Book identifies the root of the condition as the absence of the faculty by which speech is weighed, and the sentence's conclusion therefore explains the images that have been used.

The community's scholars drew a distinction between the person who has not yet heard and the person who has heard and not understood, and the verse concerns the second. What the passage establishes is that the call has reached the people described, and the sentence names the condition in which it was received, which is the condition the Book has been describing in the preceding sentences about those who follow the practice of their ancestors.

**Comparisons of Animals Elsewhere in the Book**

The Book uses animal imagery for those who fail to act on revelation in other chapters as well. It compares those who were entrusted with the Torah and did not carry it to a donkey bearing books, and it compares a person who follows his desires to a dog that pants whether it is driven away or left alone.

The similarity among these passages is in the relation between what is received and what is understood, and the community's teachers gathered them when they spoke about knowledge that does not reach the will. The verse in this place supplies the image that fits its context, since the subject is a call that has been heard and not understood.

**Prophets and Other Warnings**

The comparison also concerns the work of the prophets, and the Book describes the messengers as warners whose task is to deliver the message rather than to compel the response. The verse states what the response of some people will be, and it gives the community a picture of what its own proclamation will meet.

The community's teachers used the passage when they discussed the limits of what a preacher can accomplish. What the Book establishes is that the message reaches the ear of the people addressed, and the sentence describes the condition of those who hear it without the understanding that would change them.

The Book describes the messengers in several passages as warners whose task is to convey the message, and it states that their duty does not extend to compelling the response. The comparison in this verse shows what the delivery of the message may meet, and the community’s teachers read it as a description of the conditions under which the work of proclamation is carried out. What the reader receives is a picture of a call that is audible and of hearers who do not grasp it, and the sentence leaves the outcome with the one who knows what each person will do with what he hears.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The sentence comes after the account of those who prefer the practice of their ancestors to the revelation, and it supplies the image that describes their position. The verses that follow turn to address the believers with a command about the good things they have been provided, and the passage then sets out what has been forbidden.

The reader is carried from a description of refusal to an address to those who accept, and the contrast gives the section its shape. The verse in this place states what the refusal looks like from the outside, and the Book leaves the judgment of the people described to the one who knows what their condition will become.

The sentence stands between the report of the refusal to follow the revelation and the address to the believers about the provision they have been given, and it supplies the image that holds the two together. The Book has described people who answer the invitation with an appeal to their ancestors, and it now compares them to a flock. The reader who continues into the following verses meets the command to the believers to eat what is good and to give thanks, and the contrast between the two responses is what the passage has been building toward.
