## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:176

> That is because Allah has revealed the Book in truth. And surely those who differ regarding it are totally engrossed in opposition.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason Clause**

The verse begins with *that is because* — the formula by which the Qur'an attaches a verdict to its ground — and the ground given here justifies everything the previous two verses sentenced. The traders ate fire, sold guidance for error, and walked patiently toward punishment *because God sent down the Book with the truth*. Al-Ṭabarī read the connection precisely: the punishment applies because of their treatment of God's Book. The causal structure is worth slowing on, because it inverts a natural expectation. One might expect the sentence to be grounded in the harm the concealment did others — the communities misled, the guidance lost. The verse grounds it instead in the Book's origin: it was revealed *in truth*, and everything about the crime follows from that attribute. A lie hidden is a misdemeanor; the truth hidden is treason against reality itself. The concealers did not merely wrong their audience; they set themselves against the mode in which God chose to enter human affairs — truthful speech — and the sentence measures accordingly.

**The Book Sent Down in Truth**

The phrase *in truth* — *bi-l-ḥaqq* — is a standing description of revelation's descent, and its sense is layered. The Book is revealed with truth as its content: what it says corresponds to what is. It is revealed with truth as its purpose: not for play, not for profit, not for one faction's ascendancy. And it is revealed by the One whose speech cannot be otherwise — the truth of the Book is downstream of the truthfulness of its Sender, which is why trading it for a fleeting gain is not a commercial act but a contradiction: the seller's own conduct declares the Book worthless while his knowledge (the knowledge that made concealment profitable) declares it true. This is the inner ruin the previous verse's pairs described — guidance exchanged for error is the intellect contradicting itself; forgiveness for punishment is the will contradicting itself — and 2:176 supplies the reason both contradictions were possible: the Book came in truth, and truth can be recognized and refused in the same act. Revelation does not compel assent; it makes assent and refusal both meaningful, and this verse is the hinge between the two.

**Which Book?**

The exegetes asked whose Book the verse means, and the answers widen its reach. It could be the Qur'an — the Book the concealers around the Prophet were trading against. It could be the Torah and the Gospel, which on the Islamic account attest to the Prophet's truth, so that "differing regarding it" covers the questions of interpretation and distortion the sūrah has been prosecuting since its early verses (2:75). Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī both allowed the most comprehensive reading: the Book as God's Book collectively — Torah, Gospel, and Qur'an together — one revelation in successive installments, so that differing regarding it is differing regarding the whole enterprise of heaven's speech. The comprehensive reading is the strongest, because the passage's subjects have been plural throughout: the People of the Book who hide the testimony (2:140), the group that recognizes and conceals (2:146), the concealers cursed by all who curse (2:159). A single Book in three descents is exactly the object such parties can all be said to differ regarding, and it makes the verse the summary judgment on the whole sectarian argument the sūrah opened — the claim and counter-claim of Jew and Christian (2:135) resolved by the identity of what they were all given.

**Shiqāq: The Opposition Engrossed**

The verse's second clause names the condition of those who differ: they are in *shiqāq baʿīd* — far schism, deep opposition, the fracture-word the sūrah used of those who turn from the community's creed (2:137). The word's career in the Qur'an is consistent: the wrongdoers *totally engrossed in opposition* (22:53), the disbelievers *entrenched in arrogance and opposition* (38:2), the denier who has *gone too far in opposition* (41:52). Each instance pairs schism with a qualifier of depth or distance, and the image is spatial: opposition as a position taken far from something — not disagreement at close quarters, where correction is possible, but rupture with distance built in. The addition here, *regarding the Book*, identifies the axis of the rupture: the difference is not about weather or wages but about the one object whose whole function is to end differences. A book revealed in truth exists to be the common reference; differing *regarding it* is therefore not using a shared standard differently but destroying the standard — which is why the verse assigns no ordinary disagreement but far schism. The parties who quarrel over the Book have quarreled their way out of the only court that could have settled them.

**Differing About It, Not Differing With It**

The preposition carries a distinction the classical readers marked. To differ *with* someone regarding a text can be honest exegesis — the sūrah itself has modeled disagreement handled with grounds, reporting the exegetes' divisions and weighing them. What the verse condemns is differing that has the Book as its object in the sense of its casualty: readings that distort (2:75 — a group hears the word of God and then knowingly corrupts it after understanding it), concealments that subtract (2:174), trades that price it (2:175). The far schism is thus not pluralism but sabotage — difference manufactured from a text whose truth is known to the manufacturer. This is why the verse lands where it lands in the sequence: the traders' sentence (2:174–175), the reason (176), and then immediately the sūrah's positive counterpart — the definition of righteousness (2:177) that begins by believing in the Book and the prophets without distinction. The two verses are a diptych: far schism regarding the Book on one panel, and on the other the creed that holds every book together, makes no distinction between the messengers, and turns belief into the worked righteousness of orphans, travelers, captives, and pledges kept.

**The Distance Called Far**

The final word of the clause — *far* — deserves its own weight, because distance in the Qur'an's moral geography is measured from God. The wrongdoer is elsewhere described as having gone astray *far* (4:116's phrasing of distant error), and the Fire's own name for its inmates' condition is remoteness — the curse the previous passage defined as being distanced from mercy (2:159, 2:161). Schism regarding the Book is far, then, in the same sense: every act of distortion, concealment, and sale moves its author a measurable distance from the truth he still holds the text of, and the distance compounds, because each generation of readers inherits the corrupted copy rather than the clear original. The verse's causal opening now reads as its consolation too: *that is because God revealed the Book in truth* — the revelation's truth is not damaged by the traders, since truth does not depend on its handlers; the damage is entirely in the distance the handlers walked. The Book remains true where it is; the schismatics are simply far from it, and the far is where the punishment of 2:174–175 was already located. The reason clause thus closes the case the same way it opened it: the Book in truth, the differing in distance, and the sentence as the arithmetic of the gap between them.
