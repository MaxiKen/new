## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:93

> And when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and obey,” they answered, “We hear and disobey.” The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “How evil is what your ˹so-called˺ belief prompts you to do, if you ˹actually˺ believe ˹in the Torah˺!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Covenant Taken Under the Mountain**

The verse recalls a scene the sūrah has prepared, where the mountain was lifted above the community as the covenant was imposed and the people were told to hold firmly to what they had been given and to obey. The Book describes the mountain held over them in another passage as though it were a dark cloud that they thought would fall on them, and the image is preserved in the Qur'an as a warning attached to their own undertaking.

The scene contains both mercy and threat in the same picture. The community was given the scripture by a messenger it had asked to accompany it, and at the same moment it was shown what the refusal of that gift would mean. The sūrah's later verses return to the same image, so the reader meets it more than once in the passage.

**Hold Firmly and Obey**

The command given at the mountain has two parts: taking hold of the scripture firmly, and obedience. The Book uses the image of grasping something securely for the acceptance of revelation in several places, and it applies the same verb to the believers later in the sūrah when it tells them to hold firmly to the pledge they have been given.

The second part of the command is what the community's answer contradicts. Being told to obey and replying that they hear is possible only if the intention is not to act on what they have heard, and the sentence the Book preserves is precisely that mixture: attention acknowledged and obedience withheld.

**The Answer They Gave**

The Arabic of their reply is a deliberate juxtaposition of two verbs that are close in sound and opposite in meaning. A community answering a summons would ordinarily say that it hears and obeys; this community says that it hears and disobeys, and the Book places the two words side by side so that the mismatch cannot be missed.

The same phrase appears again in the chapter of the women, where it is used of a party who heard the scripture and then twisted words from their proper places, saying that they hear and disobey (4:46). The repetition suggests the sentence had become a saying, and the Book uses it as evidence of a settled attitude rather than a momentary lapse.

**The Love of the Calf Rooted in Their Hearts**

The verse then explains what had been occupying the community's interior while the covenant was being concluded. Their attachment to the calf was rooted in their hearts, and the image is agricultural: something planted, watered, and given time to grow. The Book's wording makes clear that this was not a sudden temptation but an accumulated attachment.

The commentators differ on the detail behind the phrase. Some traditions describe the community being made to drink water into which the remains of the calf had been mixed, as a punishment that carried their offence into their own bodies, and other readings take the sentence as a metaphor for an affection embedded in the heart's deepest layer. Both readings agree on the visible result, which is a people who could hear a divine command and answer with defiance.

**What the Book Says About the Heart and What Enters It**

The Qur'an treats the heart as the organ of understanding and intention, and it states that what a person loves shapes what he can accept. The sūrah will make the point again when it describes people who followed what they had been given up to a point, and the Book elsewhere tells its readers that their own attachment to something other than God will determine the direction of their conduct.

The Prophet ﷺ described the process in a hadith that the community has always connected to verses like this one. Hudhayfah reported that the Prophet said that trials are presented to the hearts of people as a mat is woven strand by strand: whichever heart absorbs them has a black spot placed in it, and whichever heart rejects them has a white spot placed in it, so that hearts come to be of two kinds (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 144). The verse's rooted love is the Qur'anic picture of the same mechanism.

**A Rebuke Delivered in the Imperative**

The verse closes with a command to the Prophet ﷺ to state what the community's own belief produces. The question is phrased so that the answer is obvious to anyone willing to look. If the community's belief in the Torah were genuine, it would not have produced the worship of a calf, the killing of prophets, and a refusal of the messenger who confirmed the book.

The Book's argument here is about fruit rather than about profession. A conviction that yields such conduct cannot be the belief that the scripture commands, and the sūrah leaves the community to its own evaluation of the contrast between what it claims and what it does.

**The Close of a Section**

This verse ends the sūrah's first block of charges against this community, and it ends it at the point where the community's own words have furnished the evidence. What follows moves to a different subject, the conflict between the believers and the disbelievers in the community the revelation was addressing, and the transition is marked by a change of tone rather than a change of theme.
