## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:93

> And when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and obey,” they answered, “We hear and disobey.” The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “How evil is what your ˹so-called˺ belief prompts you to do, if you ˹actually˺ believe ˹in the Torah˺!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mountain Over the Covenant**

The recital's last exhibit from this history returns to the scene of the covenant's delivery, and the delivery comes with a sign that the sūrah has filed before: *when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you*. The verse of the mount's elevation (2:63) carries the same command in nearly the same words — take hold of what We have given you with strength — and its reappearance here, at the close of the passage, marks the scene as the covenant's definitive moment: the Torah presented, the mountain lifted over the presenting, the community standing under both. The two demands the scene voices define the covenant's whole requirement: *hold firmly* — strength, the grip that does not relax when the terms grow costly — *and obey*, the hearing that completes itself in doing. The mountain's function in the tableau is read two ways in the tradition, and both belong to the verse: a sign of the covenant's weight, the revelation's gravity made geological; and a mercy of intimidation, the fear that steadies a people whose hearts, as the next clause reports, were already inclining elsewhere. The scene is therefore the covenant at maximum clarity — terms stated, witness present, sign overhead — which is what makes the answer that follows the recital's single most compressed indictment.

**“We Hear and Disobey”**

Under the raised mountain, the covenant's receivers answer: *we hear and disobey*. The formula is a liturgy inverted. The response of belief in the Qur'an's own vocabulary is *we hear and we obey* — the standing words of the faithful at every station of this sūrah — and the community's answer takes that formula and splits it at the joint: the hearing claimed, the obedience refused. The split is the verse's diagnosis of a specific religious condition: reception without submission, the ear open and the will closed, acknowledgment granted where acknowledgment costs nothing. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ will record the same community's related play with words — saying *"We listen and we disobey"* (4:46) among the twisted phrases — and the pairing shows the formula was not a slip but a practice. Its gravity here is positional: spoken at the covenant's delivery, under the mountain's shadow, *we hear and disobey* is not weakness in performance but refusal at ratification — the pledge taken with the disclaimer inside it. The recital has now documented the whole descent in order: the charter quoted (83), the bond witnessed (84), the contradiction lived (85), the trade named (86), the chain refused (87), the excuse offered (88), the prayed-for prophet rejected (89–90), the loyalty formula exposed (91), the founding calf (92) — and here, at the root of all of it, the covenant answered in the very moment of its making. Every station downstream inherits this sentence; the verse files it last because it is first.

**The Love Drunk into Hearts**

The verse's middle clause states what the answer revealed: *the love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief*. The Arabic beneath the translation is stronger than any English rendering — they were *made to drink* the calf, the love administered like a liquid taken into the body, and the exegetes gloss the drinking as the love's ingress: they were made to drink the love of the calf, directing their love to it through their disbelief (al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī). The metaphor is precise about mechanism and about degree. Liquid does not sit on the surface; it enters, saturates, becomes the body's own composition — a love *rooted* (the translation's rendering keeps the organic force) is an attachment that has passed from event to constitution. And the clause's *because of their disbelief* repeats the passage's causal law one final time: the drinking follows the kufr, as the sealing of 4:155 and the hardening of verse 74 followed their refusals — the heart's condition earned, layer by layer, until the earned condition becomes the heart itself. The tradition's own vocabulary of the heart supplies the parallel picture in a verified ḥadīth: *"Temptations will be presented to men's hearts as reed mat is woven stick by stick and any heart which is impregnated by them will have a black mark put into it, but any heart which rejects them will have a white mark put in it"* — until the hearts become two kinds, one white and unharmable by any trial, the other black and ashen like an overturned vessel, knowing no good and rejecting no evil save what its passions have drunk in (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 144a). The same drinking-verb, the same gradual weaving, the same two outcomes — the ḥadīth is verse 93's permanent commentary: hearts are made by what they drink, stick by stick, refusal by refusal.

**Because of Their Disbelief**

The causal clause deserves the verse's final weight, because it settles a question the whole passage has been circling: which came first, the closed heart or the rejecting will? Verse 74 filed the hearts hardened *after* the signs; verse 88 answered the excuse of covered hearts — Allah condemned them *for their disbelief*, the covering as consequence, not alibi; verse 90 named the covering as kufr's own sense, blessings received laid under denial. Verse 93 completes the grammar: the love of the calf was rooted *because of* the disbelief — the drinking made possible by the refusal that preceded it. The sequence is the recital's theodicy of the heart in one clause: Allah does not pour the calf into an innocent vessel; the vessel is tilted by its own prior no, and the pouring completes a direction the heart chose. This is also the clause's mercy, read in reverse: if the drinking follows the disbelief, then the drinking can be interrupted by belief — the sequence is a law, not a fate, and laws run backward for whoever turns. The sūrah's remnant-theme stands inside this clause as always: the few excepted at verse 83, the few who believe at 4:155's seal — the exceptions are the clause's proof that the causation is moral rather than mechanical, that hearts drink what they consent to drink, and that consent remains available at every stick of the weaving until the mat is complete.

**The Final Retort**

The verse closes with the command and the conditional that seal the passage: *Say: How evil is what your so-called belief prompts you to do, if you actually believe in the Torah!* The retort gathers the whole exchange of verses 88–92 and turns the community's own claim into its indictment. They said *we believe in what was sent down to us* (91); the verse examines the fruits of that belief — the calf drunk into the heart, the prophets killed, the covenant answered *we hear and disobey* — and pronounces: what this belief enjoins is evil. The conditional's sarcasm is judicial rather than mocking: *if you actually believe* — then show its promptings, and the promptings will answer the claim. Belief in the Qur'an's usage is never a bare assertion but an orientation that commands conduct, and the verse applies the test at its sharpest: an īmān whose output is calf-love and prophetic murder is not a weak belief but a false name — the heart, as the Sufi commentators read it, filled with the world and its ambitions, calling its filling faith. The retort also completes the recital's method with this community. Verse 80 asked for the pledge they could not produce; verse 91 asked for the consistency their graves contradicted; verse 93 asks for the fruits their formula cannot show. Every question is answered by their own record, and the record's uniform testimony is the passage's verdict: the claim and the conduct do not belong to the same religion.

**The File Sealed**

Verse 93 closes the recital's first great movement — the fifty-four-verse address to the Children of Israel that began with the call to remember the favor (2:40) — and the closing verse is the movement's seal in miniature: the covenant raised, the answer split, the heart drunk, the retort delivered. The file the movement leaves behind is complete in both directions. Toward the community addressed: every excuse anticipated and answered with its own history — the hearts' covering, the loyalty formula, the prayed-for victory, the numbered days. Toward the community hearing: the inheritance transferred with the warning intact — the same charter of verse 83 now binds the Muslims, the same covenant grammar applies to every *we hear and disobey* uttered under any raised mountain, and the same reed-mat weaving is running in every heart that listens. The ḥadīth's two hearts are the movement's last word to its readers: the white stone that no trial harms, and the overturned vessel that knows no good — two kinds produced not by nature but by drinking, stick by stick, choice by choice. The recital now turns from the record to its heirs; the audit that ran against a people with prophets, scriptures, and witnessed covenants opens its next pages on the community that received this very sūrah, and the verse's retort stands at the hinge, addressed to everyone who claims a belief and has not yet examined what it prompts: how evil is what your belief enjoins upon you — if you actually believe.
