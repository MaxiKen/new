## Sūrah al-Masad 111:3

> He will burn in a flaming Fire,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yaṣlā Means to Be Roasted, Not Merely to Arrive**

The verb is *yaṣlā*, from a root that means to be exposed to fire, to warm oneself at it or to be scorched by it. It is not the ordinary word for entering. Where the Qur'an wants to say that someone goes into the fire it can say so plainly; *ṣalā* adds the experience of the heat. A man who *yaṣlā* a fire is inside it and feeling it.

The Qur'an uses the same verb where the fire is described as exclusive: *“in which none will burn except the most wretched — who deny and turn away.”* (92:15–16). Abū Lahab is being placed in that category by a chapter that has spent two verses removing his defences. The specificity is the point. This is not a warning about a destination that many will reach; it is an assignment of one man to the part of the destination reserved for those who denied and turned away.

The tense completes the movement of the sūrah so far. Verse one declared his ruin in the perfect, as an accomplished fact. Verse two reported the failure of his wealth, also in the perfect. Now the future arrives: *he will burn*. What has been settled is about to happen, and the shift from "has perished" to "will burn" marks the difference between the verdict and its execution.

**Dhāt Lahab: A Fire Defined by Its Flame**

The object he enters is *nār dhāt lahab* — a fire possessing flame. *Lahab* is the blaze itself, the visible tongue of fire, as distinct from heat or burning. To describe a fire as having flame sounds redundant until the alternatives are considered: a fire can smoulder, it can be coals without light, it can be heat without a body. This one is defined by what it looks like.

The Qur'an's vocabulary of fire is precise about such distinctions. There is the fire *“kindled”* by God, *“which rages over the hearts”* (104:6–7); the scorching fire beside the scalding spring (88:4–5); the chains, shackles and blazing fire prepared for the disbelievers (76:4); the abyss that is *“a scorching Fire”* (101:9–11). Each phrase picks out a different aspect — intensity, location, instruments, depth. *Dhāt lahab* picks out visibility. The fire Abū Lahab is assigned to is one that can be seen at work, and the word chosen to describe it is the word in his own name.

That the phrase is short is itself significant. Three words of Arabic carry the entire sentence of the chapter: he will burn in a blazing fire. No duration is given, no crime is recited, no intercessor is mentioned or excluded. The terseness reads as impatience with the subject.

**The Nickname Fulfilled in Its Own Letters**

His *kunya* was Abū Lahab — father of flame. The fire he is sent into is *dhāt lahab*, possessing flame. The Qur'an takes the name the city gave him, apparently for his bright ruddy complexion, and reads it as a description of his end.

Wordplay of this kind is common in the Arabic tradition, but here it does more than decorate. A *kunya* is a claim about a person's identity, given by other people and worn publicly; Abū Lahab was called this in the markets of Mecca. The sūrah adopts the name and assigns it a referent he did not choose. The man who was known as the father of a blaze becomes the property of one. The chapter is even called by that word in the tradition — *al-Lahab* — alongside its more common title taken from the last verse.

The commentators noticed the economy of this. Only two readings of the nickname are recorded: it described his appearance, or it described his fate. The sūrah does not choose between them, because either way the name now belongs to the fire. What had been a comment on a complexion becomes a legal description.

**Three Moods in Three Verses**

The grammatical movement of this chapter is worth setting out, because it carries the argument. The first verse begins with a perfect verb used declaratively — *tabbat yadā Abī Lahab*, his hands have perished — and ends with an optative, *wa tabba*, may he perish. The second verse is negative and perfect: his wealth did not avail him. The third is future and affirmative: he will burn.

The sequence moves from what God has declared, to what has already proved true of his resources, to what will happen to him. Each step narrows the room available. After the first verse he might still have argued that his hands were his own; after the second that his money would cover him; after the third there is nothing left to argue with.

That progression is why the chapter works as a public recitation. A listener who heard it in Mecca heard not an insult but a case being closed in stages, with the stages marked by verb forms. The rhetorical force is in the grammar rather than in the vocabulary, which is why the sūrah is so short and so difficult to answer.

**A Sentence Addressed to One Man, and to Everyone Watching**

Most of the Qur'an's language about the fire is addressed to categories: the disbelievers, the wrongdoers, the hypocrites, those who deny and turn away. Groups are warned; individuals are usually left to be inferred. This verse does the opposite. It attaches a fire to a person with a name, a father, an address, and a wife.

The reversal has an effect on the audience. A warning about "the disbelievers" can be heard by a disbeliever as not quite applying to him, since categories are abstractions and he is concrete. A sentence on a named neighbour cannot. Everyone in the crowd on al-Ṣafā knew whose hands were being cursed, and everyone could see that those hands were still holding things.

So the chapter asks the audience to do a calculation about itself. If the fire is real enough to be assigned to a specific man while he stands there, then the categories are real too, and every hearer is inside one of them. The individual case is the proof of the general claim; that is the function of naming Abū Lahab at all.

**Fuel Is What He Becomes**

The Qur'an has a further way of describing the relation between the damned and the fire: they are its material. *“Certainly you ˹disbelievers˺ and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell. You are ˹all˺ bound to enter it.”* (21:98). The idols a man served are burned with him, and he is burned as fuel rather than merely as an occupant.

That image completes this chapter's account of Abū Lahab. His hands are void, his wealth does not avail, and he enters a blazing fire — where, on the Qur'an's own terms elsewhere, he is what keeps it burning. The goddess al-ʿUzzā, whose servant his name declared him to be, belongs in the same fire according to 21:98, together with the wealth and children he hoped to ransom himself with.

The next verse of this sūrah adds a person to the fuel, and the addition is the most shocking thing in the chapter.
