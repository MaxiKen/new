## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:1

> Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the clear Book.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Letters Pronounced One at a Time**

Alif, lām, rāʾ are recited separately, never blended into a word, and they signify nothing in the ordinary lexicon of Arabic. That is the first fact about them, and it decides how everything after them should be heard. Twenty-nine chapters of the Qur'an open with such letter-groups, and across all of them only fourteen letters of the twenty-eight ever appear — exactly half the alphabet, arranged in combinations of one to five, producing no name, no verb and no sentence. A Meccan hearer caught those three sounds and knew at once that he was hearing the raw material of his own speech with its ordinary job removed. The letters belong to you; the arrangement does not. Before a single proposition is stated, the opening has made a claim about origin. This book is built from components every Arab possesses in equal measure, and no Arab has built anything resembling it.

**A Signature Shared by Six Neighbouring Chapters**

The identical group stands at the head of the chapters immediately around this one. Yūnus, Hūd, Yūsuf, Ibrāhīm and al-Ḥijr each begin *Alif-Lãm-Ra*, and between the third and fourth sits al-Raʿd with the lengthened form *Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ra* (10:1; 11:1; 13:1; 14:1; 15:1). Six consecutive chapters, one signature. The clustering is not ornamental. In all but three of the twenty-nine openings the letter-group is followed almost at once by a mention of revelation, and this block is the densest instance of that pattern in the book: letters, then the Book, then what the Book exists to do. Yūsuf contributes a third element its neighbours lack, namely a narrative running nearly a hundred verses without interruption. The signature announces a book; the chapter delivers a story; the third verse resolves the tension by declaring that the story is itself a mode of revelation.

**"Clear" Describes What the Book Does**

Al-kitāb al-mubīn is an active description rather than a passive one. Mubīn names a thing that makes plain, that sets two matters apart until each can be seen for what it is. Applied to scripture it says the Book does not merely contain clarity; it manufactures it — in law, in creed, in the difference between a prophet and an impostor, between a genuine dream and a fever. The same phrase opens two other chapters word for word, *“These are the verses of the clear Book”* (26:2; 28:2), and it is invoked as an oath in a third, *“By the clear Book!”* (44:2). Al-Ḥijr fuses the two descriptions this chapter keeps apart: *“These are the verses of the Book; the clear Quran”* (15:1). The insistence matters because what follows is a story saturated with concealment — a dream withheld, a plot hidden, an identity disguised, a providence operating unseen — and it is introduced by a sentence asserting that the book holding it is the instrument of disclosure. Clarity of this kind is a property of outcomes rather than of surfaces. Nothing in the plot of Joseph is transparent while it is happening; the shirt, the well, the prison and the famine all look like disasters at the time, and each is later shown to have been a step. The adjective promises that this reversal of appearance will be made intelligible, which is a larger claim than promising that the sentences will be easy.

**This Recitation, or the Tablet Behind It**

Two readings of the definite article have competed from the earliest commentaries onward. The first takes *the Book* to be the Qur'an now being recited. The second takes it to be the Preserved Tablet, the register from which revelation descends, elsewhere described as a glorious Qur'an *“˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet”* (85:22). Neither reading is frivolous. The second accounts for the strange double status of a text spoken aloud in a Meccan marketplace and simultaneously stored beyond the world. The first is nevertheless the stronger in this place, for the very next verse speaks of sending down and of Arabic, and both are operations performed upon this Qur'an rather than upon the Tablet; the plural *āyāt*, verses, likewise fits a recited text with divisions and pauses. What both readings agree on is the decisive point: the clarity being asserted is not a human accomplishment, and it is not on offer for negotiation.

**A Meccan Chapter Arriving at Its Darkest Hour**

Yūsuf belongs to the Meccan period, and the reports place it toward the end of that period, after the deaths of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib — the year the biographers call the Year of Sorrow — which falls around the tenth or eleventh year of the prophetic mission, some two or three years before the emigration to Medina. No report fixes the date with certainty, and the surviving accounts differ about the immediate occasion. The fit between setting and content is nonetheless too close to dismiss. A prophet rebuffed and abused by his own clan is given the account of a prophet thrown into a well by his own brothers. The opening line, which reads like a formal preamble, functions in that setting as reassurance: what is about to be recited is not a diversion but the verses of a book that makes things plain. Clarity is precisely what a besieged community most requires and least expects to receive.

**The Task the Two Clauses Assign**

Everything the chapter goes on to do is licensed by this verse. The letters establish that the material is common property while the product is not. The description establishes that the product discloses instead of obscuring. Taken together they set a rule of reading for the ninety-eight verses that follow: the story is evidence for a claim about the Book, not a tale told for its own sake. Every reversal in the plot — the favourite cast into the pit, the slave placed over the treasury, the prisoner summoned to interpret a king's sleep — is a demonstration of what *mubīn* means in practice. Things are not as they appear, and the Book is the instrument by which they become apparent. A reader who accepts the opening will therefore expect the narrative to end in exposure and clarification rather than in mere survival. That expectation is met at the close, when the dream is explained aloud in a throne room and the brothers finally recognise the man they threw away. The letters and the adjective are the contract; the plot is the performance of it.
