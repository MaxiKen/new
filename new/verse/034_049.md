## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:49

> Say, “The truth has come, and falsehood will vanish, never to return.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Announcement, Not a Prediction**

*Jāʾa al-ḥaqq* — the truth has come. The verb is in the perfect tense, which in Arabic reports a completed event, and the choice of tense is the whole force of the opening clause. Nothing here is promised for later. Something has already arrived, and the sentence is a notification of a fact on the ground rather than a forecast of one. This matters because of what precedes it. The previous verse described truth being hurled like a projectile (34:48); this one reports the landing. Read in sequence, the two verses are the throw and the impact, and the tense marks the moment between them as already past. For an audience whose standard objection was that the promised events kept failing to arrive (34:29), a statement in the completed tense makes a claim they could check: whatever else is still pending, this has happened.

**Two Verbs That Describe What Falsehood Cannot Do**

The second clause is the difficult one and the more interesting: *wa mā yubdiʾu al-bāṭilu wa mā yuʿīd* — falsehood neither originates nor restores. The two verbs are technical terms in the Qur'an's vocabulary of divine action. *Abdaʾa* is to begin something, to bring it into existence for the first time; *aʿāda* is to bring it back, to restore or repeat. Together they name the two halves of creation and resurrection, and the Qur'an pairs them repeatedly when describing what only God does — He originates the creation, then repeats it. Applying the pair to falsehood, in the negative, is therefore a precise theological claim rather than a general insult. Whatever falsehood is, it does neither of the two things that define a real power.

**Why This Refers Back to the Idols**

The clause looks abstract until it is connected to the courtroom scene a few verses earlier, where the objects of worship were questioned and the whole apparatus of intermediary religion collapsed (34:40–42). Those beings were petitioned for outcomes: rain, victory, children, safety. The two verbs name exactly what would be required to deliver any of it. A power that cannot originate cannot produce anything new, and a power that cannot restore cannot bring back what is lost. Al-Furqān makes the identical charge against them in full: they have taken gods who *"create nothing but are themselves created"*, who hold no power over harm or benefit to themselves, and *"no control over death, life, or resurrection"* (25:3). The sentence in Sabaʾ is that verse compressed into four words.

**A Second Reading: Falsehood Has No Future**

The clause has been read another way, and the alternative is worth holding alongside the first. Taken as a statement about falsehood's own career rather than its productive capacity, it means that falsehood begins nothing and revives nothing — that once dispersed, it does not come back. The translation here takes this line with *never to return*. On this reading the sentence is about permanence: the arrival of truth is not one turn in a cycle that will reverse, and what has been displaced will not re-establish itself. Al-Isrāʾ supports this sense with the fullest version of the statement: *"The truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish"* (17:81). Neither reading excludes the other. A thing that cannot originate anything is also a thing that cannot re-originate itself.

**The Word bāṭil**

*Bāṭil* is usually translated falsehood, but its root sense is closer to void, null, without effect. In Arabic legal usage a contract that is *bāṭil* is not a contract that says something untrue; it is one that has no force, that fails to produce the consequences it purports to produce. This is the sense that makes the verse's two verbs fit. Falsehood is not being described as a rival claim that happens to be incorrect but as a thing with no operative power — an empty form. Luqmān states the contrast in exactly this vocabulary: *"That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth, and whatever they invoke besides Him is false"* (31:30). The pairing there is between *al-ḥaqq*, what is real and established, and *al-bāṭil*, what is void. The dispute running through this whole sūrah has been between those two categories rather than between two religions.

**Why the Coming Is Announced Before the Vanishing Is Complete**

There is a difficulty a careful reader will notice. The truth has come, in the completed tense — and yet at the moment this verse was recited, the Meccan sanctuary was still full of idols, the opposition still held the city, and the community around the Prophet was still small and harassed. How can the arrival be reported as finished while the displacement it causes is visibly still under way? The answer is contained in the second clause. Falsehood is not described as being defeated in a contest whose result is pending; it is described as having no capacity to originate or restore. A thing with no productive power does not need to be defeated, only exposed, and exposure is what the arrival accomplishes. On that account the outcome is settled at the moment of arrival regardless of how long the visible consequences take to work through. The sūrah has made a similar claim about the Day itself, calling it an appointment already fixed and unmovable while its date remains unknown (34:30).

**The Shortest Verse in the Chapter, and Its Placement**

This is the briefest statement in Sabaʾ, and its brevity is functional. It arrives after forty-eight verses of argument — the record, the mockery, the kings, the ruined kingdom, the quarrel of leaders and followers, the boast of wealth, the interrogation of the angels, the demand that they stand apart and think. Everything has been said. What remains is a declaration, and a declaration that argued its own case would undercut itself. The Prophet is reported to have recited this verse while entering Mecca at the conquest, striking the idols around the Kaʿbah with a stick as they fell, and the scene fits the verse exactly: no argument, no explanation, a fact announced while it is visibly occurring. Three more *say* statements follow, and then the sūrah turns to the moment when the void becomes undeniable to those who invested in it.
