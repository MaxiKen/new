## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:12

> Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict Stated as a Definition**

The Arabic opens with an attention particle, rendered *indeed*, then a demonstrative pronoun, *it is they*, and then a definite predicate: *the corruptors*. In Arabic, a sentence built from a demonstrative and a definite predicate does not list a quality among others; it identifies a category. The verse is not saying that these people also cause damage. It is saying that the damage is their defining activity.

The same construction has already been used twice in the sūrah, once for the guided and once for those who fail to perceive. The Book's grammar is symmetrical in this way: it uses identical formulas for the two ends of the moral scale, and the reader who has noticed the pattern from the beginning of the sūrah is being asked to place each portrait accurately. Verse 12 takes the phrase the hypocrites applied to themselves and moves it to the other column.

**Why the Participle Matters**

The word *mufsidūn* is an active participle, naming the ones who produce the corruption rather than the ones affected by it or associated with it. This is stronger than saying corruption occurs in their vicinity, and it distributes responsibility for the damage in a particular way: those who do the work carry the name, whatever their stated purpose.

The Qur'an's other declarations on the subject use the same noun in the same sense. At 2:27 it describes people who break the covenant of Allah and *“spread corruption in the land”*; at 13:25 the same conduct is attributed to those who sever what God has joined. In each case the participle is attached to conduct that can be observed, and the verse in al-Baqarah attaches it to a group whose conduct has just been described in detail.

**The Refrain About Perceiving**

The last clause repeats a phrase from the ninth verse, where the same words were used of the same people after their attempt at deception. The repetition inside four verses is a structural signal. The sūrah is telling its readers something about the hypocrites that the hypocrites do not know about themselves, and it repeats the fact so that nobody mistakes it for an incidental comment.

The Book uses the vocabulary of perception for this group rather than the vocabulary of ignorance, and the distinction is worth keeping. The person described in this verse has not failed to gather information; he has lost the capacity to judge what he has. Sūrah 7 puts the same principle in a sentence the Book offers as a general rule about human beings, that the blindness which matters is not the blindness of eyes but of the hearts that understand.

**Corruption Carried Out in Good Conscience**

The most unsettling feature of the portrait is that the people it describes do not consider themselves corrupt. Earlier in the sūrah they deny the charge in the language of service, presenting themselves as the ones repairing the damage. The verse's verdict and their self-assessment cannot both stand, and the Book tells the reader which one to hold.

The Qur'an treats this gap as typical rather than exotic. Sūrah 18 sets the truth-seeker beside a man whose garden is destroyed while he believed his position secure, and 47:22 warns the very group in question that their political choices would end in the spread of corruption and the severing of kinship. The Book's moral instruction repeatedly warns that the measure of an act is not the actor's satisfaction with it.

**Neutrality That Produces Wreckage**

Read with the city's history in mind, the verse describes a recognizable political type. The hypocrites of Madinah presented themselves as the party of compromise between a community and its enemies, and the sūrah accepts neither the compromise nor the description. A position can be held in the middle and still produce damage at both ends, and it can be advertised as the only reasonable place to stand at the very moment when the damage is greatest.

The verse's last clause accounts for why such a person cannot be warned by pointing at the wreckage. The wreckage is interpreted as the cost of everybody else's intransigence. What cannot be seen from inside that position is the contribution of the person holding it, and the Book states that the failure belongs to perception rather than to the facts.

**A Sentence Without Qualifiers**

The verse does not say that they are among the corruptors, or that they have begun to corrupt, or that they corrupt when provoked. It states an identity, and it declines to soften the sentence with an explanation. The Book's verdicts on the two groups at the head of the sūrah share this quality: the guided are the guided, and the corruptors are the corruptors, and the distinction is drawn by God.

That absence of qualifiers is what makes the verse a test rather than a description of somebody else. A reader who finishes it and finds the whole sentence pointed outward has applied it to a class of people he is pleased not to belong to. The Book's next line turns the same measure onto the reader from another angle, asking what it means to believe at all when belief has become something other people do.

**A Verdict the Community Cannot Enforce**

Nothing in this verse authorises its readers to act. The sentence states a fact about God's judgement, and the sūrah supplies no procedure by which a community could identify the people it names. The Book elsewhere directs its followers to leave the inner question alone and to answer insults with better conduct, and it never issues a list of suspects for the community to police.

What the verse supplies is a standard rather than an instrument. A reader may use it to measure a claim he is tempted to make about himself, and he cannot use it to settle the condition of a neighbour who gave him no evidence beyond an irritating opinion. The verdict belongs to the one who knows what hearts contain, and the verse's final clause, that they fail to perceive it, is the reason the question cannot be settled from outside.
