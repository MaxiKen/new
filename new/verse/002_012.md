## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:12

> Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Divine Interruption in the Middle of the Dialogue**

The previous verse ended with the hypocrites' claim still hanging in the air; this verse answers it before any human reply can arrive. The answer comes from above the dialogue: *alā* — the particle that arrests attention — followed by *inna*, the particle of emphasis, and then the verdict. No argument is offered, no evidence marshalled, no interlocutor appointed to debate. The case is simply declared settled by the only party whose knowledge covers interiors. The interruption has a formal effect worth noticing: the Qur'an has been reporting what they say and what they are told, and now it drops the reporting and speaks. Third-person narration becomes direct address to the reader. The device concedes nothing to the claim it answers — a self-portrait does not merit a rebuttal, only a correction — and it relocates the trial. The hypocrites argued their case to the community; the community is now informed of how the case actually stands in the court that never adjourns.

**The Restriction That Refutes**

The grammar of the verdict is a machine of three parts. *Innahum* — indeed they — with emphasis. *Hum* — the pronoun fronted from inside the sentence, where Arabic places it to restrict: *they, and no others*. *Al-mufsidūn* — the definite active participle: *the* corrupters, the people the noun truly names. Assembled: it is precisely they who are the corrupters. The restriction refutes by reassignment. The claim of verse 11 was not that corruption doesn't exist but that its authors are peace-makers; the retort accepts the charge's reality and hands it back to the claimants. They had assigned themselves the title *muṣliḥ*, repairer; the verse installs them in its exact opposite, *mufsid*, un-maker — and the definite article closes the office to anyone else: whatever corruption is being done in that land, its registered practitioners are these. The verse thereby legislates how labels are read in the Qur'an: the name follows the act and its consequences, never the advertisement, and no self-description survives contact with the divine ledger.

**Corruption by Way of Neutrality**

The hardest question the verse poses is honest: how is peace-making corruption? The answer is structural. In a conflict between truth and falsehood, a party that positions itself between them moves the center of gravity toward falsehood — every step the middle takes away from the truth is a step the falsehood did not have to take. The bridgekeeper supplies corruption with respectability: while the *muṣliḥ*-claim stands, the un-making proceeds under cover of a virtue no one wants to investigate. Ibn ʿAbbās's definition — corruption as open disobedience against Allah — extends naturally to those who dress disobedience in reconciliation language, since the dressing multiplies the harm: it misleads observers about what obedience costs and what compromise buys. The verse judges by outcome, and the outcome of this particular peace is *fasād* spreading through the land of verse 11. Neutrality that favors the corrosive party is not a third position; it is the corrosive party's most useful position.

**The Unperceiving, Named Twice**

The closing clause repeats verse 9's almost word for word: *wa-lākin lā yashʿurūn* — but they do not perceive it. The repetition is the passage's signature now. In verse 9 the unperceived thing was the self-deception; here it is the corruption. One blindness, two objects, and the recurrence tells the reader that insensitivity itself is the group's defining symptom — more than the lying, more than the scheming. Perception, *shuʿūr*, was the fine-grained awareness that feels a hair's touch; its absence at both hinges of the passage means the type is unified by what it cannot feel: not the trick returning on the trickster, not the rot spreading under the peace-making. The two blindnesses are one disease — the *maraḍ* of verse 10, which Allah increases. The verse's structure thus completes the diagnosis begun there: a sickness whose specific power is to anaesthetize its own symptoms, so that the sufferer's comfort becomes the physician's gravest datum.

**The Land Tastes What the Hands Send**

Corruption in this vocabulary is never victimless abstraction, and the Qur'an's widest statement of the theme gives verse 12 its scope: *"Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done, so that Allah may cause them to taste ˹the consequences of˺ some of their deeds and perhaps they might return ˹to the Right Path˺"* (30:41). The sentence carries verse 12's whole theology in miniature. The corruption is theirs — their hands, their peace-making, their un-making. The medium that carries it is not opinion but land and sea: soil, water, social order, the physical and moral habitat that spoils with its inhabitants. And the purpose clause remains medicinal — *so that they may taste* and *perhaps return*: even the consequences are arranged toward repentance, the only exit the diseased perception of verse 12 cannot see. The hypocrites of Madina corrupted a city; the verse's grammar corrupts no city alone. Every land gets the taste of what its peace-makers spread.

**A Standing Answer to Every Euphemism**

The verse's afterlife is its point. Every age produces corrupters who hold the vocabulary of repair — wars fought under the word peace, plunder under development, betrayal under realism — and every age needs the retort that strips labels back to acts. The Qur'an supplies it once, in eleven words of Arabic, and installs it as a permanent test: when claim and consequence diverge, the consequence is the truth of the claim. The closing clause sharpens the test into a warning about its users. The worst position in this passage is not the corruption but the imperceptible corruption — the un-maker who feels like a repairer, whom no rebuke reaches because every rebuke is metabolized into self-portrait. The community that reads this verse is armed and cautioned at once: armed, because the euphemism now has a fixed answer; cautioned, because the answer was needed twice — verse 11 was not enough — and the perception that fails at *fasād* may already be failing in the reader who flinches at the diagnosis.
