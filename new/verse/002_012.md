## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:12

> Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word That Carries the Verse**

The verse opens with an emphatic particle that English renders poorly: *Unquestionably*, or *No — it is they who are the corruptors* (2:12). The force of it is a reversal. The previous verse reported a claim; this one announces that the claim is not merely mistaken but inverted. The people who said they were setting things right are the people spoiling them.

Short as it is, the verse is doing structural work in this passage. It is the Quran's answer to every justification that follows from a good intention, and it establishes a principle the sūrah will use repeatedly: what an act *is* is determined by what it does, not by the name its author gives it.

The structure of the exchange is worth holding on to, because the sūrah will use it again. A claim is made in the language of virtue; the claim is then reversed, word for word, so that the virtue named becomes the vice named; and the reversal is followed by the statement that the person responsible cannot see it. Claim, reversal, blindness — the three beats appear together three times in this passage, and the repetition is the sūrah's way of saying that this is a pattern and not an episode.

**Corruption Judged by Its Outcome**

The criterion implied here is external. If *iṣlāḥ* is setting a thing into its right state, then the test of a claim to be doing it is whether the thing ends up in a better state — the land, the community, the tie between people. The Quran supplies the measure elsewhere and it is concrete: *Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people’s hands have done* (30:41). The corruption is identified from the result, and it is attributed to the people who produced it regardless of what they thought they were doing.

That is why the charge sticks. These people are not accused of bad intentions; they are accused of bad effects they refuse to measure. A broker who keeps two camps calm while poisoning both is not judged by his self-description. Kinship severed, agreements broken, neighbours who cannot rely on one another — *perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship!* (47:22) — that is the evidence, and it is visible to everyone except the people producing it.

**Why They Cannot See It**

The second clause is the one this passage keeps returning to, and its third appearance in four verses is the sign that it is the real subject. *They fail to perceive it* (2:12). Not: they deny it. Not: they conceal it. They do not register it.

The mechanism has already been described. A person who has built his self-assessment on his own pronouncements has no instrument left for checking them. Everything he consults confirms him: his intentions were good, his words were conciliatory, his standing is intact. The one thing he would have to measure is the one thing his situation prevents him from measuring, which is what the world looks like from outside his own account of himself.

**Wrong About the World, Wrong About Themselves**

The pattern in these verses is worth stating plainly, because it is the sūrah's psychology of hypocrisy in miniature. First they claim a virtue they do not have (2:11). Then the claim is reversed (2:12). Then their assessment of other people is reversed (2:13). And each reversal is followed by the same diagnosis: they do not know.

What emerges is not a portrait of clever people managing a deception. It is a portrait of people whose judgement has stopped working in both directions at once — unable to see the damage they cause and unable to see the worth of the people they despise. A deceived mind is not a mind that has lost information. It is a mind that has lost the capacity to weigh what it has.

**Sin as Self-Deception**

This verse places the wrong somewhere more unsettling than a list of prohibited acts. The corruption is real, it is measurable, and the person committing it is not the authority on whether he is committing it. Ethics built on self-report is therefore insufficient, and the Quran's remedy is to supply the report from outside: a scripture that says *No — it is they*, over the objection of the people concerned.

The consequence for a reader is uncomfortable in a useful way. If the corruptor's characteristic state is unawareness, then the absence of a guilty conscience is not evidence of innocence. The question to ask is not "do I mean well" but "what is actually happening around me," and the second question requires evidence the first one does not.

It also follows that being told is a mercy rather than an insult. The people in this verse were addressed — someone said *do not spread corruption* — and that address is the only reason their condition is not final. A community that stops telling its members what it sees in them has withdrawn the thing that could still reach them, and the sūrah treats the withdrawal as the greater cruelty.

**The Warning Is Not Withdrawn**

For all the severity, the verse is addressed to people who are still being spoken to. The sūrah has not stopped arguing, warning, and calling them; it goes on for another eight verses. That persistence is itself part of the meaning. A person who cannot see what he is doing is not beyond reach — he is beyond self-correction, and needs to be told from outside. The sūrah keeps telling him.
