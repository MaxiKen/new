## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:12

> Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Divine Repudiation That Uses Their Own Restrictive Particle**

The hypocrites had answered correction with a restricted profession — *“We are **only** peacemakers”* (*innamā naḥnu muṣliḥūn*, 2:11). The divine reply returns the same restriction against them: *“Indeed, it is **they** who are the corruptors” (2:12)* — *alā innahum hum al-mufsidūn*. The *alā* that opens the verse is the particle of alert — “No! Pay attention!” — and *hum* doubles the pronoun so that the confinement they claimed for themselves is reassigned: it is *they* and they alone, in this scene, who are the *mufsidūn*. The symmetry is exact. They had confined reform to themselves; the verse confines corruption to them. That mirroring teaches a principle of Qur'anic rebuttal: the language of the claim becomes the language of the verdict, so that the speaker is judged in the very idiom he used to acquit himself.

**Why “Corruptors” Is Stronger Than “Corrupt”**

*Mufsidūn* — corruptors — is the active participle of *afsada*, to cause corruption to happen, not merely to be corrupt in oneself. The verse could have said they *are corrupt* (*fāsidūn*); it says they *corrupt* (*mufsidūn*), making their state transitive. A corrupt person harms himself; a corruptor harms the earth that was named in the rebuke — *“upon the earth”* (2:11). That transitivity connects this verse to the larger Qur'anic lexicon where *fasād* spreads from a heart to a community to an environment. *“Do not corrupt upon the earth after it has been set right”* (7:56, 7:85-86) addresses a people whose commercial fraud had undone the *iṣlāḥ* that prior prophets had established; *“corruption has appeared on land and sea for what people’s hands have earned”* (30:41) generalizes the same causation to an age. The hypocrite’s profession of being “only reformers” is therefore not a harmless self-assessment; it is the cover under which the transitive work of *ifsād* proceeds.

**“But They Fail to Perceive It” — The Specific Blindness of This Verse**

The verse closes with *wa-lākin lā yashʿurūn* — “but they fail to perceive it.” The source correctly distinguishes this *lā yashʿurūn* from the *lā yaʿlamūn* (“they do not know”) that closes the next repudiation (2:13). The difference is not stylistic variety. *Shuʿūr* is immediate, sensory perception — to feel, to sense that something is happening — while *ʿilm* is reflective knowledge. To say the corruptors *do not perceive* is to say they lack the first prick of awareness that something is off, not merely the full account of what is off. That is why the same phrase appeared earlier about the deceivers who *“only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it”* (2:9). The disease of (2:10) has dulled the very sense that would have registered the disease. They call corruption reform and do not feel the substitution, just as they sought to deceive and did not feel the recoil. The failure to perceive is not an excuse; it is the disease’s most efficient symptom, because without the prick there is no summons to seek healing.

**Corruption That Feels Like Peacemaking**

How does corruption come to feel like peacemaking to its agent? The source’s two readings of *“We are only peacemakers”* supply the mechanism. On the sincerity reading, the hypocrite genuinely believes his mediation — his refusal to commit fully to the believers while refusing to join the open rejectors — keeps the peace by keeping channels open. He experiences his own lukewarmness as moderation. On the cynicism reading, he knows the moderation is a hedge but finds the hedge profitable, and profit, repeated, comes to feel like proof of wisdom. Both readings converge on the same self-image: the corruptor sees himself as the reasonable person between two extremes. The verse’s *alā* — “No!” — breaks that self-image not by arguing policy but by reassigning the name. The reasonable middle, in a situation where guidance has been distinguished from error (2:2, 2:5) and where warning has been declared indifferent to the persistent (2:6), is not a middle at all; it is a belonging to one side that has been mislabeled as belonging to neither.

**A Definite Article That Makes the Charge Collective**

*Al-mufsidūn* — “the corruptors” — uses the definite article to denote the class. It does not say they are *some* corruptors among others; it says they are *the* corruptors in this context, the ones whose corruption is currently at issue. The article gathers the scattered acts referenced in the hypocrisy portrait — the false profession (2:8), the attempted deception (2:9), the diseased heart (2:10) — and collects them under a single social role. That collection matters because the hypocrite’s self-defense had fragmented his acts: a word here, a mediation there, each defensible in isolation. The verse refuses fragmentation. It says the disparate acts compose a single agency — corrupting — and that agency has a definite article because its effects are definite upon the earth.

**Why the Verse Repeats “They Are”**

*Innahum hum* — “indeed they, they” — doubles the pronoun where a single *innahum* would have sufficed. The doubling is not redundancy; it is emphasis that excludes the alternative. It could have been that the corruption lay elsewhere — with the open rejectors, with the severity of the believers’ command, with circumstance — but the verse says *they*, they are the ones. The same doubling will appear in the next repudiation — *alā innahum hum al-sufahāʾ* — “it is they who are the fools” (2:13) — so that the two verses form a paired rebuttal. The first rebuttal concerns social effect (corruption); the second concerns intellectual standing (folly). Both use the same emphatic syntax to deny the hypocrite’s self-description and to install, in its place, a description he has not chosen.

**The Earth That Would Have Been Reformable**

*“Do not corrupt upon the earth after it has been set right”* (7:56) illuminates the earth that is at stake here. The earth is not presented as a neutral stage awaiting any activity; it is presented as already *set right* (*iṣlāḥ*) by the creation that made it habitable and by the guidance that taught its inhabitants how to inhabit it. The hypocrite’s *ifsād* is therefore not the first act upon a blank earth; it is an undoing of an *iṣlāḥ* that preceded him. That is why his claim to be *muṣliḥūn* is not merely inaccurate but anachronistic: he claims to be establishing what has already been established, while in fact dismantling it. The verse’s “indeed, it is they who are the corruptors” (2:12) restores temporal order: the reform was before them, the corruption is from them, and the failure to perceive the reversal is part of the corruption itself.

**A Failure to Perceive That Still Leaves Responsibility**

Finally, to be told that one fails to perceive is not to be told that one is not responsible for failing to perceive. The Qur'an elsewhere distinguishes unawareness that is inculpable from unawareness that is cultivated. The hypocrite’s unawareness belongs to the second kind. He fails to perceive his corrupting because he has already chosen the renaming that makes perception of corruption unavailable — to call it reform. The verb *yashʿurūn* therefore functions both descriptively and accusatorily: it describes the present numbness and it accuses the practice that produced the numbness. The next verse will move the same accusation from sensation to knowledge — from *lā yashʿurūn* to *lā yaʿlamūn* — so that the reader sees the full range of what hypocrisy dulls: first the feeling that something is wrong, then the knowing what is wrong, until the heart that began with a saying ends with a wandering.
