## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:55

> Yet the disbelievers will persist in doubt about this ˹revelation˺ until the Hour takes them by surprise, or the torment of a terminating Day comes to them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Doubt as a Permanent Posture**

*Wa-lā yazālu alladhīna kafarū fī miryatin minhu* — and those who disbelieve will not cease to be in doubt concerning it.

The verb is *zāla* with the negative, the construction for an unbroken state: they will not stop, they will not leave off. What is described is not a hesitation that will eventually resolve. It is a posture that will be maintained until something external ends it.

*Mirya* is the word for doubt of a particular kind — the doubt that disputes and argues rather than the doubt that simply does not know. It is used elsewhere for the scepticism that demands proofs and then disputes them. The sūrah's doubters have never been short of evidence; they have been short of the willingness to be settled by it.

**The Sūrah's Third Statement of the Doubt**

This is the third time the sūrah has named this condition, and the three statements trace its development.

In verse 5 the doubt was addressed and answered: *if you are in doubt about the Resurrection*, look at the embryo and the field. In verse 7 the doubt was denied an object: there is no doubt in it. Here in verse 55 the doubt is described as permanent, and the thing that will end it is not an argument.

The progression is the sūrah's diagnosis of unbelief in miniature. Doubt begins as a question that can be argued with, becomes a claim that can be refuted, and settles into a condition that nothing except the event itself will interrupt. The sūrah has moved from reasoning with doubters to describing them, and that shift is itself the verdict.

**"Until the Hour Comes Upon Them Suddenly"**

*Ḥattā taʾtiyahum al-sāʿatu baghtatan* — until the Hour comes to them suddenly.

*Baghtatan* is suddenness without warning, the adverb the Qur'an reserves for the arrival of the Hour and of punishment. The sūrah used the same idea in verse 1's quaking and verse 7's Hour that is coming; here the coming is described as overtaking rather than approaching.

The point of the adverb is that the doubters will not be granted a transition. A person who has spent his life in doubt about the Hour will not be given a period of adjustment when it arrives. The dispute that he maintained to the end will be ended by the arrival of the thing he disputed — and the manner of its arrival is precisely what makes the doubt fatal rather than merely mistaken.

**"Or the Punishment of a Barren Day"**

*Aw yaʾtiyahum ʿadhābu yawmin ʿaqīm* — or the punishment of a barren day comes to them.

*ʿAqīm* means barren, sterile, unable to produce. It is used in the Qur'an for a woman who cannot bear and for a wind that brings nothing. Applied to a day, it describes a day after which nothing follows: no tomorrow that repairs it, no morning that undoes what it did.

The adjective is doing the work that "final" or "last" would do less well. A final day is merely the last in a series; a barren day is one that generates nothing, and the terror is in the sterility rather than in the position. Every day a person has lived has produced a next day to which he could carry his plans. This one does not.

**Two Arrivals, Not Two Alternatives**

The verse gives two endings: the Hour, or the punishment of a barren day. The commentators took these as the general arrival and the particular one — the Hour for everyone, and for some an even earlier and more specific punishment.

Either way, the structure of the sentence is the same as the one the sūrah opened with. The end is coming; it is not scheduled by the doubters; and when it arrives it will not be argued with.

**The "Barren Day" and the Question of Badr**

Some commentators took the barren day as a reference to the Day of Resurrection — a day after which there is no other — and the mention of judgment in verse 56 supports that reading. Al-Rāzī recorded a further view: that it refers to the Battle of Badr, a day on which mothers lost sons and which held no blessing for the disbelievers.

Both readings are defensible, and they are not mutually exclusive, since the Qur'an frequently works with a near event and an ultimate one in the same phrase. What matters for reading the verse is the shared sense: a day on which the doubters' calculation stops working, whether it arrives in this life or at the end of it.

The specific application to Badr also explains the placement. The sūrah has just granted permission to fight (22:39) and described the people who were expelled; a warning that a barren day is coming is not, in that setting, an abstraction about the end of time.

**The Doubters' Own Account of Themselves**

The most uncomfortable feature of this verse is that it describes the doubters as continuing rather than as being deceived. They are not, on the sūrah's account, waiting for evidence that never arrives. They have the evidence and have chosen the posture.

That is consistent with the diagnosis offered at verse 8, where the disputant was said to lack knowledge, guidance and an illuminating scripture — three things that were available and that he had none of. And it is consistent with verse 46, where the failure was located in the receiving organ rather than in the signal.

The sūrah is therefore not offering these people a longer period in which to be persuaded. It is describing what they will do with the period: continue. The verb's persistence is the diagnosis, and the only thing the verse names as an end to it is the arrival of the event itself.

**Where the Argument Has Arrived**

Verses 52 to 55 form the sūrah's most compressed statement of why a message can be clear and still rejected. The delivery is interfered with; the interference tests some and confirms others; and the doubters continue in doubt until the event they doubted arrives.

The sūrah is not offering a solution to that problem, because on its own terms there isn't one. What it offers instead is an account of the interval: the message is established, the test is running, and the closing of the argument belongs to God on a day that will not be barren for anyone except those who doubted it.
