## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:25

> They raced for the door and she tore his shirt from the back, only to find her husband at the door. She cried, “What is the penalty for someone who tried to violate your wife, except imprisonment or a painful punishment?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Both of Them Running Toward the Same Door**

The verb is *istabaqā*, the two of them competed in speed. He is fleeing; she is pursuing; the same word covers both because both are running. The sūrah has already used this root once, in a very different scene: the brothers told their father *“We went racing and left Joseph with our belongings”* (12:17). The echo is exact and it is not decorative. In the earlier case the racing was an alibi for a crime that had already been committed; here it is the crime being committed, interrupted. Both verses use competitive movement to mark a moment when a household's account of itself is being falsified, and both are followed by a man arriving who has to decide what to believe. The chapter keeps its vocabulary tight enough that a reader can hear the second race as a reprise of the first.

**The Shirt Torn From Behind Is the Verse's Real Content**

Everything else in the scene — the doors, the pursuit, the husband — exists to produce this one physical fact. A tear at the back of a garment identifies the direction of movement and therefore who was in front. The commentators state the inference plainly: a tear at the front would mean she was defending herself against an advancing man, while a tear at the back means she caught hold of a man who was leaving. The verse does not draw the conclusion; it manufactures the evidence and stops. That restraint is characteristic. The sūrah's method is to place objects in front of characters and let the objects decide, which is also how verse 18 worked, when a shirt covered in false blood failed to convince a father because it had no tear in it. Two shirts, two cases, opposite results, and in both the cloth testifies more reliably than the people.

**The Husband Is Waiting at the Only Exit**

*Wa-alfayā sayyidahā ladā al-bāb* — and the two of them found her master at the door. The word *sayyid* means lord, master, chief, and the Qur'an uses it sparingly; here it names the ʿAzīz in relation to his wife and to Joseph at the same time, since he is master of both. The placement is the sharpest detail in the verse. Of all the doors she had locked, the two of them arrive together at the one where the man who can judge them is standing. Nothing in the text explains his presence, and the commentators do not supply a reason. The effect is that of a closed room opening onto the one witness it was built to exclude, which is the chapter's general view of concealment: it fails at the exact point where it seems most secure.

**Her Question Is an Accusation Wearing the Shape of an Inquiry**

She speaks first, and she speaks in a form that cannot be contradicted. *What is the penalty for someone who tried to violate your wife?* — a question about justice in the abstract, with the particulars already settled inside it. The man is guilty because the sentence says so; the only open matter is the punishment. The commentators note that the interrogative is functioning as a suggestion: she is proposing a sentence while appearing to ask for one. Note also the term she chooses for herself — *your wife*, literally *your people, your household*. She places herself inside his honour and places Joseph outside it, converting a private advance into an offence against the master of the house. That framing gives the husband a motive to agree with her, and the verse is built so that the reader can see the motive being supplied.

**Two Penalties Offered, and the Lesser One Is Chosen**

She names imprisonment or a painful punishment. Both are severe; the difference between them is presented as a matter for the husband's discretion, which is how a choice is made to look free. The sūrah takes the first option. Verse 33 records Joseph's prayer that prison is dearer to him than what he is being called to, verse 34 records God's answer, and verse 35 records the decision: *“And so it occurred to those in charge, despite seeing all the proofs ˹of his innocence˺, that he should be imprisoned for a while.”* (12:35) The penalty she proposed in anger becomes the sentence pronounced after an investigation that cleared him. That sequence is the chapter's most exact demonstration of how a false accusation works: it does not need to be believed to take effect, only to be available.

**Love That Protects Itself Is Not Yet Love**

The commentators draw a distinction here that the rest of the sūrah develops. Her feeling for Joseph is real, and it is also willing to destroy him. Faced with exposure she does not confess, does not shield him and does not accept blame; she prosecutes. Al-Maybudī's reading is that her love at this stage was incomplete, since a love that saves itself at the beloved's expense has not yet become what the tradition means by love. The chapter will track the change. In verse 32 she admits the attempt in front of the women of the city; in verse 51, years later and with nothing left to gain, she states the whole truth to the king: *“Now the truth has come to light. It was I who tried to seduce him, and he is surely truthful.”* (12:51) The distance between verse 25 and verse 51 is the distance this sūrah measures.

**Joseph Is Given No Speech in This Verse**

The narration hands the entire scene to her. Joseph does not answer the question, does not address the husband and does not defend himself here; his single sentence comes in the next verse, after the accusation has been fully stated. The asymmetry is realistic and it is also structural. In a household where he is property, the slave has no standing to speak first, and the chapter records the fact without comment. The reader therefore watches a man be accused in his presence by the person who attacked him, in front of the only authority available, and say nothing until the authority turns to him. What he says when he is finally allowed to speak is one clause long.
