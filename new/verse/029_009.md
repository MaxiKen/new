## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:9

> Those who believe and do good will surely be admitted by Us into ˹the company of˺ the righteous.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward Described as Company**

The promise here is phrased in terms of people rather than property. The believer is not merely given a garden; he is placed among the righteous, and the Book treats that placement as the substance of the reward. In Sūrah al-Nisāʾ the same logic is spelled out: whoever obeys God and His Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by God — the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous, described as honourable company (4:69). Sūrah al-Raʿd adds family to the picture, promising Gardens of Eternity entered along with the righteous among a person's parents, spouses, and children, with angels coming in by every gate (13:23). Eternal life in the Book is therefore social by design. The blessed are not scattered through a landscape alone; they are gathered, and the gathering itself is what the verse names as the goal of a life spent believing and acting well.

**Who the Righteous Are**

The Arabic word rendered as "righteous" comes from the root of repair. A *ṣāliḥ* thing is sound, fit for the purpose it was made for; *iṣlāḥ* is the act of setting something right that had gone wrong. Its opposite, *fasād*, names corruption, the condition of a thing that no longer works as it should. Calling a group "the righteous" is thus not a compliment about their manners; it is a claim about their condition. They are people whose machinery is in order, whose appetites, fears, and ambitions are functioning under proper authority. This is why the Book can use the word as a class name in the same breath as prophets and martyrs (4:69): the categories overlap because all of them describe people who were sound where it counted. The promise in this verse is not only the enjoyment of Paradise but residence among people of that description, a company that lies at the opposite end of the scale from the corrupt.

**Why the Promise Is Stated a Second Time**

Two verses earlier the same pair — those who believe and do good — were promised absolution and reward. The repetition here is not padding: the sentence in between has cost the believer something. A man whose parents press him to associate others with God is told not to obey them, and to keep their company courteously in this world while following the path of those devoted to God (31:15). That instruction can cost a person his family. The Book answers the loss immediately by naming an inheritance: those who believe and act well are brought into the company of the righteous, which is a family that cannot be severed by a dispute over worship. The placement of these three verses therefore works as an argument. The first states the terms, the middle one states the price, and the last one states the compensation in the only currency that outlasts the world.

**Being Brought In, Rather Than Getting In**

The verb form used here has God as its subject: "We will surely admit them" — the entry is something done to the believer, not something he accomplishes. Arabic can express arrival in several ways, and the Book chooses the causative for the reward while choosing the language of effort for the road. The two are consistent: the striving belongs to the servant, and the entry belongs to the Lord. Sūrah al-Zumar paints the scene at the end of that arrangement, with those who were mindful of their Lord being led to Paradise in successive groups until they reach gates already standing open, where the keepers greet them and welcome them in to remain (39:73). Nothing in the scene suggests that the travellers forced the gates. The doors are open before they arrive, and the welcome is spoken by others. The promise in this verse is that kind of arrival, prepared and granted.

**What the Verse Does Not Promise**

A promise gains precision from what it leaves out, and this one leaves out a great deal. No rank is assigned, and no comparison is drawn between one believer's station and another's; the sentence names a company and stops. No family is guaranteed either, in the sense of the household a person had in this world. What is guaranteed is a place among the sound, and the criterion for admission stays exactly where the earlier verse put it — belief joined to good action — so nobody enters by lineage, by tribe, or by the standing of a relative. This is the same rule the Book states when it says that no soul will bear another's burden (53:38), and it is the rule that makes the promise worth having. A place in Paradise that could be inherited or purchased would be a place in a market, and the verse is describing something else.

**Company as the Measure of the Gift**

Describing a reward by the people who share it is unusual, and it tells the reader something about how the Book rates companionship. In this world the people a person keeps decide much of what he becomes, and the Book treats that influence as serious enough to govern the choice of company and to warn against intimacy with those who mock religion. Paradise reverses nothing about that principle — it fulfils it. The righteous are gathered with the righteous, and the gathering is presented as the completion of the life that led there. The verse's promise is therefore continuous with the test announced at the head of the sūrah: a person's company in the next life is the harvest of his company, and his conduct, in this one.

**Where the Same Company Is Named Again**

The Book keeps returning to the same arrangement in other chapters. Those who obey God and His Messenger are placed with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous (4:69), and the verse that follows promises them a reward that needs no addition. In the Gardens of Eternity of Sūrah al-Raʿd, the righteous are joined by the righteous among their own families (13:23), which means the gathering is not a crowd of strangers sorted by merit but a reuniting of people who belong together. Taken with the sentence under discussion, the two passages show a consistent picture: the reward is described by its inhabitants, admission is by God's act, and the criterion is the pair of belief and good conduct that the sūrah has already used to define the tested believer.
