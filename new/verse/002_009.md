## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:9

> They seek to deceive Allah and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb That Turns Back on Its Speaker**

Arabic has a form of the verb that expresses mutual action, and the Qur'an uses it here: *yukhādiʿūn*, they seek to deceive. The form implies two parties exchanging deception, which is exactly what is not happening. On one side stands God, who knows what is concealed; on the other stands a community that can be fooled but not indefinitely. The reciprocity implied by the verb is empty, and the verse completes the thought by supplying the only party that is actually deceived.

The Qur'an states the exchange more directly elsewhere: *“Surely the hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but He outwits them.”* (4:142). The counter-action in that sentence is expressed with the same reciprocal verb form, so God's response is described in the vocabulary of the plotter's own activity. The Book does not say that God is fooled and then recovers. It says the strategy encounters a strategist.

**Two Addressees, One of Whom Cannot Be Reached**

Naming God and the believers as the objects of the deception is theologically blunt. Nothing about a human being is hidden from God, and the Qur'an never offers the hypocrite the comfort of a successful fraud in that direction. The deception of the community, by contrast, is entirely possible; it happens whenever standing is granted on the basis of speech, and every religious community has to live with that possibility.

The Book's response splits the two cases. Against the impossible deception, it reports God's counter-plan; against the possible one, it describes the cost to the deceiver. The verse's rhythm moves from the attempt to its result in a single sentence, and the sentence is constructed so that the second half answers the first. What the hypocrite hoped to do to others he does, in fact, to himself.

**Self-Deception as the Only Successful Fraud**

The verdict *they only deceive themselves* is a piece of psychology before it is a punishment. To sustain a lie about one's own belief, a person must first arrange his own mind around the story he is telling; the mask has to be believable to the person wearing it. The further the arrangement goes, the less access the liar has to what he originally knew about himself.

The Qur'an's final clause makes that condition explicit: *they fail to perceive it*. The sūrah closes three of its portraits of this group with a comment on their awareness rather than on their guilt, and the emphasis is deliberate. Fraud against God is impossible and fraud against a community is temporary, but a person can spend years successfully deceiving himself about the state of his own heart, and that is the only deception in the verse that works.

**The Book's Vocabulary for Human Scheming**

Divine counter-planning is a recurring theme in the Qur'an, and it is always described in the same register. *“Those ˹disbelievers˺ before them ˹secretly˺ planned, but Allah has the ultimate plan. He knows what every soul commits.”* (13:42). Another passage uses the imagery of reversal: *“And ˹so˺ they made a plan, but We too made a plan, while they were unaware.”* (27:50). A third states the outcome of a plot against a messenger: *“And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺, but Allah also planned —and Allah is the best of planners.”* (3:54).

The pattern behind these verses is consistent. The Book does not deny that human beings have plans or that plots succeed for a time; it denies that a plot against God's purposes can end as its author intended. The language of measure and counter-measure places God's action inside history rather than outside it, and it leaves the schemer responsible for a scheme that his opponent has already accounted for.

**Perception Lost and the Limits of Advice**

The clause about perception raises a practical problem for the community. If the hypocrites cannot see what they are doing, then no warning addressed to them can land as a warning. The sūrah is nonetheless addressed to them, and its admonitions are meant to be heard by everyone, which means the verse is written for two audiences at once: those it describes, and those who might recognise the description in themselves.

The Qur'an uses the same phrase in several places, and it usually attaches it to people who believe their position is better than it is. Sūrah 7 states the principle in its general form: such people *“have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with”* (7:179) — the faculties built for seeing the situation are the ones that have been disabled. What the verse announces is not that the hypocrite is ignorant, but that the instrument he would need in order to notice his condition is the very thing his condition has compromised.

**Why the Sentence Ends Where It Ends**

The verse could have continued to the punishment, and it does not. It stops at the point where the reader can see what the people involved cannot, and the restraint is the point: the sentence is about blindness rather than about sentence. The sūrah is still in the middle of a portrait, and the following verse will supply the diagnosis of their condition, then the increment of it, then its price.

The order of the Book's teaching matters here. The account of blindness comes before the account of the disease, the disease before its increase, and the increase before the punishment. A reader who has followed those steps discovers that the punishment is neither random nor excessive; it is the end of a path whose earlier stages are described in detail. The last clause of this verse is the first clear sign that the travellers on that path do not know where it leads.
