## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:9

> They seek to deceive Allah and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Deception Directed at a Party Who Cannot Be Deceived**

The verse names the hypocrite’s project with a verb that exceeds its object: *yukhādiʿūna Allāha* — they seek to deceive Allah. The form (*mukhādaʿah*) implies mutuality or attempted reciprocity, as if deception could be a transaction between two parties. The Qur'an immediately exposes the asymmetry: the attempt is real on the human side, but the object is not deceivable, so the act folds back upon its agent. The sūrah does not say their attempt is ignored; it says it is inverted — *“yet they only deceive themselves.”* To try to deceive the One who knows the unseen that the mindful are praised for believing in (2:3) is to mislocate the contest. The contest is not between a clever speaker and an auditor who might be persuaded; it is between a speaker and the One who constituted the speaker’s interiority in the first place. The same logic recurs in the mirror verse: *“The hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but it is Allah who deceives them”* (4:142). The second wording does not attribute trickery to God in the human sense; it says the divine recompense takes the form of letting the deceiver live inside his own device until it closes around him.

**Why the Believers Are Named Alongside Allah**

*“They seek to deceive Allah and the believers”* (2:9) pairs the two objects deliberately. The hypocrite’s performance has two audiences: the heavenly, whose judgment he hopes to evade by verbal profession, and the earthly, whose community he hopes to exploit by verbal inclusion. By naming both together the verse shows that the two deceptions are one act with two targets, so that to deceive the community that has been defined by belief in the unseen, prayer, and spending is, in the Qur'anic view, to attempt to deceive the One who defined that community. That is why the sūrah’s earlier portrait of the mindful emphasized visible acts that only an unseen belief could sustain; the hypocrite borrows the vocabulary of those acts while withholding the belief, so that the deception operates precisely at the seam between seen and unseen. The believers, as a body, become the test of whether the unseen is truly confessed.

**“They Only Deceive Themselves” — The Mechanism of Inversion**

*Wa-mā yakhdaʿūna illā anfusahum* — “yet they only deceive themselves” — uses the exceptive *illā* to restrict the effect entirely to the agent. Classical commentators gloss this as divine recompense that takes the form of the deed itself (*al-jazāʾ min jins al-ʿamal*). The scheme that was meant to secure advantage — to be counted among the believers when believers prosper, and to be excused among the rejectors when rejectors strike — produces the opposite of security: a self that no longer knows which of its own statements it can trust. The Qur'an describes that self as having a disease that is then increased (2:10), as spreading corruption while calling it reform (2:11-12), as despising others as fools while being the fool (2:13), and as mocking while being mocked (2:14-15). Each description is the same inversion expanded: the instrument chosen to gain advantage becomes the instrument of loss, and the gain is not merely withheld but reversed.

**The Plotting That Is Out-plotted**

The source rightly connects this verse to the broader Qur'anic pattern of divine “plotting” that overcomes human plotting — *“Allah is the best of plotters”* in the contexts of 10:21 where a people plot after being saved, 13:42 where the unbelievers’ plotting is encompassed, 14:46 where their plotting would have moved mountains, 16:26 where a roof falls from above the plotters, and 27:50 where a plotting against Ṣāliḥ is out-plotted. In each case the human plot relies on secrecy and timing; the divine response relies on encompassing knowledge and prior establishment. The verse in al-Baqarah participates in that pattern but makes it intimate. The hypocrite’s plot is not a military ambush but a conversational one — a sentence, *“We believe”* (2:8), offered at the right moment to the right listener. The divine out-plotting does not require a counter-army; it requires only that the sentence be left to operate according to its true nature — as a saying that has been severed from the heart that would have authorized it — so that the speaker must then live inside the severance.

**Why They Do Not Perceive the Self-Deception**

*Wa-mā yashʿurūn* — “but they fail to perceive it” (2:9) — names the privation that makes the inversion possible. The hypocrite does not notice that his deception has become self-deception because perception itself has been dulled by the disease that follows. The sūrah has already described sealed hearts and covered sight for the persistent rejector (2:7); it now describes a failure to perceive for the hypocrite that is functional rather than anatomical. He can still see and hear, but he cannot *feel* that his words no longer correspond to any stable self that could be deceived or defended. Hence the next verse calls the condition a sickness and then say God has increased it (2:10). The increase is not an additional infliction from outside; it is the natural growth of a disease whose earliest symptom is the loss of the symptom’s own perception. To deceive and not to feel that one is being deceived is the disease’s most efficient disguise.

**Failing to Perceive Is Not Innocence**

The phrase “they fail to perceive” could be misheard as exculpation — they cannot help it — but the sūrah blocks that hearing by retaining responsibility throughout. The same failure to perceive is predicated of those who corrupt while claiming to reform (*lākin lā yashʿurūn*, 2:12) and, with a companion verb, of those who call others fools while being the fools (*lākin lā yaʿlamūn*, 2:13). In each case the lack of perception does not cancel guilt; it deepens it, because the lack is itself the result of the deception that is being described. The hypocrite is not a victim of a cognitive deficit that excuses his claim; he is the author of a practice — saying without being — that produces the deficit that then shields the practice from correction. The failure to perceive is therefore both symptom and accomplice.

**A Verse That Completes the Mirror of 4:142**

Read alongside *“the hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but it is Allah who deceives them”* (4:142), this verse supplies the human side of the same transaction. The Medinan hypocrite attempts to use the community’s trust as cover; the community, by being told that the attempt deceives only its author, is taught not to dissolve trust but to relocate it — to look for the corroboration that (2:3-4) had made the definition of belief: unseen conviction, standing prayer, released provision, assent to descent, certainty about return. Without those corroborations, the profession “We believe” remains a sound without a source, and the sound, however often repeated, cannot reach the party it purports to address. The only party it reaches is the speaker, who must then inhabit a self that has been trained to produce sounds it does not hold.

**They Deceive Themselves — The Quietest Punishment**

Finally, the punishment named here is quieter than the “tremendous punishment” of the sealed (2:7) or the “painful punishment” for lying that follows (2:10), but it is not lighter. To deceive oneself while failing to perceive it is to be at once the deceiver, the deceived, and the one who cannot call for help because he does not feel the wound. The sūrah will elaborate that wound’s public forms — corruption called reform, mockery that is returned — but it begins here with the most interior form: a speech act that has lost its interior, and a speaker who has not noticed. That is why the verse ends on perception rather than on penalty; the penalty has already begun as a failure to perceive, and the remainder of the opening will show how far that failure extends.
