## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:6

> That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth, He ˹alone˺ gives life to the dead, and He ˹alone˺ is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"That" Points Back at the Whole Argument**

*Dhālika bi-anna* — that is because. The demonstrative does not refer to the last clause of verse 5 alone. It gathers the entire demonstration: the dust, the drop, the clot, the lump, the womb, the birth, the two endings of a life, and the field that greens when water reaches it.

The grammatical point is worth making because it is the verse's structure. Verse 5 gave evidence; verse 6 gives the principle that makes the evidence evidence. Without verse 6, verse 5 is a list of remarkable natural processes. With it, the list becomes a proof, because a proof needs a premise connecting the observed fact to the conclusion drawn — and here the premise is stated three times over, in three different forms.

**The First Premise: God Is the Truth**

*Al-Ḥaqq* is one of the divine names, and its semantic range is wider than "truthful." It means the Real, that which truly is, that which does not fail or dissolve. Applied to God, it sets up the contrast that verse 62 of this same sūrah makes explicit: God is the Truth and what is invoked besides Him is falsehood.

The relevance to the Resurrection is direct. If the source of all being is itself perfectly real — not derivative, not contingent, not subject to decay — then what it produces is not a trick of appearances. A world made by *al-Ḥaqq* is a world in which acts have weight and promises are kept. Denying the Resurrection, on this premise, is not an intellectual error about biology; it is a failure to take the reality of the Real seriously enough to suppose that He means what He says.

The Qur'an makes the same move when it grounds judgment in the divine attribute rather than in a threat: punishment is not vindictiveness, it is reality catching up.

**The Second Premise: He Gives Life to the Dead**

The second clause narrows from metaphysics to the specific power in question. God gives life to the dead — *yuḥyī al-mawtā*. The verb is in the present tense, denoting what He does, not only what He will do once at the end.

That present tense is the argument. The objector grants that God gives life in the ordinary course — every pregnancy, every spring. What he denies is the exceptional case. The verse collapses the distinction: the One who gives life gives life, and the dead He revives include both the field in autumn and the person in the grave. Sūrah al-Rūm states the inference in so many words: *“See then the impact of Allah’s mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead”* (30:50).

**The Third Premise: Power Over Everything**

The third clause is the widest: *wa-annahu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr* — and He is over everything capable. If the first two premises establish that God is real and that He is the giver of life, the third removes the last refuge of the doubter, which is to grant the general power while denying the particular instance.

Three premises, in ascending order of generality, is a deliberate rhetorical shape. The argument in verse 5 moved from the particular to the general — one embryo, then one field. The argument in verse 6 moves from the general to the absolute — God's reality, His life-giving, His power. The reader is walked from something he can inspect with his hands to something he can only affirm.

**The Logic of the Greater and the Smaller**

Underlying the whole passage is an inferential form the Qur'an deploys repeatedly in this context and always in the same direction: the harder creation is the one already granted, so the easier one cannot reasonably be denied. *“Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not ˹easily˺ resurrect these ˹deniers˺?” Yes ˹He can˺! For He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing”* (36:81). The same form closes Sūrah al-Qiyāmah: *“Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life?”* (75:40).

The form works because it uses the opponent's own concession. No one in the Qur'an's audience argued that the heavens were self-created; the argument was about the Resurrection alone. The Qur'an simply points out that the disputed case is the smaller one, and that a person who accepts the larger while rejecting the smaller is not reasoning at all — he is protecting a conclusion.

**A Demonstrative of Distance**

The verse opens with *dhālika* — that, the demonstrative used for what is far away rather than *hādhā*, this. The grammarians observed that *dhālika* is regularly used in the Qur'an for things God alone fully grasps: revelation, the Hereafter, the divine attributes.

The choice is not decoration. The Resurrection is a thing the reader must hold at a distance before he can hold it at all, because his own experience of death gives him no purchase on it. The far demonstrative keeps the object of the argument properly large. A near demonstrative would have brought the Resurrection down to the scale of the evidence, and the evidence — a womb, a field — is what is near. The conclusion is not.

This also explains why the verse repeats the particle *anna* with each clause: *because* God is the Truth, *and because* He gives life to the dead, *and because* He is capable of everything. Three separate causal phrases rather than one compound sentence. Each is a complete reason on its own, so that a reader who stumbles at the metaphysics of the first may still be held by the observation of the second or the plain assertion of the third.

**Why the Verse Ends Without a Threat**

The Resurrection has now been argued from embryology, from ageing, from agriculture and from the divine attributes. There is no threat attached.

That silence is consistent with the sūrah's opening move in verse 5, which granted the doubt rather than punishing it. The audience is being reasoned with, and the reasoning is complete in itself. The warnings in this sūrah fall on people who have already heard the argument — verse 9 will describe the man who turns away *after* the signs have reached him. The sequence matters: proof first, then the verdict, and never the verdict alone.
