## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:3

> Follow what has been sent down to you from your Lord, and do not take others as guardians besides Him. How seldom are you mindful!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Follow**

The verse turns from the Prophet to the people who have received the message and commands them to follow what has been sent down to them from their Lord. The address is plural, and the commentary tradition takes it to be directed at the Prophet's community or at people in general. The command uses the same verb that the sūrah of the Cattle uses at its close when it describes the Book as blessed and commands the people to follow it (6:155), and the sūrah of the Cow places the Book's guidance first among the descriptions of the people who are mindful (2:2).

The phrase from your Lord attaches the revelation to the authority it carries. The audience is not told to follow a man's opinion or the arrangement of a tribe's customs; it is told to follow what has come down from the One who sustains it. The sūrah's audience traced its religious practice to its ancestors and its sanctuary to Abraham, and the verse directs it to the revelation that has arrived among them.

**The Protectors That Are Excluded**

The second half of the command forbids the taking of protectors besides God, and the Book uses the term throughout its warnings about alliance and reliance. The sūrah of the Cattle states that the believers have no protector besides God (6:51), the sūrah of the Thunder says that they have no protector other than Him (13:11), and the sūrah of the Cave gives the same statement (18:26). The sūrah of the Spider states that those who take protectors apart from God are like the spider that builds a house, and it adds that the frailest of houses is the spider's house (29:41).

The protectors the verse excludes are not only the idols of stone. The sūrah of the Bee describes the satans as the protectors of those who disbelieve, and the sūrah of the Cave names Iblīs among those taken as a protector in place of God (18:50). The passage later in this sūrah says that those who went astray took the devils as masters in place of God while thinking they were guided (7:30). The net cast by the command therefore covers every form of reliance that displaces the revelation.

**Relatives, Patrons, and the Pressure of the Community**

The commentary tradition notes that the protectors mentioned here may refer to the associates and friends who urged the first believers toward the religion of their tribe. The early Muslims were pressed by their families and their patrons to return to the worship of the idols, and the command in front of us answers that pressure by naming the revelation as the only thing to be followed. The Book's treatment of loyalty appears in its warnings about the alliances that pull a believer away from his religion, and the sūrah of the Table addresses the question of alliance directly.

The verse's phrasing also allows the reading that nothing is to be followed apart from what has been sent down, which makes the command a statement about the source of religious authority. The two readings come to the same point. Whether the audience is warned about patrons or about the teaching it follows, the command is that the revelation from God is the standard, and everything else that claims that place is excluded.

**How Seldom You Are Mindful**

The verse closes with a rebuke: how seldom are you mindful. The Book uses the same form of reproach in several places, addressing people who wonder at the message (27:62), who are given parables and fail to draw the lesson (40:58), and who attribute the recitation to something other than God (69:42). The rebuke in front of us is placed at the head of a sūrah that will display the evidence at length.

The complaint is not that the audience lacks information. The sūrah's accounts of the created order, of the prophets, and of the destruction of the earlier communities all supply material for reflection, and the verse states that the people seldom turn it over in their minds. The Book's use of the reproach in passages about the signs of the created order shows what the reflection is meant to cover: the rain, the harvest, the ordering of the heavens, and the record of the messengers.

**What the Audience Actually Followed**

The sūrah sets the following of the revelation against the following of the ancestors, and it names the practice explicitly later in the chapter when it reports the excuse of those who commit shameful deeds and claim that they found their forefathers doing it and that God commanded it (7:28). The Book rejects that excuse in the sūrah of the Cow, the sūrah of the Table, and the sūrah of Luqmān, where the disbelievers answer the call to what God has sent down with the claim that they follow what they found their fathers upon (2:170; 5:104; 31:21).

The protectors the verse excludes therefore have a concrete identity in the audience's life. They are the idols of the sanctuary, the customs of the tribe, and the powerful figures whose approval the audience sought. The command in front of us removes all of them from the place of authority and puts the revelation in that place alone.

**The Revelation as the Only Guardian**

The Book's own name for itself in such passages is the criterion that distinguishes the acceptable from the rejected. The sūrah of the Cow commands the community to judge by what God has sent down, and the sūrah of the Table states that whoever does not judge by what God has revealed, those are the disbelievers (5:44). The command in front of us belongs to the same family of statements.

The sūrah's audience is thereby given a rule for everything the chapter will discuss. The practices of the pilgrimage, the rules of food, the treatment of the orphan and the debtor, and the conduct of war are all to be settled by the revelation, and the verse in front of us states the principle before the chapter supplies the detail. The protector who owns nothing cannot be the authority; the Lord who owns everything has spoken.

**Where the Verse Sits in the Opening Section**

The verse stands third in the chapter's opening, after the letters and the description of the Book, and it begins the sequence of warnings and instructions that runs to the tenth verse. The verses that follow describe the destruction of the communities, the questioning of those who received the message, the weighing of deeds, and the establishment of people on the earth with their provision. The command in front of us is the response that the Book requires before the rest of the program is laid out.

The opening section of the sūrah thus moves from the letters to the Book, from the Book to its messenger, and from its messenger to the community that is commanded to follow it. The reader is given the revelation, the messenger, the command, and the warning against the alternative in four short verses.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse asks how many communities God has destroyed, and it states that the punishment came upon them at night or while they were resting at midday (7:4). The question follows the command to follow the revelation, and it supplies the consequence of the alternative that the previous verse forbade.

The two verses together state the choice and its cost. The community is told what to follow, and it is shown what happened to those who followed otherwise. The sūrah's warning then becomes specific in the verses that follow, where the punishment, the questioning, and the weighing are described in order.
