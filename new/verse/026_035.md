## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:35

> who seeks to drive you from your land by his magic. So what do you propose?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pharaoh Recasts Liberation as Expulsion**

Pharaoh's accusation moves from Moses' supposed skill to its alleged political purpose. He says Moses wants to drive the Egyptians from their land. The claim changes the subject. Moses has demanded the release of the Children of Israel, but Pharaoh presents the demand as an attack on the whole population. A request that threatens an unjust arrangement is made to sound like a plan to dispossess everyone.

The phrase your land is carefully chosen. Pharaoh speaks to the chiefs as though the land belongs to them collectively and Moses is an outsider trying to take it. That language hides the Israelites' presence and labor while presenting the ruling group as the only legitimate public. It turns a question of oppression into a question of ethnic or national defense. Once that frame is accepted, Pharaoh can call resistance to slavery patriotism.

**A Tyrant Uses Fear of Loss to Preserve Control**

People who benefit from an existing order may tolerate injustice while they believe their own security is safe. Pharaoh activates their fear of losing land, status, and identity. He does not ask the chiefs to consider whether Israel is oppressed. He asks them to imagine Egyptians being expelled. Fear of imagined dispossession becomes a reason to ignore actual domination.

This tactic is powerful because it joins a ruler's interests to the public's anxieties. Pharaoh's throne and the chiefs' property appear to stand or fall together. The rulers may then defend the system not because every part of it is just, but because they have been told that reform means ruin. Moses is cast as a threat to communal survival so that Pharaoh's hold over the Israelites can continue without scrutiny.

**The Land Belongs to God Before It Belongs to Any Ruler**

The claim your land is also theologically false. The land is part of the earth Moses has already described as belonging to the Lord. Human beings may inhabit, cultivate, and administer it, but no ruler owns it in an absolute sense. Pharaoh's political title cannot turn a created place into property that God may not judge.

This does not erase legitimate attachment to home or the rights of communities. It places those attachments under justice. A people cannot use the word land to justify enslaving another people who live and work there. Nor can a ruler make his own possession of territory the measure of who counts as human. Moses' mission exposes the difference between stewardship of a land and domination of everyone within it.

**The Accusation Turns the Oppressed Into the Aggressor**

Pharaoh's wording makes Moses look like the one who will uproot the population. The actual power imbalance disappears. Egypt has armies, officials, prisons, and the authority to control movement. The Israelites have been made vulnerable. Yet the ruler's story reverses the roles so that those asking for release appear dangerous and those enforcing captivity appear defensive.

Such reversal is a common tool of oppression. A dominant group points to the anger produced by injustice and calls the anger the original problem. It treats a demand for freedom as proof that the oppressed cannot be trusted. The verse gives the reader a way to test the story: ask who has the power to imprison, who controls the land's institutions, and who is asking only to be released. Pharaoh's narrative fails that test.

**The Question Seeks a Political Verdict, Not Understanding**

So what do you propose? Pharaoh asks the chiefs for a policy response. He does not ask how they should worship or whether Moses' sign is true. He has already framed the matter as a threat to the state, and now he wants the council to help him decide how to contain it. The question turns advisers into participants in the construction of repression.

The chiefs are being offered a chance to think beyond the ruler's frame, but they are also being invited to protect their own interests. Their answer will reveal whether counsel means seeking justice or supplying a powerful man with the language he needs to act. A council is not wise merely because it gives advice. It is wise when it can correct a ruler's false description of the problem.

**Magic Becomes a Name for Uncontrolled Truth**

Pharaoh repeats that Moses' alleged magic is the means by which he will drive them out. The accusation gives a material explanation for a moral fear. If the sign is magic, then the court can fight it with more magic, more force, or more public suspicion. It need not ask why the message centers on release and why a ruler feels entitled to keep a people enslaved.

The label also implies that Moses' power is human and therefore contestable. Pharaoh's plan will be to gather specialists who can meet the supposed skill on its own ground. But the coming contest will show that the issue is not whose performance is more dazzling. It is whether truth can be swallowed by imitation. The accusation sets the stage for a public test that Pharaoh thinks he can control.

**A False Security Claim Cannot Make Injustice Just**

A ruler may sincerely fear disorder when his authority is challenged. That fear does not prove that the challenge is wrong. Justice often looks disruptive to people who have mistaken privilege for stability. Pharaoh's court must ask whether the land is truly secure when a whole community is enslaved. A calm surface can cover deep disorder.

The verse therefore teaches caution about political language that invokes land, safety, and collective identity. These goods matter, but they become weapons when used to erase the rights of a vulnerable group. Moses' call does not begin by taking land from Pharaoh's people. It begins by demanding that Pharaoh stop treating Israel as property. The ruler's accusation cannot change that basic fact.
