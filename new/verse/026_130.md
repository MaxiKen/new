## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130

> and act so viciously when you attack ˹others˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Moves From Buildings to Violence**

Hūd's criticism now reaches the way ʿĀd use their strength against other people. Their landmarks and castles display power, but their attacks reveal what that power does in practice. A society cannot claim honor from impressive works while treating human beings with brutality. The question exposes the moral cost hidden behind the public image.

The wording asks why they attack in this manner. It is not a description of an isolated loss of temper. Their violence has become characteristic, something they do as a people with confidence that no one can restrain them. Hūd calls them to see that domination is not proof of greatness.


The Arabic language of the verse carries the sense of acting like tyrants: striking with oppressive force and dealing with others in a domineering way. The question can include physical attacks, but its moral reach is wider. Threats, coercion, humiliation, and punishment without justice are all forms of power used beyond its rightful limit.

This interpretation does not deny the need for protection or lawful defense. A community may resist aggression and restrain wrongdoing. Tyranny begins when force is guided by pride rather than justice, when the strong attack because they can and when the victim's rights have no weight in the decision.

**Strength Becomes Corrupt When It Has No Accountability**

The earlier verses show a people with resources, construction, and provision. None of those gifts automatically produces cruelty. The problem arises when the people interpret prosperity as permission. If they believe that their success proves their superiority, they may treat every weaker group as an object to be controlled.

Fear of Allah would interrupt that logic. The powerful person remembers that the same Lord who grants strength can judge its use. Every blow, order, seizure, and threat is known. Accountability turns power from a private possession into a trust whose exercise must be justified.


Quran 41:15 records ʿĀd boasting about their greater strength and asks who could be mightier than they. That parallel helps explain the violence challenged here. A people convinced that it is unsurpassed can mistake victory for moral right and resistance for an insult that deserves punishment.

The Qur'anic response is not to compare human groups on their own terms. It reminds them that the One who created them is mightier. The point is not that strength is shameful; it is that no created strength can authorize rebellion against the Creator or cruelty toward creation.


Violence committed by individuals is serious, but Hūd addresses a culture that normalizes it. When a society praises the conqueror, excuses the strong, and calls victims weak, aggression becomes part of its identity. The question then concerns laws, institutions, stories, and leaders, not only private tempers.

A just society must restrain force through truthful judgment. It must protect people who cannot defend themselves and must refuse to turn collective power into a license for revenge. The people of ʿĀd are challenged because their attacks show that their civilization has not produced moral maturity.

**The Strong Are Tested by How They Strike**

Power is most clearly tested when its possessor has an advantage. It is easy to speak of justice while facing an equal opponent. The real test comes when one can harm another without immediate consequence. Hūd asks ʿĀd to examine precisely that moment: what do you do when you can strike and expect no answer?

Taqwa makes restraint possible. It does not require weakness or surrender to wrongdoing. It requires that every response be measured by truth, necessity, and proportion rather than by the pleasure of humiliating an enemy. A strong person who fears God is capable of stopping when pride demands another blow.


A tyrant may make an attack seem like a private matter between the powerful and the defeated. Hūd's warning restores the unseen witness. God sees the person struck, displaced, silenced, or deprived. The victim's lack of worldly power does not mean his claim disappears.

This assurance also disciplines those who oppose oppression. Human beings can seek justice, but they should not reproduce the cruelty they condemn. The Lord's judgment belongs to Him, and the servant must resist harm without becoming intoxicated by domination. Justice protects dignity; vengeance often seeks to replace one tyrant with another.

**Violence Cannot Rescue a Vanity-Based Order**

The landmarks of ʿĀd advertise prestige, and the castles advertise security. Their attacks enforce both claims by making others fear them. Yet a society that needs constant violence to preserve its image is already insecure. It must keep striking because one challenge to its reputation threatens the whole illusion.

Hūd offers another foundation: reverence and obedience. A people secure under God does not need to prove its worth through cruelty. It can build without boasting, defend without transgressing, and use authority without treating every neighbor as an enemy.


Hūd's question is a rebuke, but it is also an opening. The same strength that once enabled attack could protect the vulnerable, maintain roads, provide water, and establish justice. Repentance would not require ʿĀd to become incapable; it would require the people to become responsible.

The verse therefore asks every powerful community to examine its methods. When it acts against others, does it seek justice or submission? Does it correct wrongdoing or feed pride? The answer reveals whether its strength is a blessing placed under God or a weapon wielded against the people God created.

Even speech can prepare the way for such attacks when it reduces opponents to obstacles or denies their dignity. A just people resists that language before it becomes permission for violence. Hūd's question reaches the imagination that celebrates the strike as much as the hand that delivers it.
