## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:10

> ˹They will be told,˺ “This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**These Words Are Spoken to a Face**

The verse is not a description of judgment; it is a line of dialogue inside it. The translation supplies *they will be told*, and the Arabic makes the same move by putting the sentence in direct speech: *dhālika bimā qaddamat yadāka* — that is for what your two hands sent ahead.

The shift from third person to direct address is doing real work. Everything before this has been about them; this is said *to* them. A verdict pronounced in someone's absence can be disputed, appealed, or ignored. A verdict pronounced into a person's face, at the moment of his standing, closes the matter — and the Qur'an stages the closing rather than reporting it.

**"What Your Hands Sent Ahead"**

The idiom is worth taking apart, because it has been worn smooth by repetition. *Qaddamat yadāka* — what your hands put forward. Deeds are not described as accumulated, weighed, or remembered; they are described as *shipped*.

Two ideas sit inside the metaphor. The first is that a deed is a transfer: a person does something here, and the thing itself arrives there. The second is that the sender does not travel with the shipment and cannot alter it after dispatch. What arrives at the judgment is not a report about the man but the man's own consignment, and he meets it as cargo he packed.

The wording is nearly identical to a verse in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, which is why the two are always read together: *“This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation”* (3:182). There the sentence is addressed in exactly the same form, and the repetition across two sūrahs confirms that this is a fixed formula of the judgment rather than a description tailored to one group.

**"Hands" Specifically**

The dual *yadāka*, your two hands, is not a synonym for the self. Hands are the instruments by which a person changes the world, and the Qur'an's use of them for attribution is precise: what counts at the judgment is what a person *did*, in the concrete sense, with the parts of himself that act.

This matters for how the verse is read. The disputant of verses 3 and 8 was condemned for what he said, and the sentence he receives is for what his hands sent ahead. Speech in the Qur'an's accounting is a deed — it is produced by a body, in a place, with effects — and the idiom keeps that connection visible.

**The Clause That Carries the Verse**

The second half is the one the verse exists for: *wa-anna Allāha laysa bi-ẓallāmin li-l-ʿabīd* — and God is not unjust to His servants.

Note what is being denied. Not that the punishment is severe — the previous verse called it burning. What is denied is that it is *unearned*. The sentence is embedded in the announcement of the sentence, said at the worst possible moment for the person hearing it, and it is said first-person plural by the One pronouncing it.

The Qur'an's insistence on this point at the moment of punishment is not defensive. It is the answer to the objection that every punished person makes, and the Qur'an records the objection being made elsewhere — precisely so that the answer can be seen to address it.

**Why the Record Is Outside the Person**

The machinery behind "what your hands sent ahead" is the record, and the Qur'an is unusually concrete about it. *“And We have everything recorded precisely”* (78:29). At the judgment the record is produced as an object: *“Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself”* (17:14).

The external record is what makes the second clause of this verse meaningful. If judgment were a matter of divine recollection, the claim of justice would rest on the reliability of a memory; the Qur'an instead places the account outside both parties, legible to the accused, and has him read it himself. Sūrah al-Kahf describes the moment: *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, ‘Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?’”* (18:49).

The person holding the record is the only one who can dispute its contents, and the verse reports what he says instead: not a denial, but an admission of how complete it is.

**"Servants" — the Word That Carries the Argument**

The final word is *li-l-ʿabīd*, to the servants. Not to people, not to humanity, not to creatures: to those who belong to Him.

The choice is an argument in itself, and the classical theologians took it that way. Injustice is not a random defect; it has motives. A person wrongs another because he wants what the other has, or because he fears him, or because he does not know what he is doing. None of the three can apply to the Owner of everything toward what He already possesses. Wrongdoing requires a lack, and the one lacking does not wrong; the one who lacks nothing has no reason to.

Stated in that form, the claim is not consoling poetry but a deduction from the divine attribute the sūrah has already asserted three times over in verse 6 — that God is the Truth, that He gives life, that He has power over everything. A being of that description has no deficit for injustice to fill.

The word also protects the verse against its own misuse. Saying that God is never unjust to His servants is not saying that whatever happens to a person is fair by definition; it is saying that the verdict in front of him was produced by his own shipment, examined against his own record, and read out by his own account. The servanthood is what makes the justice legible rather than merely asserted.

**The Two Halves Together**

Put the two halves side by side and the verse is a complete theory of punishment in one line. The first half says that what is happening to this person was authored by him — the shipment is his. The second half says that the one inflicting it owes him no grievance. Responsibility and justice, stated in that order, and stated to his face.
