## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:93

> And whoever kills a believer intentionally, their reward will be Hell— where they will stay indefinitely. Allah will be displeased with them, condemn them, and will prepare for them a tremendous punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Whoever Kills a Believer Intentionally**

The verse states *wa man yaqtul muʾminan mutaʿammidan fa jazāʾuhū Jahannam khālidan fīhā* — and whoever kills a believer intentionally, their reward, requital, will be Hell where they will stay indefinitely. *Mutaʿammidan* — intentionally, deliberately, with determination — was explained as purposeful killing with knowledge that the victim is a believer and with a lethal means, the opposite of the mistake — *khaṭaʾ* — whose remedy was freeing, blood-money, or fasting (4:92). The sūrah has moved by deliberate steps: hostility is curbed by fighting for the oppressed (4:75), the believer fights for God and the schemes of the Devil are weak (4:76), permission is given over the hostile turners who do not withdraw or offer peace (4:89–91) with clear warrant, then that permission is bounded by the prohibition that a believer does not kill a believer except by mistake (4:92). This verse sets the far limit of that bound: intentional killing of a believer is not a mistake to be repaired but a crime whose requital is named in the same language as Paradise for the obedient — *jazāʾ* — reward. Where obedience brought Gardens forever (4:13), intentional killing brings Hell abiding. Early authorities stressed that the verse pairs intentional killing with associating others with God in several passages: *“those who do not call upon another god with God, nor kill a soul God made unlawful except with right”* (25:68), showing the gravity placed upon it.

**Four Threats Together — Hell, Anger, Curse, and Mighty Torment**

The verse joins four: *wa ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhi wa laʿanahū wa aʿadda lahū ʿadhāban ʿaẓīmā* — Allah will be displeased with them, be angry with them, condemn them, curse them, remove them from mercy, and will prepare for them a tremendous, mighty punishment. *Ghaḍab* — anger — was explained as divine displeasure at the violation of the sanctity established from a single soul (4:1) and from *“do not kill yourselves, God is ever Merciful to you”* (4:29). *Laʿnah* — curse — was read as expulsion from mercy, the same word used for those condemned for disbelief who rejected trust (4:52) and for Satan cursed by God (4:118). *Aʿadda* — He has prepared — matches the preparation language elsewhere for punishment: as Paradise was prepared for those who believe and do good (4:57), so a tremendous torment is prepared here. Classical commentators noted that this is one of the few verses where four terrors are gathered for a single sin: abiding in Hell, anger, curse, and mighty torment. The sternness carries a report: Ibn ʿAbbās was asked whether repentance avails the intentional killer, and he held that this verse was among the last revealed on the subject and nothing abrogated it (Bukhārī, Muslim), while the majority understood that repentance remains possible for the sincere because God forgives what is less than association if He wills (4:48) and accepts repentance soon after error (4:17), yet the threat remains severe to block the hand. The sūrah’s balance is therefore not softened: the warning is left as stated.

**Abiding Indefinitely — Khālidan Fīhā**

The phrase *khālidan fīhā* — abiding therein indefinitely — was explained with attention to *khulūd* — remaining, abiding. Some read it as long abiding without end for the one who deems the killing lawful, making it akin to disbelief in sanctity; others read it as the recompense deserved, with ultimate return to divine forgiveness still under *“He forgives anything else of whoever He wills”* (4:48) if repentance intervenes. The sūrah says elsewhere *“those who reject Our signs We will cast into Fire, whenever skins are burnt We replace them”* (4:56), joining abiding with renewal of torment; this verse joins abiding with anger and curse for killing a believer. The point for the community is not to settle the unseen calendar but to see that the threat has no light edge: the intentional stripping of a believer’s life that God tied to a single soul (4:1) is met with abiding, anger, curse, and mighty preparation. Where mistaken killing was met with freeing, blood-money, treaty fidelity, and consecutive fasting as repentance accepted (4:92), intentional killing is met with no such procedural remedy in this verse; the remedy is elsewhere in sincere repentance that must answer four terrors.

**A Verse That Closes the Permission Given Over Hostility**

Placed after the clear warrant over the double-faced (4:91) and the detailed law for mistake (4:92), this verse closes the permission precisely where overreach would be most dangerous: on the battlefield where a believer might be mistaken, or in the city where a believer might be envied. The sūrah has shown that envy moved those given a portion of the Book to wish you deviate (4:54) and to trade guidance for misguidance (4:44); if envy or anger were allowed to move a hand to kill a believer intentionally, the claim would be the highest misguidance. The sūrah’s earlier promise — *“whoever obeys God and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed: the prophets, truthful, martyrs, and righteous”* (4:69) — is inverted here: whoever kills a believer intentionally will be in the company of those under anger and curse, with a mighty torment prepared. For the weak for whom burdens were lightened (4:28), the verse lightens by removing ambiguity: permission to seize the hostile turner (4:89) and the double-seeking who refuse peace (4:91) is not permission over a believer; and the atonement that suffices for mistake (4:92) does not suffice for purpose. The next verse will turn to the search for gain on that same field, warning against calling to a seeker of peace “you are no believer” for fleeting worldly gain (4:94).
