## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:25

> Pharaoh said to those around him, “Did you hear ˹what he said˺?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pharaoh Turns the Court Into an Audience for Ridicule**

Moses has answered with a claim about the whole created order. Pharaoh does not address the claim directly. He turns to the people around him and asks whether they heard what was said. The question is staged for effect. It invites the court to share an expression of disbelief and to treat Moses' answer as an amusing disturbance rather than a demand that requires judgment.

The ruler's movement toward his audience shows how power works in public. A leader does not need to refute a claim if he can control the emotional response surrounding it. By making the chiefs witnesses to his mockery, Pharaoh seeks to establish the proper reaction before they have time to weigh the words. The court is instructed by laughter and amazement, not by evidence.

**Mockery Replaces an Answer**

Pharaoh's question contains no argument about the heavens, the earth, or the meaning of lordship. It signals that he has chosen performance over reply. A speaker can use ridicule when a claim threatens a settled position, because a joke may make the claim feel socially unsafe to consider. The audience then learns that agreement with the messenger would mean joining the target of the joke.

This tactic is different from honest disagreement. An honest critic identifies a premise, offers a reason, and accepts the possibility of correction. Pharaoh instead asks his courtiers to react to Moses' words as though their strangeness were already a refutation. The court is not being invited into inquiry; it is being recruited into a social defense of the ruler's authority.

**The Question Polices the Court's Loyalty**

Those around Pharaoh are not neutral listeners. Their position depends on the ruler, and his question tests whether they will mirror his response. Did you hear what he said carries an unspoken command: hear it as absurd, and show that you belong to the side of Pharaoh. The court's members must decide whether loyalty means repeating the ruler's performance or facing the truth that performance avoids.

This is why tyranny needs an audience. Power is strengthened when people laugh at the same target, repeat the same accusation, and treat the ruler's interpretation as common sense. The question turns private hesitation into public conformity. A courtier may have recognized the force of Moses' answer, but the ruler's framing makes recognition costly. Public mockery can therefore function as a form of coercion even before a formal threat is spoken.

**A Ruler Can Make Surprise Look Like Superiority**

Pharaoh's question also displays amazement. He acts as though Moses has said something so strange that the proper response is incredulity. Yet surprise does not prove that a claim is false. A familiar worldview can make a true statement feel absurd when the statement exposes its limits. Pharaoh's court is accustomed to treating the king as the highest authority; a Lord above all worlds sounds disruptive precisely because it is disruptive.

The scene teaches caution about the language of common sense. What a powerful group calls ridiculous may be the first clear description of its own dependence. A society can normalize a falsehood until truth sounds foreign. Moses' answer does not become less sound because Pharaoh performs shock. The performance reveals the court's investment in its old assumptions.

**The Audience Is Pressured Before It Is Informed**

Pharaoh's sequence matters. He first asks the chiefs to react, and only afterward will he continue the exchange. This order puts social pressure ahead of evidence. Once the court has been invited to mock Moses, any later acknowledgment of truth may look like disloyalty. The ruler is trying to settle the audience's posture before the content of the claim can be considered.

Such pressure appears whenever institutions protect themselves by ridiculing dissenters. The target is not only the speaker. It is everyone who might be willing to listen. The question tells the silent members of the court that they are being watched. Their task is to share the ruler's reaction, not to judge his reasoning. Moses stands before a political theater in which the audience itself is part of the test.

**Silence Can Be a Form of Complicity**

The verse does not record what the people around Pharaoh thought. Their silence may have reflected fear, agreement, or calculation. The omission keeps attention on the ruler's tactic, but it also leaves the courtiers responsible for their response. A person does not become innocent merely because he did not speak the original mockery. He may still strengthen it by laughing, applauding, or remaining silent when silence protects harm.

That does not mean every frightened subject can be judged as though he possessed Pharaoh's power. Responsibility follows knowledge and ability. The court, however, holds influence and is being asked to help define truth for the kingdom. Its members must eventually answer whether they used that influence to serve justice or to protect the ruler's performance.

**A Public Claim Deserves More Than a Public Reaction**

Moses' words concern the Lord of all worlds, so Pharaoh's reaction cannot settle their truth. The question to the court is about reception, not evidence. The verse therefore separates popularity from validity. A room can be made to laugh at a statement without disproving it, and a ruler can make a crowd feel united without making his authority legitimate.

The proper response to such a scene is disciplined attention. Ask what was claimed, what reason answered it, and what the mockery is trying to prevent the audience from seeing. Pharaoh's question shows that a power threatened by truth may attack the social conditions in which truth could be heard. The next exchange will continue that contest over who has the right to define reality.
