## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:86

> He replied, “I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to Allah, and I know from Allah what you do not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Complaint Is Redirected, Not Withdrawn**

Jacob does not defend his grief, explain it, or promise to reduce it. He states where it goes. The rebuke in verse 85 predicted that his remembrance of Joseph would end in collapse or death; the answer accepts the description and changes the addressee. *Innamā ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā Llāh* — I complain of my anguish and my sorrow only to God. The particle carries the sense of restriction: to God alone, not to you, not to anyone. Nothing is suppressed by the restriction. The grief continues at the intensity his sons have described; what changes is the audience for it, and the change is the whole of the verse's moral content. Changing the audience is the only change available to him. Jacob cannot stop grieving, cannot undo twenty years and cannot compel his sons to believe him; what he can decide is where the grief is spoken. The verse treats that decision as an act rather than a mood, and it is the act that keeps his patience from turning into silence.

**Two Words for a Single Condition**

The verse names two things: *bathth* and *ḥuzn*. The first denotes the innermost distress that a person cannot keep inside and pours out — the grief that escapes, the burden too heavy to carry silently. The second is grief in its ordinary sense, sorrow as a settled state. The pairing is precise and it describes the situation exactly. Jacob has been described in verse 84 as *kaẓīm*, one who holds sorrow in; here he names what is held and what escapes. A person whose eyes have turned white from weeping has both. The chapter gives him two nouns rather than one because one would not cover the case, and because the complaint he makes to God includes the part he has not been able to contain.

**Beautiful Patience, Now Defined**

The commentators connect this verse directly to the phrase Jacob used twice before. Beautiful patience (12:18, 12:83) is understood as patience in which a person complains only to God about his suffering and not to other human beings. Verse 86 is therefore the definition of the term rather than a separate statement. The three verses form a single account of a practice: the phrase is declared at the first loss, declared again at the second, and its content is stated when the practice is challenged. The chapter does not present the practice as an absence of feeling. It presents it as a direction — sorrow of a particular intensity, addressed to a particular recipient. That definition rescues patience from stoicism. A sorrow that is intense, acknowledged and addressed is not a weaker form of endurance than a sorrow that is hidden; on this account it is the harder one, because it requires the grieving person to keep speaking to God while receiving nothing in reply. Jacob has been doing exactly that for twenty years.

**Complaining to God Is Not a Failure of Patience**

The verse is careful about this. Jacob complains, and complaining is not treated as a lapse. The restriction is on the audience, not on the act. A reader who expected the chapter's ideal of patience to be silence will not find it here; the ideal is speech confined to the one listener who can do something about it. The distinction matters for how the sūrah understands endurance generally. Joseph's restraint in verse 77 was silence in front of men who would misuse what he said. Jacob's restraint here is speech directed away from men who can only rebuke him for it. Both are forms of the same discipline, and neither is numbness. The distinction protects the reader from a cheap moral. Patience is not the absence of complaint but the direction of it, and a man who complains only to God is doing something harder than a man who complains to nobody, because he is still speaking. The chapter honours both the speech and its addressee and refuses to require the suppression of either.

**"I Know From God What You Do Not Know"**

The second clause is a claim to knowledge, and it is made to the people who have just predicted his death. The commentators read it as Jacob's intuitive knowledge that Joseph was still alive — a conviction held without evidence and against a report he had accepted for decades. The claim is unusual in form. He does not say he hopes, or suspects, or wishes; he says he knows, and he attributes the knowing to God rather than to reasoning. Verse 83's hope — that God will return them all — is restated here as knowledge, and the restatement is what separates Jacob from his sons in the chapter's economy of understanding. The separation is epistemic before it is emotional. His sons have evidence — a bloodied shirt, a caravan, a decade of absence — and their conclusion follows from it; Jacob has knowledge whose source he names and whose content he does not explain. The chapter does not adjudicate between the two positions here, and verse 94 settles the matter without argument.

**The Grounds of the Claim Are Reported but Not Stated**

The commentators offer two explanations for the conviction. One holds that Jacob saw in a dream that Joseph was alive. The other reports that he was informed by the Angel of Death that his soul had not been taken. Neither account is in the Qur'an and neither can be verified from it; the text states the knowledge without stating its source, which is consistent with the way the chapter handles prophetic knowledge elsewhere. Joseph's interpretations are described as taught by God (12:21) without the teaching being narrated. Jacob's conviction is described as knowledge from God without its transmission being narrated. The sūrah's method is to report the fact of the knowledge and leave its mechanism aside.

**Verse 96 Confirms the Claim in Jacob's Own Words**

When the shirt is cast over his face and his sight returns, Jacob says to his children: *“Did I not tell you that I truly know from Allah what you do not know?”* (12:96). The repetition is the chapter's verification. The claim made in verse 86 — against a rebuke, during a famine, with two sons missing and a third stranded — is restated after the first of the three has been restored. Nothing in verse 86 argued for the claim or offered evidence for it. Its vindication arrives ten verses later in the form of a recovered son and a father's reminder that he had said so. The chapter's structure is the argument, and it is the only argument the verse offers.

**Sorrow Sent Where It Can Be Received**

The verse distinguishes between two audiences for suffering, and the distinction is the practical content of Jacob's faith. His sons can observe his grief, predict its endpoint and ask him to reduce it. That is the whole of what they can do. They cannot find Joseph, cannot release Benjamin, cannot restore his sight, and cannot tell him whether his conviction is founded. God, on Jacob's account, can do all of these, and knows whether the conviction is founded. The complaint is therefore not an escape from his family but a routing decision: the sorrow goes to the only listener who can act on it. The chapter supports the decision by what happens next — a commission in verse 87, a journey, a disclosure in verse 90, a shirt in verse 93 and a reunion in verse 99. Nothing in that sequence would have begun with the sons' rebuke, and everything in it follows from the redirection made here.
