## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:56

> but we are all on the alert.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pharaoh Reassures the Court With Military Readiness**

Pharaoh describes his people as a host on the alert. The statement is meant to answer the smallness of the Israelites with the strength of Egypt. A handful may flee, he implies, but the kingdom is prepared to respond. The language turns military readiness into reassurance, asking the court and the cities to feel secure because the ruler has forces, equipment, and command.

The claim is not only a report about soldiers. It is part of the ruler's rhetoric. Pharaoh wants the people to imagine that the outcome is already settled by the balance of power. If Egypt is ready, then pursuit appears reasonable and victory appears certain. The statement encourages the host to trust its preparation rather than question the justice of the order it has been given.

**Alert Means Equipped as Well as Watchful**

The Arabic term can suggest readiness, preparedness, and being equipped. The host is not merely awake. It is organized to move, supplied for pursuit, and confident in its ability to act. That detail explains why Pharaoh can speak so boldly about recovering the Israelites. His confidence rests on a material system that appears overwhelming compared with the departing community.

Readiness, however, has two dimensions. A force can be ready to move and unready to judge. Pharaoh's army may possess weapons and mounts, but it has not been prepared to ask whether the pursuit is just. The verse separates operational skill from moral wisdom. An equipped host can carry out a wrong command more efficiently than an unprepared one.

**The Word All Expands Pharaoh's Claim**

Pharaoh says we are all on the alert. The word all presents the court as united. It leaves no space for hesitation, dissent, or a conscientious official who might ask whether the chase is necessary. A ruler often strengthens a command by describing the whole community as already committed to it. Anyone who questions the pursuit can then be made to look like an outsider.

The claim may also be exaggerated. A leader who speaks for everyone can create the unity he wants people to believe already exists. Some may be afraid, some indifferent, and some opposed. Pharaoh's language erases those distinctions so that the pursuit appears to carry collective consent. The people are made responsible for a decision that began in the ruler's refusal.

**Military Confidence Conceals Spiritual Weakness**

The host is ready in the worldly sense, but it is not ready for what God has decreed. Pharaoh's court measures the coming encounter by numbers, equipment, and speed. Divine planning operates on another level. The host can be organized perfectly and still be unable to prevent the fulfillment of God's promise to the Israelites.

This contrast does not make preparation worthless. The Qur'an does not praise carelessness. It exposes the error of treating preparation as sovereignty. A force can prepare means, but it cannot command outcomes that belong to God. Pharaoh's readiness will make the eventual defeat more revealing because it cannot be blamed on disorder or lack of resources.

**The Host Is Ready to Protect a False Possession**

What is the army ready to defend? Not a city under attack or a people facing invasion, but Pharaoh's claim over servants who have been told to leave. Military language makes ownership sound like national security. The host is described as alert because the ruler has taught it to see freedom as a threat.

This is one of the most dangerous uses of force. Soldiers may think they are protecting the community while they are actually preserving a ruler's control over a vulnerable group. The moral question is therefore prior to the command: what is being defended, and who benefits? Pharaoh's words answer with confidence but not with justice. The host's readiness cannot make the claim legitimate.

**A Ready Host Can Still Be Morally Asleep**

Alertness to an enemy is not the same as consciousness of God. Pharaoh's people may watch roads, prepare weapons, and respond quickly while remaining blind to the Lord's command. The verse's irony lies in that contrast. They are awake to the movement of the Israelites and asleep to the meaning of the signs that led to the movement.

Taqwā would have made them guard themselves from injustice. Pharaoh's alertness instead guards Pharaoh's pride. The difference is not in the amount of attention but in what attention serves. A person can be highly vigilant in protecting status and deeply heedless about the rights of others.

**Confidence in Force Cannot Replace Truth**

The statement ends the ruler's public preparation with assurance, but it does not end the moral issue. Pharaoh has a ready host; Moses has a divine command. The coming pursuit will show that material advantage cannot decide which command is right. A ruler may count soldiers, but God counts deeds and hears the cry of the oppressed.

The verse therefore asks the audience to distrust confidence that rests only on force. Readiness can protect the weak when governed by justice, but it becomes a danger when used to recover people whom the ruler has no right to own. Pharaoh's host is alert. The question is whether it is ready to meet the judgment of the Lord it has refused.

The phrase also strips military readiness of its power to reassure the believer. A host may be prepared for battle and still be moving toward defeat; a community may look exposed and still be under divine protection. The appearance of readiness belongs to the army. The final command over events belongs to God. That difference is the boundary Pharaoh's confidence cannot cross. A ready army is still a dependent army.
