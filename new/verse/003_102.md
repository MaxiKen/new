## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:102

> O believers! Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves, and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command With a Measure Built In**

The verse calls on the believers to be mindful of God in the way He deserves, and then tells them not to die except in a state of submission. The first command has a measure attached to it, and the measure is the word that means a right or what is due.

Al-Ṭabarī explained the phrase as describing a mindfulness in which God is obeyed and not defied, remembered and not forgotten, thanked and not denied. The formulation answers the question a person naturally asks when told to give God what He deserves, since the answer is not a quantity but a way of living. The same root appears elsewhere in the Book in the rebuke that people have not shown God His proper reverence, a criticism attached to those who denied that He had revealed anything to a human being (6:91).

**The Command That Seemed Too Heavy**

The community's first reaction to the verse is part of its history. Reports record that the Companions found the command difficult and were troubled by it, since no one could claim to have given God everything He deserves, and some then suggested that the command was lightened by another verse that tells the believers to be mindful of God as far as they are able (64:16).

The reasoning is not that the earlier command was cancelled but that the two verses explain each other. The same Book states that God does not require of any soul more than it can bear (2:286), so a command to give God His due cannot mean a demand beyond human capacity. Al-Rāzī drew the conclusion directly: to be mindful of God as He deserves is to be mindful of Him to the extent of one's ability, because the Book has ruled out the impossible.

**The Two Commands Belong Together**

The verse's second half asks that a person not die except in submission, and the two commands work as a pair. The first concerns the whole of life, and the second concerns the moment it ends.

The Book uses the same combination in the advice Abraham and Jacob gave their children, where the dying clause appears in almost identical wording after the statement that God has chosen the faith for them (2:132). In both passages, the point is not to arrange a particular death but to live in a way that keeps the ending what it should be. A person has no power over the moment or the manner of his death, and the command is directed at the part he does control.

**Reverence and Measurement**

The word for reverence in this verse shares its root with the word for measuring, and the Book plays on the two senses when it rebukes those who fail to measure God as He should be measured. The rebuke appears in three places with almost the same formula, and in each case the context is the denial of revelation or of God's power.

The verse before the reader turns the phrase into a positive command. The people who denied that God had spoken to humanity were accused of misjudging Him; the believers are told to give Him the estimation He is due. That the command is framed in the language of measure shows what is at stake: the failure in view is not a lapse in feeling but a mistaken assessment of who is being addressed.

**Why the Verse Mentions Death**

A command about the state in which one dies is a command about persistence, since nobody can guarantee the end of his life. The Book states the requirement in this form because it wants the believer to connect the present moment with the final one.

The sūrah has just warned its community against being drawn back from belief, and the warning is given urgency by being attached to death. Between guidance and the end of life there is a distance whose length nobody knows, and the verse's second command fixes the direction to be maintained across however many days that distance contains. The wording also places the whole matter under God, since the One who demands the state of submission is the One who determines when it is sealed.

**The Discussion of Whether the Command Was Eased**

The reports about the community's anxiety connect this verse with a wider discussion about commands that appear to exceed human capacity, and the commentators treated the question carefully. Some argued that one verse abrogated the other; others replied that commands about God's own attributes and about the Hereafter are not subject to abrogation, and that the two statements are complementary.

Al-Rāzī's resolution is the one that fits both texts without appeal to cancellation: the believer is asked to give God His due, and the due is measured by capacity because the Book has said that no soul is tasked beyond what it can bear. The anxiety the reports describe is itself instructive, since it shows the first generation treating the verse with the seriousness the demand deserves rather than assuming that a general command costs nothing.

**The Verse in the Community's Practice**

The sentence about dying in submission became a phrase Muslims repeat and hear in the funeral prayer and at moments of loss, and the verse that carries it is one of the earliest commands the community learned to apply to the whole span of life.

What it asks for is durable rather than dramatic: a mindfulness measured by capacity, sustained over years, and maintained until the end. The sūrah's passage continues with a command to hold together and a reminder of what God did for a divided people, and those instructions belong with this one, since a community cannot maintain the state the verse describes while it is coming apart.
