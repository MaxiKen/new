## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:20

> Moses replied, “I did it then, lacking guidance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses Answers Without Denying the Deed**

Moses does not pretend that Pharaoh has invented the incident. He says that he did it. The admission is brief, but it prevents the defense from becoming a contest of propaganda. A servant of God does not need to protect his reputation by calling a known wrong something else. Truth begins with refusing to falsify the past.

At the same time, the response does not accept Pharaoh's entire interpretation. Moses does not say that the deed proves he is an ingrate or that the palace owns his future. He separates the fact of the act from the accusation built around it. This is a model of moral speech: confess what happened, reject the unjust conclusion drawn from it, and leave the matter under God's judgment rather than hiding it behind clever language.

**Lacking Guidance Describes a Moment of Error**

The translation's lacking guidance renders a phrase that can also be understood as being among those who were astray or not yet guided to the right course. Moses is not claiming that he deliberately chose disbelief. The setting in 28:15–16 indicates a moment in which he acted under confusion and then recognized the need for forgiveness. The wording presents the deed as an error of judgment before prophetic guidance had been given to him.

This nuance matters. Human beings can be wrong without every wrong being the same kind of rejection of truth. A person may act impulsively, misunderstand a situation, or intervene with an effect he did not intend. Such distinctions do not remove responsibility. They make responsibility more accurate. Moses' answer neither glorifies the act nor accepts Pharaoh's attempt to define his whole character by it.

**A Confession Can Be Stronger Than a Denial**

Pharaoh expects the charge to make Moses defensive. Moses instead uses a plain admission. Confession takes away the ruler's power to reveal the deed as though it were a secret discovery. It also prevents the mission from resting on a false image of the messenger. Divine truth does not need a servant who appears never to have erred; it needs a servant who speaks honestly about error and obeys after it.

That strength should not be confused with carelessness about sin. Admission is not an excuse. Moses does not say that the Egyptian's death was irrelevant because his intention was good. He acknowledges a state of lacking guidance and allows the event to stand as a matter that required divine mercy. His honesty is therefore joined to humility, not to self-congratulation.

**The Reply Refuses Pharaoh's Moral Trap**

Pharaoh has tried to force Moses into one of two positions: deny the deed and look dishonest, or accept the label of ungrateful rebel and surrender the mission. Moses chooses neither. He admits the past while refusing to let the ruler's language control its meaning. The deed was an error; it was not a license for Pharaoh to enslave a people or to claim lordship over God's messenger.

This is how a person should answer a manipulative accusation. Do not deny a real wrong merely because an enemy mentions it. Do not accept a false conclusion merely because it is attached to a true fact. Examine the connection between the fact and the judgment. Pharaoh's care of Moses, Moses' past act, and the Israelites' oppression are related in the ruler's speech, but they do not form the moral chain he claims.

**The Past Does Not Exhaust a Person's Present Duty**

Moses' phrase then marks a time: I did it then. The word does not erase continuity, but it distinguishes the earlier act from the present commission. A person is responsible for what he has done, yet repentance and divine guidance can make the later self answerable for a new course. If no change were possible, confession would have no purpose and guidance would be only a label for the already perfect.

The Qur'an frequently places human beings between past error and present return. Moses' history is especially important because he will confront a ruler who wants to freeze him in that history. The verse protects the possibility of transformation without offering cheap absolution. The old act remains part of the record; it no longer has the right to dictate the servant's obedience to God.

**Guidance Is Needed Before Power Can Be Used Well**

Moses had strength and a sense of justice before this encounter, but strength without guidance led to a disastrous result. The phrase therefore carries a lesson about good intentions. Wanting to defend the oppressed does not guarantee that one's action will be wise or proportionate. Moral energy needs knowledge, restraint, and direction from God.

That lesson applies beyond the palace. A person may oppose wrong and still cause new harm if he acts through anger or confusion. The answer is not indifference to injustice. It is the discipline of seeking guidance before acting and returning in repentance when action goes wrong. Moses' admission gives the mission a foundation more reliable than confidence in his own instincts.

**A Prophet's Authority Comes After Humble Correction**

Moses' admission also answers the idea that prophets must have a past untouched by human weakness. The Qur'an presents his authority as a gift and a commission, not as a reward for self-made perfection. His history magnifies the role of God's guidance. The messenger speaks truly because God has sent him, while his earlier error remains a reminder that he is a servant and not a second lord.

This humility will keep the coming confrontation from becoming a personal quarrel between two proud men. Pharaoh wants to make it about gratitude to the palace. Moses' answer returns it to the deeper matter: a human being once acted without right guidance, and God now commands him to speak for justice. The admission is the first step in refusing Pharaoh's frame.
