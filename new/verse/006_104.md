## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:104

> Indeed, there have come to you insights from your Lord. So whoever chooses to see, it is for their own good. But whoever chooses to be blind, it is to their own loss. And I am not a keeper over you.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Insights Given From the Lord**

Indeed, there have come to you insights from your Lord. The Book's word for what has come is a plural of perception or discernment, and the commentaries describe it as the clear evidences by which a person sees the truth of the message. The sūrah of the Story uses the same word for the Scripture given to Moses, calling it an insight for the people, a guide, and a mercy (28:43), and the sūrah of the Kneeling describes the Qur'an as an insight for humanity, a guide and mercy for people of sure faith (45:20).

The verse therefore describes the message as something the audience already possesses rather than as something still being offered. The signs have been recited, the argument has been made, and the insights have arrived. What the sentence does is place the responsibility for using them where it belongs, which is the subject of its next two clauses.

**Whoever Chooses to See**

So whoever chooses to see, it is for their own good. But whoever chooses to be blind, it is to their own loss. The two clauses use the language of sight and blindness, which the sūrah has already established in its earlier questions about whether the blind and the seeing can be equal (6:50). What the verse adds is the element of choice: the reader is described as the one who decides, and the consequences are attached to the decision rather than to the perception.

The Book states the same arrangement in several places. The sūrah of the Cave says that the message is the truth from the Lord and that whoever wills may believe and whoever wills may disbelieve (18:29); the sūrah of the Ant has the Prophet told to recite and then to say that whoever chooses guidance does so for his own good and that the messenger is only a warner (27:92); and the sūrah of the Troops says the same about guidance and straying (39:41). The formula protects the freedom that the Book's invitations presuppose.

**The Language of Benefit and Loss**

The verse attaches the outcome to the person who chooses, and the attachment is stated in the vocabulary of personal profit and damage. The Book's ethical framework regularly describes the consequences of conduct in these terms, since it presents the commands of God as instructions for the good of the people addressed rather than as requirements that serve Him. The sūrah of the Bee states that whoever does right does so for his own soul and whoever does wrong does so against it.

The two clauses in this verse give the same structure to belief and denial. Seeing benefits the person who sees, and blindness harms the person who closes his eyes. Nothing in the sentence suggests that either outcome is a matter of indifference to God; the whole sūrah has been describing the consequences that follow from each choice, and the verse states the direction in which the consequences fall.

**"I Am Not a Keeper Over You"**

And I am not a keeper over you. The sentence returns to a refrain the sūrah has used twice before. The Prophet was told to say that he is not a keeper over the people at the end of a passage about the truth that had come to him (6:66), and the same denial appears in the account of his own announcement that he follows what is revealed to him (6:50 context). The sūrah of Yūnus and the sūrah of the Troops attach the same statement to the message (10:108; 39:41).

The repetition is a feature of the sūrah's method rather than a lapse, and it marks the points at which the audience is being told that the messenger's role has limits. The insights have come from the Lord, the choice belongs to the person who hears them, and the man who delivers them is not responsible for the outcome. What the Book does with this recurring statement is to remove the pressure that the Prophet's grief over his people had placed on him.

**What the Verse Assumes About the Reader**

The sentence assumes that a person can see and can refuse to see, and it treats the refusal as an act rather than as a condition. The sūrah's account of the deniers has consistently described them in the vocabulary of activity: they turn away, they argue, they deny, they mock, and they take the message as play. The choice in this verse is the ordinary form of all those actions, and the Book's ethical seriousness depends on it.

The verse also assumes that the reader has received what he needs in order to decide. The insights have come, and the Book's invitation is not to a leap in the dark. That is why the sūrah can state the consequences so plainly, and why it leaves the choice with the person who hears.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse describes the varying of the signs and the accusation that will follow from it: the disbelievers will say that the Prophet has studied the earlier scriptures, and the Book will answer that the message has been made clear for people who know (6:105). The two verses belong together, since both concern the response of the people who were receiving the clearest possible presentation of the message.

The sūrah's structure in this section is worth noticing. It states the message, states the freedom of the reader, states the limit of the messenger's responsibility, and then describes what the audience did with all of it. The response recorded in the next verse is an accusation about the source of the revelation, which is the same ground the sūrah has been covering since its opening verses.
