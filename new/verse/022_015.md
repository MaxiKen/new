## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:15

> Whoever thinks that Allah will not help His Prophet in this world and the Hereafter, let them stretch out a rope to the ceiling and strangle themselves, then let them see if this plan will do away with ˹the cause of˺ their rage.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse With More Than One Door**

This is the most difficult verse in the passage, and the commentaries say so by disagreeing. The disagreements are worth setting out because they turn on identifiable features of the Arabic rather than on taste.

The first question is who the pronoun refers to. Most commentators take "will not help him" as referring to the Prophet ﷺ: whoever supposes that God will not give His Prophet victory in this world and the next. Others read it as referring to the presumptuous man himself — whoever thinks that God will not help *him*.

The second question is who is being addressed. Readings include the Prophet's enemies, who were enraged by his success; the tribes of Asad and Ghaṭafān, who expected him to fail; and, on one reading, a group of believers so impatient for victory against the idolaters that their frustration had curdled. The wording is general — *man kāna yaẓunnu*, whoever thinks — and generality is what allows the readings to coincide.

**"A Rope to the Sky"**

The third question is the image. The Arabic is *fal-yamdud bi-sababin ilā al-samāʾ* — let him stretch out a rope to the *samāʾ*. That word means sky, heaven, or, in ordinary speech, the ceiling of a room. Both senses are live, and both were used by the commentators.

On the ceiling reading, favoured by the tafsīr attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and followed by many others, the picture is a man in his house fixing a rope overhead. On the sky reading, he stretches a rope upward to reach the place the help comes from and cut it off. A third reading takes *sabab* in its extended sense of a means or a stratagem rather than a physical rope: let him use whatever means he can devise, reach as high as he can, and try.

The verb that follows, *thumma l-yaqṭaʿ* — then let him cut — is what makes the ceiling reading the more natural one. A man who has stretched a rope above him and then severs something is most simply read as having cut his own breath off. Yusuf ʿAlī's note on the two renderings puts the point well: on the ceiling reading the enemies are told to choke with rage; on the sky reading they are told to climb up and try to intercept the help, which would make them fools.

**What the Verse Is Not Saying**

Some readings have taken the verse as licensing despair, and that misreading needs to be closed off explicitly. The verse is not a command, and it is not advice. It is a *reductio*, delivered in the imperative mood the way a person says "go ahead, then" — the form of an instruction used to expose the futility of the thing instructed.

Nothing here permits suicide, which the Qur'an forbids in the plainest terms elsewhere. The force of the sentence depends on the absurdity of what it describes: a man so consumed that he would hang himself to stop being angry, and even that not working. The absurdity is the argument. If the extreme remedy fails, no lesser scheme will succeed.

**Rage Is the Thing Being Diagnosed**

The last clause names the condition: *fa-l-yanẓur hal yudhhibanna kayduhu mā yaghīẓ* — let him see whether his scheme removes what enrages him. The word is *ghayẓ*, the hot anger that a person cannot act on, and the verse treats it as the illness rather than the symptom.

This is where the verse connects to what precedes it. Verse 11 described a man whose religion collapsed under a trial; verse 13 described a man whose patron was worthless; verse 15 describes a man who is furious, and the fury is about God's help going to someone else. The sūrah is tracing a single condition through several faces, and the face here is resentment — the conviction that God is backing the wrong side, or backing nobody, and that the speaker has been cheated.

Theological resentment has a signature, and the verse names it precisely: the sufferer believes that if he could just reach the source and cut the line, he would feel better. The sūrah's response is to grant the attempt and ask him to check the result. God's relationship with His Prophet will be what it is, in this world and the next, and no rope will alter it.

**Despair Is the Thread That Ties the Passage Together**

Read as a group, verses 11 to 15 form one argument about what happens when a person's faith is conditional on outcomes. The man on the brink wanted benefits. The man with the idols wanted a patron who delivers. The man here wants a different result and is enraged that he cannot get it.

What they share is a religion that is a transaction. Each one has decided in advance what God owes him, and each one is undone when the debt is not paid on schedule. The sūrah's answer to all three is the same, and it is not comfort: the help of God is not a negotiable item, and the correct response to not receiving it on your timetable is not rage, not desertion, and not a rope.

Verse 16 supplies the other half of the answer immediately. The revelation comes down as clear signs, and God guides whom He wills — which means the outcome the resentful man is contesting was never a matter of his approval in the first place.
