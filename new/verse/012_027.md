## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:27

> But if it is torn from the back, then she has lied and he is truthful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Argument Deliberately Cut in Two**

The witness's proposal is a single conditional pair, and the Qur'an divides it across two numbered verses, giving the unfavourable branch a verse of its own. Verse division in this chapter is not metrical padding; it controls pace. Splitting the test at its hinge forces a pause exactly where the outcome turns, so that the reader arrives at the second branch having already registered the first. In recitation the effect is stronger, since the two verses are recited as two units and the listener hears the reversal land as a separate event. A single verse containing both branches would have read as a technical rule. Two verses make it a verdict, and the second one carries the weight of a judgement pronounced in court.

**The Direction of the Tear Is a Fact About Motion**

The reasoning inside the branch is physical and can be stated without reference to anyone's character. A garment rips where force is applied against resistance. If a man approaches and a woman pushes him away, her hands work at his front and the cloth fails there. If a man leaves and a woman grabs him, her hands work at his back and the cloth fails behind him. The tear records which of the two was moving away. Nothing in the inference depends on testimony, on status or on credibility; it depends on the mechanics of a struggle, and the mechanics are the same in every household. The commentators present the point in exactly these terms, and the Qur'an's decision to put the rule in the mouth of a witness rather than in the voice of the narrator makes it a piece of human reasoning that the text endorses rather than a revelation the characters must accept.

**Two Verbs, Two Persons, One Sentence**

The branch is stated with maximum compression: *she has lied, and he is truthful*. The woman is assigned the verb of falsehood; the man is assigned the quality of truth. The asymmetry between the two halves is worth noting. She is described by an act she performed — she lied — while he is described by a standing attribute — he is a truthful person. A lie is an event; truthfulness is a character. The witness therefore does more than settle the case. He characterises both parties, and in doing so he hands Joseph a description that the rest of the chapter will keep returning to. The formula also has the shape of a legal finding, the kind a judge pronounces when the evidence closes: this claim fails, that party is reliable.

**A Test Anyone in the Room Could Have Applied**

No expertise is required to look at a shirt. The witness proposes a procedure that the husband, the servants, the woman herself and Joseph could each perform, and whose result none of them could dispute afterwards. That property is what makes the verse important beyond the scene. An adjudication that depends on a specialist or on an authority can be captured by whoever controls the specialist. An adjudication that depends on an object in plain view cannot. The chapter returns to this idea at its climax, when Joseph refuses release until the king examines the case of the women who cut their hands (12:50) — again insisting on a public inspection of facts rather than a private assertion of innocence. Joseph's method throughout is to move disputes onto ground where evidence, not rank, decides.

**"He Is Truthful" Is a Description the Chapter Will Reuse**

The word applied to Joseph here returns twice, both times in the mouths of people who had no reason to favour him. The cupbearer, sent back to a prison he had left years earlier, addresses him with it: *“Joseph, O man of truth!”* (12:46). The ʿAzīz's wife, confessing to the king with nothing left to protect, closes her statement with it: *“Now the truth has come to light. It was I who tried to seduce him, and he is surely truthful”* (12:51). Three attestations, from a family witness, a fellow prisoner and the woman who accused him, spanning years and separated by every difference of interest. The chapter is assembling a record. By the time Joseph is released, his truthfulness is not his own claim but a fact established by hostile and indifferent witnesses, which is the only form such a claim can usefully take.

**The Accuser Becomes the Accused in Fifteen Words**

Verse 25 gave the woman the initiative: she spoke first, framed the question, proposed the penalties. Verse 27 removes all of it. The branch that applies is the one in which she lied, and the man she prosecuted is described as truthful. No further argument is offered and none is needed, because the next verse reports only that the husband looked at the shirt. The reversal is total and it happens without anyone raising their voice. This is the sūrah's characteristic movement — a position built elaborately over several verses and then dismantled by a single detail — and it is the first time in the Egyptian section that the movement runs in Joseph's favour.

The branch requires very little, and that is the point. The witness does not interrogate the woman, does not ask Joseph to swear, does not call servants or search the house. He proposes one observation and two outcomes, and the case ends when the observation is made. The Qur'an's interest in the episode is therefore not in the drama of the accusation but in the sufficiency of the evidence. A household that had every structural reason to convict an innocent slave is prevented from doing so by a tear in a garment, and the prevention works because the test was proposed before the inspection rather than after it. Procedure, not goodwill, is what saves him.

The husband now states his own conclusion.
