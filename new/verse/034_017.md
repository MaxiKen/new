## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:17

> This is how We rewarded them for their ingratitude. Would We ever punish ˹anyone in such a way˺ except the ungrateful?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict Attached to the Event**

The flood and the bitter orchards have been described; this verse supplies the accounting. *Dhālika jazaynāhum bimā kafarū* — that is how We repaid them for their *kufr*. The verb *jazā* is the word for settling a debt or paying a wage, the same root behind *jazāʾ*, recompense, which the sūrah used earlier for the reward of the believers (34:4). Applying a commercial verb to a natural disaster is the verse's first claim: what happened at Maʾrib was not weather. It was a settlement. This matters because the alternative reading was available and attractive. A dam fails, engineers examine the masonry, and the event closes as a technical failure with no meaning attached. The Qur'an does not deny the masonry. It insists that a sequence which begins with a two-word instruction, continues with turning away, and ends with the loss of exactly the thing that had been given is a sequence with an author.

**The Word Translated "Ingratitude"**

Everything in this verse turns on *kafarū* and *kafūr*, and the translation's choice of *ingratitude* rather than *disbelief* is exact for the context. The root *k-f-r* first means to cover something over — a farmer who buries seed is described with it, and night is said to *kafara* the daylight. From that comes the double sense the word carries throughout the Qur'an: covering a truth, and covering a favour. Sheba's offence is reported in the second sense. They are not shown denying a creator, arguing against a prophet, or setting up rivals; they are shown turning away from an instruction to be thankful. The verse then uses the intensive form *kafūr* in the closing question, which names not a single act of ingratitude but a settled disposition of it. The two verses immediately before this one make the reading unavoidable. The command was *be grateful* (34:15), the failure was turning away (34:16), and the charge here is the exact negative of the command.

**Ingratitude Presented as a Form of Unbelief**

Having said that, the verse does not let ingratitude remain a minor social fault. By using the word that elsewhere names outright rejection of God, it puts the two on a single scale. The logic is available in the Qur'an's own vocabulary: to cover a favour is already to cover the Giver, since a gift unacknowledged is a gift attributed to nobody, or to oneself. A people who eat from an irrigation system they did not devise the rains for, and who stop connecting the harvest to any source outside themselves, have made a theological claim with their behaviour rather than their speech. Al-Zumar puts the alternatives in exactly this pairing: *"Allah is truly not in need of you, nor does He approve of disbelief from His servants. But if you become grateful ˹through faith˺, He will appreciate that from you"* (39:7). Gratitude and *kufr* are set against each other there as the two available responses, with nothing in between.

**A Question That Is Really a Limit**

The second sentence is phrased as a question: *wa hal nujāzī illā al-kafūr* — do We requite any but the ungrateful? Arabic rhetorical questions of this shape function as emphatic negatives, so the sense is that nobody else is repaid in this manner. Early readers noted that the interrogative particle *hal* can itself carry negation in some constructions, which yields the same meaning by a different route, and that the verb admits a passive reading — *is any requited but the ungrateful* — with no change to the doctrine. What the question accomplishes is a restriction on the scope of the punishment just narrated. It reads as a threat, and it functions as a boundary: this treatment is reserved, not general. The distinction it draws is the one the Qur'an draws everywhere between the sins of those under mercy and the settled refusal of those who have covered the source. Believers sin and are forgiven; the *kafūr* is repaid.

**Why the Punishment Fitted the Offence So Precisely**

Look at what was actually done to Sheba against what other peoples in the Qur'an received. Some were drowned, some struck by a blast, some had the earth close over them, some had stones rain down (29:40). Sheba lost its irrigation. The punishment is drawn entirely from the material of the blessing — same valley, same water, same two plots, merely released from restraint and left to grow what it grows. A people who failed to notice the source of their provision were not destroyed by an unrelated catastrophe; they were shown what their land amounts to without the arrangement they had stopped crediting. This precision is what makes the event legible as a *jazāʾ* rather than a misfortune. The verse's opening word, *dhālika* — that, this is how — points back at the specific form of the loss, not merely at the fact of it.

**The Escape Route Left Open in the Wording**

Something in the closing question cuts the other way, and it is easy to miss under the severity. If this treatment falls only on the settled ingrate, then it is avoidable by the simplest possible means — not by heroism, not by wealth, not by lineage, but by acknowledging where things came from. The sūrah has already said the Lord in question is forgiving, in the very sentence that described the good land (34:15), and it placed that name there before any failure had occurred. Read together, the two verses frame Sheba's ruin as the outcome of an unused remedy. This also explains why the story is being told to Mecca at all. A city living off a trade route and a sanctuary, secure while others were raided, was in the identical structural position, and the point of the account is not that Sheba was uniquely wicked. The next verse returns to what they had been given, and shows that the orchards were only half of it.
