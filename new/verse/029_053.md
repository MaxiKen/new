## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:53

> They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the punishment. Had it not been for a time already set, the punishment would have certainly come to them ˹at once˺. But it will definitely take them by surprise when they least expect it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge That Recurs in the Book**

The demand for the punishment to be produced on the spot is one of the most frequently reported features of the disbelievers' speech in the Qur'an. They ask when the threat will come to pass if the message is true (10:48; 21:38), some of them go so far as to pray for stones to fall from the sky or for a painful punishment to overtake them if the claim is genuine (8:32), and the Book records the same attitude toward the Hour in Sūrah al-Shūrā, where those who disbelieve in it ask to hasten it mockingly while the believers are wary of it, knowing it to be the truth (42:18). The verse under discussion gathers this behaviour into one sentence and answers it, and the answer is not an argument but a fact about time.

The recurrence also tells the reader that the demand is not a feature of one audience's temperament. It reappears with the same logic in several chapters, which suggests a standing tendency in settled communities to measure a message by the speed of its results.

**What the Challenge Assumes About Delay**

The mockery rests on an inference the Book keeps exposing: that a punishment which has not arrived is a punishment that will not arrive. Behind it lies a picture of the messenger as a man making threats he cannot execute, and of the delay as evidence that the threats were hollow. The Book treats the inference as the central error of the heedless, and it states the correction in another chapter with unusual directness: if God punished people immediately for what they have done, He would not have left a single living being on the earth, but He delays them for an appointed term, and when the term arrives He sees His servants (35:45). Delay in this understanding is not incapacity; it is the space in which a person can still turn.

Behind the demand sits an assumption about what a delay means. People who take it as evidence that the warning is empty have already decided that a true message would produce an immediate consequence, and the sūrah's answer addresses that premise rather than the challenge itself.

**The Appointed Term and Its Uses in the Book**

The phrase rendered as a time already set appears throughout the Qur'an in several senses, and the commentators list them: the span of a human life, the term set for the sun and the moon, the appointed end of the heavens and the earth, the deadline for a debt, and the moment fixed for a punishment. Its use here belongs to the last category, and Sūrah Nūḥ makes the point in the voice of a prophet: God will forgive and delay a people's end until the appointed time, and when the time set by God comes it cannot be delayed, if only they knew (71:4). The term is therefore a fixed quantity in the Book's understanding of history, recorded before the events it governs, and it is not a quantity that the challenged party can influence by shouting.

**"Had It Not Been for a Term"**

The conditional in the middle of the verse explains why the mockery has produced no result. The punishment has not been withheld because of doubt about the situation or because the accusations made it weaker; it has not come because its date has not arrived. The sentence implies that the matter would already have been settled, and the implication is a rebuke rather than a reassurance. At the same time the Book treats the same delay as a mercy, and the sūrah made the point at its opening when it said that those who die while insisting on their course will answer for what they were doing. The interval between the crime and the answer is the space in which a person can still change his position.

The clause also answers the question of why anything lasts at all. If a judgment came the instant a community earned it, nobody would have an opportunity to change, and the term the verse names is the interval granted for exactly that purpose, which is why the Book speaks of such periods as a mercy.

**Surprise as the Method of Arrival**

The verse's last clause states that the punishment will take them suddenly while they are unaware, and the Book applies this description to the ruin of communities across several chapters. It reports the people who were seized by surprise after their complaints had been converted into plenty (7:95), and it says of a heedless people that their provisions were poured out until, at the moment they took pride in what they had been given, the seizure came upon them unawares (6:44). The pattern is not incidental. A warning that arrives on the day the warning was issued would function as a transaction; a warning that arrives on a different day tests whether the listener took the message seriously in the meantime.

The manner of arrival also disposes of any expectation that the sentence can be scheduled. People who ask for judgment in this life usually assume they will know when it is coming, and the Book answers with the image of an arrival that finds a town unprepared, which removes the interval they were counting on.

**Patience Commanded With a Fixed Routine**

The Book answers the demand for haste not with an argument about timing but with a schedule. The Prophet is told to be patient with what they say and to glorify his Lord before sunrise and before sunset, in the hours of the night, and at both ends of the day, with the promise that he will be pleased with the reward (20:130); the same opening command is repeated with a shorter list in another chapter (50:39). In each case the answer to provocation is a routine of worship rather than a rebuttal. The verse under discussion shows the demand for an early sentence being made; the passages about patience show what the Prophet was told to do while the demand stood unanswered. What the impatient were asking for would have ended the interval in which worship was still possible.

**How Believers Are Supposed to Read the Same Delay**

The verse is addressed to a Prophet who had been asked to speed up the judgment, and the correction applies to his own impatience as much as to the mockery of his opponents. The contrast drawn in Sūrah al-Shūrā is precise: those who disbelieve ask to hasten it, and the believers are afraid of it precisely because they know it is real (42:18). The two attitudes are separated by knowledge rather than by temperament. A person who is certain of an appointment does not request that it be moved forward, and the Book cites no instance of a prophet asking for the deadline to be shortened. What they ask for is deliverance, and what they receive is a schedule. Nor does the verse treat the mockery as a small matter, since the attitude it describes is the practical form of a settled decision: a person who jokes about the judgment has already placed himself outside the conversation the judgment is meant to provoke, and the sūrah has spent many verses describing what happens to communities that reached that stage. The Prophet is not told to answer the mockery with counter-mockery, and he is not told to prove anything; he is told what will happen, and the sarcasm of his opponents is left where it was spoken. That restraint is itself instructive for a community under the same pressure, since it establishes that the correct response to a demand rooted in disbelief is a statement about God's timing rather than a performance assembled for the demanders.
