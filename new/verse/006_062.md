## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:62

> Then they are ˹all˺ returned to Allah— their True Master. Judgment is His ˹alone˺. And He is the Swiftest Reckoner.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim of Ownership**

The verse describes the return of every soul to God and calls Him their true Master, and the word translated as Master is one the Book uses for the person who owns, protects, and manages the affairs of another. Slaves in Makkah had such a master, and clients attached themselves to a patron, and the whole city was organised around relationships of that kind. The verse states that the relationship which actually covers every human being is the one that most people treat as a matter of law and property.

The Book uses the same word in a passage about intercession, where it says that the believers have God as their Patron while the disbelievers have no patron at all (47:11), and the sūrah states plainly that apart from Him no one has a protector and no one has an intercessor (6:51). The Makkans' patrons could not carry a client past death, and the verse tells them whose clients they have been all along.

**A Question Asked on That Day**

The Book describes a scene in which the ownership of everything is publicly announced. On the day when all appear before God, nothing about them will be hidden from Him, and the question will be asked: who does all authority belong to this day? The answer given is to Allah, the One, the Supreme (40:16). The Book says elsewhere that true authority on that day will belong only to the Most Compassionate, and that it will be a hard day for the disbelievers (25:26).

That scene explains what this verse means by a true Master. On the day of the question, every title that a person relied on will be shown to have been delegated at best and imaginary at worst, and the Book's word for it is that authority belongs to God alone. The Makkans' clan loyalties, their property, and their names would all have been visible as borrowed things.

**Judgment Is His Alone**

The sūrah has already said once that the decision belongs to God, when the Prophet was told to say that he had no power over the timing of the punishment and that God is the best of judges (6:57). The same claim returns here as the conclusion of the passage on knowledge and death. What began with God knowing every leaf and every grain ends with God alone passing sentence on what every soul has done.

The Book states that God will pass judgment between people on that day in fairness, without the least injustice, and it says that every soul will then be rewarded for what it did, with no wrong done at all (40:17). The two claims belong together: the judge who knows everything has no need of witnesses, and the judge who is the best of judges has no need of advocates.

**The Swiftest Reckoner**

The name that closes the verse appears in several places in the Book, usually in the context of a settlement that takes no time to reach. The sūrah of the Cow uses it when it says that some people receive their reward and that God is swift in reckoning (2:202), and the sūrah of Ghāfir uses it in the announcement that every soul will be repaid in full (40:17). The name is not about haste for its own sake; it describes an accounting that requires no deliberation, since the record is complete and the judge is the one who wrote what the record contains.

The Book also describes the fear that this swiftness produces in the guilty, when their descendants and neighbours and the entire crowd watch the books being distributed. The speed of the reckoning is a mercy for one party and a disaster for the other, and the same attribute is used for both, which is the way the Book regularly speaks about the attributes of God.

**The Chain That Ends Here**

Follow the verses as a unit. God holds the keys of the unseen and knows every falling leaf and buried grain (6:59). God takes the souls of people by night and knows what they do by day, reviving them each morning until the appointed term is complete (6:60). God has appointed angels who record everything and angels who take the soul at death without fail (6:61). Then the souls are returned to their Master, who alone judges and who settles accounts without delay (6:62).

Read that way, the four verses give the complete account of a human life in the order in which it happens. The audience wanted to know about the hidden and the future, and the sūrah has answered by describing the process that connects this world to the next without leaving out a single step, and without giving the audience any access to the timings they were asking about.

**The Contrast With Earthly Masters**

The closing sentence of the passage is deliberately stark. On the one side stands the judge who knows everything, holds everything, and needs nothing; on the other side stand people whose patrons could not answer for them and whose gods had no share in the making of the world. The Book records the idolaters themselves conceding that their gods are names they gave (12:40), and it reports God's challenge for them to show anything their associate-gods had created (35:40).

The verse does not answer that contrast with argument. It states the fact of the return and leaves the audience to reckon with the master they had chosen. The next verses turn from the general scene to the specific behaviour of people in danger, who call upon God alone and then forget Him the moment they are safe.
