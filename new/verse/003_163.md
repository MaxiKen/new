## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:163

> They ˹each˺ have varying degrees in the sight of Allah. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Statement of Rank**

The verse states that the two groups are ranked in degrees with God, and it closes with the statement that God sees what they do. The sentence answers the question posed in the verse before it by denying the equality the question had proposed.

The commentators read the ranking in two ways. Al-Ṭabarī observed that the two groups are at different levels in this world, since their conduct and the conditions it produces differ. Al-Rāzī read the verse as a statement about the next life, where those who are punished occupy different levels of suffering just as those who enter the garden occupy different levels of delight.

**Ranks and the Book's Teaching About Reward**

The Book states repeatedly that people will be dealt with according to what they have done, and the language of degrees is one of its ways of expressing the principle. One passage states that each will be assigned ranks according to his deeds, and another states that God will pay every soul in full for what it has earned.

The verse before the reader takes its place in that group. The sentence does not describe the rewards and punishments in detail; it states that they are graded, and the grading itself teaches the community that conduct has a weight which the settlement will reflect. The Book's readers are expected to see the point and to draw the consequence for how they live.

**Why Degrees Rather Than a Single Division**

The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter include levels for the people of the garden and levels for those who are punished, and the tradition of interpretation discussed the differences at length. The verse in this sūrah supports the picture by stating that both groups are ranked.

The idea has a practical dimension for the community that recites it. If the outcome were a single division, a person who had already secured his place would have no reason to improve it, and a person who had made one mistake would have no reason to recover. The statement that the ranks differ maintains both incentives, and it matches the Book's frequent exhortation to compete in good works.


The phrase that the ranks are with God places the scale of measurement outside the community's own estimates. The Book uses the same construction when it commends those who strive with their wealth and their lives and describes them as greater in rank in the sight of God.

The phrase matters because human communities tend to construct their own hierarchies from wealth, descent, and reputation. The verse states that the ranking that counts is held elsewhere, and the sūrah has been demonstrating the difference between the two scales throughout its account of the community's history.

**The Statement That God Sees What They Do**

The verse closes with a sentence the sūrah has used at several points, and the repetition connects the passage's theme of divine observation to the subject of ranks. The God who ranks the two groups is the God who sees what each of them does.

The Book's statements about its own knowledge are frequently attached to instructions and warnings in this way, and the effect is to make the sentence a matter of practical consequence rather than abstract doctrine. The community is told that no conduct is invisible and that the scale of ranks is applied by the one who has full information about the lives it is applied to.

**The Contrast With Human Judgement**

The sūrah's account of the battle included the community's own arguments about who had acted correctly and who had not. The verse places the assessment of conduct in the hands of God and does not invite the community to settle the question for itself.

The Book's consistent teaching about the ranking of people is that the judgement belongs to God, and the community is warned elsewhere against claiming purity for itself. The sentence in this verse gives the same warning in the form of a statement, and it leaves the community free to attend to its own conduct rather than to compare itself with others.

**The Doctrine as a General Principle**

The verse's closing statement that ranks exist with God can also be read as a general affirmation that reality is arranged in levels. The commentators noted this reading and connected the sentence with the Book's descriptions of the gradations among the creatures.

Read in that way, the verse teaches the community to think of the world as orderly rather than flat, with differences of degree that reflect the wisdom of the one who made it. The reading does not displace the verse's primary application to the two groups, and it gives the sentence a range that extends beyond the immediate contrast, since a claim about the order of creation is also a claim about the one who ordered it.

**What the Sentence Contributes to the Passage**

The verse is short and stands between the comparison of the two figures and the reminder of the favour God has shown the believers. Its brevity suits its function, which is to close the comparison without elaborating it.

The passage as a whole moves from the question about trust and defrauding to the two destinies and then to the favour of the message itself. The sentence in this verse marks the end of the contrast and prepares for the shift to gratitude, and the community that reads the sequence is given a reminder of the scale on which its conduct will be weighed, which is a matter the sūrah has raised repeatedly in its account of the battle.
