## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:99

> Say, “O People of the Book! Why do you turn the believers away from the Way of Allah—striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked, while you are witnesses ˹to its truth˺? And Allah is never unaware of what you do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Denial to Obstruction**

The previous verse asked the People of the Book about their denial of God's revelations. This verse asks them about something else, which is what they do to the people who believe, and the shift from belief to conduct is the passage's subject.

The charge is that they turn believers away from the way of God and work to make that way look crooked, while they themselves are witnesses to its truth. The sentence therefore names an act, a purpose, and a state of knowledge, and the three together form the accusation. What is being described is not a private conviction but a campaign directed at the community of the believers.

**Turning Away and the Sense of the Verb**

The commentators discussed whether the turning described here is physical or figurative, and the weight of their explanation favours the second. The Book does not accuse these people of standing in the road; it accuses them of misdirection, of presenting the way as something other than it is so that those who hear them begin to doubt.

On this reading the act is one of distortion, and its target is the confidence of the believers. The commentators added that when these people were asked about the matter directly, they denied that the messenger was foretold in their books, which is the denial the previous verse was about. The two verses thus describe one behaviour from two sides: the withholding of testimony and the effort to unsettle those who accepted it.

**Making the Way Appear Crooked**

The phrase about making the way crooked recurs in the Book in a set of verses that share a striking profile. In each of them, the people described hinder others from the path of God, seek to make it appear crooked, and disbelieve in the Hereafter.

One passage places them among those who favour this life over the next, and describes them as having gone far astray. Another groups them with those who bar the way and then deny the final accounting. The repetition shows that the Book treats the obstruction of others as belonging to a settled pattern of conduct rather than to an isolated mistake, and the sūrah's version of the phrase adds a further element that makes it more serious than the others: the accused know the truth of what they are bending.

**"While You Are Witnesses"**

The clause about being witnesses is the part of the sentence the commentators explained most often. Some took it as a reference to the presence of the messenger among them, since a community living in the same city as the Prophet is in a position to observe him. Others took it as a reference to their own scriptures, which contained what the messenger was teaching, so that their witness came from the books they recited.

Both readings converge on the same point, which is that the knowledge in question was not hidden from them. The Book has used the language of witnessing before in this sūrah in its account of the covenant, and it will use it again when it describes these people as bearing witness to the truth while rejecting it. The accusation's sharpest edge is the combination of the two facts the verse places side by side: they know, and they work against what they know.

**A Wrong Done to Others**

The verse's placement after the passage about the House of worship and the pilgrimage gives the accusation a wider significance than a dispute between scholars. The believers are being kept from a way, and the way is the same one the sūrah has traced from Abraham through the prophets.

That is why the Book treats the misleading of others as more serious than a private refusal. A person who rejects a message damages himself; a person who draws others away from it damages them as well, and the Book's vocabulary for the offense places it among the wrongs that corrupt the community rather than the individual. The sūrah has already warned its own community about the possibility of turning back, and this verse shows the mechanism by which such a turning is produced from the outside.

**The Closing Clause**

The sentence ends by stating that God is never unaware of what they do. The wording differs slightly from the previous verse's witness clause, and the difference in the Book's usage is worth noting: the witness is what establishes the record, and the absence of unawareness is what guarantees that the record will be used.

The two clauses work as a pair across the two verses, and the second one is placed where the harm has spread to other people. The Book's statements about God's knowledge at the close of such passages are not decoration; they are the answer to the calculation a person makes when he believes that the consequences of his conduct will not reach him. What the verse tells its audience is that the community they are working against has a protector who sees the work.

**The Movement of the Passage**

The verses that follow turn from the People of the Book to the believers, and the change of address marks the conclusion of this stage of the sūrah's argument. The interrogation has been conducted and the matters at issue have been named.

The result is a warning that runs in both directions. The earlier community is told that its knowledge increases its responsibility, and the community now receiving the sūrah is told in the next verse what would happen to it if it followed their guidance. The passage therefore ends not with a verdict on the opponents but with an instruction to the believers, which is the sūrah's consistent practice when it has finished stating a case.
