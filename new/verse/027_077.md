## Sūrah an-Naml 27:77

> And it is truly a guide and mercy for the believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Returns to Its Opening Words**

This is almost the second verse of the sūrah repeated. There the Book was called *a guide and good news for the believers*; here it is *a guide and mercy for the believers*. One word has changed.

The repetition is structural. An-Naml opened by describing the Qur'an, spent seventy verses on narrative and argument, and now returns to the same description as the argument closes. What sits between the two statements is the demonstration — four peoples, six questions, a queen who submitted and three nations that did not. The claim made at the start is restated at the end, and everything in between was the case for it.

**Mercy in Place of Good News**

The substituted word repays attention. *Bushrā* is an announcement about the future: something good is coming. *Raḥmah* is a present quality of the thing itself: the Book is mercy, now, in the hands of whoever holds it.

The change tracks where the chapter has arrived. Verse 2 was addressed to people at the start of a text, being told what the Book promises. Verse 77 comes after the accounts of Thamūd's emptied houses, the rain that fell on Lūṭ's people, and Pharaoh's court refusing what it knew. A reader who has been through that material is not in a position to hear about good news in the abstract; what he has just been shown is what happens to communities without a guide. Against that, the Book's presence is itself the mercy. The pairing appears elsewhere in the Qur'an for both this revelation and the Torah, always in the same order.

**Guidance Named Twice**

The one element that does not change across the two verses is *hudan*, guidance. It is the first thing said about the Book at the chapter's opening and the first thing said about it here.

That constancy identifies the primary function. Everything else the Qur'an is called in this chapter — clear, a criterion between disputing parties, good news, mercy — describes an aspect or an effect. Guidance is the thing itself. The chapter's whole vocabulary has been organised around this word: the deniers of verse 4 wander blindly, Sheba's people are left unguided, the queen's test in verse 41 is framed as whether she will be guided, and verse 63 asked who guides travellers through the darkness of land and sea. A Book described as guidance is being placed in the same category as the stars a caravan steers by.

**Restricted to the Believers Again**

As in verse 2, the benefit is specified for *al-mu'minīn*. The Book is mercy for those who believe, which raises the same question the earlier verse raised and answers it the same way.

Nothing here says the Qur'an was sent only to believers, and the chapter has just addressed most of its recent verses to people who reject it. What the restriction names is who actually receives the benefit, not who is offered it. Medicine is described by whom it heals. The seventy-five verses between this statement and its twin have supplied the evidence: the same signs that guided some were called sorcery by others, the same interval that let Sheba's queen investigate was spent by Thamūd demanding their own destruction. The material was identical in each case. What differed was the reception.

**The Argument Has Come Full Circle**

Set verses 1–3 beside verses 76–77 and the chapter's frame is visible. It opens: these are the verses of the clear Book, a guide and good news for the believers, those who pray, give and are certain of the Hereafter. It closes this section: this Qur'an recounts to the Children of Israel most of what they dispute, and it is a guide and mercy for the believers.

Both ends describe the same object and name the same beneficiaries. What the closing pair adds is the second audience and the substituted attribute — the Book now addresses a scriptural community as well as a pagan one, and it is mercy as well as news. That widening is the chapter's net movement. It began with an Arabian audience and two letters of the alphabet, and it ends with a claim to adjudicate between traditions. The judgment itself, and who will deliver it, is the subject of the next verse.

**A Pairing Used of the Torah Too**

The two words appear together in several other chapters, and in some of those places they describe the Book given to Mūsā rather than the Qur'an. Al-Anʿām applies the phrase to the scripture of Mūsā twice in three verses; al-Aḥqāf does the same.

That shared description carries a claim about continuity. The Qur'an is not presenting itself as a new kind of thing but as the latest instance of a recognised kind, and it uses the identical vocabulary for its predecessor. The placement here reinforces the point, since the previous verse has just addressed the Children of Israel directly. A book that says it clarifies their disputes, and then describes itself with the same two words their own scripture is given, is making an argument about lineage rather than replacement. What follows in the next verse — that God will judge between the parties — assumes the same framework.

**What "Mercy" Means in Practice**

The abstraction can slide past without landing, so it helps to ask what the chapter has actually shown mercy to consist of. In an-Naml it is consistently information arriving in time to be acted on.

Sheba received a letter before an army. Lūṭ's household was extracted before the rain. Ṣāliḥ's people were given a warning they spent on plotting. In each case what was offered was notice — the interval between the situation and its consequence, filled with an account of what was happening. That is precisely what a book is. Calling the Qur'an mercy, in a chapter organised around those four histories, is not a pious epithet but a summary of the pattern the narratives have established. The mercy is that the message came first.
