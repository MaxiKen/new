## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:53

> while they had already rejected it before, guessing blindly from a place ˹equally˺ far-away ˹from the Hereafter˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason the Late Declaration Fails**

The previous verse showed them reaching for faith across a distance they could not cross. This one explains why the distance exists, and the explanation is not about the geography of the next world. *Wa qad kafarū bihi min qabl* — they had already rejected it before. The word *qabl*, before, is doing the work. Their present position is not an arbitrary penalty imposed after the fact; it is the continuation of a stance they took while the matter was open. A man who spends a lifetime declaring a thing false has, by the time it appears in front of him, already placed himself at the maximum distance from it. The verse presents the two moments as one trajectory rather than as a crime followed by a sentence.

**The Verb That Ties This Verse to the Prophet's Statement**

The phrase translated *guessing blindly* is *wa yaqdhifūna bi-l-ghaybi*, and the verb is the surprise. *Qadhafa* is the same word used five verses earlier when the Prophet was told to say that his Lord *hurls the truth* (34:48). One verb, two subjects, opposite results. God hurls the truth and it lands on falsehood and crushes it. They hurl at the unseen, and the throw goes nowhere. The Arabic also carries a second sense of *qadhafa* that English cannot hold in the same word: to cast an accusation at someone, to sling a charge. That sense is live here, since what they threw at the unseen was precisely accusation — the Qur'an is a fabrication, the messenger is mad, the resurrection is absurd. Both readings describe the same act. Something was thrown, and the thrower could not see the target.

**Throwing at What You Cannot See**

The image deserves to be taken literally for a moment, because it is exact. A man who hurls a stone into the dark may hit something or may not, but either way he did not aim, and his confidence about where it landed is worthless. This is the verse's charge against every claim the opponents made about hidden matters. They asserted that the Hour would not come (34:3), that they would not be punished (34:35), that the message was sorcery (34:43) — all statements about a region they had no access to. The sūrah demonstrated that lack of access twice: once by showing that even the jinn who served Solomon could not detect a death in front of them (34:14), and again by pointing out that this people had received neither scripture nor prior warner (34:44). They had no instrument and threw anyway.

**"From a Place Far Away" — Repeated Deliberately**

The phrase *min makānin baʿīd* closes this verse exactly as it closed the previous one, and the repetition is the passage's sharpest stroke. In verse 52 the distance was where they now stand, reaching uselessly for faith. Here the same distance describes where they stood when they were making their confident pronouncements. The sūrah is saying that their position has not changed. They were always far from the matter they spoke about; the only difference is that the remoteness was invisible to them then and is unbearable now. A man shouting judgments about something he cannot see is at exactly the same distance from it as a man reaching for something he cannot touch.

**Why Speculation Is Treated as a Moral Failure**

It would be possible to read the deniers' behaviour as an intellectual error — they guessed and guessed wrong. The verse frames it as something worse, and the framing is consistent with the whole sūrah. Guessing about the unseen is not a neutral activity when guidance about it has been delivered and is available for examination. These men had a warner in front of them whose honesty they had publicly attested, reciting a text they could evaluate, and they preferred conjecture. The chapter's alternative was stated plainly seven verses earlier: stand up, singly or in pairs, and think (34:46). That instruction was an offer of the only method by which a person without access to the unseen can responsibly proceed. Throwing in the dark is what remains when the offer is declined.

**The Pronoun Left Open Here Too**

As in the previous verse, the object of their earlier rejection is a bare pronoun: they had rejected *it* before. Commentators have read the referent variously as the truth discussed in verses 48 and 49, as the Qur'an, as the Prophet himself, or as God. The range is not a weakness in the text. Each candidate produces a sentence that is true of these speakers, and the Arabic construction allows a reader to hear more than one at once. There is also a small point of symmetry worth registering: verse 52 had them believing in *it* too late, and this verse has them rejecting *it* too early, with the same unspecified pronoun in both. Whatever the object was, their relation to it was wrong at both ends — dismissed when it could have been examined, accepted when acceptance had stopped meaning anything.

**Two Ways of Being Certain**

The sūrah closes its argument by placing two kinds of confidence side by side, and the contrast has been running since the third verse. The Prophet was made to swear an oath that the Hour would come, and the oath rested on a stated source: revelation from the Knower of the unseen (34:3). The deniers matched his certainty with their own and rested it on nothing — no book, no messenger, no instrument, nothing but the fact that the thing in question could not be seen. Both parties were equally sure. Only one could say where the certainty came from. The distinction survives the chapter's setting entirely, since confidence about hidden things is not a habit peculiar to seventh-century Mecca. One verse remains, and it describes what closes behind people who spent a life throwing in the dark.
