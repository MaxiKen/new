## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:190

> Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Surely Allah does not like transgressors.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Permission, and the Dispute About It**

This verse opens the sūrah's legislation of war, and the tradition debated whether it opens the Qur'an's. The exegetes record the question directly: is this the first verse revealed permitting the Muslims to fight, or does that priority belong to the sūrah of the Pilgrimage's permission — granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged (22:39)? The reports supporting each are given, and al-Ṭabarī declines to decide, finding the narrations insufficiently conclusive — a characteristic exercise of his evidentiary discipline. What the verse's own context supplies is the setting: the reports of occasion place it in the period of the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah, when the Prophet and his Companions set out for the ʿumrah unarmed and were turned back, and the settlement's terms left the community's relationship to Meccan violence unresolved. The legislation that follows addresses a people who have been persecuted, expelled, and fought for thirteen years, who have just been barred from their own sanctuary by treaty rather than battle — and the first word of the war-law to such a people is a limitation, not a license: fight *those who fight you*.

**In the Cause of God**

The command's first phrase sets the war's final cause: fight *in the path of God*. The formula excludes every other motive the Arabian battlefield offered — tribal honor, booty as end, vengeance as appetite, prestige as currency — and names the one legitimate aim: the cause that is God's, which the surrounding verses will define as the ending of persecution and the securing of worship (2:193). The cause-clause is thus the verse's first limit and its most comprehensive: a war fought in God's path is answerable to God's stated ends, and any operation serving another end has left the permission even if it keeps the vocabulary. The tradition heard the clause as governing conduct continuously, not merely intentions at the outset — the fighter in God's path is the one the reports describe as asked whether he fights for courage, for zeal, or for show, and answered that only the one who fights so that God's word is uppermost fights in God's path. The verse installs that test as law: the cause is named before the conduct because conduct untethered from a named cause degenerates into the very transgression the second clause forbids.

**Against Those Who Fight You**

The permission's object is reciprocal: those who fight you — and the classical exegesis built the whole defensive architecture on the phrase. The one who does not fight you is not within the permission; the fighting is answer to fighting, not initiation of it. The verse that follows will extend the reciprocity spatially — expel them from where they expelled you — keeping the response tied to the injury's geography as well as its fact. The tradition's elaboration is extensive and consistent with the clause: the reports prohibit the killing of women, children, monks and hermits, the chronically ill, the aged, and the peasants not participating in hostilities; Ibn ʿAbbās is cited stating the categories flatly — do not kill women, children, the old, or those who offer peace. ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz wrote to his commanders on the verse's principle: do not fight those who do not fight you. The phrase thereby becomes the root of the Islamic law of war's discrimination principle — the distinction between combatant and non-combatant — and the verse's reciprocity is not merely a casus belli rule but a targeting rule: the permission reaches the fighters and stops at the boundary of the fighter's person.

**Do Not Exceed the Limits**

The second command is the verse's famous prohibition: *do not transgress* — the iʿtidāʾ whose forms the tradition enumerated. The exegetes read the non-transgression as governing every excess the reciprocity could invite: killing beyond the combatants, retaliation beyond the injury, pursuit beyond the need — and the Qur'an's own parallel legislation of retaliation states the standard it applies here: if you retaliate, retaliate with the like of what was done to you (16:126); the recompense of evil is evil like it (42:40). The verse's closing names the divine disposition toward the excess: God does not love the transgressors — the same formula the food-legislation used of the wasters (2:168) and the sūrah will use again, marking transgression not merely as forbidden but as forfeiting love. The prohibition's placement is the verse's structural point: the permission to fight and the ban on exceeding it arrive in one breath, so the community never holds the license without holding its limit. Every war-law that follows — the Sacred Mosque's clause, the persecution's ranking, the cease-fire's acceptance — is the working-out of these two clauses together: fight the fighters, and fight them within bounds.

**The Verse Beside Its Neighbors**

Read against the Qur'an's wider peace-legislation, the verse's limits show their framework. The sūrah of the Examined Woman distinguishes the two populations precisely along this verse's line: God does not forbid you from being kind and just to those who did not fight you over religion nor expel you from your homes — God loves the just (60:8); what is forbidden is alliance only with those who fought and expelled and abetted (60:9). The peace-orientation is legislated independently: if they incline to peace, incline to it (8:61); the good-and-evil asymmetry of the sūrah of Explained in Detail commands repelling evil with what is better, until the enemy becomes as an intimate friend (41:34); and the patience-counsel to the Prophet himself is to bear what they say and depart from them courteously (73:10), with the reminder that he is not there to compel anyone (88:22). Against this background 2:190 is not an isolated permission but a bounded exception inside a scripture whose default is patience, kindness to the non-fighting, and peace when peace is offered — the exception's boundaries drawn by reciprocity and non-transgression, and its exercise governed by the cause that alone makes it God's.

**The Sanctuaries and the Sequence**

The verse's setting at Hudaybiyyah also charges what follows it. The community addressed had just been prevented from the Sacred Mosque without battle; the next verse will legislate the Mosque's special regime — no fighting at it unless attacked there — and the third will name the war's ultimate object: the end of fitnah, the persecution that had driven the community out and barred it from its sanctuary. The sequence thus moves from the general permission (fight the fighters, without excess) to the sacred exception (the Mosque's inviolability) to the war's terminus (persecution ended, religion God's alone) — and every step is limitation rather than escalation. The verse that opens Islamic war-law opens it, in other words, by naming the war's enemies as the people already at war with the believers, its conduct as bounded by non-transgression, and its cause as God's rather than theirs. A permission shaped this way is a permission designed to end wars rather than multiply them — which is what the treaty-context of its descent suggests it was first given to do: to arm a community for the fighting already imposed on it, and to forbid, in the same sentence, every use of the arms beyond that need.
