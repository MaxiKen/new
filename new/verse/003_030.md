## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:30

> ˹Watch for˺ the Day when every soul will be presented with whatever good it has done. And it will wish that its misdeeds were far off. And Allah warns you about Himself. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Expect What Is Coming**

The verse opens in the imperative, though the verb is supplied by the translation: watch for, prepare for, be present to the thought of a day. The Book frequently addresses its readers in this way, treating the Last Day as an appointment that requires a settled attitude rather than an item of information.

The appointment is one at which nothing is argued and nothing is supplied. The Arabic construction presents the day of every soul as a scene of display, in which what a person did arrives with him. There is no mention of a prosecution, a witness box, or a defence, because those belong to the courtroom stage that the Book describes elsewhere; this verse shows the moment before the argument begins, when a person simply sees what he is carrying.

**Every Soul Presented With Its Good**

The verse says that each soul is presented with whatever good it has done, and the Book's other accounts of this moment describe the presentation in detail. At the resurrection of the earth's smallest deeds, every atom's weight of good and of evil is seen (99:7–8). At the opening of the record, the wicked are handed a document that omits nothing, and they cry out at its completeness (18:49). The scales are set up on that day, and no soul is wronged in the least (21:47).

Together these passages give the scene its character. It is not a trial in which a defendant hears charges he must answer; it is an inventory in which the whole of a life arrives to be seen. The verse's own emphasis falls on the good, which is presented first: whatever good a person has done comes forward. The mercy in that ordering deserves attention, since it means the deeds a person may have forgotten are not lost, and are the first thing named.

**The Wish for Distance**

The second sentence reports the reaction rather than the content: the soul will wish that its misdeeds were far off. The Arabic verb describes a hope for something to be remote, postponed, or placed outside one's reach.

Other passages show the same wish in more vivid terms. The wrongdoers would offer everything the earth contains twice over to ransom themselves from the punishment they see, and what they see will be what they never expected (39:47); and the person who dragged others toward error will tell his companion on that day that he wishes they had been as distant from each other as the east is from the west (43:38). The Book speaks of this regret without sentimentality, as a fact about how people will perceive their own records when they can no longer revise them. It is a description of an emotion that arrives too late to be useful, and the Book's regular advice is to have it now.

**The Warning Repeated a Third Time**

The verse returns to the formula *Allah warns you about Himself*, which appeared at 3:28 and here again. The Book rarely repeats a warning three times in one passage, and the repetition is a measure of the pressure the passage intends to apply.

The function of the formula in this position differs slightly from its earlier use. There, it warned against a betrayal of allegiance; here, it is placed immediately after a description of the record being opened. A reader who has just been shown the scene is being told who is behind it, and the phrase leaves no room to treat the day as an impersonal process or a natural event. What is coming is not a system that decides fates; it is the decision of the one who warned about His own seriousness beforehand.

**"Ever Gracious to His Servants" — the Sentence That Follows the Warning**

The verse ends in tenderness. After the warning comes a description of God as *raʾūf* — deeply kind, compassionate — toward His servants. The Book uses the same pair of qualities of the Prophet himself: he is described as ever caring and merciful to the believers (9:128).

The placement of the attribute carries the doctrine of the passage. A warning issued to servants whom the warner knows to be weak is a form of care; a warning that gave no opportunity for amendment would be a sentence. The Book's logic is that the kindness and the warning come from the same source and serve the same end, and it says so by putting them in the same verse, one clause apart.

**Why the Comfort Needs the Warning**

The verse's generosity can be misread as reassurance, so the order within it matters. The soul is shown the wish it will have, the seriousness of God is asserted, and only then is the reader told that the one who warns is gracious to His servants.

The sequence means that the kindness belongs to the warning rather than replacing it. A benevolent speaker who concealed the consequences of a road would not be benevolent; he would be careless. The Book's pastoral method here is to make the destination vivid, confirm the authority of the one who set it, and then tell the traveller how kind the guide is. The reader who finds that combination strange has not yet pictured the road.

**A Person's Own Testimony**

The verse leaves no need for prosecution because the evidence is carried by the accused. The Book says as much at 75:13, where each person is informed of what he sent ahead and left behind, and at 81:14, where every soul on that day knows precisely what it has brought.

This is the quietest terror of the scene and the reason it needs no argument. Nobody in it will be misinformed about himself, and nobody's account of himself will convince anyone. The verses after this one turn to the community's claims of love and obedience, which belong to the same register: what a person says about himself matters less than what he has actually brought.
