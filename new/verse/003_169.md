## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:169

> Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead. In fact, they are alive with their Lord, well provided for—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prohibition of Thinking Them Dead**

The verse forbids the believers to regard those who were killed in the way of God as dead, and it states what they are instead. The instruction addresses the community's grief directly, and it also answers the arguments that the preceding verses reported.

The wording is a prohibition rather than a description, and the commentators read it as directed at the way the community was speaking about its losses. The people who had died on the field were being discussed in the terms the hypocrites had supplied, and the verse replaces those terms with its own account of their state.

**The Word for Those Who Were Killed**

The verse uses the verb for being killed, which describes a death by violence, and it applies the phrase about the way of God to it. The community had just buried men who had died in exactly that manner, and the verse does not avoid the word for what happened to them.

The Book's willingness to name the violence is part of the consolation's honesty. The sentence does not tell the community that the men had not been killed; it tells them not to consider those killed in God's cause as dead. The distinction between the manner of the death and the state of the dead is the whole content of the instruction.

**The Statement That They Are Alive**

The verse states that they are alive with their Lord, and the commentators discussed what kind of life is meant. The Book's language is used in the passage about the martyrs in another sūrah, where the believers are told not to say that those killed in God's cause are dead, since they are alive, though the community does not perceive it.

The two verses together give the teaching its shape. The life is real and it is not perceptible to the community that remains, and the phrase about being with their Lord locates it in a state that the Book describes without setting out its stages. The community is not told how to picture the state; it is told the fact that governs how it should speak.


The verse adds that they are provided for, and the word describes the supply that sustains a living thing. The participle's form indicates a condition that continues rather than an act completed.

The Book uses the same term for the provision it gives in this life, and the commentators read the sentence as a statement that the men who died in God's cause have been given something better than what they left. The community at Madinah had lost men who had been supporting households, and the verse tells it that the dead have not been left without provision.

**The Book's Other Statements About the Martyrs**

The Book returns to the state of those who die in God's cause in several places. One passage promises that those who emigrate for God's cause and are then killed or die will be given a good provision, and it names God as the best of providers. Another promises that those killed in God's cause will not have their deeds rendered void.

A third passage describes the company of the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous as those with whom the obedient will be gathered. The verse before the reader is the shortest of these statements, and the others fill in its content.


The verse in the sūrah of the Cow adds the observation that the community does not perceive the state of the martyrs. That clause explains why the instruction in this sūrah is phrased as a prohibition: the community's senses reported only the burial.

The Book's teaching here concerns the limits of what people can observe, and it asks them to speak on the basis of what they have been told rather than on the basis of what they can see. The community that had watched the fighting and the burials is told that its observation did not extend to the matter it was now discussing.

**What the Verse Says About Death**

The sentence is one of the Book's clearest statements that death is not the end of a person's existence. It describes a transition from one state to another rather than a cancellation, and it places the dead in the care of their Lord.

The Book's treatment of death in the passages about the martyrs is the sharpest form of a teaching it states in general terms elsewhere, where it describes every soul as tasting death and returning to God. The verse before the reader applies the general teaching to a particular case that the community was grieving, and it gives the community language for it that does not depend on what the community could see with its own eyes on the day of the burial.

**Why the Statement Follows the Argument**

The declaration about the martyrs comes after the sūrah has dealt with the claim that the dead could have been saved, and its placement is part of its meaning. The community had been told to stop making the argument about whether the men should have gone out, and it is now told where they are.

The passage thus moves from a refusal of the wrong question to the statement of the right fact. The community's losses are not explained away, and the status of the dead is not left to the estimation of the survivors. The next verse continues the description of their state and adds the detail that they are aware of those who remain behind.

**The Requests of the Slain**

The Book's account of those who died in God's cause includes the report that they ask to be sent back to the world so that they may die again in His way, a report that al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī record from the traditions attached to this passage. The request is the clearest evidence of the state the verse describes, since a person content with what he has received would not ask to exchange it for a repetition of the experience that brought him there. What the next verse adds, that they rejoice in those who have not yet joined them, belongs to the same picture. The community in this world is told that its dead are aware of it, and the warning about the bars between the living and the dead is answered by the two sentences rather than by any claim about how the awareness works.

**Where the Dead Are**

Al-Rāzī considered the reading that the verse means the slain will be alive later, in the Hereafter, and rejected it. His argument is that everyone will be alive in the Hereafter, so the sentence would say nothing distinctive, whereas the Book's statement that the slain are provided for and rejoicing in those who have not yet joined them shows that something is being said about their state now. On his reading the phrase means that they are alive at that moment, in proximity to their Lord, and that God knows them as such. The verse and its companion in the sūrah of the Cow were used extensively in the community's theology for the position that souls are alive between physical death and the resurrection, against those who taught otherwise. The traditions describe the state of souls at that stage in several ways, and the Book's verses leave the question of where the dead are unanswered, which is why the commentators described the matter with caution and left the detail to God.
