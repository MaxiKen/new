## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:9

> O Prophet! Struggle against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be firm with them. Hell will be their home. What an evil destination!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse Repeated Word for Word**

This line is identical to one in the ninth sūrah: *“O Prophet! Struggle against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be firm with them. Hell will be their home. What an evil destination!”* (9:73). The Qur'an repeats material often, but exact repetition of a full verse is less common, and it invites a question about what the second placement adds.

The answer lies in the surroundings. In the earlier sūrah the command sits among passages about military campaigns, the treatment of those who stayed behind, and the exposure of the hypocrites of Madina. Here it sits between a call to sincere repentance and a set of examples drawn from the households of prophets. The same words, dropped into a chapter about family life and inner states, acquire a different function. They mark the boundary of the chapter's gentleness: the sūrah has corrected a prophet with a question, invited two women to repent, and called believers to turn — and it now states that not everyone is being addressed this way.

**Two Adversaries Who Require Different Handling**

The command names two groups, and the commentators have consistently read the striving as taking different forms against each. Against open deniers it was understood to include armed struggle where that was called for, alongside argument and the call to God. Against the hypocrites — who professed faith publicly and worked against the community privately — it was understood as applying the law to them, since they could not be fought as enemies while they identified themselves as Muslims.

The distinction is legally important and has been widely ignored by later readers of both kinds. Hypocrisy was never treated in the Prophet's practice as grounds for execution or expulsion, which is why the known hypocrites of Madina lived, prayed and traded in the city until they died. What the verse requires against them is firmness rather than tolerance of their conduct: their schemes exposed, their claims not accepted at face value, and the community's business not entrusted to them. That is a sustainable policy for a society that contains people whose loyalty is uncertain, and it does not require identifying who they are with any certainty.

**Firmness as the Absence of Accommodation**

*“and be firm with them”* (66:9) uses a verb built on harshness, and the word deserves care. The Qur'an that issues this command also describes the Prophet ﷺ as lenient by God's mercy and warns him that hardness would have scattered the people around him (3:159). The two statements are addressed to different objects. Gentleness is the standing posture toward the community, including its faltering members; firmness is the posture toward those whose project is the community's undoing.

What the verse forbids, in practice, is accommodation — the softening of a position to avoid a confrontation, the quiet dropping of a demand because pressing it would be awkward. Read against the chapter's opening, this is a pointed placement. The sūrah began with a prohibition adopted *“seeking to please your wives”* (66:1), an accommodation made for the sake of peace and corrected from above. The same instinct, applied to the community's opponents, is the failure this verse forecloses. A man who cannot hold a line inside his household is unlikely to hold one outside it, and the chapter has treated both as the same discipline.

**Why the Struggle Verse Sits Here**

Placing this command immediately before the examples of the wives of Noah and Lot produces a deliberate sequence. Those two women were inside the households of prophets and were counted with the deniers; they are introduced in the very next verse, after a line about disbelievers and hypocrites whose home is Hell. The chapter is arranging its categories so that the reader cannot draw the boundary of the community along the lines of family, proximity or public profession.

This also completes the chapter's account of what a believer's life involves. Its earlier verses dealt with the inner work: hearts that tilt, confidences kept, repentance that is genuine rather than performed, families taught rather than indulged. This verse turns outward and names a second obligation that the inner work does not replace. A person can be scrupulous at home and entirely passive about what surrounds him, and the sūrah declines to accept that as sufficient. The word *jāhid* covers effort of every legitimate kind, and it is the only imperative in the chapter addressed to the Prophet ﷺ alone that concerns anything outside his house.

**The Destination Named Without Argument**

*“Hell will be their home. What an evil destination!”* (66:9) closes the verse with a statement and an exclamation rather than a case. The word rendered *home* is *maʾwā*, a place of shelter and return — the place one goes back to at the end of the day. Using it of the Fire is bitterly precise: what these people spent their lives building toward turns out to be the place that receives them.

The exclamation that follows, *biʾsa al-maṣīr*, is a formula the Qur'an uses to close such statements, and its brevity is the point. No description of the punishment is offered here, no detail added to what the chapter already gave three verses earlier about fuel and keepers. The destination is named and the sentence stops. For a chapter that has been unusually generous with explanation — completing an elliptical conditional, listing the Prophet's supporters, itemising the qualities of better wives — the refusal to elaborate at this particular point reads as deliberate. Some things are set out at length because the reader is meant to weigh them. This one is stated because it is settled.
