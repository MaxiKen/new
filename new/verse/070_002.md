## Sūrah al-Maʿārij 70:2

> for the disbelievers—to be averted by none—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Punishment Has Named Owners**

Verse 1 announced a falling torment; verse 2 delivers its address label. "For the disbelievers" — lil-kāfirīna — assigns the demanded punishment to those who demanded it, closing the circle with judicial neatness. The definite article matters: not some hypothetical deniers but the disbelievers, the known party of mockers surrounding the Prophet. Exegetes keep the reference concrete, hearing the Quraysh scoffers who treated resurrection-talk as entertainment. Yet the label outgrows its first wearers, as Qur'anic categories always do, extending to everyone who replicates their stance toward warning. Grammar reinforces ownership through the lām of entitlement: this punishment belongs to them the way wages belong to earners. No transfer, no substitution, no clerical error will redirect it. The challenger asked for whom the torment was; the verse answers with a mirror. It was, all along, for him.

**No Hand Can Turn It Back**

"To be averted by none" renders lā dāfiʿa lahu — no defender, no repeller, no shield exists for it. The noun dāfiʿ evokes someone who pushes back an attacker, and its negation leaves the punishment's path utterly clear. Wealth cannot bribe it, armies cannot block it, intercessors cannot intercept it, and the idols so confidently invoked will stand as helpless as their worshippers. This absoluteness distinguishes divine punishment from every human threat, which some power can always deflect. Here the mighty and the sheltered discover a common nakedness. Theologians hear in the phrase a pure note of divine sovereignty: to decree torment that none can lift belongs to God alone, and claiming such power for any other is itself the unbelief being punished. Helplessness before heaven is the human condition; this verse simply removes the anesthesia. When the demand of verse 1 meets the irreversibility of verse 2, bravado curdles into dread.

**Certainty Doubled in a Single Line**

Two locks fasten this verse shut. The punishment is wāqiʿ, already falling, and now also undeterrable, past all rescue. Falling plus unstoppable equals arrived: the outcome stands so assured that only the timetable stays hidden. Rhetoric of this kind — paired certainties in one breath — recurs wherever the Book confronts delay-based doubt. Deniers reasoned that postponement meant cancellation; the verse replies that descent plus irreversibility makes postponement mere scheduling. Each half of the line guards the other: "for the disbelievers" prevents the threat from floating abstractly, while "to be averted by none" prevents it from softening hopefully. Together they form a sealed chamber with the mocker inside. Translators capture the compression with em-dashes, setting the second clause as a grim aside. But the Arabic needs no punctuation to convey finality. Nine syllables close every exit, and the door they close is the Day itself.

**A Challenge Thrown Back at the Challenger**

Irony crowns the verse. The man who demanded punishment to prove its absence learns that his demand has been entered as evidence — and that the sentence allows no appeal. Mockers imagined themselves prosecutors cross-examining the Prophet; the Book reveals them as defendants sentencing themselves. This reversal follows a deep Qur'anic pattern in which the weapons of denial turn in their wielders' hands. Those who demanded angels receive instead the terror of angels; those who begged for hastening get hastening as wrath. The mechanism is moral rather than magical: persistent scorn hardens into destiny, and destiny then wears scorn's own face. Verse 2 thus answers verse 1 the way an echo answers a shout, except the echo carries farther and returns heavier. What left Makkah as laughter comes back from heaven as law. The challenger wanted a sign; he has become one, exhibited across centuries to everyone tempted by his wager.

**Unbelief Chooses Its Own Destination**

Agency threads through the grammar. The disbelievers are not random victims of a falling sky but travelers who selected their road, demanded its destination, and now find the ticket nonrefundable. "For the disbelievers" therefore reads as consequence rather than caprice: persistent denial earns its own harvest, and the harvest is gathered without interference. This preserves both divine justice and human freedom in one stroke. God sends the punishment, yet the punished chose the camp in which it falls; sovereignty and responsibility meet without collision. Pastoral wisdom hides here too. Hearers tempted by the mockers' swagger get shown its terminus plainly, while there is still time to change roads. The verse threatens, but its threat functions as rescue-signal — severity in service of mercy. Only those who insist on the label need fear its postage. Everyone else may read the address, shudder, and step back from the company of the doomed.

**Severity Serves as a Final Mercy**

Threats this absolute can sound cruel until their purpose surfaces. The verse seals every exit not to torment the living but to redirect them — terror now prevents ruin later. Physicians inflict pain to prevent death, and the Book's severest passages work the same way, wounding complacency to save souls. Had the punishment been described as escapable, the mockers would have calculated odds and hired defenses; "averted by none" forecloses the arithmetic entirely. Despair of escape becomes hope of another kind: since the Day cannot be dodged, it must be prepared for, and preparation remains open. This is severity as kindness, the fence at the cliff's edge rather than the cliff itself. Hearers who shudder at the verse have already begun benefiting from it. Only hearts that hear "none" and shrug have truly grasped neither the threat nor the mercy folded inside its severity.

**The Day Lurks Behind the Words**

"Punishment" here points chiefly to the Last Day, though Badr supplied its down payment. The demand of verse 1 concerned eschatology — the Hour the Prophet preached — and verse 2 keeps that horizon fixed. Worldly calamities can sometimes be averted: walls hold, ransoms pay, storms pass. Only the final reckoning carries the absolute "none," because only there does every buffer dissolve. Exegetes therefore hear the eschatological register beneath the historical occasion, reading Badr as installment and Resurrection as sum. The verse's brevity suits that vastness; a line this short can hold the Day only by refusing detail. Detail will come soon enough — skies like molten brass, mountains like wool, children offered as ransom. For now the sūrah contents itself with ownership and irreversibility, two nails sufficient to fasten attention. The next verse will then name the height from which this unstoppable sentence descends.
