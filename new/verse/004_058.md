## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:58

> Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners; and when you judge between people, judge with fairness. What a noble commandment from Allah to you! Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Return Trusts to Their Rightful Owners**

The command *inna Allāha yaʾmurukum an tuʾaddū al-amānāt ilā ahlihā* — indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners — gathers the sūrah’s long training about entrusted wealth into a single principle. *Amānāt* — trusts — was read as every trust: property of orphans to be returned without substitution (4:2) and after testing with witnesses (4:6), dowries given graciously as a free gift (4:4) and not reclaimed even where a heap was given (4:20–21), obligatory shares after debts and bequests (4:11–12), and deposits and pledges to be fulfilled (4:33). The sūrah has shown what happens when trusts are eaten as fire (4:10), devoured by falsehood (4:29), or exceeded beyond limits with humiliating abiding (4:14). To return trusts *ilā ahlihā* — to their rightful owners — was stressed as returning to those who are entitled, not to those who claim loudly. The phrase *ahl* — people of, those worthy and entitled — echoes the sūrah’s earlier *mawālī* — heirs appointed — and *dhī al-qurbā* — relatives present at distribution. Trusts are not returned to the strongest claimant but to the rightful one, as God appoints. The verse was linked to many occasions, from the keys of the Kaʿba to deposits generally, showing that the rule spans the sacred and the civil; what is entrusted — keys, property, testimony, office — must be returned to its proper holder.

**When You Judge Between People, Judge With Fairness**

The second command *wa idhā ḥakamtum bayna al-nāsi an taḥkumū bi al-ʿadl* — and when you judge between people, judge with fairness — pairs return of trusts with justice in judgment. *Ḥukm* — to judge, to decide — was read as any arbitrament: a judge between litigants, a family mediator between spouses (4:35), an executor dividing an estate, or a ruler distributing provision. *ʿAdl* — fairness, equity — was explained as putting each thing in its place, giving each his *naṣīb* — share — without favour. The pairing matters because returning trusts correctly often requires a judgment — who is the rightful owner, whose share is a half or sixth, whose dowry is due, whose trade was by mutual consent. The sūrah has prepared judgment with procedure: witnesses at handover (4:6), four witnesses before sanction for *fāḥishah* (4:15), two mediators from families (4:35), and the promise that God is Witness over all things (4:33). To judge with *ʿadl* in that setting is to align human judgment with the divine appointment of shares and with the prophetic clarification of how to apply them. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“when you judge, judge with justice; Allah loves the just,”* and this verse is the sūrah’s instance at the point where trusts meet disputes.

**What a Noble Commandment From Allah to You**

The exclamation *niʿimmā yaʿiẓukum bihi* — what a noble commandment, what an excellent exhortation Allah gives you — was read as praise for the two commands together. *Niʿimmā* — how good, how excellent — is commendation that marks the instruction as beneficial in itself, not only as duty. To be told to return trusts and judge with fairness is itself a favour, because a community that does so lives without the rancour that comes from withheld shares and crooked judgments. The sūrah has just contrasted the blaze prepared for those who reject signs with skin renewed for tasting (4:56) and Gardens with vast shade for those who believe and do good (4:57); this noble exhortation is the path between them made practical. Returning trusts and judging fairly are *ʿamal ṣāliḥ* — good deeds — that faith performs, and they lead to the Gardens’ shade. The phrase also echoes the sūrah’s repeated *“Allah wishes to make things clear and guide you to noble ways and turn to you”* (4:26); the exhortation is good because it clarifies, guides, and opens the turn. The community that hears *niʿimmā yaʿiẓukum* is being told that the instruction is not a burden added to weakness but a lightening, as humankind was created weak (4:28).

**Surely Allah Is All-Hearing, All-Seeing**

The closing *inna Allāha kāna samīʿan baṣīrā* — surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing — gives the watcher for trusts and judgments. *Samīʿ* — All-Hearing — was read as hearing the claim and the counterclaim, the kind word and the harsh, the just pronouncement and the distorted *rāʿinā* played with tongues (4:46). *Baṣīr* — All-Seeing — was read as seeing the handover, the mixing of wealth (4:2), the hasty consumption before the orphan grows (4:6), the trade by mutual consent or falsehood (4:29), and the mediation where harmony is desired and God brings it about (4:35). The sūrah has used similar closings — All-Knowing, All-Wise (4:11, 4:26), Most Forbearing (4:12), All-Aware (4:35), Witness over all (4:33), never wronging by an atom but multiplying good (4:40). Here hearing and seeing are chosen because trusts and judgments are acts of speech and sight: the judge hears testimony and sees records. To know that God hears and sees steadies both parties — the holder of the trust who might be tempted to withhold, and the judge who might be tempted to favour. What is heard and seen by Him need not be perfectly proved before people to be recorded; yet human hearing and seeing in courts and family meetings are the way the community aligns its practice with His.

**A Verse That Holds the Sūrah’s Ethics in One Line**

Placed between the promise of Gardens and the call to obey Allah and the Messenger and those in authority (4:59), this verse summarizes the sūrah’s public ethic in two imperatives. Return the trust, judge with fairness — the first governs what is held, the second governs how disputes about what is held are decided. Between them they cover the estate with orphans and heirs, the dowry with the bride, the market with mutual consent, and the home with mediators. The noble exhortation is that a community that does both will have little need for the punishments named — humiliating (4:37), blaze sufficient (4:55), Fire with skin renewed (4:56) — because the occasions for them will shrink. For the weak for whom burdens were lightened, the two commands are also achievable: returning a trust is a discrete act, and judging with fairness is a single stance taken again and again. The All-Hearing who hears the return and the All-Seeing who sees the judgment are the same who multiply an atom’s weight of good many times over (4:40), so that the noble commandment to return and to judge fairly is itself the path to the vast shade where trust is no longer at risk.
