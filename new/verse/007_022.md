## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:22

> So he brought about their fall through deception. And when they tasted of the tree, their nakedness was exposed to them, prompting them to cover themselves with leaves from Paradise. Then their Lord called out to them, “Did I not forbid you from that tree and ˹did I not˺ tell you that Satan is your sworn enemy?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fall Through Deception**

The verse states that the adversary brought about the fall of the pair through deception, and the Book's accounts of the event present the act as the result of a lure rather than of a contest. The sūrah of the Cow reports the same sequence in which the enemy caused the pair to slip from the Garden (2:36), and the sūrah of Ṭā Hā reports the temptation and the eating in the same order (20:120–21). The Book's descriptions of the enemy's methods elsewhere state that his promises are delusion, and the verse in front of us shows the first working of the method.

The phrase describes the manner of the fall, and the commentary tradition draws attention to the absence of any compulsion. The pair were not forced to eat; they were persuaded by a false description of the prohibition and by an oath. The Book's teaching about responsibility in the following verses rests on that fact, and the sūrah's warning to the children of Adam about the same deception treats them as free to accept or refuse.

**The Exposure of Nakedness**

The verse states that the nakedness of the pair was exposed to them when they tasted the tree, and the Book's account in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā states the same result (20:121). The exposure is described as a disclosure rather than a change in their bodies, and the tradition of commentary reports that a covering of light or of the Garden's clothing had concealed them until the act. The account therefore presents the loss as a consequence carried by the act itself.

The sūrah's following passage builds on the loss. The verse that tells the children of Adam that God has sent down clothing to cover their nakedness and to serve as adornment, and that the best clothing is the fear of God, follows the account of the pair's attempts to cover themselves (7:26). The sūrah's instructions about dress at worship and about modesty take the incident as their starting point.

**The Leaves of the Garden**

The verse reports that the pair began to sew together the leaves of the Garden to cover themselves, and the detail is one of the narrative's most concrete. The commentary tradition identifies the leaves with those of the fig tree, and it notes that the pair's own effort at covering is recorded alongside the divine gift of clothing that the sūrah announces shortly afterwards. The contrast between the improvised covering and the clothing that God provides is part of the passage's teaching.

The Book's presentation of clothing in the following verses includes both the covering of the body and the adornment that goes beyond it, and the sūrah's instruction to wear one's adornment at every place of worship is placed in that context. The leaves in front of us are the first clothing of the human race, and the sūrah's treatment of dress begins from them.

**The Divine Question**

The verse reports the call of the Lord to the pair asking whether He had not forbidden them the tree and told them that Satan was a manifest enemy. The question is not an inquiry after information, and the commentary tradition reads it as a rebuke that establishes the terms of the responsibility. The pair had been warned in the command itself, and the enemy had been named.

The form of the question appears elsewhere in the Book as a means of confronting those who have no answer, and the sūrah's later passages use the same device when they report the questions put to the condemned. The verse in front of us places the question at the moment of the fall, and the pair's answer in the following verse is the model of the response that the sūrah commends.

**The Enemy Named as a Manifest Enemy**

The verse states that the pair had been told that Satan was their manifest enemy, and the sūrah's warnings to the children of Adam repeat the description. The verse that addresses the children of Adam shortly afterwards says that he and his tribe see them from where they cannot see him (7:27), and the sūrah of the Cow instructs the community not to follow the footsteps of the enemy. The description in front of us is therefore given twice in the same narrative: once to the pair and once to their descendants.

The Book's treatment of the enemy's enmity runs through its accounts of the prophets and their peoples, and the sūrah's later passages describe the leaders of misguidance who will be gathered into the Fire with their followers. The verse in front of us states the warning that the pair received, and the sūrah's instruction to their children repeats it as a warning that remains in force.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands twenty-second in the chapter, and it completes the account of the temptation that began with the whisper. The pair's confession follows in the next verse, and the narrative then reports the command to descend to the earth and the provision that has been placed there. The verse in front of us is the point at which the account moves from the forbidden act to its consequence.

The reader of the sūrah has been given the boundary, the deception, the oath, the fall, the exposure, and the rebuke. The chapter's instruction to the children of Adam about clothing, food, and worship takes its force from this sequence, and the reader is placed among the descendants who are addressed.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the words of the pair, that they have wronged themselves and that if God does not forgive them and have mercy on them they will be among the losers (7:23). The rebuke in front of us receives the answer that the sūrah presents as the right response to a fall, and the narrative then states the descent and the terms of life on the earth.

The two verses together contrast the fall with the confession, and the sūrah's teaching about repentance is built on the contrast. The command was given, the enemy promised, the pair crossed the boundary, and they answered the question with an admission rather than an argument.
