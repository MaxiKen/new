## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:113

> Yet they are not all alike: there are some among the People of the Book who are upright, who recite Allah’s revelations throughout the night, prostrating ˹in prayer˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Qualification That Prevents a Blanket Judgment**

The verse begins by stating that the People of the Book are not all alike, and it then describes one part of them: an upright community that recites God's revelations through the night while prostrating themselves. The sentence follows the previous verse's account of a people struck with humiliation.

The Book's judgment is thus qualified immediately after being stated. Some commentators understood the qualification as a distinction within the People of the Book itself; others read it as a comparison between the believers and the other communities. The two readings agree that the verse forbids the reader to close the account on a whole people, which is the practical point of the sentence.

**What the Word for Upright Carries**

The word describing the group means standing, and it carries the senses of firmness, justice, and the standing posture of prayer. Al-Ṭabarī noted its association with fairness, and others explained the upright as those who maintain the limits God has set and do not distort what they hold.

The word's double sense fits the verse's content, because the same group is then described as praying through the night. What is being described is a portion of a community that remained straight in its conduct and constant in its worship, and the description is offered as the reason the blanket judgment of the previous verse cannot be applied to everyone.

**Night Worship in the Book's Vocabulary**

The Book returns often to the practice of praying in the dark. One passage describes people who abandon their beds to call on their Lord in hope and fear and to give from what they have been provided (32:16). Another places among the righteous those who are patient, truthful, obedient, and who seek forgiveness before dawn (3:17).

This sūrah itself contains the instruction to rise in the last part of the night for additional prayer (17:79), and the command to recite the Book in measured tones comes in the same context. The group described in the verse under discussion is therefore performing a practice the Book commends to its own community, and the passage is acknowledging the practice wherever it is found.

**Why the Night Is Named**

The night carries a significance in these passages beyond the hour. Prayer in the dark is hidden from people, and the Book's descriptions of it repeatedly pair hope with fear, which is the state of a person alone with his Lord.

A practice that no one sees is a practice that cannot be performed for display, and the verse's description of a community reciting the revelation in the small hours is a description of sincerity as much as of devotion. That is why the sūrah's mention of the group at this point in the argument matters: after a passage about communities that fought against the truth and communities that were drawn away from it, the Book names people whose worship was conducted where only God could see it.

**The Reports About the Occasion**

The commentaries record several occasions for the verse's revelation. One describes the believers waiting in the mosque for the Prophet to lead the night prayer, and his telling them that no people from any religion were remembering God at that hour besides them. Other reports name groups from Najrān, from Abyssinia, and from Byzantine territory who followed the religion of Jesus and affirmed the truth of the message. A further report describes a people who prayed between sunset and the end of twilight.

**How the Verse Sits Between Two Judgments**

The verse stands between the statement that most of the People of the Book were false to what they held and the description of the specific deeds of the upright group. Its position gives the passage its shape.

A reader who has followed the argument from the earlier chapters of this sūrah has heard the community's case against the People of the Book developed at length. This verse interrupts that development to name a part of them that the case does not cover, and the sentences that follow describe that part in detail. The Book's habit of holding both facts in view at once is the reason the passage can be read without producing contempt for a people as such.

**The Standard the Description Sets**

The group is identified by conduct: standing firm, reciting the revelation, and prostrating in worship at night. Nothing in the description depends on lineage or history.

That makes the verse usable as a standard rather than only as a historical note. The community receiving the sūrah is given the marks of the people it is told to respect and recognise among others, and those marks are practices its own revelation commands. The verses that follow add the rest of the description, and the whole picture supplies the answer to the question of what belief looks like when it is found outside the community that holds the final message.

**Recitation and Prostration as One Act**

The verse pairs the recitation of God's revelations with prostration, and the pairing describes a single act of worship rather than two practices that happen to be listed together.

Reading and bowing belong together in the Book's treatment of prayer, where the recitation is the content of the standing and the prostration is its completion. The night prayer that this sūrah commands combines them, and the command to recite in measured tones occurs in the same passage. The group described in the verse is therefore engaged in the practice the receiving community is itself instructed to perform, which is why the description can serve as a standard of recognition rather than only as a report about others.
