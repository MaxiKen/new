## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:189

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ownership Stated**

The verse states that to God belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth and that He is powerful over all things, and the sentence supplies the fact that governs everything the passage has been discussing. The Book states the same ownership in many places, and it usually does so where claims of power or standing are in question.

The sentence's position in the passage is significant, since it follows a description of people who were pleased with their own standing. It follows a warning to people who took pleasure in their position and expected credit for what they had not done, and it states where the position they valued actually belongs. The sovereignty over the heavens and the earth is not distributed among the people who believe they hold it.

**The Book's Recurring Formula**

The Book states that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to God in a series of passages, and the contexts vary widely. One passage uses the statement to answer those who said that the Messiah was God, and it asks who could prevent God if He willed to destroy the Messiah and his mother and everyone on earth.

Another passage states that the kingdom belongs to God and that the final return is to Him. A third states that whatever is in the heavens and the earth belongs to Him, and that He will call people to account for what they reveal and what they conceal. The sentence in this verse belongs to that group, and its function is consistently to place every claim of authority under a higher one.


The verse adds that God is powerful over all things, and the phrase is one the sūrah has used at several points, including the close of the passage about the affliction after the battle. The declaration covers the exercise of the sovereignty that the first half of the sentence has asserted.

The Book's pairing of ownership with power is deliberate. The claim that the heavens and the earth belong to God is a statement of right, and the statement of power explains why the right cannot be contested. The two together leave the reader with no place to stand outside the arrangement the verse describes.

**Why the Sentence Follows the Warning**

The verse before the reader comes after a description of people who were pleased with themselves and sought praise for what they had not done, and it names the owner of everything they were competing over. The connection between the two verses is the subject of standing.

The community's opponents had their positions, their learning, and their claims to be the people of God. The verse states that sovereignty belongs elsewhere, and the placement gives the sentence the quality of a correction. What the earlier verse warned against was a misreading of one's position, and this verse supplies the correct account of where position comes from, since all standing in the world is held on terms set by its owner.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Treatment of Dominion**

The sūrah has already dealt with the subject at its opening, where it addresses God as the owner of all dominion who gives it to whom He wills and takes it from whom He wills. The passage describes the granting and removal of authority as matters within a single hand.

The verse before the reader states the same principle in general terms. The sūrah's argument with the community's opponents has repeatedly touched on the question of who holds authority over guidance, and the answer is given here in the form of a statement about the whole creation, which leaves no room for a rival claim.

**The Final Return Joined to the Sovereignty**

The Book frequently pairs the statement of ownership with the statement that the return is to God, and the pairing gives the ownership a practical consequence. The one who owns everything is the one to whom everything returns, and the account of human conduct is settled within that ownership.

The verse before the reader states the first half of that pair, and the passages in the other sūrahs supply the second. The community that recites it is being given a fact about the arrangement of the world rather than an instruction about a particular matter, and the Book uses such statements to reorder the reader's sense of what matters.

**The Position of the Oppressed**

The verse has a particular resonance for people whose cause is being defeated and whose opponents appear to hold every advantage. The sentence states that the sovereignty they see exercised is held on loan from the one to whom it belongs, and the Book's uses of the formula consistently occur in such settings.

The community at Madinah had seen its enemies gain ground and had been told about the fate of the groups whose conduct the sūrah has been describing in the passages about the People of the Book and the idolaters. The verse's statement of ownership supplies the frame in which all of that is to be read, and the sentences that follow turn to the signs of God in the creation.

**A Statement of Fact and Its Use**

The verse is short and consists of two declarations, and the community has used it as a statement of the ground on which its confidence rests. The Book places such sentences at the ends of arguments, and their function is to leave the reader with the fact that resolves the particular difficulty.

The sūrah's closing movement begins with this declaration and continues into a description of the people who draw the right conclusion from what they observe in the world around them. The next verse turns to that observation, and the passage builds from the statement of ownership to the reflection of those who understand.
