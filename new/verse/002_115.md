## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:115

> To Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah. Surely Allah is AllEncompassing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence About Direction**

The verse states a fact about the ownership of the horizons and then draws a conclusion about prayer: wherever a person turns, he is facing God. The Book's argument moves from a claim about sovereignty to a practical assurance, and the sūrah will return to the subject of the direction of prayer in the verses that follow.

The sentence stands in the middle of a passage about the Kaʿbah, the Temple, and the obstruction of worship, and it prepares the reader for the change of direction that the sūrah announces later. Its function is to establish that the horizons themselves belong to God, so that no direction has any claim to exclusivity on its own account. The sentence also anticipates the dispute the sūrah is about to describe, when the change of direction became a subject of argument among the communities of Madinah. By assigning the horizons to God, the verse removes the ground on which any direction could claim a privilege of its own, and it prepares the reader for the instruction that follows without pre-empting it.

**The Question of the Direction of Prayer**

Commentators connected the verse to two matters. The first is the practical one, since the community's prayer was at first oriented toward Jerusalem and later toward the Kaʿbah, and some readers took the sentence as permission to pray in any direction at all. Reports indicate that the permission was understood to be abrogated by the verse that established the new direction, and other commentators applied it to voluntary prayer offered while travelling on a mount.

The second matter is doctrinal, and it concerns what it means that God is present wherever a person turns. The commentators were careful to exclude any suggestion that God occupies a place, since a body can be in only one location at a time, and the phrase was explained in several ways: as an affirmation that the worshipper meets God's acceptance, that God is the one addressed in every direction, or that no direction is devoid of His knowledge and care.

**The Reported Occasions**

The commentaries preserve several settings for the sentence. One describes Muslims who could not determine the direction of prayer during the night and later discovered that they had faced the wrong way. Another connects it to the death of the Negus, the king of Abyssinia who had sheltered the earliest refugees, when some objected that he had not faced the direction of prayer used in Madinah. A third concerns a question about which direction to face when calling upon God.

Where several occasions are preserved for a single verse, the commentators often concluded that the occasion is not decisive for its meaning. What the verse states is a principle that the community needed for several reasons at once, and the reported settings illustrate its uses rather than limiting it.

**The Face of God**

The phrase rendered as facing God is literally a reference to His face, and the Book uses the expression for the divine presence and acceptance rather than for a bodily feature. Elsewhere the Qur'an states that everything will perish except His face, and that there remains the face of the Lord, full of majesty and honour, and those passages shaped the understanding of this one.

The Islamic objections to describing God in bodily terms rest on the Book's own statement that nothing is like Him, and the commentators applied that principle here. The verse speaks about direction in order to deny that worship is bounded by geography; it does not suggest that the divine being can be encountered by turning a body toward a point in space.

**All-Encompassing and All-Knowing**

The two names at the end of the sentence describe the scope of God's generosity and knowledge. The first term carries the sense of breadth, and readers noted that it can also mean unstinting, which fits a verse about worship being available in every place. The Book uses the same wording when it states that God's mercy encompasses all things.

Al-Rāzī drew a connection with the previous verse's account of ruined places of worship and observed that although buildings can be destroyed, the remembrance of God cannot be confined to them, since He is present wherever a worshipper turns. That observation gives the two sentences together a consoling force: the opponents of the community could close doors, but they could not restrict access to their Lord. The two names also describe what the community needed in its situation: breadth to reach those excluded from the sanctuaries, and knowledge to weigh the intentions of worshippers who could not determine the direction they were facing. The Book's names are frequently chosen to match the subject of the sentence, and that is the case here.

**Wherever You Turn**

The Book states in other passages that God is with His servants wherever they are, and that He is the first and the last, the outward and the inward. The verse in this place belongs to that group of texts, and it has been read by some as one of the scriptural foundations for the teaching that nothing is outside God's presence.

What the sentence gives the sūrah's first audience is a practical confidence and a theological correction in one. A community that had been expelled from its sanctuaries, blocked from the Kaʿbah, and slandered for its direction of prayer is told that the horizons belong to God and that none of the restrictions imposed on it could make worship impossible. The Book's insistence that God is present to His servants wherever they are gives the community a confidence that does not depend on access to a particular building. The sūrah has just described people who closed places of worship, and the verse answers that conduct by stating that no wall can confine the relation between a worshipper and his Lord.

**How the Verse Was Used in Practice**

The permission stated in the sentence found a place in the community's practice beyond the question of the canonical prayer. Reports describe the Prophet ﷺ praying while riding in the direction his mount was facing when he offered voluntary prayers on a journey, and the jurists connected that practice with this verse and with the reports that accompany it. The sentence was also cited in cases where a person could not determine the direction of prayer, since the Book's statement that the horizons belong to God settles the matter for anyone who has done what he can. What the verse does not do is release the community from the obligation to face the Kaʿbah when the direction is known, since the sūrah itself establishes that direction later in the passage. The reading the commentators preferred is therefore one of provision rather than of licence: the Book supplies a way for worship to continue where circumstances make the ordinary rules impossible, and it leaves the standard in place wherever it can be met.
