## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:115

> To Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah. Surely Allah is AllEncompassing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lord of Every Direction**

The verse answers the crime of the previous one with a declaration of ownership, and the ownership is geographical in its totality: *to Allah belong the east and the west*. The pairing names the sun's whole journey — its rising and its setting, and by merism, everything between: the full circle of directions, the complete compass of turning. The Qur'an's direction-verses run as a family around this declaration: He is *the Lord of the east and the west, there is no god except Him* (73:9); *Lord of the two easts and the two wests* (55:17), the two solstitial risings and settings; and the oath-form at 70:40 — *I do swear by the Lord of all the points of sunrise and sunset* — the plural of plurals, every degree of every horizon. Sūrah al-Nūr's Light Verse places its blessed olive tree *neither to the east nor of the west* (24:35), beyond the directions entirely, and the placement completes the doctrine from its other side: the light that illuminates the directions is not itself directional. Verse 115's function in the family is the consequence it draws: since every direction is His property, *wherever you turn you are facing towards Allah* — the turning of a creature inside owned space cannot exit the Owner's frontage. The verse arrives after the mosque-ruin verse with its answer already shaped: houses can be barred and destroyed, but the qibla of reality is not a building — it is the whole of created space, and its every point faces its Owner.

**The Occasions: A Verse With Several Doors**

The commentators attach this verse to a cluster of occasions, and the cluster is instructive in its variety. The dominant connection is to the prayer-direction: the community prayed first toward Jerusalem, and after the qibla's turn to the Kaʿbah (legislated at verse 144), some held that this verse had permitted prayer toward any direction before being superseded. A report preserved by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qushayrī gives the verse a pastoral occasion: Muslims who traveled by night, unable to discern the direction, prayed facing where they judged — and later learned they had faced wrongly; the verse arrived as reassurance that their prayer stood. Al-Jurayrī's circle connected it to the permission for voluntary prayer on mount, facing the direction of travel. And the tradition preserves the occasion of the Negus: when the Abyssinian king who had sheltered the Makkah's refugees died, the Prophet ﷺ announced *your brother has died; pray for him* — and when some objected that he was not a Muslim, the verse of Āl ʿImrān on the believing People of the Book descended (3:199); when they objected that the Negus had not faced the qibla in his prayers, this verse descended. The exegetes' own method with such clusters is neutrality: the occasions do not decide the verse, whose doctrinal content is profound and universal. The legal tradition followed the same line — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī titles its qibla-chapter for the ruling that prayer offered mistakenly toward a wrong direction need not be repeated — and the chapter's placement makes this verse's reassurance a standing principle of the law: direction is commanded, error within sincere effort is absorbed, and the Face is not confined to the point the compass found.

**“There Is the Face of Allah”**

The verse's central clause has been read at several depths, and the tradition records each. The plain readings take the turn's destination functionally: wherever you turn, there is Allah — His qibla, in al-Ṭabarī's gloss; His contentment, in al-Rāzī's — the turning of sincere prayer arrives regardless of the direction's accident. The deeper reading takes the Face in the Qur'an's own usage of it: everything perishes except He Himself (28:88); every being on earth passes away and only the Lord's own reality, full of majesty and honor, remains (55:26–27). The Face is what survives every perishing — and the verse's claim is that whichever way a creature turns, it is toward the Surviving. Al-Rāzī draws from the clause's grammar an argument he considers decisive against corporealism: the phrase *wherever you turn* is itself the strongest proof that Allah is not a body, since a body occupies one place and cannot be in several at once — the ubiquity of the facing is possible only for One beyond location. The Qur'an's with-you verses supply the same doctrine's companion texts: *He is with you wherever you are* (57:4); He is with them wherever they are (58:7). The clause therefore does double work: it consoles the sincere turner, whose direction may err while his facing does not, and it instructs the theologian, whose language about Allah must be purged of place — the Face is everywhere not by diffusion but by transcendence of the everywhere.

**All-Encompassing, All-Knowing**

The two closing names gather the verse's two horizons. *All-Encompassing* — *wāsiʿ*, the Vast, which al-Qushayrī notes can also render the unstinting, the generous: an embrace that is simultaneously spatial and dispositional. The name's scriptural range is wide — *My mercy encompasses everything* (7:156), your Lord of vast forgiveness (53:32) — and al-Rāzī connects it directly to the verse's occasion in the passage: despite the ruin of mosques announced in verse 114, Allah's remembrance cannot be hindered, because the One whose worship the barred houses held is Himself the Vast — wider than every house, so that no bar, no demolition, no confiscated sanctuary can put His mention under constraint. *All-Knowing* completes the pair from the other attribute: the Vast knows each turning — the night traveler's sincere guess, the mount-rider's facing, the dead king's prayers in a direction he never learned — and the knowledge is the guarantee that the vastness is not indifference. The two names together answer the occasions' common anxiety: the direction uncertain (He is Vast), the effort unseen (He is Knowing), the prayer possibly misfaced (He encompasses and records). The verse's theology of worship closes on the same note the sūrah's theology of prayer always closes on: the form is commanded because form disciplines the heart, and the heart is accepted because the Knower of the heart owns every direction the form could face.

**The Verse of Presence**

The esoteric tradition reads verse 115 as one of the scriptural foundations of its central doctrine, and the reading belongs to any complete account of the verse's reception. The omnipresence the clause announces — whatever way you turn, the Face — was developed in the Sufi readings into the doctrine of the divine presence pervading existence: He is the First and the Last, the Most High and Most Near (57:3), manifest in all things while *there is nothing like Him* (42:11) — the two affirmations held together without collapse, presence without pantheism, transcendence without absence. On this reading the verse is the oneness-of-being doctrine's Qur'anic anchor: not that creation is God, but that there is nowhere to turn that is not already facing Him, because the turning itself occurs inside His encompassment. The verse's practical fruits in the tradition are two, and both answer verse 114. For the worshipper whose house is taken: the qibla survives the mosque, because the true qibla was never the walls — the direction stands wherever the Vast is turned toward, and He is Vast past every confiscation. For the worshipper whose house stands: the reminder that no building holds the Face, so no building's possession settles anything — the entry rule of verse 114 remains, humility at the door, the Name as the only business inside. Between the ruined house and the standing one, verse 115 installs the doctrine both need: the east and the west are His, the Face is where sincerity turns, and the Encompassing knows every turning by name.
