## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:76

> Absolutely! Those who honour their trusts and shun evil—surely Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Previous Claim**

The verse responds directly to the justification reported a line earlier, where the community had defended its conduct toward outsiders by claiming that its obligations did not reach them. The answer is not an argument about the clause; it is a statement about who enjoys God's approval.

The Book often answers a false principle this way, by replacing it rather than refuting it. The claim under discussion was a rule about which people a community owed honest dealing; the answer given is a description of the conduct God approves of, and the description turns on the fulfilment of trust. The reader who compares the two sentences sees that the disputed clause has been set aside rather than engaged, and the ground of the comparison is what the Book is looking for in people, which the closing phrase names as mindfulness of God.

**The Word for What Must Be Honoured**

The term rendered "their trusts" covers more than a deposit held for safekeeping. It describes everything that has been placed in a person's care, including property, agreements, and the obligations of a role. The Book uses it for duties of office, for the treatment of dependents, and for the whole of a community's inheritance of revelation.

Reading the word in its widest sense explains why the verse can answer a question about commercial dealing with a statement about divine approval. The deposit of a coin and the inheritance of a book are both trusts, and the Book's treatment of the two is the same in kind: what has been entrusted remains the property of the one who entrusted it, and the person holding it is accountable for how he handles it. A community that had convinced itself that one class of trust did not bind it had, by that reasoning, loosened a principle that reached far beyond the case in front of it.

**Mindfulness as the Second Condition**

The verse names two things: the fulfilment of trusts and mindfulness of God. The second is not a decoration on the first. In the Book's usage, *taqwā* is the guardedness that keeps a person aware of his accountability; it is what makes the fulfilment of a trust a matter of conscience rather than convenience.

The pairing appears in the sūrah's earlier description of the people who inherit the Garden, and the same two elements recur in the Book's portraits of the righteous. What the sūrah is doing here is placing the conduct it has been describing against a standard the community itself would have recognised — honest dealing and awareness of God — and pointing out that the two belong together. A person who fulfils a trust because he fears consequences is performing the act; a person who does it because he is mindful of God has understood what the act is for, and it is the second kind of fulfilment that the verse attaches to God's approval.

**God Loves the Mindful**

The verse's last clause states the reward in the vocabulary the sūrah has been using since the passage about love opened. In an earlier verse the Book told its readers that if they love God, following the Prophet would produce love in return; here the love is attached to a specific conduct, and the conduct is the honest handling of what one holds.

The Book's attributions of divine love are instructive when they are gathered together. God loves those who do good, those who are mindful, those who rely on Him, and those who act justly; the list is made up of conduct rather than of status. The community addressed in this passage had a great deal of status and was in the middle of an argument about it, and the verse ends by naming the conduct God's approval follows. The verse therefore supplies both the answer to the claim and a description of what is being asked in place of the system the claim represented.

**What the Verse Confirms About the Community's Diversity**

The passage as a whole has been distinguishing between people within the same community, and this verse continues that work. The previous verse said that among the People of the Book there are those who return a large deposit and those who will not release a small one; this verse states the principle that decides between them, and the sūrah will later state explicitly that they are not all alike.

That pattern is important for how the passage is read. The Book is not conducting a case against a people; it is describing a conduct and naming the standard that judges it, and the standard applies inside as well as outside the group. The verses that follow continue in this vein, moving from the honest trustee to those who trade their covenant for a small price, and the reader is being shown a range rather than a verdict on a whole community.

**A Standard That Applies to the Reader**

The clause about trust is one of those sentences in the Book that addresses every reader regardless of his community, since everyone holds something that belongs to someone else. Property, a position, a confidence, and a text are all entrusted, and the book's measure is the same for all of them.

The sūrah leaves the reader with the connection between the two halves of the sentence: the fulfilment and the mindfulness belong to one another, and the approval follows from them. What the passage does next, in the following verses, is to describe the conduct of the parties who did the opposite, and the contrast is drawn so that the reader can see both the standard and what departing from it looks like.
