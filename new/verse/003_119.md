## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:119

> Here you are! You love them but they do not love you, and you believe in all Scriptures. When they meet you they say, “We believe.” But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹May you˺ die of your rage!” Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Love That Is Not Returned**

The verse addresses the believers as people who love those who do not love them back, and the commentators set out several ways of understanding the love in view. It could mean the affection that believers are expected to hold for all people as fellow creatures of God; it could mean the wish that others should be guided, which is a form of good will; or it could mean that the believers do not wish their opponents to fall into difficulty.

The three readings share a common feature: the believers' stance is generous, and it is contrasted with an attitude that is not. The sentence is therefore not a complaint but a description of an asymmetry, and the verses that follow state the remedy for living with it.

**"You Believe in All the Scriptures"**

The verse names the believers' acceptance of every revelation as part of the contrast. Muslims affirmed the scriptures that had come before, including those held by the communities that opposed them, while those communities rejected the message that came last.

The Book says as much in the sūrah of the Cow, where the believers are described as accepting God, His angels, His books, and His messengers without making distinctions among them. The point in this verse is the imbalance it produces: the community that believed in everything is being opposed by people who believe in part, and the sentence makes that disproportion part of the accusation.

**Biting the Fingertips in Rage**

The verse describes what those people do when they are away from the believers: they bite their fingertips in anger. The gesture is an idiom for a fury that has nowhere to go, and the Book uses it for the reaction of people whose opposition has achieved nothing.

The same double speech appears elsewhere in the Book, where it is said of such people that they tell the believers they believe and then tell their own associates that they were only mocking (2:14). The verse before the reader puts the reaction in the body rather than in words, and the physical image conveys a rage that is visible to the onlookers and useless to the one who feels it.

**The Command to Say, "Die in Your Rage"**

The verse instructs the Prophet to tell them to die of their rage, and the commentators read the phrase as naming the cause of their fury rather than as a direct request for their death. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī explained it as a statement that they will not see what they want: their rage is produced by the success of the community they oppose, and that success is not going to stop.

The Book uses this kind of statement at points where an opponent's emotion is being described, and the sentence is a response to the situation rather than a threat. What it tells the reader is that the intensity of an adversary's feeling is not a measure of his prospects.

**The Knowledge of What Hearts Conceal**

The sentence ends with the statement that God knows best what lies hidden in hearts, and the clause returns to the theme of the verse before it. The gap between what people say and what they hold is the subject of this passage, and the verse closes it with a reminder about who has access to the hidden side.

The sūrah repeats this formula at several points in the passage, and its function is consistent. The believers cannot know what is being planned against them, and they are not required to; what they are required to do is to act on what has been made known to them. The verse supplies the assurance that the unknown part is not ungoverned.

**The Emotional Register of the Verse**

The passage before this one issued warnings and instructions. This verse speaks about the community's own affections, and it does so with an honesty that is unusual in a text concerned with conduct.

The community is told that it loves people who do not love it, which is a description of a painful situation rather than of a moral failing. The Book's method here is to name the situation exactly, so that the instruction to be careful about intimacy sits alongside the recognition that the believers' disposition toward others was generous. Both facts are stated in the same passage, and neither cancels the other.

**The Stance the Verse Teaches**

The Book does not ask the community to stop loving people in order to protect itself. It asks for a distinction between the affections that a believer holds for others and the disclosures he makes to them.

That distinction is the verse's practical content. A person may wish good for those who oppose him, and may still decline to place his affairs in their hands; the passage has already given that instruction in the previous verse. The reader is being taught to keep his good will and his confidences in separate places, which is a harder discipline than either hostility or trust.

**The Verse in the Passage's Sequence**

The passage is building a portrait of the opposition and the community's response to it. The previous verse described people who would spare no effort to cause harm; this one describes their reaction to the community's successes and failures; the next continues the description and supplies the believers' remedy.

The order moves from what such people are to what they feel and finally to what the community should do, and the sequence ends in the instruction about patience and mindfulness. The verse before the reader is the middle term, and its function is to make the community's own generosity and the opposition's resentment visible at the same time.
