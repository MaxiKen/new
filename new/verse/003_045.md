## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:45

> ˹Remember˺ when the angels proclaimed, “O Mary! Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him, his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary; honoured in this world and the Hereafter, and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Announcement to a Mother About Her Child**

The verse resumes the angelic address begun at verse 42. Mary has been told that God has selected and purified her; she is now told that she will have a son, and the son is described before he exists. Everything the sūrah will say about him in the disputes that follow begins from this announcement, and the announcement comes to his mother.

The narrative order deserves notice. The Book does not introduce Jesus through controversy, through the claims of a community, or through a genealogy of prophets. It introduces him as news given to a woman, in the same way John was announced to his father, and the sūrah's later argument about his nature is conducted against that background rather than instead of it.

**"A Word From Him" — the Phrase and What It Carries**

The child is called a word from God, and the phrase has occupied commentators and theologians since the earliest generations. The Book uses the same language elsewhere when it addresses the People of the Book: the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, is described as the fulfilment of God's Word through Mary and a spirit from Him, in a verse that immediately forbids the extremes of doctrine (4:171).

Commentators explained the phrase in several ways. Some read it as a statement about the means by which God's command became manifest in the world, comparing it to the way a ruler's justice may be said to be present in his deputy; others linked it to the fact that Jesus came into being by the command "Be" without the ordinary cause, so that his association with the word is direct and unmediated. The sūrah itself does not press an explanation; it states the honour and, three verses later, states the means, and it leaves the reader with both.

**The Two Names and the One That Anchors Him**

The announcement gives the child two names and a lineage: the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary. The honorific *al-Masīḥ* corresponds in meaning to the Hebrew word for anointed, a title applied in the earlier scriptures to kings and priests, and used in Israel's history for figures well outside the line of prophets.

The third element of the name is the one that does the most work in the sūrah. Jesus is called the son of Mary, before he is anything else, and the Book uses this form of his name throughout. It anchors him to a human mother, which is a claim about his nature as well as a marker of identity, and it also honours the woman who raised him. Every later statement in the sūrah about his prophethood rests on a name that never loses the mother.

**"Honoured in This World and the Hereafter"**

The child is promised honour in both worlds, and the promise covers the whole span of his story. In this world, the Book says, God delivered him from those who disbelieved and elevated his followers above the deniers until the Day of Judgment (3:55), and the record of his miracles is preserved in the scripture he brought.

In the Hereafter the honour is stated in the same Book that insists on his servanthood: he is a servant whom God favoured and made an example for the Children of Israel (43:59). The two descriptions belong on one page. The Book's presentation of Jesus is consistently double: extraordinary in his birth and station, a servant in his relation to God, and it never lets either half of that description displace the other.

**"One of Those Nearest to Allah"**

The last phrase names a company: those brought near. The Book calls the foremost believers the ones nearest to God (56:11), and the same category is used of the blessed in the Hereafter, whose record is witnessed by the nearest to God and whose drink flows from a spring reserved for them.

Being placed in that company is the highest honour the sūrah can name, and it is worth reading carefully because it is a ranking within creation rather than a statement about divinity. The company has members. A person does not join a company by being its origin; he joins it by being brought near to someone else, and the phrase itself says so. What the announcement promises the child is proximity to God, which is the promise made to the righteous generally, in the sharpest form available.

**Servant and Sign: How the Sūrah Holds Both Together**

The chapter will repeatedly insist on a description that the announcement's language might appear to soften. Jesus speaks in the cradle and identifies himself, in the words the sūrah records elsewhere, as a servant of God who has been given scripture and prophethood (19:30).

Holding the honour and the servanthood together is what makes the sūrah's Christology coherent. A being created by a command, born of a woman, sustained by God's provision, who speaks as an infant and returns to his Lord, is a sign; and a sign is only a sign if it points away from itself. The announcement in this verse is the sūrah's fullest statement of what he was given, and the next two verses supply the conditions of his birth and the record of his speech.

**What the Announcement Asks of Its Reader**

Everything the verse promises is given to a child who has not been conceived, in a message delivered to a woman with no husband. The reader is asked, before any argument about doctrine is joined, to hold a set of facts that have no explanation inside them: a word from God, a name, an honoured station, and a place among the nearest.

The sūrah's argumentative work about the nature of Jesus begins from these facts and never departs from them. What follows in the passage is the question the mother asks and the answer she receives.
