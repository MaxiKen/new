## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:34

> Men are the caretakers of women, as men have been provisioned by Allah over women and tasked with supporting them financially. And righteous women are devoutly obedient and, when alone, protective of what Allah has entrusted them with. And if you sense ill-conduct from your women, advise them ˹first˺, ˹if they persist,˺ do not share their beds, ˹but if they still persist,˺ then discipline them ˹gently˺. But if they change their ways, do not be unjust to them. Surely Allah is Most High, All-Great.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Caretakers, Not Controllers**

The verse opens with *al-rijālu qawwāmūna ʿalā al-nisāʾ bi mā faḍḍala Allāhu baʿḍahum ʿalā baʿḍ wa bi mā anfaqū min amwālihim* — men are the caretakers of women, as men have been provisioned by Allah over women and tasked with supporting them financially. The word *qawwām* — caretaker, maintainer, one who stands and keeps upright — is stronger than a claim to superiority; it describes a role of standing guard, providing, and managing affairs so that the household remains upright. That role is grounded in two facts, not in a claim of greater worth. First, *bi mā faḍḍala Allāhu baʿḍahum ʿalā baʿḍ* — as God has provisioned some over others — which the sūrah earlier framed as bounty to be asked for rather than envied (4:32), and which elsewhere appears as differences in strength and in means. Second, *wa bi mā anfaqū min amwālihim* — and as they spend from their wealth — the financial maintenance the sūrah has already secured through the dowry as a gracious gift (4:4), the prohibition on reclaiming it (4:20–21), and the inheritance fractions that assign the larger share to the party bearing maintenance. The verse therefore ties authority to responsibility: the caretaking is not license to dominate but assignment to provide. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“do not crave what Allah has given some over others”* (4:32), and here it directs that what was given — provision and spending — be used to keep the other standing.

**Devoutly Obedient and Protective When Alone**

The description *fa al-ṣāliḥātu qānitātun ḥāfiẓātun lil-ghaybi bi mā ḥafiẓa Allāh* — righteous women are devoutly obedient and, when alone, protective of what Allah has entrusted them with — pairs obedience to God with guardianship in absence. *Qānitāt* — devoutly obedient, humbly constant — was read as obedience to God that includes fidelity to the marital trust, while *ḥāfiẓāt lil-ghayb* — protective in the unseen, when alone — was read as guarding chastity, property, and household secrets when the husband is not present, because God has guarded them. The phrase *bi mā ḥafiẓa Allāh* links her guarding to God’s guarding; she protects what He has enabled her to protect. The sūrah has already described wealth as a standing support to be kept upright (4:5) and the orphan’s property as requiring witnesses and reckoning (4:6). Here that ethic of guarding appears inside marriage: what is entrusted — wealth from dowry and maintenance, the home, the children, and honour — is to be kept faithfully in absence as in presence. The verse elsewhere praises *“those who guard their chastity except with spouses”* and the same guard is implied here, so that the home remains a shelter — *muḥṣin* — as lawful marriage was defined (4:24–25).

**Three Steps When Ill-Conduct Is Sensed**

The counsel *wa allātī takhāfūna nushūzahunna fa ʿiẓūhunna wa ahjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿ wa aḍribūhunna* — if you sense ill-conduct from your women, advise them first, if they persist, do not share their beds, but if they still persist, then discipline them gently — sets a graded response to *nushūz* — rising up, ill-conduct, breach of marital harmony. The first step *ʿiẓūhunna* — advise, admonish — is speech that reminds of God, of limits, and of the covenant as a firm commitment (4:21). The second step *ahjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿ* — do not share their beds, forsake them in the sleeping place — was read as withholding intimate closeness within the home, not as expelling or humiliating in public, a pause that signals seriousness without severing. The third step *aḍribūhunna* was read by early authorities and by Prophetic clarification as a light, non-injurious strike that does not wound, break, or humiliate, and many commentators and jurists emphasized that the Prophet ﷺ never struck a wife and described the best men as those who do not do so, with some holding that abandoning the third step is more excellent. The grading matters: each step is only reached if the prior did not amend, and each is bounded. The sūrah elsewhere commands *“live with them in kindness”* (4:19) and says to appoint mediators when a split is feared (4:35), showing that the aim is amendment and reconciliation, not punishment for its own sake.

**But If They Change Their Ways, Do Not Be Unjust to Them**

The limit *fa in aṭaʿnakum fa lā tabghū ʿalayhinna sabīlā* — but if they change their ways, do not be unjust to them, do not seek a way against them — closes the sequence. *Fa lā tabghū* — do not seek, do not pursue — forbids continuing the discipline after amendment, harassing with reminders, or reopening the matter to assert dominance. The verse requires that when obedience returns — *aṭaʿnakum* — in the marital sense of harmony restored, the path against them ends. The sūrah has applied the same principle to the pair disciplined for *fāḥishah* — relieve them when they repent and mend (4:16) — and to trade where consent ends devouring. Here it applies to the home: once the breach is mended, the household returns to kindness. The Qur’an elsewhere says that God loves not the aggressor, and this clause ensures the caretaking role does not become aggression. The caretaker who was tasked to spend from his wealth (4:34) and to guard the family as standing support (4:5) is now told that his very success in amending requires him to stop. The guard does not become a warden; the home does not become a place of tallying past faults.

**Surely Allah Is Most High, All-Great**

The closing *inna Allāha kāna ʿaliyyan kabīrā* — surely Allah is Most High, All-Great — places the household under a higher authority than either spouse. *ʿAliyy* — Most High — and *Kabīr* — All-Great — remind the caretaker that his standing over maintenance is itself under a greater standing. The sūrah has used similar closings to temper power: All-Knowing, All-Wise over shares (4:11, 4:26), Most Forbearing over division (4:12), Witness over pledges (4:33), and here High and Great over domestic authority. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse therefore warns the husband who might misuse *qiwāmah* — caretaking — that there is a higher *qiwām* — a higher Maintainer — who sees. That reminder is not abstract. The Prophet’s teaching that the best of men are best to their wives makes the standard concrete, and the sūrah’s next verse — appoint a mediator from his family and one from hers when a split is feared (4:35) — shows that the home is not a private realm where power is unchecked but a community matter where families are called in. To be caretaker is therefore to be accountable to the Most High, and to know that the authority given to keep the household upright will itself be weighed. The sūrah’s ethic holds: as God has provisioned some over others, let that provision be spent to keep the other standing, and as God has guarded, let what is guarded in absence be kept faithfully, with admonition and pause before any discipline, and with no injustice once the way has returned.
