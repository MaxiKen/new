## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:13

> He ˹also˺ subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth—all by His grace. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Everything in Both Realms, Named Without a List**

The previous verse named one thing, the sea. This verse names everything, and it does so without a catalogue: whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. The claim is enormous and it is made in a single breath, with the same verb of subjection attached. Other chapters state it in the same sweeping form, as when the reader is asked whether he has seen that God has subjected what is in the heavens and what is on the earth and lavished on humanity favours both seen and unseen (31:20). Nothing is excepted — not the sun, the rain, the minerals in the ground, the animals that can be tamed, the plants that can be eaten, or the fire that can be lit. The verse does not say that a portion of the world has been made useful and the rest is neutral. It says the arrangement is total, and the phrase "all by His grace" marks it as a gift rather than a wage.

**Being Given Use of What You Do Not Own**

Subjection is a legal-sounding word in English, and the Arabic behind it is careful. It describes something placed under command and made serviceable; it does not describe a transfer of ownership. The Qur'an never tells its readers that the world is theirs to do with as they please. It tells them, in other places, that they have been appointed as successors on the earth and given rank over other creatures as a test (6:165), and it recalls the announcement in which God tells the angels that he is placing an authority on the earth (2:30). Both passages keep the ownership where it belongs. The practical consequence is that waste, cruelty, and destruction are not merely imprudent; they are the misuse of property that was never handed over. What a person has is a grant of use, and a grant of use can be reviewed by the one who granted it.

**The Grace That Arrived Before Anyone Could Earn It**

The verse ends its clause with the word for grace, the same word used for the bounty a sailor seeks. Here it is expanded to cover the whole of the created order, and the word's meaning matters: it names what is given beyond obligation, not what is owed. Nobody earned the sun's heat, and no generation paid for the atmosphere it breathed. The Qur'an piles up this kind of statement because gratitude is easier to argue for than to feel, and it lists the gift before it asks for the response. Two nearby verses from other chapters press the point further, speaking of the sun and moon made to follow their courses and the ships driven across the water for human benefit (14:33; 22:65). Repetition of this sort is not decoration. It is the slow construction of an accurate picture of the human position, which the sūrah needs before it can ask anything of its reader.

**Serving What Was Made to Serve You**

There is an inversion the verse is quietly preventing. If the world has been placed at a person's service, then a life spent in service of the world has the order backwards, and the chapter states the remedy in a sentence of praise used by travellers: “Glory be to the One Who has subjected these for us, for we could have never done so ˹on our own˺. And surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return.” (43:13–14). The words that acknowledge the gift end by naming the return, and the two belong together. Wealth, comfort, and the produce of the earth are instruments, and instruments have a purpose beyond themselves. A man who spends his life arranging instruments and never uses them for the work they were built for has not been prudent. He has been defeated by his own equipment, and the verse's closing phrase names the faculty that would have noticed.

**Reflection as Slow Work**

The audience here is described as people who reflect, using a verb that pictures thought turning a matter over and over. It is the least dramatic of the descriptions attached to the signs in this passage — the others call for reason, certainty, understanding, gratitude — and it may be the most demanding, because reflection takes time and leaves nothing visible behind. A person can measure a coastline or count a harvest without ever turning a matter over in his mind, and the Qur'an's account of the created order is aimed at the second activity. The verse supplies the raw material; the reader does the turning. Between the two there is no shortcut, and no amount of information about the world will produce the thought the verse is asking for, unless the person who holds the information decides to think it through to the end.

**A World Fitted to a Creature That Thinks**

The arrangement described in this verse has a curious feature. The world is not merely usable; it is intelligible. Water has a chemistry a mind can follow, seasons have a pattern a farmer can predict, and the heavens have regularities that permitted calendars to be drawn before telescopes existed. A universe that could be understood by the creatures living in it is not the bare minimum needed for survival, and the Qur'an treats that fit as part of the gift. The verse's word for reflection, then, is not a demand to perform an intellectual exercise for its own sake. It is an invitation to use the one faculty that matches the world it inhabits, on the evidence that everywhere surrounds the person who possesses it.
