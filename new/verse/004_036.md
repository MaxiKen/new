## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:36

> Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him. And be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, near and distant neighbours, close friends, ˹needy˺ travellers, and those ˹bondspeople˺ in your possession. Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Worship Alone, Then Kindness Outward in Circles**

The verse begins with the foundation of all duty: *wa ʿbudū Allāha wa lā tushrikū bihi shayʾā* — worship Allah alone and associate none with Him. After thirty-five verses of law about orphans, dowries, shares, and marriage, the sūrah returns to the root that makes those laws worship rather than mere administration. Association — *shirk* — is not only bowing to an idol; it is placing any partner beside God in devotion, trust, or obedience, including wealth, lineage, or desire that the sūrah has shown can rival revelation. The command is followed immediately by kindness in expanding circles: *wa bi al-wālidayni iḥsānan wa bi dhī al-qurbā wa al-yatāmā wa al-masākīn wa al-jāri dhī al-qurbā wa al-jāri al-junub wa al-ṣāḥibi bi al-janb wa ibni al-sabīl wa mā malakat aymānukum* — be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, near and distant neighbours, close friends, needy travellers, and those in your possession. The list moves from the closest tie — parents — to the widest — the traveller far from home — and to the most dependent — those in your possession. The sūrah has already guarded each of those groups separately: parents with a fixed third or sixth (4:11), orphans with return and witnesses (4:6), the poor and needy present at distribution (4:8), and bondspeople with permission, dowry, and half penalty (4:25). Here they are gathered in one ethic of *iḥsān* — kind, beautiful doing — that flows from worship without association. The One worshipped alone is the One whose mercy is to be mirrored in layers outward.

**Why Parents Follow Worship Without Gap**

The pairing *wa bi al-wālidayni iḥsānan* directly after worship was explained as showing that gratitude to the Creator is followed by gratitude to the means of creation. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“Be grateful to Me and to your parents”* (31:14), and the same adjacency appears here. Parents are named first among people because the sūrah opened with *“He created you from a single soul, and from it its mate, and spread countless men and women”* (4:1), rooting family in creation; to be kind to parents is therefore to honour the channel of that spreading. The sūrah has already secured parents’ shares equally when offspring exist (4:11) and has told the child not to crave what another was given but to ask God for bounty (4:32); kindness to parents completes that picture with conduct, not only fractions. *Iḥsān* here was read as good companionship, gentle speech, provision without reminder, and obedience in what is lawful. The early Medinan community included migrants who had left parents in Makkah and hosts who embodied hospitality; both were asked to see *iḥsān* as more than material support. Kindness here is the moral counterpart to the fixed share there: one gives the portion, the other gives the manner.

**Relatives, Orphans, and the Poor Near the Heart of the Law**

The triad *dhī al-qurbā wa al-yatāmā wa al-masākīn* repeats the core of the sūrah’s first laws. Relatives were to be honoured as womb-ties sharing the root of mercy (4:1); orphans were to have wealth returned without substitution (4:2), tested and witnessed (4:6), and not consumed as fire (4:10); the poor and needy present at distribution were to receive provision with kind words (4:8). To list them together here as objects of *iḥsān* shows that the sūrah’s ethic is not episodic but sustained. The same persons who receive legal protection also deserve habitual kindness beyond the moment of settlement. The Qur’an elsewhere says *“righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east or west, but that you believe and give wealth, in spite of love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveller”* (2:177), and that verse helps read this one: the donation at distribution (4:8) is a single instance of a larger generosity that does not wait for an estate to divide. A community that learns to give to relatives, orphans, and the poor when it has little will give justly when it has much, because the habit precedes the occasion. The sūrah therefore lifts the specific rulings into a character.

**Neighbours Near and Far, and the Companion at Your Side**

The list widens to *al-jāri dhī al-qurbā wa al-jāri al-junub wa al-ṣāḥibi bi al-janb* — near and distant neighbours, and the close companion. Near neighbour was read as the neighbour who is also kin, or whose home is close, while the distant neighbour is the one farther in kin or distance, and the companion at the side includes the spouse, the travel companion, and the colleague in work or study. The inclusion matters because the sūrah has focused on household law — dowries, shares, caretaking — and could be heard as concerned only with kin. By naming neighbours and companions, it extends *iḥsān* to the streets around the house and the road beside the traveller. The Prophet ﷺ emphasized neighbourhood rights so strongly that companions thought the neighbour might be made an heir, a report that shows how seriously the community heard this verse. The sūrah has already said *“you are from one another”* (4:25) and *“you know not which of them are nearer to you in benefit”* (4:11); those principles apply to neighbours as well, whose benefit may come at night in need, in security, or in daily peace. To honour the neighbour near and distant is to honour the single soul from which all spread (4:1) at the scale of daily life.

**The Traveller and Those in Your Possession**

The closing pair *ibni al-sabīl wa mā malakat aymānukum* — needy travellers and those in your possession — reaches the most transient and the most dependent. The traveller — *ibn al-sabīl*, literally child of the road — is far from his own support; the sūrah elsewhere lists him among those entitled to provision, and the Medinan community of migrants knew that state personally. Those in your possession — bondspeople — were under the household’s direct authority; the sūrah has already regulated their marriage with permission, fair dowry, and chastity (4:25), and has encouraged freeing as a path. *Iḥsān* to them was read as kind treatment, sufficient provision, not overburdening, and mercy in command. The sūrah began by spreading humanity from one soul and then protected the weakest holders of property; here it protects the weakest persons under a household’s hand. The verse closes with *inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu man kāna mukhtālan fakhūrā* — surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful — whose full description spills into the next verse (4:37) as stingy, promoting stinginess, and concealing bounty. Arrogance and boastfulness were seen as the inner block to the entire list, because the one who worships alone does not see himself as source, and the one who is kind in circles does not see himself as higher. That block will be detailed next, but this verse already names its antidote: worship without partner, and kindness that moves outward until it reaches the traveller passing by and the servant inside the home.
