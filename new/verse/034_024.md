## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:24

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who provides for you from the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah! Now, certainly one of our two groups is ˹rightly˺ guided; the other is clearly astray.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question, Then the Answer Supplied by the Questioner**

The structure of this verse is unusual and deliberate. The Prophet is told to ask, and then told to answer his own question before the audience can reply. *Qul man yarzuqukum min al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ. Qul Allāh.* The second *say* is the striking one. A debater who answers his own question is either weak or supremely confident, and here the confidence has a specific basis: there was no other answer available. The Quraysh did not credit the rain to al-Lāt or the harvest to Manāt; they held that God was the provider, as the Qur'an reports of them repeatedly. So the question is not a trap designed to extract a confession. It is a way of putting on record something the opponents already grant, in public, in their own hearing, before the conclusion is drawn from it. Supplying the answer immediately also denies them the chance to invent a new position in the pause.

**Provision as the Test Case for Divinity**

Of all the divine acts the verse could have asked about, it asks about *rizq* — provision, sustenance, the steady supply of what a living thing needs. This is a shrewder choice than creation. A man can concede that the world was made long ago by a power he now has no dealings with; the concession costs him nothing in daily life. Provision is continuous and present-tense. It concerns this year's rain, this caravan's profit, this child's food, and it is exactly the domain in which people actually turn to gods for help. The verse names both sources — *from the heavens and the earth* — which covers the whole supply chain a farmer or a trader would recognise: rain and light from above, crops and water and ore from below. Fāṭir puts the same question in almost the same words and adds the conclusion the hearers must draw: *"Is there any creator other than Allah who provides for you from the heavens and the earth?"* (35:3).

**Two Groups, One Right, One Wrong**

Having secured the shared premise, the verse states the disagreement as a clean logical alternative: *wa innā aw iyyākum la-ʿalā hudan aw fī ḍalālin mubīn* — surely we or you are upon guidance or in clear error. The wording is peculiar and every reader of the Arabic has noticed it. It does not say *we are guided and you are astray*, though that is plainly what the speaker holds. It sets the two parties and the two conditions side by side and declines to assign them. Commentators have read the particle *aw* here as functioning like *and*, giving the direct sense; others read the sentence as leaving the pairing genuinely open — one of us is right, one of us is wrong, and I have not said which. On the second reading, the verse is doing something rhetorically sophisticated. It establishes that the two positions cannot both be correct, which is the only thing the opponent must accept before he can be argued with at all.

**Why the Insult Is Withheld**

The restraint in this phrasing has been read as a courtesy extended to an opponent, a door left open. There is good sense in that reading. A man told flatly that he is astray defends his position; a man told that one of the two of them must be astray is invited to work out which, and the working out is done in his own head where no audience is watching him lose. The Qur'an uses this technique more than once, and it is the same instinct that produces the instruction later in this sūrah to stand apart, singly and in pairs, and think (34:46). Argument that leaves the conclusion to the hearer has a better chance with a proud man than argument that hands it to him. But the courtesy has a hard edge. What the sentence definitely forecloses is the comfortable position that both parties might be somewhat right — that the new preaching and the old worship could be two roads to the same place.

**Clear Error, Not Honest Difference**

The adjective attached to the wrong option is *mubīn*, clear, manifest, evident — a word from the root *b-y-n*, which also gives *bayān*, plain speech. Whichever party is wrong is not slightly off; it is visibly and demonstrably off. This closes the second escape route. The first was pluralism, ruled out by the either-or; the second is the plea that the matter is too obscure for anyone to be blamed for getting it wrong. If the error is manifest, then the person in it is in a position to see it, and the failure is one of attention rather than of information. The sūrah has made this charge before against those who would not look at the sky and the ground around them (34:9). Here it makes it against people who agree about who provides for them and then act as though someone else does.

**The Distance Between What They Said and What They Did**

The deepest point of the verse lies in the gap it exposes. The opponents' answer to the question is correct: God provides. Their practice contradicts their answer, since the beings they petition and the sanctuaries they maintain belong to a different theology altogether. The verse does not accuse them of believing something false. It accuses them of believing something true and living as though it were not — which is a harder charge to answer, because it cannot be met by adjusting a doctrine. Al-Zukhruf records the same inconsistency among the same people, who would say that God created them and then turn away. Having established that both sides agree on the source of provision and disagree about everything that follows from it, the sūrah moves on to what each party will and will not be answerable for.
