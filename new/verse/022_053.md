## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:53

> All that so He may make Satan’s influence a trial for those ˹hypocrites˺ whose hearts are sick and those ˹disbelievers˺ whose hearts are hardened. Surely the wrongdoers are totally engrossed in opposition.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Clause Explains the Interference**

*Li-yajʿala mā yulqī al-shayṭānu fitnatan* — so that He makes what Satan casts a trial.

This is the answer to the difficulty the previous verse created. If Satan is permitted to interfere with the delivery of revelation, the obvious question is why. The verse answers that the interference is not permitted as a defeat; it is made into a *fitnah*, a test.

That is a theodicy, and it is worth stating in its strongest form. God does not author the interference — the verse attributes the casting to Satan and the testing to God. What God authors is the use to which the interference is put: it becomes a sorting mechanism.

**A Fitnah Is Not a Temptation Only**

The word *fitnah* covers trial, testing, temptation and sedition, and the Qur'an uses it for all of them. The root sense is of putting something to the test by fire — assaying metal to see what it is.

Applied to the interference of verse 52, the sense is that the same event reveals what was already in a person. Nothing is created in him by the test; what is created is the visibility. That is why the sūrah can describe the interference as a trial without making God the author of anybody's unbelief.

**"Those in Whose Hearts Is Sickness"**

*Alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍ* — those in whose hearts is sickness. The noun *maraḍ* is disease, and the Qur'an uses it for the heart repeatedly and consistently.

The commentators understood it as covering hypocrisy, doubt, ignorance and the absence of settled faith. The usage supports them: *“There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase”* (2:10), with the same phrase applied elsewhere to the waverers (9:125; 33:12) and to those who say one thing and believe another (47:20).

What is striking is the progression the Qur'an describes. The sickness *increases*. It is not a static condition that a person has; it is a condition that develops by being indulged, and the verses that mention it almost always add the increase.

**"Those Whose Hearts Are Hardened"**

*Wa-l-qāsiyati qulūbuhum* — and those whose hearts are hard. A second group, and the pairing with the first matters.

The sick heart is unsettled; the hard heart has settled, in the wrong direction. One wavers and the other has stopped moving. The Qur'an's vocabulary for hardness is precise: *“Can ˹the misguided be like˺ those whose hearts Allah has opened to Islam, so they are enlightened by their Lord? So woe to those whose hearts are hardened at the remembrance of Allah!”* (39:22). Hardness is defined by what it does not respond to.

Sūrah al-Ḥadīd gives the mechanism and the cure in one verse: *“Has the time not yet come for believers’ hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah and what has been revealed of the truth, and not be like those given the Scripture before—˹those˺ who were spoiled for so long that their hearts became hardened?”* (57:16).

**Why the Heart Is the Site**

The sūrah has been consistent about where the problem lies, and the consistency is a claim about human nature rather than a turn of phrase.

Verse 32 located the value of the rites in the reverence of hearts. Verse 35 described the humble as those whose hearts tremble at the mention of God. Verse 46 diagnosed the failure as blindness of the hearts in the breasts. Verse 54 will describe knowledge ending in hearts that humble themselves.

The heart, in this usage, is neither the organ of emotion opposed to reason nor a metaphor for the self. It is the faculty that receives, weighs and settles. When the Qur'an says it is sick or hard, it means that the apparatus by which a person evaluates a claim has been damaged — which is why more evidence does not help, and why the damage is described in the language of disease and hardness rather than of ignorance.

**"The Wrongdoers Are in Far Schism"**

*Wa-inna al-ẓālimīna la-fī shiqāqin baʿīd* — and the wrongdoers are in a schism far away.

*Shiqāq* is from a root meaning to split or to be at the side of: it is the state of standing apart in opposition, a fracture rather than a disagreement. The same word is used for the fracture between a community and the truth it was given.

The word *baʿīd*, far, completes the description. These people are not near the truth and slightly off. The sūrah has used this language before — the far straying of verse 12 — and it uses distance consistently to describe the gap between a person and what he was made for.

**Why a Test Is Not a Trap**

The obvious objection to verse 53 is that a God who makes Satan's interference into a trial is setting people up. If the interference produces the unbelief it punishes, the test is not a test but a mechanism.

The classical answer has two parts, and both are in the text. First, the casting is attributed to Satan and the testing to God — two different agents, two different acts. What God authors is not the interference but its function. Second, the two groups in this verse are described as already having the condition that the test reveals: the sickness and the hardness are mentioned as existing before the trial operates on them.

That second point is the one that matters. A trial in the Qur'an's usage never creates the trait it exposes. It is the difference between a test and a seduction, and the sūrah is careful to keep the two apart: the verb of making trial belongs to God, and the verb of casting belongs to Satan.

The practical corollary is uncomfortable for the reader. The question the verse poses to anyone facing a confusing piece of evidence is not "is this clear?" but "what does my heart do with it?" — and both answers are treated as disclosures rather than as verdicts.

**The Two Groups and the Same Event**

The verse's structure is the point it is making. One event — the interference with the delivered message — produces opposite effects in two different kinds of people.

In those whose hearts are sick, it confirms the doubt they were already nursing. In those whose hearts are hard, it confirms the refusal they had already settled on. Neither group was made a disbeliever by the event. Both were shown by it.

That is the mechanism by which a test works, and the sūrah states it here so that the next verse can describe the opposite effect in the opposite kind of person.
