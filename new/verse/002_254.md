## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:254

> O believers! Donate from what We have provided for you before the arrival of a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession. Those who disbelieve are ˹truly˺ the wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Spend Before the Day**

The narrative sequence closes on the messengers and their preferments, and the sūrah turns to its addressees with an imperative built on a deadline: donate *before the arrival of a Day*. The spending commanded is min mā razaqnākum — from what We have provided you — and the phrasing reclaims the wealth at the moment of its use: what the giver spends was never his except by provision, and the verse spends it for him before the Day spends it without him. The deadline is the verse's engine. Al-Ṭabarī states the mechanism plainly: the Day named is the Day of Judgment, when the time for performing good deeds has passed — the spending commanded here is possible only in the interval, and the interval is what the verse is selling. Spending from God's provision is one of the sūrah's founding descriptions of the believer — the portrait at its opening lists belief in the unseen, the prayer, and spending from what is provided (2:3) — and this verse returns that founding mark to the community as an urgent command with an expiration date attached.

**The Three Currencies Refused**

The Day is defined by three refusals, and each names a currency the world runs on. No bargaining — the world's transactions: ransom, price, negotiation, the deal struck at the margin. No friendship — the world's networks: the ally who intervenes, the connection who vouches, the kinship that opens doors. No intercession — the world's hierarchies of access: the patron who stands between the accused and the judge. Every ordinary strategy for surviving judgment — pay, know, be vouched for — is voided in one clause, and the voiding is total because the three currencies exhaust the three ways influence moves among people. The Qur'an states the same economics elsewhere: the sūrah of Abraham orders the believers to donate *before the arrival of a Day in which there will be no ransom or friendly connections* (14:31); the sūrah of the Believer pictures the Day *when the hearts will jump into the throats* and the wrongdoers have *neither a close friend nor intercessor to be heard* (40:18). The verse's function is to price the interval: while bargaining, friendship, and intercession still exist among people, the one transfer that reaches past the Day is the donation made before it.

**The Intercession Question**

The third refused currency deserves the precision the commentators give it, because the Qur'an's intercession-teaching is not a flat denial. The sūrah's own early verse stated the Day's rule absolutely — *No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given* (2:48) — while other verses condition intercession rather than abolish it: who can intercede with God except by His leave. Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī record the reconciliation here: the ability to intercede is unavailable, on this Day, to the disbelievers — the refusal is scoped to those who refused God. The tradition's picture of the Day includes intercession exercised by permission — above all the Prophet's intercession for his community and for the world, attached to the praiseworthy station promised him (17:79) — and the theological literature extends the permission to others by rank, al-Ghazzālī among the saints and the faithful, the Shīʿī tradition through the Imams. The verse's clause, read in that light, does not erase the institution but empties it of everything the world meant by it: no intercession that is bought, owed, or leveraged — only what the Judge permits, to whom He permits, for whom He permits.

**Friends Become Enemies**

The friendship-refusal has its own Qur'anic elaboration, and the elaboration sharpens the warning. The sūrah of Ornaments states it as a reversal: *Close friends will be enemies to one another on that Day, except the righteous* (43:67). The exception is the verse's hidden door: the friendships that survive the Day are the ones that were already righteous — bonds whose content was the good rather than the useful. The sūrah of Smoke adds that on the Day *no friend will avail a friend in any way*, and the sūrah of the Stairways pictures the loyal friend not asked about by his loyal friend (70:10) — each soul too occupied to plead another's case. Read together, the network-verses tell the believer something actionable in the interval: the worldly friendship that will be worthless on the Day is the friendship of mutual advantage, and the only social capital that crosses is the righteousness shared — which is why the verse's command is a command to spend: the donation is precisely the act that converts wealth, the most worldly of currencies, into the only form that the refusals do not touch.

**The Wrongdoers Named**

The final clause fixes the blame's location: *Those who disbelieve are truly the wrongdoers*. The identification is the Qur'an's standing equation — kufr as ẓulm in its fullest sense — and its placement after the Day's three refusals assigns the wrongdoers their fate precisely: the people for whom there will be no bargaining, no friendship, and no intercession are named here, and the naming is the verse's warning to the believers who still hold the interval. The commentators connect the clause to the command's logic: the disbeliever is called a wrongdoer because he misappropriated what was provided — the wealth that was God's provision spent as though it were self-earned, kept as though keeping were ownership — and the Day's refusals are the foreclosure on that misappropriation. The believers are addressed as believers and commanded to donate; the disbelievers are described in the third person and condemned. Between the two sentences stands the whole meaning of the verse's timing: the addressees can still choose which sentence describes them, and the donation is the choosing.

**The Verse as Threshold**

Positioned where it sits, the verse opens the sūrah's second great theme-block — spending — and opens it with the eschatology that will govern every spending-verse to come. The parables of the grain and the garden, the prohibitions on reminders and injury, the ranking of secret over public, the recipients who do not beg: all of it operates inside the deadline this verse states. The three refusals also gather the sūrah's earlier material — the Day described in its opening eschatological verses, the intercession-question raised at 2:48, the spending-from-provision commanded from 2:3 — and compress it into a single imperative. The reader is left with the verse's arithmetic, which is deliberately simple: everything a person owns is provided, everything provided can be donated, everything donated before the Day crosses it, and on the Day the three currencies of the world are refused to everyone. The command is therefore not a tax on the believer's wealth but the only available exchange rate — and the wrongdoers named at the end are the ones who waited for a better one.
