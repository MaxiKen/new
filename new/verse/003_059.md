## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:59

> Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him, “Be!” And he was!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comparison That Ends the Argument**

The verse gives the answer to a claim made about Jesus, and the answer is a comparison with another man. The Book does not respond to the argument about his birth by denying the extraordinary nature of it; it concedes the miracle and produces Adam, who has no father and no mother at all.

The logic is the whole of the verse. A person who concluded from the virgin birth that Jesus must be divine has to explain why the same conclusion does not apply to the first human being, who came into existence with no parents whatsoever. The Book's argument is that the absence of a father is evidence of God's power rather than of the son's nature, and the case of Adam makes that clear without any need for further reasoning.

**Why Adam Is the Decisive Case**

The choice of Adam rather than another figure is deliberate, and it is worth seeing what the comparison does that a comparison with, say, Isaac would not have done. Isaac had a father and a mother, one of whom was beyond the age of childbearing; his case would have been an example of God overcoming a natural obstacle. Adam's case is categorically different: he had no parents at all, and his existence began with dust and a command.

If being created without a human father is evidence of divinity, then Adam has the better claim to it, since he was created without a human ancestry entirely. Since nobody makes that claim for Adam, the inference from the virgin birth collapses. The Book's argument is thus not that the miracle did not happen; it is that miracles of origin are statements about the one who makes them.

**Dust and the Command**

The verse describes the creation of Adam in two stages: God created him from dust, then said to him, Be, and he was. The Book states the same creation in its opening sūrah, where the creation of the first man is followed by the account of his knowledge and the prostration of the angels, and the phrase is used consistently to describe a bringing-into-being that has no process.

The two elements of the sentence belong together. Dust is the material, and it has no life in itself; the command is the act, and it has nothing in it that requires the cooperation of what it addresses. The Book's picture of creation is that God's speech is sufficient for anything to exist, and this verse applies the picture to the very beginning of the human race so that the reader can measure the nativity of Jesus against it.

**What "Likeness" Means Here**

The Arabic word translated likeness describes a resemblance with respect to some particular aspect, and the commentators were careful about what the aspect is. Adam and Jesus share certain attributes: both came into existence without the ordinary means, both were created by the command of God, and both were servants rather than sharers in their Lord's nature.

The comparison is not a claim about their equivalence in every respect. The Book gives Adam a role in the story of humanity that it does not give to Jesus, and it gives Jesus a mission within Israel that Adam could not have had. What the verse asserts is a likeness in the matter at issue, which is the manner of their coming into being, and it uses that single point of resemblance to close the argument about divinity.

**The Formula "Be" Throughout the Book**

The command appears in the Book wherever creation is described, and its repetitions form one of the Qur'an's most consistent themes. The Originator of the heavens and the earth, when He decrees a matter, tells it to be and it is (2:117). God's affair, when He wills something, is to say to it Be and it is (36:82). He who created the heavens and the earth in truth will say on the Day of Judgment Be and it will be (6:73), and the same words describe the giver of life and death when He decrees a matter (40:68). In the sūrah of the Bee, the formula states that God's command, when He wills a thing, is a single word (16:40).

Placing this verse in that series shows that the creation of Jesus is not an isolated exception but an instance of the way God has always acted. The Book's doctrine of creation is that existence follows a command, and the fact that a particular existence had an unusual origin is a difference in the circumstances, not in the kind of act.

**A Creature Cannot Be Its Own Maker**

The verse's argument carries an implication that the sūrah has already stated and will state again: a person brought into being by a command cannot be the one who issues commands in his own right. The Book is explicit about this in the case of Jesus, whose own words are recorded as a confession of service.

The comparison with Adam therefore does more than settle a local dispute. It establishes the rule by which every claim about a created being must be measured, and the rule applies to prophets, to angels, and to anything else that might be treated as more than a servant. What the verse gives the reader is a criterion: whatever came into existence was made, and whatever was made worships.

**The Argument Answered Before It Was Made**

The sūrah's account of the delegations from Najrān and their arguments about Jesus is given in the opening of the chapter, and this verse is the Book's central rebuttal of the position they took. The rebuttal is brief, and it is complete.

What the reader is left with is a pattern of reasoning that can be used wherever the question arises. The Book acknowledges the wonders of a prophet's life without adjusting its account of God, and it defends both by placing the wonder inside the doctrine of creation. The verses that follow draw the conclusion and address the disputants directly.
