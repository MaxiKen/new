## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:43

> These are the parables We set forth for humanity, but none will understand them except the people of knowledge.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Parables Offered to Everyone, Understood by Few**

The sentence makes a distinction that this chapter has been circling since its opening: the material is public and the comprehension is not. The parables are set before humanity without restriction, and the same image which the spider's web provides is available to any hearer. What divides the audience is not access but recognition, and the Book locates the difference in knowledge rather than in intelligence or social standing. The chapter's first verses raised the same question about people who say they believe and expect to be left untroubled; the sūrah's method throughout has been to place the evidence in the open and let the response reveal what a person is.

The verse also presents the parables as public property. They are set before humanity rather than reserved for a school, which is why the Book's images draw on spiders, flies, mountains, and rain instead of technical vocabulary, and why a failure to profit from them is treated as a fault in the audience rather than a shortage in the material.

**What Knowledge Means in This Sentence**

The word rendered as knowledge in the Book's usage is not a quantity of information. It describes a person coming to know a thing as it actually is, and the Book attaches it to those who act on what they have understood. Al-Tustarī is reported to have said that God addresses the parables to humanity at large, because the things that testify to power point to the Powerful, and that only the elect comprehend them, so that knowledge is rare and comprehension of God rarer still. His point is that a person does not arrive at this knowledge by his own unaided reasoning, and that whoever imagines he has done so has been deluded. The sentence therefore describes a gift that requires the recipient's attention rather than an aptitude that some people simply possess.

**The Parable as a Test of the Hearer**

A comparison in the Book does more than illustrate; it sorts. The reader who accepts the image of the spider's web must also accept what it says about the reliability of his own arrangements, and that acceptance is where most readings stop. The same structure appears in the Book's wider use of comparisons, and the Book says of itself that God is not ashamed to set forth a parable of a gnat or something smaller, adding that He thereby leads many astray and guides many (2:26). The verse is one of the clearest statements in the Book that revelation is not neutral in its effects. Its content can be heard by anyone, and its function is to separate those who take the point from those who do not.

**Why the Chapter Needs This Statement**

By this point the sūrah has reported the fates of five communities, and a reader might reasonably ask why the evidence never persuaded them. The verse supplies the answer at the level of the individual: the same argument that ruins a person can also be declined, and the decline is not a failure of the argument. The Book makes the point about itself rather than about its audience, and it does so without condescension. In the following verse the sūrah turns from the closed archives of the past to the world that is still standing, and the sentence about knowledge prepares the reader for the jump in scale.

Stating the matter here also protects the chapter's earlier arguments from being read as a display of cleverness. The sūrah has argued with its opponents at length, and this sentence reminds the reader that those arguments were never expected to work by their own force, since comprehension of them is granted or withheld at a level below debate.

**The Adornment of a Full Heart**

The Book treats understanding as something that depends on the condition of the person who receives it, and it names the obstruction elsewhere with the language of rust and seals. It says of the hypocrites that their hearts were sealed so that they could not comprehend (63:3), and it warns that those who persist in rejecting a sign after it has been explained lose the capacity to benefit from signs in general. The classification in this verse is not arbitrary, then; the Book presents knowledge as a capacity that grows with use and atrophies with neglect. A person who has already decided what he will accept before he hears an argument is not in a position to understand the argument, whichever argument it is.

Knowledge described as adornment also implies visibility. A person whose understanding has settled into his character carries it in his dealings, his speech, and his patience, and a word connected with beauty suggests an effect meant to be noticed by others rather than kept private.

**Where the Sentence Points**

The verse closes the section on the spider's web and opens the way to the next subject. What follows shifts from the comparison of houses to the structure of the heavens and the earth, and from the question of who understands to the question of what the creation itself declares. The Book's habit is to end a passage about human failure with a reminder that the failure is not universal, and the phrase about the people of knowledge performs that function here. The reader is left with a plain question: what kind of hearer is he, given that the same sentence has just been set before everybody and understood by a few?

The sentence also sets the terms on which a reader should hold his own grasp of the material. A person who recognizes a parable's force does so by permission rather than by cleverness, and reading the verse this way keeps knowledge from becoming a possession its owner can take credit for.

**The Parable That Requires an Admission First**

Comprehension of a comparison in the Book usually demands something of the hearer before it yields anything. To understand the spider's web as a description of one's own arrangements, a person must first be willing to consider that his arrangements might be of that kind, and that willingness is not a feature of intelligence. The chapter has documented the refusal in every form it takes: the town that answered with violence, the market that dismissed a warning about its scales, the tribes that treated their messenger as an obstacle, and the king who commissioned a tower. None of them failed for want of information. Each had been told what it needed to know, and each declined to apply it to itself, which is why the verse locates understanding in a category of people rather than in a supply of facts.

**A Closing Statement That Keeps the Door Open**

The sentence is a conclusion, but it is also an invitation, and the two functions sit together without strain. A reader who finds himself outside the description has just been told exactly what distinguishes the people inside it, and nothing in the verse suggests the category is closed. The Book's warnings are consistently written so that the person hearing them can still move, and a statement about who understands a parable is one of the most useful things a person can encounter before he has decided what to do with what he has heard. The following verse turns directly to the creation itself, which is the Book's usual way of showing the reader that there is still something to look at.
