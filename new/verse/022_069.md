## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:69

> Allah will judge between you ˹all˺ on Judgment Day regarding your differences.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Deferral in This Sūrah**

*Allāhu yaḥkumu baynakum yawma al-qiyāmati fī mā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn* — God will judge between you on the Day of Resurrection concerning that in which you used to differ.

This is the third time the sūrah has deferred an adjudication to the Day of Resurrection, and the recurrence is the sūrah's method rather than its habit.

Verse 17 deferred the question of which religious community is right. Verses 56 and 57 executed that judgment in the eschatological scene, where the criteria turned out not to be communal membership. Verse 69 brings the deferral back down into the present and hands it to the Prophet as a rule of conduct.

**"Concerning That in Which You Differ"**

The scope of the phrase is wider than the context might suggest. The immediate disagreement is about the rites, since verse 67 has just said that every community was given its own.

But *mā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn* — that in which you used to differ — is a relative clause with no restriction, and the sūrahs that use the same formula apply it to religious disputes generally: *“Say, ‘Allah knows best what you do.’ Allah will judge between you ˹all˺ on Judgment Day regarding your differences”* is the shape of the instruction, and it is used for disagreements between communities rather than only within them.

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the same deferral for the disputes of the People of the Book: *“Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and I will settle all your disputes”* (3:55). Sūrah al-Māʾidah does the same for the plurality of laws (5:48).

**The Deferral Has a Date**

*Yawma al-qiyāmati* — on the Day of Resurrection. The deferral is not open-ended, and the specificity of the date is what distinguishes it from indifference.

A dispute referred to an indefinite future has been shelved. A dispute referred to a named day that the sūrah has spent its length establishing — the Hour that is coming (22:7), the day on which sovereignty is God's alone (22:56) — has been scheduled.

That difference is everything. The sūrah is not telling the Prophet to stop caring which community is right; the sūrah's own verses are entirely clear about what belief and sound action lead to. It is telling him that the adjudication belongs to a specific day and a specific Judge, and that living in the interval meansinviting rather than litigating.

**Why Deferral Is Not Indifference**

The obvious objection to a verse like this is that it sounds like a way of not answering. The reply is that the answer is given, and it is given in the form of a date.

A deferred judgment is not a declined one. What is being said is that the adjudication will happen, that it will be executed by the only party qualified to execute it, and that the interval is not a period of uncertainty about the outcome but a period in which the outcome is simply not yet delivered.

That distinction matters practically. A person who believes the dispute will never be settled behaves in one way; a person who believes it will be settled on a known kind of day, by a known kind of Judge, behaves in another.

**What the Deferral Does for the Community**

The social consequence is the one the sūrah is after, and it is larger than it looks.

A community that has been told it is right, and told that the adjudication belongs to God, is released from the obligation to win every argument in the meantime. That release is what makes it possible to live alongside people who disagree without either silencing them or conceding to them. The alternative — a community required to settle every dispute here — has only two instruments available, and both are force.

The sūrah has just legislated the limits of force (22:39–41, 22:60). This verse supplies the other half of that legislation: the disputes that cannot be settled by force are not abandoned, they are reserved.

**The Verse's Connection to Verse 17**

The verb is the same one used at verse 17: *yaḥkumu bayna*, He judges between. There the parties were named — believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magians and associators — and the sūrah declined to rank them.

Verse 69 does not name the parties; it says *between you*. The shift from the third person to the second is what turns a doctrine into an instruction. The Prophet is being told that he is a party to the disagreement and that his standing in it is not the matter.

That is the same thing verse 67 told him when it instructed him to invite rather than to dispute, and verse 68 told him when it gave him one sentence to say. The three verses are a single posture: state the claim, decline the argument, reserve the verdict.

**The Sūrah's Closing Posture**

Placed where it is — after the signs, after the judgment scene, near the end of the chapter — this verse is the sūrah's final word on how to disagree.

Notice what it does not say. It does not say that the disagreements are unreal, or that all parties are equally correct, or that the differences do not matter. It says that the differences will be settled by a Judge who knows what each party did with what it was given — which is the sūrah's argument from its first verse to its last.


It is, finally, a verse about scale. The disagreements that consume a community — which rite, which community, which practice — are placed beside a day on which sovereignty belongs to God alone, and the comparison is left to do its work. What looked like the whole argument turns out to be one item on a list that a single Judge will settle.
