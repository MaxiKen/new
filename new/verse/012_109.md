## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:109

> We only sent before you ˹O Prophet˺ men inspired by Us from among the people of each society. Have the deniers not travelled through the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? And surely the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter is far better for those mindful ˹of Allah˺. Will you not then understand?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Messengers Were Men From Their Own People**

The verse states a fact about the whole history of revelation, and the fact answers an objection the Qur'an records elsewhere: why has no angel been sent down to this messenger. The answer is that messengers have always been human beings drawn from the populations they were sent to, receiving inspiration rather than possessing a different nature. The Qur'an makes the same point repeatedly — a messenger from among yourselves, speaking the language of his people, eating food and walking in the markets. Verse 109 belongs to that series, and its placement at the end of a narrative sūrah gives it an illustration ready to hand: Joseph was a man, sent among a people who did not know him, whose knowledge came by revelation rather than by status or training.

**"From the People of the Towns"**

The commentators discuss the specification at length. Some understand it as excluding nomads, on the grounds that town dwellers were more knowledgeable and more refined and therefore better fitted to receive, carry and transmit a message to others. Others note that the wording has also been taken to exclude women from prophethood. A third reading holds that the emphasis falls not on gender but on humanity — men rather than angels — which is the point the first clause has already made and which the Qur'an argues explicitly elsewhere. The verse does not settle these questions, and the chapter's own narrative is compatible with several of the readings: Joseph was raised in a household, lived in a city, served a government and administered a country's stores.

**"Have They Not Travelled in the Land?"**

The second clause turns from the messengers to their audiences and asks a question about evidence. Travel in the land and observe how the end of those who came before turned out. The appeal is to remains, to recorded outcomes, to the visible results of previous refusals — the things a traveller actually encounters. Verse 105 described people passing by signs in the heavens and the earth with indifference; verse 109 asks them to travel to the same signs and look at them deliberately. The repetition of the appeal across four verses is the chapter's method of insistence: the evidence is not new, it is nearby and reachable, and the failure identified is not an absence of information but an absence of attention. The question is answerable by anyone who has moved through a country. Ruined settlements, cultivated land and abandoned structures are visible to a traveller without instruction, and the verse assumes they have been seen rather than explaining them. What it asks about is the heart — whether the seeing has reached the organ that would draw a conclusion from it.

**"The Home of the Hereafter Is Better"**

The third clause returns to a ranking the chapter stated inside its narrative section. Verse 57 declared that the reward of the Hereafter is far better for those who are faithful and mindful; verse 109 makes the same claim about the abode itself. The echo is close enough to be deliberate and loose enough to be a restatement rather than a quotation. The verse places the ranking immediately after the appeal to travel and observe destroyed peoples, which gives it a comparative frame: what remains of those nations is visible in the land and amounts to ruins, and what remains for the mindful is described as better. The two remainders are set side by side without further argument. The juxtaposition is the argument. What remains of earlier generations is their ending, and what remains for the reader is a home described as better; the verse sets both remainders in front of the same person and offers no comment on the comparison. Understanding, in this verse, consists of noticing which of the two a person is actually living toward.

**"Will You Not Then Understand?"**

The closing question is addressed in the plural and follows the ranking rather than the historical appeal. The sequence is: messengers have always been men from their own people; the ends of previous deniers are visible to anyone who travels; the Hereafter is better for the mindful; will you not understand? The question does not specify what is to be understood, and the omission leaves it open to all three preceding clauses at once. The form is characteristic of the Qur'an's closing arguments, and here it follows a hundred verses of narrative that supplied the material for understanding throughout without ever asking the reader to draw a conclusion from it until now. The request is modest in form and large in implication. Understanding is presented not as the acquisition of new information but as the use of what has already been given: the travelled land, the reported endings, the story just completed. The chapter has supplied the material for a hundred verses and now, at the end, asks the single question that material was assembled to prompt.

**The Verse Restates the Chapter's Own Case**

Read against the story just completed, verse 109 describes the account the reader has been given. A man was sent among a people not his own, received knowledge by inspiration rather than by study, and was recognised by a ruler with no prior acquaintance with him. The ends of those who plotted against him are recorded in the same narrative: a household disgraced by a public confession, a plot that produced the opposite of its intention, a prison that produced an office. And the ranking of the two abodes was stated by the narrator in verse 57 and enacted by Joseph in verse 101, when he asked to die submitting rather than to keep any of what he had been given. That prayer is the chapter's own demonstration of the preference. A man holding the granaries of Egypt, reunited with his family and vindicated before a court, asks to die in submission and to be joined with the righteous rather than to keep any of it. Verse 109 states the ranking; verse 101 has already enacted it, in the words of the person with most to lose by it.

**Why the Verse Belongs at the End**

The chapter could have closed with verse 108's declaration. Verse 109 continues past it into a general statement about revelation, its messengers and their outcomes, and the continuation serves a purpose: it places Joseph's story inside a series rather than leaving it as a single case that might be dismissed as unusual. The reader who has just been told that this account came from revelation is now told that every account of the same kind has had the same shape — a human messenger, a people, delay, refusal, an end. Verse 111 will draw the conclusion from that series in explicit terms. Verse 109 supplies the series, and supplies it in the plural so that the rule covers cases the reader has not been shown.
