## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:94

> ˹Today˺ you have come back to Us all alone as We created you the first time —leaving behind everything We have provided you with. We do not see your intercessors with you—those you claimed were Allah’s partners ˹in worship˺. All your ties have been broken and all your claims have let you down.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address at the Resurrection**

Today you have come back to Us all alone as We created you the first time. The verse gives the content of what is said to the wrongdoers after their souls have been taken, and it states the condition they arrive in. The Book describes every person as standing alone before God, and it uses the fact of the original creation as the measure of the final one: the person who begins his existence with nothing returns with nothing.

The Book states the same isolation in other places, and its descriptions of the day include people who are so overwhelmed that each one is occupied with his own condition. The sūrah of Maryam says that every person will come to God on the day of resurrection individually, and the sūrah of ʿAbasa describes a day when a man will flee from his brother, his mother, his father, his wife, and his children, each of them having enough concern of his own.

**The Things Left Behind**

Leaving behind everything We have provided you with. The sentence names what has been stripped away, and it includes everything a person used to make himself secure: wealth, property, position, and the alliances that protected him in his city. The Book describes the goods of this world as provided for use in the present life, and it says that whatever is sent ahead for the soul is what a person will find waiting for him (73:20).

That contrast was a favourite of the commentators on this verse. Al-Rāzī observed that the connections between a person and his possessions are severed at death while the knowledge of God and virtuous conduct a person has acquired are sent ahead to be met in the Hereafter. The sūrah's audience, which measured a man by what he owned, is shown the moment when the measure stops working.

**The Intercessors Who Do Not Appear**

We do not see your intercessors with you, those you claimed were Allah's partners in worship. The verse takes the Makkans' own religious claim and tests it at the point where they expected it to pay off. The sūrah has described their belief that the idols were intercessors with God (6:51; 6:70), and it has described the impossibility of ransom or plea on that day (6:70).

The Book records the same scene in many other passages, saying that the beings the idolaters invoked will disown them and that the ties between them will be severed (6:24; 7:37; 14:22). The verse in front of us states the separation as a fact of the judgment rather than as a warning about it, and the sentence is spoken to the people who had built their lives around the claim.

**The Severing of Ties**

All your ties have been broken and all your claims have let you down. The Book's word for the ties is the one used for the connections between people and between a person and his patrons, and the sentence announces that the whole network has been dissolved. In a society organised around tribal protection, this was a total statement: the claims that gave a man standing in Makkah were precisely the ones that would not be available to him there.

The Book uses the same vocabulary when it says that the disbelievers will see the punishment and that every cause will be cut off from them (2:166), and when it describes the followers of the wrongdoers grieving over those they followed. The sūrah's audience is being told that the structures of loyalty on which their religion rested would not survive the transition, and the verse states it in their own language of ties and patrons.

**What the Verse Says About Intercession as a Doctrine**

The sūrah has mentioned intercession several times and has consistently denied it to anyone besides God. It said that those warned about the gathering will have no protector and no intercessor besides Him (6:51), and it said that those who take their religion as play will find no protector or intercessor other than God, even if they offered every ransom (6:70). This verse completes the sequence by showing the intercessors themselves absent from the scene.

The Book's teaching on intercession in other passages is that it occurs only by God's permission and for those He permits, which is why the sūrah's denials are phrased as denials of any intercessor besides Him. What the verse eliminates is the assumption the Makkans had built their religious practice on: that the beings they associated with God held a claim on Him that they could exercise for their clients.

**The Argumentative Force of the Scene**

The verse works as an argument by showing the audience what their own position would look like when the arrangement they relied on was checked. The Makkans were proud of their ancestry, their wealth, and their alliances, and they were confident in the intercession of their gods. The sentence strips away all three at once, and it does so in the voice of the One who is described at the end of the passage as the All-Wise and All-Aware.

The sūrah then moves to the signs of God's power in the created order, and the transition is one the Book makes often. Having described the isolation of the wrongdoer before his Lord, the sūrah turns to the evidence of the Lord's power that was available to him all along, beginning with the sprouting of the seed and the splitting of the dawn.
