## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:9

> But those whose scale is light, they have doomed themselves for wrongfully denying Our signs.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Light Scale**

The verse names the second half of the division that the previous verse opened, and it states that those whose balance is light are the ones who have lost their souls. The Book uses the light scale in the same way elsewhere: the sūrah of the Believers states that those whose scales are light are the ones who lose themselves and abide in Hell (23:102–3), and the sūrah of the Clatterer states that the one whose scale is light will have his refuge in a pit (101:8–9). The image of the balance is thus applied consistently to the two groups.

The lightness is described as the absence of weight, and the Book's presentation of the matter does not suggest that the measure failed. The balance is exact, and what the person brought to it was insufficient. The verse in front of us identifies the state of the one who arrives at the reckoning with a record that does not outweigh his evil.

**The Ruin That Is Named**

The verse states that they have ruined their souls, and the phrase describes a loss that the person has inflicted on himself. The Book uses the language of loss for the condemned throughout, and the sūrah of the Cave states that those whose efforts went astray in the life of the world will think they are doing well, and that they are the ones who reject the signs of their Lord and the meeting with Him, so their deeds are void (18:103–5). The ruin in front of us is of that kind.

The Book's treatment of the self as the subject of loss appears in its repeated statement that God does not wrong people, but that people wrong themselves. The verse in front of us states the outcome of that self-wronging: the soul is the thing that is forfeited, and no one else bears the cost.

**The Wrongful Denial of the Signs**

The verse names the cause of the loss as the wrongful treatment of God's signs, and the phrase covers the denial of the revelation and the rejection of the evidence that supports it. The Book uses the phrase the signs of God for the verses of the Quran, for the phenomena of the created order, and for the fate of the earlier communities. The sūrah of the Cattle describes the signs of the heavens and the earth that people pass by without attention, and it states that those who deny the signs of their Lord and the meeting with Him have wasted their works.

The denial is described as wrongful because the evidence for the signs was available. The sūrah in front of us presents the history of the communities, the created order, and the messenger's testimony as the material from which the audience could have drawn the right conclusion, and the verse states that the loss followed a denial that was not innocent.

**Where the Deeds of the Deniers Go**

The Book describes the deeds of those who deny as material that loses its substance, and its images for the loss are several. The sūrah of the Light states that the deeds of those who disbelieve are like a mirage in a desert which the thirsty takes for water until he reaches it and finds it to be nothing. The sūrah of the Cave states that the works of the deniers are scattered dust, and the sūrah of the Bee describes their deeds as ashes on which the wind blows on a stormy day.

The imagery explains the light scale without diminishing the seriousness of the reckoning. The deeds were performed, and the record contains them; what they lack is the weight that would have carried them through the measure. The verse in front of us states the outcome, and the Book's images supply the picture of what was placed on the scale.

**The Fairness of the Outcome**

The verse's description of the loss follows the previous verse's statement that the weighing is just, and the two verses are meant to be read together. The loss is not the product of an uneven balance but of a record that was light. The Book's statement in the sūrah of the Prophets that no soul will be wronged in the least on the day of the scales (21:47) establishes the same principle.

The tradition of commentary treats the balance as an assurance to human beings rather than a device for the divine judgment, and the verse in front of us supports that reading. The one who loses is told exactly what he lost and why, and the explanation removes any complaint about the standard.

**What the Audience Takes From the Verse**

The sūrah's audience has been warned about the destruction of the earlier communities and has been told to follow the revelation that has come to it. The verse in front of us states what the refusal of that revelation produces in the long run, and it places the outcome in the balance of the reckoning rather than in the ruins of the world. The two warnings together cover both the life of the world and the life to come.

The Book's presentation of the two outcomes as a division appears in the sūrahs that describe the two groups in adjacent verses, and the sūrah of the Heights places the same division at the head of the chapter. The reader who follows the argument to its end in the sūrah's later passages finds the same two groups described in the dialogue between the people of Paradise and the people of the Fire.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands ninth in the chapter's opening section, and it completes the pair that describes the weighing of deeds. The verses that follow turn to the establishment of people on the earth and the provision that has been placed there, and the account of Adam's creation begins with the eleventh verse. The balance in front of us closes the sequence of the judgment that the chapter's opening had set out.

The reader of the sūrah has now been given the letters, the Book, the command, the warning, the destruction of the communities, the questioning, the account, and the weighing. The chapter's program is stated, and the narratives that fill the rest of it are the working out of the terms.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that God has established people on the earth, provided them with means of livelihood, and that they seldom give thanks (7:10). The verse turns from the reckoning to the present life, and it names the provision that makes the denial of the signs a matter of ingratitude rather than ignorance.

The two verses together place the audience between two accounts: the record of what it does and the provision by which it lives. The sūrah's opening section then moves into the story of Adam, where the same themes of command, refusal, and consequence are presented in their first form.
