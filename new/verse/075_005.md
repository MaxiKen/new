## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:5

> Still people want to deny what is yet to come,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Unbelief Described as Appetite**

The sūrah has just answered the objector's challenge about bones and fingertips, and the answer was decisive. What follows is therefore remarkable: the very next statement is not that he was unconvinced but that he wants something. *Yurīdu* — he wills, he desires. Unbelief is here located in the will rather than the intellect. A person who had been shown the argument and found it thin would be described as mistaken; this verse describes him as wanting. The distinction runs through the Qur'an's diagnosis of rejection, which seldom treats denial as the conclusion of an inquiry and regularly treats it as the pressure of a preference. The objector cannot be argued out of a position he did not reason his way into, and the sūrah is candid about that: the evidence was given in v. 4, and nothing in v. 5 suggests it failed to land. It was unwelcome.

**What the Word Means: the Root f-j-r**

The verb *yafjuru* carries a family of senses that all descend from one image: breaking through. Dawn is *fajr* because it splits the dark. *Fujūr* is transgression because it breaks the bound that should hold a person in. From that root the commentators took two readings of this verse. The first: man wants to go on breaking the limit in the time that lies ahead of him, meaning he persists in sin for the rest of his life. The second: he denies the Hereafter that lies before him, spoiling it in advance by refusing it. The first reading has the root's ordinary moral sense on its side; the second has the sūrah's argument on its side, since the whole chapter is about refusing the Day. Neither requires the other to be discarded — a person who wants to keep breaking the bound and a person who denies the reckoning are doing the same thing from the inside out, because the prospect of being answered for it is exactly what makes the bound feel like a bound.

**The Time That Is Always Ahead**

The phrase *amāmahu* — what is in front of him — is where the verse does its sharpest work. Sin is placed in front of the person, and so is his repentance, and the two are never reached at the same moment. Al-Qushayrī described the condition precisely: a man resolves to turn back and then defers it, saying he will repent later, and dies before he does. He sets down the intention and never unties the knot, and the condition of genuine turning is not regret alone but a resolved intention not to return to what he did. If the sweetness of the lapse is still in the heart and the thought of repeating it is still pleasant, then what he calls repentance is only a wish about himself. The verse is short, but it names the most successful of all self-deceptions: the future in which one is finally going to change.

**Why Delay Feels Safe**

Nothing in the texture of ordinary life warns a person that the delay is dangerous. The goods keep arriving, the warnings lose their urgency, and the silence of heaven starts to look like approval. The Qur'an names this and refuses to let it be read as a verdict: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise.”* (6:44). Extended time is not a certificate; *“They are only given more time to increase in sin”* (3:178), and the One who grants the extension is the One who said *“I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.”* (7:183). A person who treats his own survival as evidence that nothing is wrong has misread the situation entirely. Delay is the substance he is being tested with, and he is spending it.

**The Reversal at the Edge of Death**

What the deferrer cannot imagine is how the same request will sound in his own mouth at the end. The Qur'an records the plea several times, always in the same grammar of belatedness: *“When death approaches any of them, they cry, ‘My Lord! Let me go back, so I may do good in what I left behind.’”* (23:99–100). The answer is a barrier, not a negotiation. Others ask for the smallest of extensions: *“My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.”* (63:10), and the reply is flat — *“But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes.”* (63:11). The cruelty is not in the refusal. It is that the plea is identical to the one the same person declined a hundred times while it was still possible to act on it.

**Where the Sūrah Places the Blame**

By naming desire rather than doubt, the verse settles a practical question about how the message is to be delivered. If the obstacle were intellectual, the remedy would be more argument, and the Qur'an would be obliged to keep producing proofs until the last objection was met. Since the obstacle is appetite, the remedy is a warning that breaks the trance of *later*. That is why the sūrah moves at once from the denial to the state of the denier when the Day arrives, and why the door is held open until the breath stops: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The offer runs right up to the moment the request becomes impossible.
