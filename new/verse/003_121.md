## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:121

> ˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you left your home in the early morning to position the believers in the battlefield. And Allah is All-Hearing, AllKnowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Morning Recalled by Command**

The verse opens with a command to remember, and what is to be remembered is a specific morning: the Prophet leaving his household at daybreak to assign the believers their positions in the battlefield. The sentence preserves a piece of military history inside the revelation itself.

Most commentators place the scene at the battle of Uḥud, which some read as the battle of the Trench. The reasoning for Uḥud rests on the events the sūrah describes in the verses that follow, and the details in this sentence fit the day on which the Prophet placed his forces outside the city. The Book's purpose in recalling the morning is instruction rather than history, and the details it records are the ones the passage will use.

**The Consultation Before the Fighting**

The historians describe a disagreement over strategy before the battle. The Prophet consulted the community, and a majority advised remaining inside Madinah and using the city's fortifications; another group pressed to go out and meet the enemy. He decided to march out, and when some of those who had urged caution saw his decision, they asked him to stay; he replied that a prophet who had put on his armour should not take it off again.

The scene shows the community's internal life at a moment of danger, and the sūrah's mention of the morning belongs to the same stage of events. The Prophet positioned the believers himself, and the verse's phrasing presents the arrangement of the army as an act of leadership that the revelation records.

**The Withdrawal of a Portion of the Force**

The historians also report that the leader of the hypocrites in Madinah withdrew before the fighting with a large part of the force, on the grounds that his advice had not been followed. The numbers are usually given as about three hundred out of nearly a thousand.

[UNVERIFIED: the report that ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy withdrew from Uḥud with approximately three hundred men, which the historical and tafsīr sources relate without a chain I have examined.]

The withdrawal is the background to the verse's attention to positioning. A force that has already lost part of its strength is a force whose commander must place the remainder with care, and the sūrah's reminder of the dawn placement is a reminder of that care.

**Positioning the Believers**

The verse does not describe the arrangement, and other sources fill in the detail. The most important of them concerns the archers whom the Prophet placed on a hill to protect the army's rear, and the instruction he gave them.

A narration from al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (3039) reports that the Prophet appointed ʿAbdullāh ibn Jubayr over fifty archers and told them to hold their position, not to move even if they saw the enemy defeated and fleeing, until he sent for them. The instruction and its precise conditions became the crux of the day's events, and the sūrah's account of what followed will turn on what happened on that hill.

**God as All-Hearing and All-Knowing**

The verse closes by naming God as the one who hears and knows, and the two names frame everything the passage will describe. What was said in the consultation, what was intended in the withdrawal, and what each man carried in his heart on the march out were all heard and known.

The names also answer the anxiety of the moment. A community that has just discovered that part of its force has abandoned it needs to know that the matter is not beyond the knowledge of its Lord, and the verse's ending places the whole day inside that knowledge before the first engagement is described.

**The Day as a Lesson for Later Communities**

The battle ended in losses for the believers, and the sūrah treats the outcome as instruction rather than as a defeat. Its explanation of what went wrong is given in the verses that follow, and the faults it identifies are the ones a community in any age can commit.

That is why the Book records the morning's arrangements in such concrete terms. A reader who is told only that a battle went badly learns nothing he can use; a reader who is shown the positions assigned, the orders given, and the reasons they were not kept is being given a case study in the obedience that success requires.

**Why the Verse Addresses the Prophet Directly**

The command to remember is addressed to the Prophet, and the form is common in passages that recall his own actions. What is remembered is not something that happened to him but something he did.

The effect is to place the community's history within the revelation rather than outside it. The Book does not narrate the Prophet's decisions from a distance; it addresses him about his own morning, and the community that recites the passage hears its own story told to its own leader. The verse that follows continues the same recollection and turns it toward the condition of two groups of believers during the march.

**The Morning the Community Left the City**

The verse's detail about the early departure carries the whole setting of the day. The army left Madinah at dawn and took up positions at the foot of the mountain, with the city behind it and the open ground in front.

That arrangement put the community outside its own defences by its own decision, which is why the verse's next clause about positioning matters. The Prophet had argued the matter with his companions, had been urged in both directions, and had decided to march. What the revelation records is the morning on which the decision took effect: a leader on the ground at first light, placing his men, with the outcome still unknown to everyone present.
