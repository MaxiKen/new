## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:66

> Then We drowned the others.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pursuers Meet the End of Their Own Pursuit**

The verse describes the fate of those who followed Pharaoh into the sea: they are drowned. The sentence is brief because the moral sequence has already been established. Pharaoh refused the command to release Israel, mobilized the cities, called the departing people a threat, and sent a ready host after them. The drowning is not an arbitrary disaster detached from those choices. It is the judgment that overtakes a force committed to recovering the oppressed.

The word others distinguishes the drowned host from Moses and those delivered with him. The separation is complete. The same sea that opened as a path for the Israelites does not become a possession of the pursuers. God's sign has a different consequence for each group according to the purpose with which each entered the scene.

**Drowning Is a Divine Judgment, Not a Victory of Human Force**

The Egyptians are not defeated by an Israelite army. No human host is credited with overpowering Pharaoh. God Himself is the agent of the drowning. That attribution matters because the story is about the limits of tyranny before divine sovereignty, not about one earthly power replacing another.

The Israelites' deliverance does not turn them into conquerors who must destroy Pharaoh by their own strength. They are carried through the sea while God judges the pursuers. This protects the rescued community from pride and keeps the moral source of the reversal clear. The Lord who opened the way also closed the path on those who used it to pursue injustice.

**The Same Water Reveals Two Opposite Relations to God**

The sea is not good for one group and evil for another by nature. Its role changes according to God's command and the people's relation to that command. For Moses and his companions, the water becomes a passage. For the Egyptians, it becomes the place where their pursuit ends. The contrast shows that creation is under divine governance and does not belong to the purposes of whoever reaches it first.

This also gives the event symbolic force without reducing it to symbolism. The oppressed pass through what looked impossible because God commands a way. The oppressor is swallowed by the same setting because he enters in defiance. The physical event carries a moral distinction: obedience finds rescue, while arrogant pursuit meets judgment.

**The Host's Readiness Cannot Defeat the Decree**

Pharaoh had described his people as alert and prepared. The army had equipment, numbers, and the confidence of a state that believed its resources could recover the Israelites. None of that readiness alters the outcome. Preparation is a means, not a guarantee. A force can be organized for victory and still be moving toward its own destruction.

The lesson is not that planning is useless. It is that planning cannot become a substitute for justice or a claim to control the result. Pharaoh's army prepared to defend an unlawful possession. The better equipped it was, the more clearly its defeat exposed the limits of its purpose. Human readiness remains subject to the Lord who gives and removes power.

**Judgment Makes the Earlier Warning Complete**

God had told Moses that the Israelites would be pursued. The pursuit happened at sunrise, and the host was drawn to the divided sea. The drowning now completes the warning's hidden meaning. Pursuit was not proof that the Israelites had been abandoned. It was the route through which the tyrant's claim would be brought to its end.

This sequence gives the rescued community a way to remember danger without glorifying danger. They were not saved because Pharaoh's threat was imaginary. They were saved because God governed a real threat and judged the people who made it. The drowning confirms that the Lord's promise can include a frightening middle stage before deliverance is seen in full.

**The Fate Is a Warning to Every Pursuing Power**

The verse is tied to Pharaoh's host, but its principle extends beyond Egypt. A ruler who chases a people because he cannot accept their freedom may possess a large army and still be moving toward judgment. The power to pursue is not proof of the right to pursue. A state can make an unjust claim appear permanent until God removes its confidence.

This warning should not be used to celebrate the suffering of every defeated opponent. The text concerns a tyrannical host whose pursuit is part of a sustained system of oppression. It teaches that judgment belongs to God and that believers must not assume the role of divine executioner. The proper response is humility before the Lord who alone knows the whole moral account.

**The Drowned Host Leaves No Rival Lord Behind**

Pharaoh had claimed authority over worship and bodies. His drowning strips that claim of its public force. The host that enforced it is gone, and the Israelites are no longer required to measure their future by the ruler's permission. The sea has become a boundary between the old order and the community God has delivered.

The short sentence carries finality. There is no negotiation after the drowning and no recovery of Pharaoh's position through a new threat. The tyrant's claim ends where his pursuit ends. The Lord who saved Moses and those with him has shown that no army can turn oppression into an everlasting right.

The finality also protects the rescued from the fear of return. Pharaoh's host cannot regroup behind them or reclaim the people through another order. The sea has closed the chapter of that pursuit. Deliverance has become a settled fact, not a temporary escape. The rescued need not live as though Pharaoh's army is still one step behind them.
