## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:127

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael, ˹both praying,˺ “Our Lord! Accept ˹this˺ from us. You are indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Father and Son at Work**

The verse places two men at the same task: Abraham and Ishmael raising the foundation of the House. The Book elsewhere describes the site as assigned to Abraham, and this sentence presents the construction as a shared labour, with the prayer that accompanies it attributed to both.

The sūrah's account of Abraham has been building toward this scene, and the participation of Ishmael is important for the argument the passage is developing about the claims made in Abraham's name. The Book presents the sanctuary as the work of a father and a son whose descendants would later divide over the legacy, and it records their shared prayer before the divisions existed. The shared labour also answers a claim the sūrah is handling, since the Book presents the sanctuary as the work of the line that both communities honoured. The scene is recorded before the disputes that divided their descendants, and the reader receives it as the historical ground beneath the later arguments.

**The Raising of the Foundations**

The Arabic describes the two men raising or lifting the foundations, and the Book's other references to the House suggest that they were restoring what had been established earlier rather than beginning from nothing. Some passages state that the first house established for humanity was the one at Bakkah, a name connected with the site, and the commentators collected accounts of an earlier origin for the building.

The traditions they reported include stories of Adam and of a structure that was raised to the earth and later disappeared in the flood, so that Abraham's work was to bring out the foundations again. Al-Ṭabarī recorded these accounts while judging that they were not reliable as history, and other commentators accepted them, and the difference between the two positions shows how the community handled material of uncertain transmission.

**The Prayer Offered During the Work**

The prayer recorded in this verse is the first in a series that continues through the following two verses, and it consists of a single request: accept this from us. The two men had been given a task and had performed it, and what they asked for was not reward but acceptance.

That posture is characteristic of the Book's account of worship, where the work is done and the outcome is then entrusted to God. The same attitude appears in the Book's descriptions of those who spend, fight, or give, where the deed is performed and the hope is that it will be received, since nothing a person does obliges God. The prayer also establishes the pattern the following verses continue, since the petitions that come after it concern submission, descendants, rites, and a messenger. The request for acceptance is therefore the first item in a sequence that reaches from the building of the House to the community's life.

**"You Are the All-Hearing, All-Knowing"**

The two names at the end of the prayer give the reason for the confidence expressed in it. God hears the request and knows the intention behind it, so that nothing in the work escapes His notice and no nuance of the worshipper's purpose is lost.

The Book attaches these names to prayers and to statements about worship throughout, and they appear again in the sūrah's treatment of the community's own practices. For a reader performing a task with mixed motives, the names in the prayer carry the assurance that what God weighs is not only the visible work but the intention with which it was done. The two names also bear on the work itself, since what the two men were building was for God's worship and the acceptance they sought concerned the intention as much as the structure. The Book's attachment of these names to acts of worship occurs throughout the sūrah.

**The Claim This Scene Answers**

The sūrah has been addressing communities that treated Abraham as their own and traced their standing to him, and it is now describing what he actually did. He built the House with Ishmael, prayed for the city, and asked for a community and a messenger from among his descendants, and the Book uses those facts to test the claims made about him.

The direction of the argument is important. The passage does not set out to answer the communities' claims point by point; it narrates the founding of the sanctuary and records the prayers, and the reader is left to see which claims the narrative supports. By the time the sūrah turns to the direction of prayer, the ground has been laid.

**The Place of the Verse in the Sūrah**

The scene at the House stands near the middle of the sūrah, and the sūrah returns to the theme in later passages about the pilgrimage and the sacred months. Its function in this place is to ground the community's rites in the practice of a man whom the Book praises and whom the earlier scriptures themselves honour.

The next verses continue the same prayer, moving from the request for acceptance to petitions for submission, for descendants, for the rites, and for the raising of a messenger from among the people. Read together they present the sanctuary's founding as an act of worship accompanied by a hope for what would come after, and the sūrah will later state that the hope was fulfilled.

**Building and Praying at the Same Time**

The sentence places the construction and the supplication together, and the combination is one of the passage's most instructive features. The two men had been given a task, and they performed it while asking God to accept what they were doing. The Book's accounts of worship usually present the act and the hope in that order, since nothing a person does obliges God to receive it. The prayer is also the first in a series that runs through the following verses, so that the reader hears the founders of the sanctuary asking for the maintenance of what they had built: submission for themselves, a community from their descendants, the rites, forgiveness, and a messenger to teach. What the verse therefore presents is not a monument but a project entrusted to God at every stage, and the prayers of the two men supply the model for the community that would later face the same House.
