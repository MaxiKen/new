## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:127

> And ˹remember˺ when Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael, ˹both praying,˺ “Our Lord! Accept ˹this˺ from us. You are indeed the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Raising the Foundations**

The verse narrates the building, and its verb carries the tradition's whole debate about the House's age. *Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael* — the verb *yarfaʿān*, raising, lifting up — describes work on foundations that exist rather than ground broken anew, and the exegetical literature preserves the lore that accounts for the difference: accounts of a much more ancient origin for the Kaʿbah, going back to Adam — the father, having ceased to hear the voices of the angels after his fall, praying for their return and receiving the promise of a House on earth around which people would circle as the angels circle the Throne; the House sent down, standing through the early ages, withdrawn or buried at the flood, and its foundations brought to light again by Abraham and his son rather than built from nothing. Al-Ṭabarī's own position on the lore is characteristically careful: he recounts the stories and judges them unreliable as actual history, while many other commentators hold them true. The weighing does not need to settle the archaeology to read the verse, and the verse's own wording holds what all readings share: the raising was commissioned, joint, and prayerful, and its object was *the House* — definite, known to the builders, an entity whose identity preceded their work on it. Whether the foundations were Adam's or bare ground, the recital files the patriarchs as the House's raisers, and their raising as the act the following clause turns into worship.

**The First House**

The Qur'an states the House's rank in its own terms, and the statement is the verse's doctrinal anchor: *surely the first House of worship established for humanity is the one at Bakkah — blessed, and a guidance for all people* (3:96). The name *Bakkah* is the verse's own antiquarian evidence — an alternate form of Makkah that the tradition connects to the biblical Valley of Baca in the Psalms (84:6), a link the commentators note across the two scriptural archives. The firstness claimed is of establishment *for humanity* — the earliest house set up for human worship, which is compatible with the Adam-lore and independent of it: whatever the foundations' age, the House's standing as humanity's first appointed sanctuary is the Qur'an's own assertion. Sūrah al-Ḥajj supplies the building's commission from its other end: *when We assigned to Abraham the site of the House* (22:26) — the location designated, the builder placed at the designated point, with the charge that follows in that verse running exactly parallel to verse 125's purification-command. The two verses together fix the building's theology: the site is assigned, not chosen; the House is established, not invented; and the raisers' work, however monumental in its labor, is execution of a divine plan whose firstness, blessedness, and guidance-function belong to the plan rather than to the masons. The verse's audience — a community praying toward this House, months from legislating the direction permanently — received in these clauses their qibla's full credentials: first, blessed, guidance, assigned, raised by the two names their own genealogies traced to.

**“Accept From Us”**

The builders pray while they build, and the prayer's single petition is the verse's spiritual center: *our Lord, accept from us*. The request names the work's actual risk. Construction can fail structurally — but the patriarchs, raising a house whose site was assigned by Allah, do not pray about walls; they pray about standing. The deed's acceptance is not guaranteed by the deed's correctness: an act can be complete in its performance and unaccepted in its account, and the prayer confesses the gap — acceptance belongs to the Receiver, not to the act's arithmetic. The tradition's grammar of acceptance runs through the recital: the fast accepted, the charity received, the pilgrimage *mabrūr* — each pairing a completed human act with a requested divine response, and each keeping the response on mercy's side of the ledger. The prayer's timing is its instruction: it is spoken *during* the raising, not after it — the builders' hands moving while their tongues ask, the acceptance sought for work in progress, which makes the prayer a continuous liturgy of labor rather than a closing ceremony. The two Names that seal the petition state the acceptance's mechanism: *You are indeed the All-Hearing* — the prayer reaches its addressee, the asking is not spoken into vacancy; *the All-Knowing* — the work and the intention behind it are both held in knowledge, the seen stones and the unseen sincerity registered together. The hearing answers the prayer's utterance and the knowing answers the prayer's unspoken content, and the pair is the builders' complete warrant: nothing in the raising — not the effort, not the fear of inadequacy, not the intention no colleague can verify — falls outside the two attributes the petition names.

**The Two Builders**

The verse joins the two names in one sentence of joint labor, and the joining is the recital's genealogical politics at its most concrete. Abraham, the imām tested and appointed at verse 124; Ishmael, the son of the Arabian line, named here not as attendant but as co-raiser — the foundations lifted by both, the prayer said by both (the verse's grammar puts the petition in their shared mouth), the acceptance sought for both. The tradition's accounts of the building divide the labor between them — one laying stones, one handing them, in the reports the exegetes preserve — and whatever the division's details, the verse's headline is partnership: the House's construction belongs to the two lines' common ancestor-pair, and the prayer over it is a single prayer in a dual voice. For the recital's Madinan audience the fact carried its full charge. The Israelite claim ran through Isaac; the Ishmaelite line ran to the Arabs; the House now being raised in the narrative stood as the work of both brothers under their father, its purification-charge (125) laid on both, its acceptance-prayer uttered by both — an institution older than the division of the lines and jointly owned across it. The verse thereby performs, in narrative form, the covenant-law verse 124 stated in doctrine: the office and the trust descend through fulfillment and righteousness, held here by father and son together, with the wrongdoer — of either line, of any generation — excluded by the answer Abraham received. The two builders pray as one party, and the House they raise is, in the recital's design, the standing rebuke to every later monopoly of the patriarchs.

**The Prayer That Outlives the Building**

The verse's final reach is its prayer's permanence. The walls the patriarchs raised have been rebuilt, damaged, and re-raised across the millennia — the tradition records the House's repairs through pre-Islamic and Islamic centuries — but the petition spoken during the first raising has never left the building's liturgy: *our Lord, accept from us* is recited at the House by every generation of its servants, and the two Names that seal it govern every act performed there. The prayer's transfer extends past the precinct. Every worker for the sacred — the teacher mid-lesson, the giver mid-gift, the builder of any house where Allah's Name is mentioned — inherits the verse's posture: labor in motion, acceptance in question, the request made during the work rather than after its showcase, and the warrant located in the Hearing and the Knowing rather than in the work's visible quality. The verse installs this posture as the recital's labor-law, and its two clauses — the raising and the asking — are never separated in it: no building scene without the prayer, no prayer without the building underway. The patriarchs' fear was not that their work would fall but that it might not be received, and the fear is the verse's gift to every builder after them — the anxiety that keeps the deed for Allah, spoken in the sentence that asks Him to take it. The petition continues beyond this verse, and the House stands as its answered monument: accepted, purified, made center and sanctuary, first of the houses and guidance for the people — the acceptance asked in humility and filed, by the recital's own telling, in the permanence of what was built.
