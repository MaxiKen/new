## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:68

> But if they argue with you, then say, “Allah knows best what you do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Concession the Verse Builds In**

*Wa-in jādilūka* — and if they dispute with you.

The conditional is doing something the previous verse did not. Verse 67 instructed the Prophet not to let them draw him into argument over the rites. Verse 68 assumes they will do it anyway.

That is not an inconsistency; it is the Qur'an's usual way of legislating for the world as it is. The instruction in verse 67 was about where the Prophet's effort belongs. The instruction here is about what to do when the instruction is ignored, and the sūrah supplies it in advance because the outcome is not in doubt.

The verb *jādala* is the same one used in verses 3 and 8 for the disputant who argues without knowledge, and the echo is intentional. The people described in verse 68 are the same type the sūrah described at its opening, and by this point in the chapter the reader has been given a full account of them.

**What to Say, and Why That**

*Fa-qul Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā taʿmalūn* — then say: God knows best what you do.

The sentence is a formula of disengagement, and it is worth taking apart, because a careless reading hears it as a shrug.

It is not a shrug, and it does three things at once. It declines the argument, since it offers no counter-claim for the opponent to attack. It relocates the question to a knowing that the opponent cannot dispute, since nobody claims that God is ignorant of what people do. And it warns, because the knowledge being invoked is the knowledge that will be acted on.

The last of those three is what makes the sentence effective rather than evasive. "God knows best what you are doing" is not a way of saying the matter is settled in the speaker's favour. It is a way of saying the matter is recorded.

Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ records the same disengagement as the practice of the believers, with the account books kept separate: *“When they hear slanderous talk, they turn away from it, saying, ‘We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. Peace ˹is our only response˺ to you! We want nothing to do with those who act ignorantly’”* (28:55). Deeds assigned to their doers, peace offered once, ignorance declined as a conversation partner. Verse 68's single sentence is that paragraph reduced to its operative clause: the record invoked, the exchange ended.

**The Knowledge Invoked Is the Sūrah's Own Theme**

The sūrah has been insistent that nothing escapes the record. Verse 10 described the book a person will be handed and told to read. Verse 70, two verses from here, will say that everything in the heavens and the earth is in a clear record. Verse 76 will say that God knows what is before them and what is behind them.

Verse 68 is the first of those statements turned into a reply. What makes the formula work is that it is not rhetorical: on the sūrah's own account, there is a record, it is complete, and it will be produced.

Sūrah al-Mujādalah states what the invoked knowledge will do on the day it is acted on: *“He will then inform them of what they have done. Allah has kept account of it all, while they have forgotten it”* (58:6). Kept by God, forgotten by them — the asymmetry is what makes verse 68's sentence a warning rather than a shrug. The disputant who will not stop arguing has forgotten most of what he has done; the record he is being referred to has forgotten none of it, and the informing is dated to the resurrection of all together.

**Disengagement Is Not Defeat**

The Qur'an's instructions for handling disputes of this kind are consistent, and they are worth assembling because they are frequently misread as weakness.

The pattern is always the same: state the claim, give whatever evidence the audience can check, and then, if the argument continues, decline it and refer the matter to God. The Prophet is told at verse 49 to announce his office rather than to produce the punishment; at verse 67 to invite rather than to dispute; and here to say one sentence and stop.

What this protects is not the messenger's dignity but the message's time. A dispute of this kind has no natural end — the sūrah has already described people who dispute without knowledge and who will not stop (22:55) — and every hour spent in it is an hour not spent inviting.

The disengagement is the Qur'an's portrait of the ideal believer's manners, not a concession to weakness: *“The ˹true˺ servants of the Most Compassionate are those who walk on the earth humbly, and when the foolish address them ˹improperly˺, they only respond with peace”* (25:63). Responding with peace to provocation is listed as the defining behaviour of the servants of the Merciful — placed first in the catalogue, before the night prayer and the charity. What verse 68 prescribes for the Prophet is what the Qur'an prescribes as the mark of every believer grown to full stature.

**Restraint Where a Threat Would Be Easy**

One further feature deserves notice: the reply contains no threat, no insult and no prediction.

Compare it with what the sūrah says elsewhere about these same people — garments of fire, boiling water, iron rods. In a face-to-face dispute, the Prophet is given none of that to say. He is given a sentence about knowledge and left there.

The restraint is the instruction's most imitable part. What the sūrah permits itself in describing the outcome is not what it puts into the messenger's mouth during the argument.

The restraint has its own technical term, given to the Prophet as a standing command: *“Be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say, and depart from them courteously”* (73:10) — in the Arabic, *wahjurhum hajran jamīlan*, a gracious avoidance. Patience with what is said, departure from those saying it, and the departure itself qualified as gracious. Verse 68 is that instruction executed: the single sentence about knowledge is what gracious avoidance sounds like in practice — no threat issued, no prediction ventured, the case transferred and the speaker gone.

**The Verse's View of Argument Itself**

There is an assumption embedded in this instruction that deserves to be made explicit, because it shapes everything the sūrah says about disagreement.

The assumption is that some arguments cannot be won, and that this is a fact about the other party rather than about the case. The sūrah has described people who dispute without knowledge (22:8), whose hearts are sick (22:53), whose hearts are hard (22:53), and who will not cease to be in doubt until the Hour arrives (22:55). Against that description, more argument is not merely futile; it is a category error.

What can be done instead is exactly what the verse prescribes: state the position, decline the exchange, and name the fact that the matter is recorded. That is not a retreat from the disagreement but a recognition of what kind of thing it is.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf compresses the whole posture into three imperatives: *“Be gracious, enjoin what is right, and turn away from those who act ignorantly”* (7:199). Graciousness toward people, right enjoined where it can be heard, and turning away where it cannot — the third imperative is not a failure of the second but its boundary. Verse 68's rule belongs to that third clause: the argument that cannot be won is not fought harder but left, and the leaving is itself commanded.

**The Formula's Second Life**

The sentence the Prophet is given — *God knows best what you do* — is not unique to this verse, and its other occurrences tell us how the Qur'an intends it to be heard.

It appears in the sūrahs both as a reply to opponents and as a statement about the interior of a community: a reminder that motives are not visible to the people who judge them and are entirely visible to the One who will. In both uses the function is the same — to close a line of investigation that cannot produce a result here and to place it where it can.

That is what makes the sentence more than a conversational exit. It transfers the case, and in doing so it removes the speaker's need to win.

The God-knows-best formula closes disputes the Qur'an declines to adjudicate here: *“Indeed, your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided”* (6:117). Straying and guidance — the very things the disputants claim to discern in each other — are reserved to a knowing the disputants do not share. That is why the sentence transfers the case: it names the one assessment neither party can perform, and places the verdict where assessment is possible.

**Where This Leaves the Believer**

Read with verses 67 and 69, this verse completes a three-part rule for disagreement: invite, do not dispute, and if disputed, say one sentence and refer the matter upward.

The rule is demanding in an unobvious way. It requires a person to forgo the satisfaction of winning, to accept being thought defeated, and to leave the verdict with someone else. The sūrah offers no guarantee that the argument will be won in this life; it guarantees only that it will be settled in the next.

The cost of forgoing victory has its own promise attached, in the verse on repelling evil with good: *“Good and evil cannot be equal. Respond ˹to evil˺ with what is best, then the one you are in a feud with will be like a close friend”* (41:34). The single sentence of verse 68 is a small instance of the best response — knowledge named instead of insult returned — and the verse holds out what disengagement rarely promises: that the feud itself may end, not by being won but by being answered with something it cannot feed on.
