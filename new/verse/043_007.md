## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:7

> **But no prophet ever came to them without being mocked.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rule Without Exception**

The sentence is built as a universal negative. Wa-mā yaʾtīhim min nabiyyin illā kānū bihi yastahziʾūn: not one prophet came to them but they used to mock him. The particle min before nabiyy is the min of generality, which turns "a prophet" into "any prophet at all", and the exception illā then admits no case outside the rule. The grammar makes the claim total. There was no earlier people who welcomed their prophet with respect and no prophet who escaped ridicule. This is a hard statement, and its hardness is the point. If mockery were an occasional response, the Prophet ﷺ might wonder what he had done to provoke it. The verse tells him that it is the universal response, built into the encounter between a messenger and a people who do not want to change. The same rule is stated in almost the same words in 15:11 and 36:30, and in 36:30 it is preceded by a lament over the servants: what regret is upon them. The lament is not for the prophets but for those who laughed.

**Why Mockery and Not Argument**

The verb is yastahziʾūn, from the tenth form of h-z-ʾ, and it means to treat someone as a joke, to make light of him. The earlier peoples are not described as refuting their prophets, debating them, or even ignoring them. They laughed. There is a reason the Qurʾān names this response rather than another. Argument concedes that the other party is worth arguing with; mockery denies it. When the people of Nūḥ passed him building the ark on dry land they laughed at him (11:38), and when the people of Shuʿayb heard his call they asked with sarcasm whether his prayers commanded him to tell them what to do with their wealth (11:87). Mockery is the weapon of those who have no answer but do not want to appear defeated. It is also a defence against being moved. A man who laughs at a message has put a wall between himself and the possibility that it is true. The Quraysh used the same wall, calling the Prophet ﷺ a poet, a madman, and a sorcerer, and the verse tells him that the wall is very old.

**Kānū: A Habit, Not an Incident**

The verb of mocking is placed after kānū, "they used to", which in Arabic marks continued or habitual action. The earlier peoples did not mock their prophets once and then reconsider. They kept at it, as a settled practice, for as long as the prophet was among them. This detail matters for the consolation the verse offers. It would be a thin comfort to tell the Prophet ﷺ that others were laughed at once. The verse tells him that others were laughed at continually, as he was, over years, and that the laughter did not stop until either the people repented or the punishment came. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī both read verses 6 and 7 as a single unit of consolation: the abundance of prophets in verse 6 and the universality of mockery in verse 7 together tell the Prophet ﷺ that nothing in his experience is new. The habitual tense adds one more thing. Since mockery was the habit of every earlier people, the Quraysh's mockery is not a sign of their sophistication or independence. It is the most ordinary thing a people has ever done.

**Consolation and Indictment in One Clause**

The verse speaks to the Prophet ﷺ and about the Quraysh at the same time, and the two messages pull in opposite directions. To him it says: be at ease, this is the common lot of prophets. About them it says: they are behaving exactly as the destroyed nations behaved. The Quraysh who heard this verse recited could not miss the second message, because the people referred to in the previous verse were the awwalīn, the ancients, whose ruins they knew, and this verse says those ancients mocked their prophets. The next verse will say what happened to them. So the Quraysh were being told, in the middle of their own mockery, that mockery was the last thing many peoples did before they were removed. Al-Ṭabarī draws the practical conclusion for the Prophet ﷺ: the derision he faces is no greater than what those before him faced. The verse could add a second conclusion for the mockers, but it does not need to. Anyone who has walked past the dwellings of Thamūd can supply it.

**Laughter as the Test of a Messenger**

There is a quiet inversion in this verse that the exegetes draw out from the wider Qurʾān. If every prophet was mocked, then being mocked is a mark of standing in the line of prophets rather than a mark against it. The Quraysh treated their ridicule as evidence that Muḥammad ﷺ could not be what he claimed; a real messenger, they assumed, would command respect. The verse turns the evidence around. A real messenger, on the record of history, is precisely one who is laughed at by his people. This is why the Qurʾān elsewhere promises that the mockers will be dealt with (15:95) and reminds the Prophet ﷺ that messengers before him were mocked and that what they mocked came back upon them (6:10, 21:41). The comfort is not that the laughter will stop soon; the Prophet ﷺ was mocked for years after this verse was revealed. The comfort is that the laughter proves nothing about him and a great deal about those doing it. It places him where Nūḥ, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and Mūsā stood, and it places his opponents where their peoples stood. The next verse says where those peoples went.
