## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:58

> treasures, and splendid residences.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The List Moves From Nature to Human-Made Splendor**

The verse completes the list of what the tyrants leave behind: treasures and splendid residences. The earlier gardens and springs represented cultivated abundance; these items represent wealth stored and status displayed. The sequence moves from the resources of the land to the structures and possessions that society builds around them. Pharaoh's world contains every visible sign by which a ruling class measures success.

Yet all of it is named within the story of departure. The treasures do not protect their owners, and the residences do not become refuges. Their grandeur is real as a worldly fact but powerless as a shield against divine judgment. The list asks the reader to distinguish between the existence of wealth and the meaning assigned to it. Possession can be a trust, or it can become a stage on which tyranny performs permanence.

**Treasures Turned Into Evidence of False Security**

A treasure is valuable because people believe it will secure the future. Pharaoh's court has accumulated enough wealth to imagine that nothing essential can be lost. The pursuit of the Israelites exposes that belief. The rulers leave the treasures because they think they can return after recovering the fugitives. Their confidence rests on the assumption that the future belongs to them.

God's judgment interrupts that assumption. Wealth can be left behind in one moment, and ownership can change without the former owners consenting. The treasures that seemed to prove Pharaoh's strength become silent witnesses that strength was temporary. A person who stores wealth without preparing for accountability may discover that the storehouse remains while the owner is gone.

**Splendid Residences Cannot Make a Tyrant at Home**

A residence is more than a building. It represents belonging, rank, memory, and the right to inhabit a place without fear. The tyrants' splendid residences embody the social order they have built. When they are brought out from them, the narrative shows that home is not secure when it is founded on the dispossession of others.

This does not mean that beautiful homes are blameworthy. The moral issue is the relationship between splendor and oppression. A residence becomes part of the indictment when its comfort depends on a system that denies another people freedom. The verse names the buildings because the buildings are not outside the judgment of the order that produced them.

**Worldly Station Is Not a Lasting Rank**

The homes and treasures also mark the tyrants' station. Their architecture announces who commands, who serves, and who may enter. They live high in a hierarchy that treats status as proof of worth. God brings them out of that station, showing that social rank has no permanence when it is used against justice.

A person can be honored by society and condemned by God, just as a person can be marginalized by society and honored by God. The visible arrangement does not reveal the final order. Pharaoh's residences may make him appear secure, but the Lord can remove him from them and give what he valued to people he tried to keep below him.

**Abundance Becomes a Moral Test**

The tyrants possess enough resources to respond to the Israelites with generosity or at least restraint. Their abundance increases the seriousness of their refusal. They are not driven by hunger to seize every possible laborer. They are rulers with gardens, springs, treasures, and residences who still insist on owning a vulnerable people.

Wealth therefore functions as a test. It can widen the ability to do good, but it can also widen the distance between a ruler and the people affected by his decisions. The more a person has, the more easily he can mistake comfort for divine approval. The verse shows that material abundance does not answer the question of whether power is just.

**Leaving Wealth Behind Reverses the Court's Story**

Pharaoh's court had presented the Israelites as a threat to the land. The list now reveals who actually possesses the wealth and status being defended. The tyrants leave their gardens, springs, treasures, and residences because they are pursuing the people. The public can see that the rulers' concern is not a lack of resources but the loss of control.

This reversal is central to the narrative. The court called liberation an attack on national prosperity, yet prosperity belongs to the tyrants who refuse release. God's judgment removes the rulers from the symbols they used to justify their authority. The story refuses to let splendor settle the moral question.

**Possessions Remain While Ownership Changes**

The verse does not say that the gardens, treasures, and residences vanish. They remain as created things even when the tyrants leave. This distinction matters. The world is not evil because tyrants possessed it. The wrong lies in the claim that possession guarantees status and that status authorizes oppression.

When ownership changes, the objects reveal their created nature. They were never eternal extensions of Pharaoh's person. They could serve another community, another order, or another use. The tyrants' departure breaks the illusion that wealth belongs to its possessor by necessity. God gives and removes, and no residence can become a permanent throne.

The list is also a warning to those who inherit comfort without asking how it was made. Splendid residences can conceal forced labor, unequal law, and the fear of people outside the walls. A just use of wealth begins by recognizing those hidden relations. Pharaoh's court sees only what it owns; the divine account includes the people whose lives made that ownership possible. Wealth cannot hide those relations from the Lord who judges both the house and the hands that built it. No wall can outlast divine judgment. Monumental scale is not evidence of permanence; it is another occasion to remember the One who grants and removes power.
