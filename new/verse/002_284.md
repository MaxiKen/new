## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:284

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it. He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sovereignty Premise**

The sūrah's closing movement opens by restating its property-law at the widest possible scale: *To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth*. The ownership-clause is the same formula āyat al-kursī carried (2:255), and its placement here does the same preparatory work: the accounting that follows is legitimate because the Accountant owns everything the account concerns — the wealth whose documentation the preceding verses legislated, the hearts whose concealments the testimony-clause policed, and the souls the Day-return verse (2:281) gathered from every economic identity into their singular kasb. The commentators read the clause as the ground of the verse's second sentence rather than its decoration: universal ownership makes universal accounting coherent, since nothing in any soul's contents falls outside the Owner's jurisdiction. The sūrah that began by describing the Book as the guidance in which there is no doubt ends its legislative career by naming the jurisdiction in which there is no outside.

**The Disclosed and the Concealed**

The verse's center is its audit-clause: *Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it*. Al-Ṭabarī records the specific reading first — the disclosure and concealment refer to the testimony the preceding verses legislated, the witness's moral responsibility to discharge his duty truthfully, whether he testifies openly or holds the truth back in his heart — and then the universal reading, which the tradition largely adopted: the clause covers the heart's entire contents, spoken or unspoken. The universal reading is what shook the verse's first hearers, and the reports the exegetes preserve are vivid: many Companions were distressed and grieved on hearing it, fearing they could never escape the reality it described; some worried it meant accountability for the fleeting thought itself; some wept from fear. The account of their distress is preserved together with its resolution — the two verses that follow, the sūrah's final pair, were revealed in part to quiet exactly this disquiet — and the pairing is the tradition's hermeneutic key to the whole closing: the audit-clause states the range of God's knowledge, and what follows states the range of His assignment.

**Accounting Is Not Punishment**

The tradition's clarification of the verse is precise and comes from al-Ṭabarī's own commentary: bring you to account need not mean punish. The muḥāsabah — the reckoning, the calling-to-account — is the Day's procedure, and in Islamic belief every person will first be brought to account for all his deeds before entering Paradise or Hell, except the elect who pass to Paradise directly (the sūrah's own verse on whom God wills among the reckoned-without-punishment, 2:212's discussion). The verse's forgiveness-and-punishment clause then names the accounting's two possible outcomes as divine dispositions: *He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills* — the will operating at the audit's end, not the audit's fact. The sūrah of the Cave's picture supplies the accounting's texture, quoted by the exegetes here: the record laid open, the wicked crying out — woe to us, what a book this is, leaving nothing out, small or great, but numbering it all (18:49). The verse's claim is thus total in knowledge and open in outcome: every heart-content is registered, and the registered stands before a will whose two movements are forgiveness and punishment — the dread the Companions wept at being the dread of the registration's completeness, which the verse affirms, and of the outcome's severity, which the verse leaves with the will that also forgives.

**No Abrogation, Two Descriptions**

Some held that the capacity-principle of the sūrah's final verse (2:286) abrogates this verse's audit-clause, and the commentators answer the claim on two grounds. The first is textual: there is no contradiction between the two — God's knowledge-and-reckoning of the heart's contents and God's not-tasking any soul beyond its capacity describe different relations (the audit's range and the obligation's measure), and both stand. The second is categorical: these are descriptions of God's acts, not legal rulings, and descriptions of the divine action are not subject to abrogation at all — naskh operates on commands and prohibitions, not on statements of what God knows, wills, and does. The clarification matters for reading the whole closing sequence: the verse is not a law later repealed but a doctrine permanently stated — the heart's contents are within the divine jurisdiction, full stop — and what the following verses adjust is not the jurisdiction but the community's burden under it. The Companions' weeping, on this reading, was a misreading of category: they heard a description of God's knowledge as an assignment to their hearts, and the sequence's remainder exists to separate the two — the knowledge total, the assignment capacity-bounded, the difference between them the mercy the final verses legislate.

**The Intentions Ḥadīth**

The tradition's operative commentary on the audit-clause is the ḥadīth the exegetes quote at length — the divine accounting of the heart's movements, in four cases. Whoever intends a good deed and does not perform it: God records it with Him as a complete good deed. Whoever intends it and performs it: God records ten good deeds, up to seven hundred times, and beyond. Whoever intends an evil deed and does not perform it: God records it with Him as a good deed — the tradition elsewhere explains, because he left it for God's sake. Whoever intends it and performs it: God records one evil deed. The commentators use the report to draw the distinction the weeping Companions needed: the accounting separates a psychological state from an executed choice — the difference between the concrete intention blocked by circumstance beyond the agent's control and the passing thought the heart sincerely struggles against. The unperformed good is rewarded as performed, the abandoned evil is rewarded as a good, the committed evil is recorded singly against the committed good's seven-hundredfold ceiling: the arithmetic of the audit, in the ḥadīth's form, is systematically tilted toward mercy — the same tilt the verse's forgiveness-whomever-He-wills clause names as the will's prerogative and the ḥadīth displays as the will's practice.

**Power Over All Things**

The verse's closing name gathers its three movements: *Allah is Most Capable of everything* — the qadr that owns the heavens and earth, registers the heart's concealed and revealed, and holds the forgiveness and the punishment in one will. The commentators connect the power-clause to the audit's feasibility: an accounting of every heart-content across every soul is an operation of infinite capacity, and the name states the capacity the clause assumes. The power is also the verse's answer to the sūrah's economic sequence, which ends here: the documentation legislated because human memory fails, the pledge because the road lacks scribes, the trust because instruments run out — and the divine power because nothing in the final audit depends on instruments at all. The God who teaches the scribe (2:282), who knows what the trusted debtor does (2:283), and who owns both worlds (this verse) is the God before whom the sūrah's last movement now begins — the believers' creed and their prayer, revealed, the tradition holds, into the very distress this verse's audit-clause created. The sovereignty-statement is the closing sequence's foundation: the heart is audited by its Owner, the outcome rests with its Forgiver-and-Punisher, and the community's response — stated in the two verses that follow — is the response the audit was sent to elicit: belief, hearing, obedience, and the prayer of the capacity-bounded to the All-Capable.
