## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:25

> There are some of them who ˹pretend to˺ listen to your recitation ˹of the Quran˺, but We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears. Even if they were to see every sign, they still would not believe in them. The disbelievers would ˹even˺ come to argue with you, saying, “This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Listeners Who Do Not Hear**

The verse opens with attendance. Some of them do listen to the recitation, and their bodies are present in the gathering, which makes their condition the more revealing. What reaches them is not a rumour but the text itself, recited in their own language. Nothing arrives; the sentence reports the veil over their hearts and the deafness in their ears, and it does so without suggesting that either came from a shortage of recitation on the Prophet's side.

The Book uses the same language for the same condition elsewhere. Their hearts are sealed along with their hearing, and their sight is covered (2:7); veils are cast over the hearts and deafness placed in the ears, and when the Lord alone is mentioned they turn their backs in aversion (17:46); they are described as those who turned a blind eye to the reminder and could not stand listening to it (18:101).

**The Two Impediments and What Each Blocks**

The veil over the heart prevents understanding, and the deafness in the ears prevents reception. The pairing is exact, and it distinguishes two separate failures that a casual observer would merge. A person may hear words without grasping them, and a person may grasp a meaning without accepting the words he heard. The verse closes both routes at once, and it locates the obstruction inside the listener rather than in the message.

Commentators describe the veil as a darkness of the soul that obscures the light of the message, which fits the sūrah's opening statement that God made darkness and light (6:1). What is at stake in the verse is thus not the quality of the recitation. It is whether the lamp can be seen by a person who has closed the shutters.

**The Question of Who Closed the Shutters**

Since God is the one said to have placed the covering and the deafness, the verse raises the question every reader of the Book eventually asks. Some commentators take it as proof that belief and disbelief are decreed, and the veil as a sign of a fixed disposition toward denial. Others object that this would make the call to believe meaningless for those so described, and would give the disbelievers an argument against God, since no soul is tasked beyond its capacity (2:286).

The stronger reading, and the one the surrounding verses support, is that the covering follows the refusal rather than preceding it. The Book states the sequence where it says that God leaves to stray those who are already in a state of disbelief and wrongdoing, and where it describes a person who takes his own desire as a god and is then left to stray knowingly (45:23). The hardening is the consequence of a decision, not the cause of it, and the same chapter that reports the veil reports the deliberate turning away in the verse immediately before (18:57).

**Signs Would Not Change the Outcome**

Having described the impediment, the verse removes the last comfort available to the sceptic: even if they were to see every sign, they still would not believe in them. The Book repeats this in several places with a firmness that leaves no room for negotiation (7:146; 10:96). The clearest case is recorded in the sūrah of the Moon, where the splitting of the moon, granted in answer to a demand for a sign, produced the reply that this was the same old magic (54:1–2).

A demand for evidence therefore cannot be taken at face value when it comes from this quarter. The sign would arrive, be examined, and be renamed, because the obstacle was never the strength of the proof.

**The Debate That Follows the Recitation**

The verse's last clause records what happens after the listening. The disbelievers come to argue, and the argument they bring is that the Qur'an is nothing but ancient fables. The same charge appears in a series of reports — the recitation is familiar, they could produce something like it, and the material is old (8:31); it is what the Lord has revealed, they are told, and they answer that it is ancient fables (16:24; 68:15); and others allege that it was written down and rehearsed morning and evening (25:5).

The charge is not a counter-argument, since it says nothing about whether the material is true. It is a social verdict dressed as a literary one, and the Book quotes it because it shows what the arguing was for. People who have decided not to accept a message do not need to refute it; they need a phrase that makes it uninteresting to their neighbours.

**Who the Verse Was Reported to Concern**

Some commentators report that this passage concerned leading figures of the Quraysh, including Abū Jahl and Abū Sufyān, who sat near the Prophet and heard the recitation without accepting it. The attribution is reported in the tafsīr literature rather than established by a firm chain, so it is given here as a report and not as a fact about named individuals.

What can be established from the text itself is the shape of the condition: attendance without comprehension, comprehension without submission, and submission replaced by a slogan. The verse describes a class of listeners rather than a single occasion, and it ends by naming what they carry away from the recitation instead of what they heard in it.
