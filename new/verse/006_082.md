## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:82

> It is ˹only˺ those who are faithful and do not tarnish their faith with falsehood who are guaranteed security and are ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Question of Security**

The previous verse asked which party had more right to security and told the audience to answer if they knew. This verse supplies the answer as a statement of fact: those who believe and do not tarnish their faith with falsehood are the ones guaranteed security and guided. The sentence names the two conditions and then attaches the two results, and the structure matches the pairing the sūrah used earlier when it described the believers and the deniers (6:48–49).

The Book states the same arrangement in other words when it says that whoever submits themselves to God and does good will have their reward with their Lord, and that there will be no fear for them nor will they grieve (2:112). The security in this verse is therefore not a general sense of safety but the specific condition of a person who has placed his trust where the power actually lies.

**The Word That Alarmed the Companions**

The condition about tarnishing faith with falsehood produced one of the most famous exchanges in the early community, and the Book's commentators record it in connection with this verse. According to the report in al-Bukhārī, when the verse was revealed the Companions were distressed by it and asked which of them had not mixed his belief with wrongdoing, since the word used here can mean any injustice or sin. The Prophet answered that the verse did not mean that, and directed them to the saying of Luqmān to his son: never associate anything with God in worship, for associating others with Him is truly the worst of all wrongs (31:13).

The report explains the verse's scope and shows how the earliest readers handled it. The wrong that tarnishes faith is the specific wrong of assigning partners to God, since the sūrah's whole argument has been aimed at that practice. The Companions' alarm came from reading a general word in a general sense, and the Prophet's answer narrowed it to the one offence the passage was about.

**Why the Word Is a Comfort as Well as a Warning**

Read with the report in view, the verse is one of the sūrah's clearest statements about what does and does not destroy a person's faith. Ordinary failure, repented of, does not put a believer outside the security the verse describes, and the sūrah gave the same assurance earlier when it told the Prophet to announce that God has taken mercy upon Himself and that whoever does evil in ignorance and then repents and mends his ways will find God forgiving and merciful (6:54).

What the verse will not allow is the opposite of the association it condemns: the assumption that a person may combine the worship of God with the worship of other beings and still hold the guarantee. The whole Abraham section has been devoted to showing that such a combination is not a form of religion but the abandonment of it, and this verse states the consequence in the vocabulary of security.

**Guidance as the Second Result**

The verse ends by saying that those who meet the condition are rightly guided, which adds the inward result to the outward one. The Book treats guidance as both a gift that precedes belief and a state that follows it, and the sūrah earlier described the Prophet as one whom God had guided while his people were arguing with him about God (6:80). The sentence here follows the same pattern: the person who holds to the faith without mixing it is confirmed in the direction he has taken.

The Book's description of the guided elsewhere attaches the same two marks. It says that whoever God guides is truly guided and that whoever He leaves to stray will find no guardian (7:178), and it describes the people of understanding as those who reflect on the creation and the alternation of night and day and are led by that reflection to the acknowledgment of their Lord (3:190–191). Guidance in the Book's usage is not a status conferred on a group but a condition that shows itself in conduct and in the direction a person is facing.

**The Verse as the Centre of the Pericope**

This sentence sits at the middle of the Abraham passage and gives the reader the point the whole passage has been arguing. The founder of the community's tradition asked which side had more right to security, and the answer names the two things Abraham did: he believed, and he purified his belief of everything his people mixed with it.

The verse that follows names Abraham's discourse as the argument God gave him against his people, and it states that God elevates in rank whoever He pleases (6:83). The two sentences belong together. The security promised here is what the argument in the previous verses was seeking, and the elevation named in the next verse is God's response to the one who held to it.

**What the Passage Now Leaves Behind**

With this verse the sūrah finishes the dispute with the Makkans about Abraham's legacy, and it does so by making the test consist of belief and purity rather than descent. The audience had claimed Abraham as an ancestor and used his name against the Prophet; the sūrah has taken the claim and turned it into a question about their own practice.

From this point the sūrah turns to the line of prophets who followed Abraham and to the pattern of guidance and reward that runs through their stories. The next verse states what God's treatment of Abraham demonstrates about His own practice, and the verses after it set that story inside a longer chain of revelation.
