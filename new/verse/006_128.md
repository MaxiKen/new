## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:128

> ˹Consider˺ the Day He will gather them ˹all˺ together and say, “O assembly of jinn! You misled humans in great numbers.” And their human associates will say, “Our Lord! We benefited from each other’s company, but now we have reached the term which You appointed for us.” ˹Then˺ He will say, “The Fire is your home, yours to stay in forever, except whoever Allah wills to spare.” Surely your Lord is All-Wise, AllKnowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gathering and the Address**

Consider the Day He will gather them all together and say, O assembly of jinn! You misled humans in great numbers. The verse opens one of the sūrah's accounts of the resurrection, and it begins with an address to the jinn as a body. The Book's word for the gathering of a whole order of beings at once is used elsewhere for the assembly of the communities, and the sūrah of the Cattle records a comparable address a few verses later, when both the jinn and human beings are asked whether messengers did not come to them from among themselves (6:130).

The Book's accounts of the partnerships between the jinn and human beings appear in several places. The sūrah of the Jinn records that some men used to seek refuge with some jinn, so that they increased one another in wickedness (72:6); the sūrah of Saba' records the disavowal of the jinn on the day of judgment, when they declare that it was the people who followed them and not the reverse (34:41); and the sūrah of the Heights describes the entry of the evil groups of jinn and humans into the fire together (7:38). The address in this verse belongs to that family of passages.

**The Admission of the Human Associates**

And their human associates will say, Our Lord! We benefited from each other's company. The reply admits the relationship and describes it as a mutual advantage. The Book's statements elsewhere fill in what the advantage consisted of: the humans received the jinn's services in fortune-telling, protection, and ritual instruction, and the jinn gained followers and influence among the people. The sūrah has already described the association of the jinn with God by the audience (6:100), and the sentence in front of us gives the human side of the arrangement.

The Book treats the partnership as a real one rather than as a metaphor, and it records the jinn's own report of it in the sūrah that bears their name. The admission in this verse is the voice of the people who had made the arrangement, spoken at the moment when its consequences are being drawn.

**The Term Appointed**

But now we have reached the term which You appointed for us. The reply ends with the acknowledgement that the arrangement has run out. The Book's word for the term is the one it uses for the fixed span of every community and person, and the sūrah of the Cattle has already described God's holding of the souls and His revival of them by day within the span He has set (6:60). The sūrah of the Heights and the sūrah of Yūnus state that every community has its term which cannot be advanced or delayed (7:34; 10:49).

The sentence gives the scene its shape. The partners in the worldly arrangement have arrived at the end of it, and the usefulness they found in one another has expired. What the Book presents is a reckoning in which the relationships that organised life in the world are dissolved, and the verse's speakers state the fact of the dissolution as their own admission.

**The Sentence Passed**

Then He will say, The Fire is your home, yours to stay in forever, except whoever Allah wills to spare. The sentence states the outcome of the partnership, and it includes an exception clause. The Book uses the same form in the sūrah of Hūd, where the duration of the punishment is described as lasting while the heavens and the earth endure, except what the Lord wills (11:107), and the exception is understood in the light of the Book's statement that God does what He intends.

The commentators discussed the exception at length, and they connected it to the possibility of God's mercy reaching those whom He chooses. What the verse states is the permanence of the fire for the people described and the absolute freedom of God in the matter, and the attributes that close the sentence support the reading.

**The Names at the End**

Surely your Lord is All-Wise, All-Knowing. The two names explain the judgement that has just been pronounced: the knowledge that saw the arrangement in its reality and the wisdom that made the sentence fit it. The Book attaches this pair to its accounts of the reckoning in many places, and the sūrah has used the vocabulary of God's knowledge throughout its treatment of the hidden and the open.

The names also answer the objection that a person might raise about the length of the punishment. The Book's statements about the fire are always accompanied by its statements about the justice and knowledge of the judge, and the verse in front of us follows that pattern. The God who sentences is the God who knows what each partner did and who has measured the response.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that God makes the wrongdoers allies of one another because of what they have earned (6:129). The sentence follows the scene of the partnership and the judgement, and it generalises the arrangement: the mutual support of the wicked is itself a feature of the order that the Book describes, and it is a consequence of their deeds rather than a description of their strength.

The sūrah's account of the reckoning continues with the address to the communities and the confessions of the inhabitants of the fire, and the passage as a whole serves the argument the chapter has been making since its opening verses. The verse in front of us supplies the first scene of that account and states the end of the partnership the audience had entered with the unseen.
