## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:74

> They have not shown Allah the reverence He deserves. Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idiom of Measuring**

*Mā qadarū Allāha ḥaqqa qadrih* — they did not measure God with the measure due to Him.

The verb *qadara* and the noun *qadr* belong to the vocabulary of measuring, estimating, and assigning a thing its proper worth. The phrase is an idiom for failing to size someone up correctly — for treating a great thing as small.

That is the precise charge, and it is worth distinguishing from the ones around it. The verse does not say that these people denied God, or that they hated Him, or that they were ignorant of Him in the ordinary sense. It says that they got His size wrong.

The idiom appears three times in the Qur'an, and the comparisons are what fix its meaning. In Sūrah al-Anʿām the same sentence is directed at people who refused the possibility of revelation itself: *"And they have not shown Allah His proper reverence when they said, 'Allah has revealed nothing to any human being'"* (6:91). In Sūrah al-Zumar it is attached to the largest image of divine scale in the scripture, the earth held in a grip and the heavens rolled up in a hand (39:67). One case is a denial that God speaks to men, one is a denial that He is as large as He is, and the third is the one in front of us. All three are failures of estimation, and in none of them is the existence of God the point at issue.

**How a Person Measures God**

The failure is diagnosable, and the sūrah has spent its whole length supplying the correct measurement.

God is measured by what He does. He creates from dust, brings the dead field to life, holds the sky from falling, sends the rain, raises those in the graves, and will judge between every community on a day that belongs to Him alone. A person who has taken all of that in and assigns partners to God has not disbelieved in His existence; he has underestimated His claim.

The commentators put it two ways that amount to the same thing: al-Rāzī took the failure as a failure to give God the honour due to Him, since they set up stones beside Him; al-Ṭabarī took it as a failure to know Him as He ought to be known. Both are failures of estimation rather than of existence.

The two are not in tension, and holding them together is what makes the charge usable. Honour is what is owed; knowledge is what produces it. A person who knows what the sūrah has spent seventy-three verses describing and still sets up partners beside Him has not missed an argument. He has heard it and assigned it the wrong weight.

**Why the Charge Follows the Parable**

The placement is exact. The parable of the fly has just established what the idols are: things that cannot create an insect and cannot retrieve from it.

Verse 74 draws the inference that the parable left implicit. A person who prays to something weaker than a fly has a correspondingly small estimate of the One that thing is being set beside. The two verses are one argument, and the second is the conclusion.

This is why the verse's closing names matter so much: God is *qawiyy*, strong, and *ʿazīz*, mighty. Both are stated immediately after a demonstration of how weak the alternative is, and the contrast is the sentence.

The inference is a measurement in the literal sense. An idol that cannot recover what a fly takes from it is not a lesser god; it is a thing without the capacity the worshipper is attributing to it. Setting that beside the Creator is not a comparison of two powers. It is a misjudgement of the distance between them, and the verse's word for the misjudgement is the idiom of measuring.

**The Parallel in Sūrah al-Zumar**

The same charge appears in one of the most famous passages of the Qur'an, in a description of the Resurrection: the whole earth will be in His grip on that Day and the heavens folded in His right hand, and the verse then says that they did not measure God with the measure due to Him.

The parallel confirms what the idiom is for. Measuring God is not a matter of correct doctrine in the abstract; it is a matter of matching one's estimate of Him to what He will be seen to be when the estimate can no longer be revised.

The wording of that verse makes the connection unmissable: *"They have not shown Allah His proper reverence—when on the Day of Judgment the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand"* (39:67). The adverb *when* is doing the work. The failure of estimation is located at the moment of the largest possible disclosure, which is the sūrah's point exactly: the estimate is wrong now because it will be shown to be wrong then.

**The Charge Is Not Only for Idolaters**

The wording is general, and there is no reason to confine it.

A person can avoid idols entirely and still hold an estimate of God that is too small — a God who is primarily a resource, or a God whose commands are negotiable, or a God whose warnings are understood as rhetoric. Every one of those is a failure of measurement, and the sūrah has described people in exactly those terms: the man on the brink who assumed the religion was a transaction (22:11), and the doubters who concluded from a delay that nothing was coming (22:47).

That is why the verse sits where it does, in the sūrah's closing movement, addressed to an audience that already accepts the argument. The idols are gone from the room by now; the underestimation may not be.

The remedy the verse supplies is not an exhortation to try harder but a pair of names. The charge is that they did not measure God; the correction is to be told what He is — strong, and beyond reach. Estimation is repaired by knowledge of the one being estimated, which is why the sūrah has spent its length giving that knowledge rather than pleading for reverence.

**Estimation Is Tested by Behaviour**

The sūrah has given a testable account of what a correct estimate of God produces, and it is worth assembling, because the verse's charge is otherwise abstract.

A person who measures God correctly prays to Him alone, since setting up partners is the definitional act of underestimation. He is patient under what happens to him, since impatience is the assumption that the arrangement could have been better managed. He does not demand that the punishment be hurried, since hurrying it is the assumption that God's schedule needs improving. And he does not treat his own life as his own, since a correct estimate of the Creator includes a correct estimate of the creature.

Every one of those failures has appeared in this sūrah, and each was presented as a failure of measurement rather than of information. The man on the brink knew that God existed; he simply held a small estimate of what that meant.

**The Verse's Grammar Is an Understatement**

The Arabic opens with *mā qadarū Allāha* — they did not measure God. The negation is of the verb rather than of the object: the failure is in the act of measuring, not in the existence of what was to be measured.

That distinction is worth holding, because it explains why the sūrah does not treat idolatry as simple ignorance. These are not people who have never heard of God. They are people who have heard, and who have assigned the hearing the wrong weight — a failure the Qur'an describes elsewhere with the same vocabulary of covering over.

The understatement is itself rhetorical. The verse could have said they insulted God, or denied Him, or set up rivals to Him. It says instead that they got the measurement wrong, which is the more damning formulation, because an insult at least acknowledges the size of what is being insulted.

**What the Two Closing Names Do**

*Inna Allāha la-qawiyyun ʿazīz* — God is Strong, Mighty.

*Qawiyy* is strength: the capacity to do what is intended without obstruction. *ʿAzīz* is might in the sense of being beyond reach — the one who cannot be coerced, opposed or worn down.

Together they state the correct measurement in two words. The failure described in the first half of the verse was a failure to hold both, and the remedy the verse offers is not an argument but a pair of names.
