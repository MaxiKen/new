## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:14

> ˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the believers to forgive those who do not fear Allah’s days ˹of torment˺, so that He will reward each group for what they used to commit.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Forgive People Who Have Not Apologised**

The instruction is strange at first hearing. The believers are told to forgive people who have shown no regret, sought no pardon, and in several cases were actively working to harm them. Nothing in the verse asks the offenders to change first. If anything, the description attached to them is unflattering — those who do not fear the days of God — so the command is not based on the worthiness of its object. This is the Qur'an's characteristic way of handling the desire for revenge. It does not deny the injury, does not call the injury small, and does not pretend the offenders are decent. It moves the whole matter to a higher jurisdiction, where the case will be heard by someone who saw every detail, and it tells the injured party to leave the file on that desk. Forgiving, in this verse, is not a judgment about the offender's character. It is an act of trust in a court that has not yet sat.

**What the Days of God Are**

The phrase translated "Allah's days of torment" has a longer history than the verse's occasion. The days of God are the days in which he acted in the world: the drowning of Pharaoh's army, the rescue of Noah, the destruction of nations that mocked their messengers, and the deliverances granted to the weak. A person who does not fear those days has concluded, in effect, that history contains no verdicts — that the powerful have always won and that no power above them intervenes. The Qur'an treats that conclusion as a form of blindness with consequences, and it also treats compassion as compatible with it. The believers are not asked to believe that nothing happened to those who oppressed them. They are asked to live as people whose lawsuit has been filed, whose case is strong, and who do not need to enforce the verdict themselves.

**The Incident Reported Behind the Command**

Reports tie the verse to an expedition against the tribe of Banū al-Muṣṭaliq and to a well where the Muslims had stopped. A servant of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, the leader of the hypocrites of Madinah, is said to have returned without water because the servant of ʿUmar had taken the well for the Prophet's household, and ibn Ubayy is said to have responded with a bitter proverb comparing the newcomers to a dog that is fed until it turns on its feeder. The report continues that ʿUmar heard it, took up his sword, and was on his way to kill the man when the verse arrived. Exegetes note that the verse may already have been revealed earlier and been recited on this occasion to calm him. Whether the words were new or repeated, the effect asked for is the same: a community with real grievances is told to hold its hand.

**The Other Report: A Loan to God and a Saying in the Market**

A second account attaches the verse to an insult of a different kind. When the words of the Qur'an that ask who will lend God a good loan were recited (57:11), a Jewish resident of Madinah named Finhas is said to have remarked that Muhammad's Lord must be poor, since he borrows. ʿUmar is said to have set out after him with his sword, and the report continues that the angel Gabriel came to the Prophet with the instruction that the believers forgive those who do not fear the days of God. Both accounts are reported rather than established, and both show the same situation: a hot word, a drawn sword, and a revelation telling the injured community to stand down. Whatever the exact occasion, the command is where the sūrah places it, in the middle of a chapter about signs and their rejection.

**Why Restraint Was the Stronger Option in Madinah**

The believers in Madinah were a minority with powerful enemies and a treaty to manage, and the verses of this period repeatedly restrain them for reasons that are not merely moral. Killing a leader of the hypocrites would have plunged the city into a war the Muslims could not afford, and it would have handed their opponents an argument. The Qur'an's instruction therefore has a prudential edge as well as a generous one, though it never presents restraint as cunning. Other passages set out the standard in plain terms, asking the believers to respond to evil with what is best (41:34), to pardon and forgive as they hope to be forgiven (24:22), and to forgive the relatives and spouses who lagged behind (64:14). None of these verses promises that the offender will change. They promise that the one who forgives loses nothing in the end.

**The Clause That Explains Why Forgiving Is Safe**

The verse ends with the reason, and the reason is not a lesson in ethics: so that he will reward each group for what it used to commit. Justice is being deferred, not cancelled. The offender's account is still open, and the injured party's restraint is being recorded as something in his own favour. That structure explains why the Qur'an can ask so much of people who have been wronged. It is not telling them to pretend the injury did not matter; it is telling them that a book is being kept in which nothing is lost. Both parties will be paid in full, in the same court, according to what they actually did, and the believers are being asked to wait for a payment that God has already guaranteed. The command is heavy, and the guarantee behind it is the reason it can be carried.
