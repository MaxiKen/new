## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:200

> O believers! Patiently endure, persevere, stand on guard, and be mindful of Allah, so you may be successful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Closing Address**

The sūrah ends with an address to the believers, calling them to patience, to constancy in patience, to perseverance, and to reverence of God. The four injunctions are stated in succession, and the verse closes with the promise that those who fulfil them may prosper.

The address gathers the sūrah's main themes into its final sentence. The community has been told about the trials it would face, the losses it had suffered, and the taunts it would hear; the last verse tells it what to do in the conditions the sūrah has described. The four injunctions are placed at the very end of the sūrah, so that the community carries them away as the summary of the whole chapter.

**Patience and Contest in Patience**

The first two injunctions come from the same root, one naming patience itself and the second naming a contest in patience in which a person should outlast the one he faces. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī explained the second form as outdoing the enemy in endurance and firmness.

The pairing turns patience from a private disposition into a shared contest. The Book's earlier instruction about the believers and their opponents used the same language of competition when it promised victory to the steadfast, and the verse before the reader applies the same standard to the whole community. The qualities named describe a person who neither surrenders to hardship nor lets it make him harsh.

**The Word for Perseverance**

The third injunction renders a word whose root carries the senses of tying, fortifying, and joining. The verb is used for holding a frontier post in defence of the community, and it is connected with the tethering of horses, as in the verse of the sūrah of the Spoils that speaks of horses kept ready for battle.

The word's range explains why the commentators treated this as the hardest of the four injunctions. What it names is a steady presence at the place where the community is exposed, and the term belongs to the language of garrisons and watch-posts rather than to that of private virtue. The commentators connected it with the frontier posts of the early community and with the discipline of a person who keeps his station.

**The Reports About the Watch**

The tradition preserved reports about guarding the frontier. One states that a single day spent in that service is better than the world and everything in it, a report found in Bukhārī and Muslim. Other reports mention that the merit of such service continues after a person's death, since the defence of the community allows others to carry on their religious duties.

The reports gave the verse a practical meaning for the community's defence, and the jurists discussed the conditions under which the duty applies. The sentence before the reader was used as one of the foundations of the discussion, and the reports supplied its content.

**The Spiritual Reading of the Term**

The tradition also preserved a spiritual reading of the same word. In one report the Prophet described performing ablution under difficult conditions, walking repeatedly to the mosque, and waiting for one prayer after another as the reality of the watch, and the waiting was explained as remaining in remembrance and devotion between two of the prescribed prayers.

Al-Nawawī, the jurist and commentator, compared this spiritual watch with the martial one in his explanation of the report. The two readings were held together, and the verse was applied to both the defence of the community's territory and the discipline of the person's own soul. Together they gave the community a way of treating the verse as a programme for ordinary life rather than only for the field of battle.


The commentators mention a well-known report in which the Prophet told the companions returning from a battle that they had come back from the lesser struggle to the greater one, and identified the greater struggle as the servant's struggle against his own passions. The report is often cited in works of spirituality in connection with this verse.

The distinction it draws is the one the sūrah's closing injunctions require. The community was engaged in the visible struggle that the sūrah has been describing, and the verse reminds it that the same qualities of patience and perseverance are needed in the struggle it conducts within itself.

**The Reverence That Closes the Verse**

The last injunction returns to the quality that the sūrah named at its opening, where the Book described itself as a guidance for those who are mindful of God. The same quality was the condition attached to the community's patience in the verse about the trials it would meet.

The sūrah therefore closes where it began, and the four injunctions are gathered under the last of them. Reverence of God is presented as the disposition that makes the other three possible, and the community is told what it must maintain if the rest is to hold.

**What the Sūrah Leaves Its Readers With**

The verse promises prosperity to those who fulfil the injunctions, and the promise is stated with the word the sūrah used for success in its earlier passages. The prosperity named is the outcome of the whole sūrah's teaching, and the community is left with it at the end of the chapter.

The address is the last word of the sūrah, and it turns to the believers after the long account of the other groups. What the sūrah has given them is a history, a set of warnings, and a description of the reward and the punishment; what it asks of them at the end is patience, constancy, perseverance, and the reverence of God.
