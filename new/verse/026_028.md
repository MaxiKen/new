## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:28

> Moses responded: “˹He is˺ the Lord of the east and west, and everything in between, if only you had any sense.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses Answers Mockery With a Wider Horizon**

Pharaoh has tried to make the claim about Moses' mind. Moses does not defend himself with an emotional protest. He continues describing the Lord. The east and west name the two horizons through which the sun's course is observed, and everything between them completes the scope. The ruler's court is one point within this ordered world, not its center.

This answer has a different rhythm from the previous description of heavens and earth. It moves across the daily experience of sunrise, sunset, direction, and changing light. Pharaoh can see his kingdom as a stable possession, but the horizons announce a world in motion under a power that no palace controls. The Lord's sovereignty is displayed in the order that frames every human activity.

**East and West Mark the Reach of Rule**

The pair east and west is a way of speaking about the whole earth. It does not reduce God to a direction or a physical location. It places every direction under His lordship. Pharaoh may claim a land, but he does not govern the rising and setting that define the world's day. The created order refuses to be enclosed by a royal boundary.

The phrase everything in between prevents the audience from imagining that divine authority concerns only distant extremes. The space between horizons includes homes, farms, roads, courts, and bodies. It includes the Israelites' labor and Pharaoh's throne. The Lord is not merely Lord at the edges of the world. His care and judgment reach the ordinary middle where power is exercised and injustice is hidden.

**The Repetition Is a Deliberate Expansion**

Moses has already named the heavens and earth and what lies between them. Now he gives another pair that opens the same truth through a different image. The movement is not a failure to answer. It is a method of removing every narrow frame Pharaoh might use. If the ruler refuses the cosmic description, he must still face the ordered horizons that structure his own daily life.

The repeated descriptions also teach how theological knowledge grows. One image may show God's total creation, another His command over direction and time, and another His relation to human generations. None exhausts the Lord, but each blocks a false limitation. Moses is not trying to provide a finite portrait. He is giving signs by which a finite mind can recognize dependence.

**Reason Must Notice What Power Takes for Granted**

Moses ends with if only you had any sense. The Arabic appeal is related to understanding and sound reason. It is not an insult about intelligence in the modern psychological sense. It asks Pharaoh to use the faculty that should connect visible order with its Lord. The ruler has knowledge of administration and force, but knowledge becomes foolishness when it refuses the conclusion implied by the world it manages.

This is a moral account of reason. Reason is not just the ability to calculate means. It includes the willingness to follow evidence beyond self-interest. A ruler may plan campaigns, organize cities, and command officials while failing to reason about his own dependence. Moses calls that failure a lack of sense because intelligence used to protect arrogance has become a tool of blindness.

**The Horizons Defeat the Myth of a Closed Kingdom**

Pharaoh's court can imagine Egypt as a self-contained order whose laws and gods are defined by the throne. The east and west break that enclosure. The kingdom receives light from beyond itself and sits within a world it did not create. Its borders do not mark the limit of reality or the limit of accountability.

This point is especially important for oppressed people. A tyrant's system can make captivity feel total because every visible institution repeats the same claim. Moses points beyond the system without denying its immediate power. The horizons promise that Pharaoh's order is a portion, not the whole. The Lord who governs the wider world can open a way that the court cannot permanently close.

**Created Order Is a Sign, Not a Replacement for Revelation**

Moses' reference to east and west does not mean that observation alone tells a person every detail of worship or law. It establishes a foundation: the world is ordered, sustained, and not self-owned. Revelation then gives the command that Pharaoh must hear. Creation points toward God's authority; the messenger states what that authority requires in the present case.

Keeping these roles distinct prevents two errors. One error treats nature as religiously empty, so injustice can be judged only by human power. The other claims that observation by itself supplies every revealed instruction. Moses joins the two correctly. The horizons support the claim that God is Lord, while the revealed mission applies that claim to Pharaoh's treatment of Israel.

**The Answer Refuses to Let Ridicule Set the Terms**

Pharaoh mocked the messenger, but Moses lets the order of creation set the terms again. He will not spend the exchange defending his dignity before a ruler who has already chosen contempt. He returns to the evidence and asks for sense. That is a form of restraint. It keeps the conversation about lordship even when the opponent wants to make it about insult.

The verse thus gives the confrontation a stable center. The east and west continue under God's rule whether Pharaoh laughs or not. A court's reaction cannot change the horizons. Moses' duty is to name what remains true and invite the ruler to understand it before his power is judged by it.

**The Two Horizons Also Expose Human Time**

East and west mark the daily passage that no ruler can suspend. Pharaoh may present his order as permanent, but each sunrise and sunset places his reign inside time. The Lord's command over the horizons reminds the court that power is temporary even when its monuments appear fixed. A ruler who understands this will treat authority as a trust; a ruler who denies it will try to make a temporary office into a god.
