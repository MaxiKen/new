## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:17

> It is certainly upon Us to ˹make you˺ memorize and recite it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Burden Is Transferred**

Having prohibited haste, the verse immediately supplies the reason why haste is unnecessary. The two tasks the Prophet was straining at — fixing the words in memory and producing them correctly — are taken over by the Speaker. *Inna ʿalaynā* is emphatic: upon Us, and no one else. The guarantee is not phrased as help offered to someone who is doing his best; it is phrased as a transfer of responsibility. The Prophet's anxiety about losing the revelation is answered by reassignment rather than reassurance, which is the stronger response. A promise of assistance leaves the person still carrying the thing; a declaration that it falls upon God removes it from him altogether.

**Gathering It in the Chest**

The first of the two tasks is *jamʿuhu*, its gathering, and the early authorities were specific about what was meant. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as God's promise to gather the Qur'an in the Prophet's breast (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4928), and the same collection narrates him saying that the Messenger used to bear the revelation with difficulty and move his lips with it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5). The image is of scattered pieces being brought into one place inside a man, and it suits a revelation that arrived in fragments over twenty-three years — a few verses at a time, in response to events, questions, and circumstances no one could have scheduled. Two features of that gathering deserve attention. First, it occurred inside a man, not on a page: the primary vessel of the Qur'an in the first generation was the chest, and the writing down of the text ran alongside a memorisation that was never treated as secondary. Second, it was completed across the whole period of revelation, which means the promise covers the intervals as well as the moments of descent. A revelation that arrived in fragments required a gathering that ran continuously, and the verse promises that nothing was lost in the gaps between the fragments.

**Qurʾān: a Word That Is Also an Act**

The second term, *qurʾānahu*, is a verbal noun used here in its active sense: its reciting, the act of bringing it out. The word *qurʾān* is built on the root meaning to gather and to read aloud, and both senses are present in this one short verse — the gathering inside and the reciting outside. The grammatical point carries theological weight. The text is not called a book that must then be performed; it is called a recitation, an event of the mouth and the ear, which is why the Qur'an's own self-description insists on its oral character: *“Indeed, you ˹O Prophet˺ are receiving the Quran from the One ˹Who is˺ All-Wise, All-Knowing.”* (27:6). A scripture defined as recitation requires a guarantee that the recitation will be right, and that is exactly what this verse gives.

**The Promise of Preservation**

The assurance given here is stated elsewhere as a general promise covering the whole text: *“We will have you recite ˹the Quran, O Prophet,˺ so you will not forget ˹any of it˺, unless Allah wills otherwise.”* (87:6). The exception clause is important, since it prevents the promise from being read as mechanical; the guarantee is personal and continuing, not a once-for-all deposit. The same protection is claimed for the scripture itself: *“It is certainly We Who have revealed the Reminder, and it is certainly We Who will preserve it.”* (15:9). Taken together, the two verses describe a single arrangement in which the preservation of the text and the preservation of the reciter are the same divine act, and neither is left to the resources of the man who received it. The two promises also answer the two forms of loss that a revealed text can suffer. It can be lost through forgetting, which the gathering in the chest addresses, and it can be lost through alteration, which the recitation addresses, since a text that is constantly recited in public by many memorisers cannot be quietly revised by anyone. The Qur'an's own account of its preservation takes in both risks.

**What the Verse Removes**

Anxiety about the revelation did not end with the Prophet, and the verse speaks to everyone who has ever worried about retaining what he has learned. Read against its occasion, it establishes a principle about where the outcome of religious effort rests. The Prophet's diligence was not rebuked — he was hurrying to secure something precious, and diligence is not the thing being corrected. What was corrected was the assumption that the security of the text depended on his grip. The same relocation applies to the student who fears forgetfulness and the teacher who fears distortion. Effort belongs to the servant; the result is placed elsewhere, and the Qur'an is explicit about which party carries it.

**How the Community Understood It**

Muslim practice from the first generation onward has treated this verse as the basis of an institution rather than only as a comfort. If the collection of the Qur'an rests with God, then its preservation among human beings is maintained through human effort rather than in competition with it, and the community organised itself accordingly: recitation from memory, transmission by chain, and the writing down of what had been recited and checked. The Qur'an describes the text as held in honoured, purified pages in the hands of angel-scribes (80:13–16), and as *“a well-preserved Record”* (56:78). Those descriptions are not poetry about an ideal scripture. They are the theological account of why the copy in a child's hands and the copy in a museum in Samarkand are recognisably the same book. The practical consequence is that the community has never treated memorisation as a specialism. The science of variant readings, the chains of transmission, and the certificate a student receives to teach what he heard are all elaborations of one conviction: the gathering that this verse places with God is real, and human effort cooperates with it rather than replacing it.
