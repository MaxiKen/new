## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155

> Ṣâliḥ said, “Here is a camel. She will have her turn to drink as you have yours, each on an appointed day.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sign Arrives as a Living Trust**

Ṣāliḥ answers Thamûd's demand for proof by identifying a she-camel as a sign from God. The sign is not merely an object to admire. It is a living creature placed within the people's daily economy and given a protected share of water. The miracle therefore enters the ordinary world where the community's habits and interests operate.

Quran 7:73 also calls the she-camel a clear proof from the Lord and instructs Thamûd to leave her to graze. The parallel makes the source of the sign clear. Ṣāliḥ presents it, but he does not claim to have created or controlled it. The camel points beyond the messenger to the Lord whose authority is being tested.


The verse assigns a turn of drinking to the camel and a turn to the people. The arrangement is not an invitation to chaos or an abolition of human need. It establishes a boundary within shared provision. A creature that has become a divine sign must not be treated as an ordinary possession to be pushed aside whenever the powerful want more.

The schedule also makes justice visible. Water is limited, and limited resources expose whether a community can accept restraint. Thamûd is asked to live with an allocation that acknowledges another creature's claim. The test is not only belief in a miracle; it is willingness to let belief change behavior.


The source discussion preserves two readings of the appointed arrangement. It may mean that the camel has a designated day and the people have another, or that each receives an assigned share of the water. The exact scheduling detail is less important than the moral structure: access is regulated, and neither side may erase the other's right.

This interpretive range does not weaken the command. Both readings make the resource a trust rather than a free-for-all. Thamûd must accept a limit that its appetite did not design. The divine sign turns water use into an act of obedience and exposes the community's capacity for fairness.

**The Camel Tests the Community's Treatment of the Weak**

A camel cannot negotiate with the people or defend its own claim through political speech. Its protected share therefore tests how Thamûd treats a dependent creature. A community's morality is revealed by what it does when it has complete power over another being.

The sign also reaches human dependents indirectly. If the people cannot honor a divinely assigned share for an animal, their claim to protect the powerless is suspect. Justice begins with restraint toward those who cannot repay the favor or threaten the strong in return.


The camel is extraordinary as a sign, but the required response is practical. Thamûd must organize water, respect a schedule, and resist the urge to take more than its allowance. The miracle does not make obedience mystical or vague. It gives a concrete command through which faith can be seen.

That concreteness is a mercy. A person can claim to believe in God while keeping belief separated from daily decisions. The assigned drink makes separation impossible. Every encounter with the water asks whether the people accept the Lord's authority over resources they want to control.


Thamûd may believe that its land and wells belong entirely to it. The camel's allocation challenges absolute ownership. The people can use provision, but they cannot treat God's earth as a place where the strong define every right. The Creator can assign a share that human preference would deny.

This does not erase careful management. It teaches that management is subordinate to justice. A community may plan distribution, maintain sources, and prevent waste, but it cannot call exploitation legitimate simply because it has the power to enforce it. The sign places a divine limit inside the people's system of possession.

**The Sign Is Also a Test of Ṣāliḥ's People**

A miracle can divide a community because it turns an abstract claim into a decision that cannot be postponed. Thamûd must either respect the sign or expose its rebellion. The camel's presence removes the comfort of discussing religion only in theory.

Ṣāliḥ does not ask the people for a payment or a private oath. He asks them to honor a public arrangement. Their response will show whether the demand for a sign was an honest search for truth or a challenge made in the hope that no evidence would ever be enough.

**Divine Signs Deserve Careful Treatment**

The camel is called a sign, which means that mistreating her is not a minor act detached from belief. The people are not free to admire the miracle in speech while violating the command attached to it. A sign creates a relationship of reverence and restraint.

For the reader, the verse links faith to how resources and creatures are treated. Recognize God's signs not only by wonder but by obedience. When a limit is placed around a blessing, accept the limit as part of the sign. The appointed share is a small boundary carrying a large lesson about who owns the earth.

It also teaches that a sign can reorganize ordinary priorities without abolishing them. Thamûd still needs water, workers still need order, and the community still needs a fair method of distribution. Divine instruction enters those practical needs and gives them a moral shape. The people are not asked to stop living; they are asked to live under a limit they did not invent.

That limit is a mercy because it restrains competition before competition becomes violence. A people that accepts an assigned share learns that scarcity does not justify taking everything. It can plan, wait, and share because trust in God is stronger than the fear that another creature's portion will leave it with nothing.
