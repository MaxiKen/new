## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:53

> ˹They prayed to Allah,˺ “Our Lord! We believe in Your revelations and follow the messenger, so count us among those who bear witness.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer Built From the Preceding Verse**

The disciples have affirmed their belief and their support to the messenger; they now say the same things to God. The verse is their supplication, and its three clauses repeat, in the language of worship, the three clauses they had spoken to a human being.

That movement from a public commitment to a private prayer is the Book's ordinary pattern. A person declares his position among people, and then presents it before God, where the declaration is either confirmed or exposed. The disciples do both in the space of two verses, and the sūrah supplies no gap between the pledge and the prayer in which the pledge could be forgotten.

**"We Believe in Your Revelations"**

The first clause is belief, and the object is specific: what God has sent down. The Book uses the plural to cover the whole body of revelation given to the prophets, and the disciples' declaration places the earlier scriptures and the message of Jesus in one object of faith.

The wording avoids a narrowness that a reader might expect. These are men who belong to the community of Israel and who accept a prophet raised among them; their confession is not "we believe in our prophet" but "we believe in Your revelations". The distinction matters in a sūrah that repeatedly criticises people who accepted part of what they were given. The disciples' statement covers the whole; there is no portion of revelation they set aside.

**"And Follow the Messenger"**

The second clause names an action rather than a feeling: they follow the messenger. Belief in revelation has been converted into a relationship with the person who brings it, and the Book treats the two as inseparable, which is why it commands obedience to the messenger alongside belief in God.

Following in this vocabulary is a continuous practice. It describes a person walking a road behind a guide, and the Book applies it to the community that accepted the Prophet of this sūrah in expressions that make the model explicit. A disciple is not someone who has approved a message from a distance; he has placed himself behind the one delivering it, and his own conduct becomes the trace of that placement. The disciples' prayer, in other words, is not a statement of opinion about God. It is a description of a journey already begun.

**"Count Us Among Those Who Bear Witness"**

The request is unusual in its modesty and its precision. The disciples do not ask for reward, protection, or victory; they ask to be registered in a category. The category is *the witnesses*, and the Book uses it as one of its most honourable designations, applied to the community of the Prophet as a whole: you have been made an upright community so that you may be witnesses over humanity, and the Messenger a witness over you (2:143).

Bearing witness in this vocabulary carries two senses at once, and the sūrah's usage requires both. A witness is a person who knows something and a person who says what he knows, and the Book's witnesses are those whose lives attest what they have understood. The disciples ask to be counted — the verb is that of a clerk entering a name in a register — which means they are asking for something no effort can produce on its own. A person can perform acts; he cannot inscribe himself among the witnesses of God.

**The Order of the Three Requests**

The three clauses form a progression from inner conviction to public role. Belief is held in the heart; following the messenger is visible in conduct; being counted among the witnesses is a status that belongs to God's own record.

The sūrah has used this sequence before, and it will use it again. Its logic is that each stage depends on the one before it: a witness who does not follow would be testifying to a road he has not walked, and a follower who does not believe would be performing a compliance he does not hold. The disciples' prayer is therefore a description of the order in which a religious life is built, and it is offered in the form of a request, which is where the Book usually leaves the matter.

**Who the Witnesses Are in the Wider Book**

The theme is one of the sūrah's deepest concerns. At the opening of the passage about the witness-bearing community, God is called the foremost witness that there is no god but Him, with the angels and the people of knowledge testifying after Him (3:18). Later in the sūrah, those who refuse the invitation are invited to bear witness to the submission of the believers (3:64). The disciples' prayer in this verse slots into that structure: God witnesses to Himself, the learned witness to what they know, and a community of ordinary believers is asked to carry the same testimony before humanity.

The hierarchy is worth keeping in view, because it explains why being counted among the witnesses is such a serious request. A witness whose testimony is false does not merely lie about a fact. He contradicts the testimony of God, the angels, and those who know, which is why the Book treats a deliberate misstatement about God as the gravest of offences.

**A Community Prayer for Measured Requests**

The disciples' supplication is short, and its brevity is instructive for anyone who uses it as a model. There is no request for safety in the danger the previous verse described, no request for the punishment of the hostile, and no request for the satisfaction of seeing the doubters proved wrong.

What the men ask for is the least a servant can ask and the most a person can hope for: to be remembered as a witness. In the setting the verse describes — a prophet looking for support among people who mostly refuse him — the prayer is a striking choice. What the disciples want from God is a place in the record, and the sūrah presents their request without comment, as if it were the plainest thing a person could want.
