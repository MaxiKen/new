## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:120

> When you ˹believers˺ are touched with good, they grieve; but when you are afflicted with evil, they rejoice. ˹Yet,˺ if you are patient and mindful ˹of Allah˺, their schemes will not harm you in the least. Surely Allah is Fully Aware of what they do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Portrait of a Settled Enmity**

The verse reports that when good comes to the believers their opponents grieve, and when harm comes to them they rejoice, and it then gives the community the condition under which such reactions will not harm it. The pairing of reactions is the summary of everything the passage has been describing.

Ibn Kathīr drew attention to what the pair reveals. Someone may oppose a community out of interest or disagreement, and still regret the community's hardship; the people described here do the opposite, and the fact that another's good news produces grief is a measure of how deep the hostility runs. The verse does not present them as rivals but as people whose own condition is affected by the fate of a community they do not belong to.

**Moses' Plea in the Same Key**

The Book records the same sentiment in the words of Moses, who pleaded with God not to let his enemies rejoice over his misfortune and not to count him among the wrongdoers (7:150). The scene is one of distress, after the people had taken a calf in his absence.

The parallel is instructive for the way the Book treats such feelings. Being the object of an enemy's satisfaction is a real source of pain, and the Book records it without reproach; what the verse before the reader adds is the practical instruction that goes beyond the feeling. A community cannot prevent an opponent's reaction, and it is told what to do with it.

**The Condition: Patience and Mindfulness**

The verse names two things that neutralise the schemes: patient endurance and mindfulness of God. The two words recur in the Book's instructions to the believers under pressure, and the sūrah has already used mindfulness in its command to give God His due.

Patience in the Book's usage is not passive resignation. It describes holding to a course under conditions that make holding difficult, and the same word is used for fasting, for standing firm in battle, and for enduring harm without retaliation. The verse's promise is attached to that kind of resolve combined with the awareness of God that keeps a person's conduct within the limits the Book has set.

**What It Means That the Schemes Will Not Harm Them**

The phrase that the schemes will not harm the believers at all is the same claim the passage made earlier, where it stated that such people could inflict nothing more than annoyance. The two statements stand at the two ends of the same argument.

The opposition's plans are real, and the sūrah has described their content: turning the community from its faith, stirring up old quarrels, and working to make the path appear crooked. What the verse denies is the ability of those plans to achieve their purpose, and the denial is conditional on the believers' own conduct. The limit on the harm is in the hands of the community it threatens.

**The Context in Which the Community Heard It**

The verses that follow this one describe the battle of Uḥud, and the passage before the reader was recited in a community that had already fought one battle and was about to face another. The reactions described in the verse are those of the community's opponents in Madinah and Makkah.

That setting explains the practical tone of the sentence. A community under pressure needs something it can do, and the verse gives it patience and mindfulness rather than information about the intentions of its enemies. The Book's guidance in such passages is consistently about the conduct of the believer and consistently silent about the details of the plot.

**God's Awareness of What They Do**

The verse closes with the statement that God is fully aware of what such people do, and the formula appears at the end of several verses in this passage. It is the Book's answer to the gap between what a community can see and what is being done against it.

The phrase does not add a new instruction; it changes the frame in which the instruction is carried out. The believers are told to be patient, and they are told that the actions of their opponents are not unobserved. The Book's habit of ending its warnings about opposition with this statement keeps the community from the two errors the passage has been guarding against: underestimating the hostility and treating it as a matter over which nobody has authority.

**The Counsel the Verse Leaves**

The verse gives the community two things to do and one thing to know. Patience and mindfulness are the actions, and the awareness of God is the knowledge that makes the actions possible.

That structure is repeated throughout the Book's instructions to communities facing difficulty, and it is the reason this verse can stand at the end of a passage full of warnings without leaving the reader in fear. The danger has been described in detail, the people who pose it have been identified, and the response has been reduced to two words that any believer can put into practice.

**The Movement Into the Next Section**

The passage that began with an address to the believers about going astray closes with an instruction about endurance, and the sūrah then turns to the battle of Uḥud and the lessons of that day.

The change of subject is not a change of theme. The verses that follow test the counsel given here, since the community that is told patience and mindfulness will protect it is about to be shown a situation in which it lost its footing. The connection is deliberate, and the reader arrives at the account of the battle with the instruction of this verse still in hand.
