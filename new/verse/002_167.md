## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:167

> The ˹misled˺ followers will cry, “If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us.” And so Allah will make them remorseful of their misdeeds. And they will never ˹be able to˺ leave the Fire.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Wish for Revenge, Not for Return to God**

The followers' cry is the verse's whole psychology, and its content is startling on first hearing. Given the chance to return — *if only we could have a second chance* — they would not say: we would believe, we would follow the messengers, we would pray. They say: *we would disown them as they disowned us*. The wish is for symmetrical betrayal. The followers, having seen the leaders' repudiation, want nothing so much as to repay it — to stand where the leaders stood and cast them off in their turn. The tradition notes the alternative reading's implications and the mainstream takes the wish at face value, and the face value is devastating: even at the door of a second life, the heart formed by misguidance produces the currency of misguidance. The wish exposes what their following really was all along — not conviction but attachment, not belief but belonging — and belonging, once betrayed, wants only revenge. A repentance that begins with the leaders' disowning would be a return toward God; this wish never mentions Him, which is why the verse grants it nothing.

**The Second Chance That Defines the First**

The *karr* — the return, the second turn at the world — is the Hereafter's most requested impossibility, and the Qur'an's treatment of the request is consistent: the return is asked for, is refused, and the asking itself becomes part of the punishment. In the sūrah of the Believers the same plea is staged at the moment of death — send me back, that I may act rightly in what I left behind — and answered as mere words, with the partition of the barzakh standing between the speaker and the world until the Day they are raised. The theological work of these refusals is done on this side of the grave: the wish for a second chance is proof that the first chance was real. If the world were not the turn — the only turn in which deeds are earned — no one in the Fire would ask for it. The followers' cry thus retroactively confirms the sūrah's whole moral architecture: the test announced in the verses of patience (2:155), the doors of repentance open while breath lasts (2:160), the sentence closing at death (2:161). The Fire's population does not dispute the fairness of the arrangement; they dispute its finality, and the dispute is the arrangement's vindication.

**Deeds Displayed as Regret**

The verse's middle clause describes the mechanism of the punishment beyond the Fire's own torment: *thus does Allah show them their deeds as a source of regret* — *ḥasarāt*. Al-Ṭabarī preserves two readings of the display. In the first, the wrongdoers see what they would have possessed in the Hereafter had they acted righteously — the reward's shape rendered visible beside the deprivation, so that the knowledge itself is the torment. In the second, they see the houses of the Garden and long for them, while the Garden's own inhabitants see them and ask God to bless them — two populations looking at each other across the distance the deeds fixed. Both readings agree on the structure: regret here is not a mood but an exhibit. The deeds are *shown* — presented, made visible in their consequences — and the showing is divine action, part of the sentence. This is the Qur'an's answer to the question of how remorse functions after death: in the world, remorse can convert into amendment; here, amendment is closed, so remorse converts into nothing and consumes its subject. The deed that was light enough to commit is now heavy enough to look at forever.

**The Conversations the Fire Cannot End**

The verse belongs to a series in which the Qur'an gives the Fire a social life — one that inverts every comfort of the world's false societies. The followers ask that their leaders' torment be doubled, and are told the doubling has already fallen on all (7:38); the leaders answer that the followers were no better (7:39); Satan disowns everyone, confessing he had no authority, only a call that was answered (14:22); the allies of idolatry curse one another on the Day (29:25); and here the followers wish to disown in return. The pattern is exact: the Fire is populated entirely by failed relationships, and its inhabitants' only remaining activity is the litigation of those failures. Speech continues — accusation, excuse, wish — but no speech there is performative: nothing said in the Fire changes anything in the Fire. The world's conversations at least could convert into repentance, reconciliation, return; the Fire's conversations are the residue of that power, spoken after the power expired. This verse contributes the series' saddest entry: the wish to betray, which is what love-of-the-wrong-object becomes when it can no longer be anything else.

**"And They Will Never Leave the Fire"**

The verse ends with the clause that al-Ṭabarī wielded in the classical argument for the Fire's endlessness: their regret and their desire for another chance will not remove them from the Fire. The reasoning is textual and severe: a wish that persists is a wish unfulfilled, and the verse reports the wish without reporting its grant — so the state continues, wanting and not leaving, forever. Read with its neighbors, the clause is the third hammer in a row: the cursed condition fixed at death (2:161), the abiding without lightening or delay (2:162), and now the impossibility of exit stated in the plainest verb available — *they shall not leave*. The Qur'an's eschatology of permanence is not built on a single proof-text but on this accumulation: duration clauses, refused-wish scenes, and explicit non-exit statements reinforcing one another across sūrahs. And the accumulation is addressed, like all of it, to the living: every statement of the Fire's finality is published while its readers still hold the one thing the Fire's population has lost — a turn in the world, in which the deeds are still being chosen, the ropes still being tied, and the second chance still going by its real name, which is the first.

**The Verse's Place in the Argument**

Within the passage, this verse completes the love-arc begun two verses earlier. The equals loved as God should be loved (2:165) were the leaders and objects of the followers' devotion; the disowning scene (2:166) showed the devotion's leadership side collapsing; this verse shows the followers' side collapsing into the wish for revenge. Misplaced love is thus traced through its whole lifecycle — attachment, exposure, betrayal, and the desire to betray in return — and every stage is worse than the last, until the exit closes. Against this arc the passage has quietly set the only alternative it will name: the believers whose love is more ardent because it is undivided, and the reports of those who love one another for God's sake, whose bond is the one kind the Day does not cut. The verse does not preach the alternative; it finishes the autopsy of its opposite. The reader is left holding the comparison unspoken — bonds that survive the Day and bonds that become the Day's litigation — and the sūrah, characteristically, moves on to law: what to eat, what to avoid, how to live while the turn lasts.
