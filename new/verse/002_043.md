## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:43

> Establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and bow down with those who bow down.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Commands Turn From Heart to Limb**

Verses 40–42 worked on the interior: remember the favor, keep the covenant, believe, do not deny, do not sell, do not mix, do not conceal. Verse 43 puts the body to work, and its three imperatives are the sūrah's first ritual legislation in the Israel address: *establish the prayer, render the zakāh, and bow with those who bow*. The triad will be recognized at once by anyone who has read the sūrah's opening — it is the portrait of verse 3 in the imperative mood: the God-conscious *believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided*. What was there a description of a type is here a command to a people, and the transfer makes the sūrah's claim explicit: the qualities that defined the *muttaqūn* at the head of the Book are not a new religion's invention but the old religion's requirement, restated to its original heirs. The verse asks the People of the Book, in effect, to become what their own charter always described.

**Iqāmah and Ītāʾ: The Two Standing Verbs**

The verb-pair *aqīmū al-ṣalāh wa-ātū al-zakāh* is among the Qur'an's most repeated formulas, joining the two acts in dozens of verses until they function as a single institution with two limbs — the vertical and the horizontal, time given to Allah and wealth given to His dependents. The commentators raise the natural question for this context: are these the Islamic canonical prayer and alms-tax, in which case the verse commands these hearers to enter the new community's practice outright? And they answer from the Qur'an itself that ṣalāh and zakāh are not possessions of one dispensation: Ismāʿīl *"used to command his people to pray and give alms"* (19:55); Mūsā at Ṭuwā is told to establish the prayer (20:14). The institutions predate their Islamic forms, which means the command here operates on two levels at once — keep the prayer your own Torah already binds you to, and keep it in the congregation now calling to prayer. What is demanded is not innovation but fidelity, which is precisely how the Qur'an conducts its argument with this community throughout.

**“With Those Who Bow”: The Clause of Congregation**

The third imperative adds what the first two do not contain: company. *Wa-rkaʿū maʿa al-rākiʿīn* — bow *with* those who bow. The preposition is the command's center, and the exegetes read it socially: do not worship apart from the worshipping community. Prayer performed in isolation is valid prayer, but this clause is not about validity; it is about the row. The same formula appears addressed to Maryam in the temple — *"prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down"* (3:43) — which shows the phrase naming the congregation's worship across dispensations. In the Madinan setting the clause carried an edge: the community of Muḥammad ﷺ was visibly bowing, and the verse invites the People of the Book into the same row rather than a parallel one. Congregational worship is thus legislated here as the seal of sincerity — the scholar who believes and pays but will not be seen bowing *with* this community has told the truth about what he actually withholds.

**Why Bowing Names the Group**

The word chosen for the shared posture is *rukūʿ* — the bow, the standing fold at the waist — rather than *sujūd*, the full prostration, and the choice is expressive. In the canonical prayer the *rukūʿ* is the communal posture par excellence: the row bends together, simultaneously, at the call, heads level — a visible grammar of equality before Allah that the verse deploys as a name for the worshippers themselves, *al-rākiʿūn*. The Qur'an elsewhere uses bowing-and-prostrating as the composite portrait of the believers (9:112), and Sūrah 48 closes its description of the Prophet's ﷺ companions with the same paired image. To be commanded to bow *with the bowers* is therefore to be commanded into a body — one posture, one direction, one time — and the body is the unit the verse saves. A faith kept private is a faith one generation from extinction; a faith that joins the row is transmitted by the row. The clause is the sūrah's shortest ecclesiology: belonging is not the context of worship but part of its command.

**An Invitation Structured as Continuity**

Read as a whole, the verse performs its demand by continuity rather than rupture. Every element it commands is already owed by its addressees under their own covenant — the five-verse formula of 5:12, recalled in verse 40's covenant, lists prayer and zakāh among its terms — and the only genuinely new item is the congregation named in the third clause. The strategy is characteristic of the sūrah's address to Israel: not *abandon your religion* but *complete your obedience where a worshipping community now stands*. The exegetes acknowledge frankly that if the prayer and zakāh here are the Islamic forms, the verse amounts to a command to become Muslims — and that is the invitation, delivered in the softest available terms, as the fulfillment of a promise their own books hold. Conversion by continuity respects the convert's history: nothing true is left behind, and the row already bowing is shown to be the same row their prophets stood in.

**The Triad's Third Filing in the Sūrah**

This triad — faith's acts of prayer, alms, and communal bowing — is legislated here for the second time in forty verses and will be defined a third time in the sūrah's great verse of *birr*: righteousness as belief in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets, then charity from cherished wealth, then *who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make* (2:177). Description (2:3), command (2:43), definition (2:177): the sūrah returns to the same structure until it becomes the reader's own furniture. The progression between the filings is instructive — verse 3 described a people, verse 43 summons another people to the same marks, and verse 177 will universalize the marks into righteousness itself, adding patience and pledge-keeping to the core. Verse 43 stands at the hinge: the moment the opening portrait stops being a description of the blessed and becomes a demand on the recalcitrant — bow, pay, establish, *with them* — and the whole weight of the sūrah's coming legislation is prefigured in three verbs.
