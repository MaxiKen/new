## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:43

> Establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and bow down with those who bow down.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Commands in One Sentence**

The verse gives its audience three instructions in a single line, and it arranges them from the individual to the communal. Prayer is first, the alms-tax second, and the act of bowing in company third. The first concerns a person's standing before God, the second concerns his obligations to the society around him, and the third concerns his visible membership in a body of worshippers.

The Book pairs prayer and almsgiving so regularly that the combination functions as a single idea in its vocabulary. The two together cover the two directions in which religion is expressed — toward God and toward people — and the sūrah's own description of the mindful at its opening named them in the same pair, with the giving described as coming from what God has provided. The command here is given to a community whose covenant included these terms, and the reader of this verse is being asked to recognise that the requirements are continuous with the ones his own tradition set.

**"Establish" Rather Than Merely "Perform"**

The Arabic verb for prayer in this verse is the one that means to set something upright and keep it standing. The Book uses it consistently for this command, and the choice distinguishes a practice that is maintained from an act that is completed once. A person who establishes a practice has arranged his day and his obligations around it; a person who performs an act has satisfied a requirement and moved on.

That distinction is important in a passage addressed to a community with a long ritual history. The Book does not tell them to begin worshipping; it tells them to make worship structural. The instruction is therefore about the shape of a life rather than the addition of a duty, and the Book's choice of verb carries the difference into every other passage where the same command appears.

**Almsgiving as a Term of the Covenant**

The second command names the obligatory payment of a portion of wealth, and the Book folds it into the same covenant whose terms it describes to this community elsewhere: *“If you establish prayer, pay alms-tax, believe in My messengers, support them, and lend to Allah a good loan, I will certainly forgive your sins.”* (5:12). The verse at hand is a compressed version of that list, and the two items it selects are the ones the Book treats as the foundation of a religious community's public life.

The Book's usage of the term is not confined to one religious community. It states that earlier prophets commanded their people to pray and to give, and it presents the practice as part of the worship of God from the beginning. The sūrah is therefore not introducing a novelty and not imposing an Islamic institution on an unrelated tradition. It is telling this audience that the elements of its own covenant are still standing.

**"Bow With Those Who Bow"**

The third command is surprising for its specificity. Prayer and almsgiving are named as duties; the third is the act of bowing, and it is qualified by the people one bows with. The Book could have said "worship" and left the details to the reader; instead it names a physical posture and attaches it to a congregation.

The instruction carries two meanings at once. It requires participation in communal worship rather than a private observance, and it requires that the participant take his place beside others rather than preside over them. The physical fact of a row of worshippers lowering and rising together is one of the Book's recurring pictures of a community, and the command here makes that picture a requirement rather than an option.

**A Command Given to Israel and Kept by the Muslims**

The three acts named in this verse reappear in the Book's instructions to the new community, and the sūrah uses the same verbs. That continuity is the point of the verse in its context. The Book is addressing a people whose tradition already contained prayer, charity and prostration, and it names those practices rather than replacing them, so that the new revelation appears as the continuation of what the community already does.

The differences that later Islamic law introduced — the times, the forms, the amounts — do not change the underlying continuity the verse is asserting. What the sūrah assumes is a shared religious grammar, one in which a person stands before God, gives from what he has, and joins a congregation. The Book asks this community to keep the grammar it already knows rather than abandon it in favour of something else.

**Worship as a Public Fact**

The verse's three commands, taken together, describe a religious life that can be seen. Prayer has a timetable that others can observe, almsgiving moves property, and bowing is performed in the presence of other people. Nothing in the sentence is hidden, and the sūrah's audience is being asked for a form of piety that shows itself in conduct rather than in claims.

That theme runs through the entire address to this community, which will go on to fault scholars for teaching what they do not practise and to insist that the book in their hands be acted upon. The command in this verse sets the standard first, before any accusation is made: worship established, wealth shared, and a body of worshippers joined.

**The Congregation as a Public Standard**

The command to bow with those who bow makes worship a visible fact of community life, and the Book elsewhere uses the posture to describe the people it approves. A later passage lists the marks of the believers and includes among them the acts of bowing and prostrating themselves, together with encouraging good and forbidding evil (9:112). Posture appears on both lists, which means the Book treats the physical form of worship as part of what identifies a community rather than as a private detail.

A person who takes his place in such a row has accepted a discipline he cannot customise. The timing is set by others, the recitation is led, and his own condition is exposed to whoever stands beside him. The verse asks this audience for that kind of participation, and it does so before it raises any of the objections that will occupy the rest of the passage.
