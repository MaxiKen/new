## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:14

> When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Audiences, Two Sentences, One Posture**

The sūrah’s anatomy of hypocrisy has moved from saying (2:8) to deceiving (2:9) to disease (2:10) to renaming corruption as reform (2:11-12) and believers as fools (2:13). It now shows the posture that makes those renamings sustainable: a doubled tongue. *“When they meet those who believe they say, ‘We believe,’ but when they are alone with their shayāṭīn they say, ‘We are with you; we were only mocking’”* (2:14). The verse is structured as two temporal clauses — *when they meet…* and *when they are alone…* — with two quoted sentences, each calibrated to its audience. The first sentence borrows the believers’ own profession; the second disavows it as performance. The hypocrite is not described as holding a third position; he is described as holding both positions alternately, so that neither audience hears the other sentence. The disease of the heart is thus shown to have become a discipline of the tongue and a choreography of presence.

**The Occasion: “Watch How I Divert These Fools”**

The source preserves an occasion of revelation: a man who boasted to his circle, “Watch how I divert these fools from you,” then heaped praise upon some Companions, and upon returning to his companions said, “When you see them, do as I do.” When the Companions reported the two performances to the Prophet, the verse was sent down exposing the intentions (IK) [UNVERIFIED: occasion with “Watch how I divert these fools” — tafsīr report in Ibn Kathīr without graded prophetic chain; transmitted as sabab al-nuzūl athar, treated here as exegetical report, not as hadith with isnād]. Whether the wording is exact, the vignette captures the verse’s logic: the hypocrite’s art is not private doubt but public diversion, and the diversion requires an audience that is praised to its face. The phrase “these fools” echoes the *sufahāʾ* of the previous verse, so that the contempt that was uttered in private in (2:13) is now enacted as flattery in public. The double life is not accidental; it is rehearsed.

**“Their Shayāṭīn” — Human Satans as Well as Jinn**

*Shayāṭīn* — satans — is the plural of *shayṭān*, and the verse says *shayāṭīnihim* — “their shayāṭīn,” their satans. The possessive matters. These are not the generic Satans who whisper to all; they are the particular satans to whom this group belongs and returns. The Qur'an elsewhere confirms that *shayāṭīn* can be human, as when it describes every prophet as having an enemy drawn from satans among people and jinn (6:112). In that light the hypocrites’ “evil associates” are not only unseen whisperers but the leaders, brokers, and fellow temporizers whose approval the hypocrite seeks when alone. The gloss that takes *shayāṭīn* as chiefs, peers in hypocrisy, open rejectors, or interior whisperers is therefore not a menu of alternatives but a set of overlapping roles: the human organizers of the hypocrites’ social world and the unseen instigators who organize their suggestions. To be “alone” with them (*khalaw*) is not to be physically solitary; it is to be in the conference where the other sentence is safe to say.

**“We Are With You” — The Emphatic Reassurance**

*“We are with you”* — *innā maʿakum* — uses *inna* and the pronoun *innā* to reassure. The hypocrite does not merely report his location; he pledges allegiance, and he does so emphatically because the allegiance has just been jeopardized by the other sentence. The sentence he said to the believers — *“We believe”* (*āmannā*) — was itself *without* an emphatic particle; it was a bare claim. The sentence he says to his satans carries *inna* precisely because the prior claim requires disavowal. The grammar mirrors the psychology: the lie told to the believers does not need emphasis, because the believers are hoped to be credulous; the lie told to the satans needs emphasis, because the satans must be kept. That asymmetry reveals where the hypocrite’s true anxiety lies: not with the believers he has just left, but with the associates whose approval he cannot afford to lose.

**“We Were Only Mocking” — Mockery as the Name for Belief Itself**

*“We were only mocking”* — *innamā naḥnu mustahziʾūn* — again uses *innamā* (only) to confine identity, as *“We are only reformers”* did (2:11). But here the confinement takes a darker turn: belief itself — the profession “We believe” just offered to the believers — is redescribed as mockery. The hypocrite does not say “We were polite” or “We were diplomatic”; he says “We were mocking,” making the believers’ most serious act the object of ridicule. That choice of verb is not incidental. The sūrah will answer it immediately: *“Allah will mock them”* (2:15), so that the mockery is returned. By naming his own profession as mockery, the hypocrite does not merely excuse a lie; he reclassifies the believers’ entire world — the unseen, the prayer, the spending — as something that can be mimicked without cost.

**We Believe — A Sentence That Has Become a Costume**

The bare *āmannā* — “We believe” — that the hypocrite offers to the believers is the same *āmannā* that the mindful live by (2:4) and that the Prophet’s community says in its creed — *“We believe in Allah and what was sent down to us”* (2:136). The difference is not in the letters but in the life that follows the letters. For the mindful, *āmannā* is corroborated by establishing prayer and spending from provision; for the hypocrite, *āmannā* is a costume put on when meeting and taken off when alone. That is why the verse pairs *laqū* — “when they meet” — with *khalaw* — “when they are alone.” Meeting is public, where the costume is useful; being alone is private, where the costume is unnecessary and is therefore described as what it was: mockery. The heart’s disease (2:10) has become a wardrobe.

**A Verse That Does Not Need to Decide Sincerity**

As with the “reformers” claim, the sūrah does not pause to ask whether the hypocrite, in the moment he says “We believe” to the believers, sincerely believes he is believing, or knows he is mocking. The grammar of *mustahziʾūn* — mockers — is habitual, a participle of ongoing practice, not a single act. Whether a given utterance is cynical or self-deceived, the habit is mockery, and the habit is what the verse names. That is why the occasions of revelation for this verse vary in detail but converge on performance: the man who diverts, the circle that rehearses, the return to companions who are told to “do as I do.” The details differ; the structure — belief as performance for one audience, mockery as confession to another — remains.

**Why “Satans” and Not “Friends”**

To call the associates *shayāṭīn* rather than *aṣdiqāʾ* (friends) is not name-calling; it is role-description. A friend is one who wishes good; a *shayṭān* is one who distances (*shaṭana*) from good. The hypocrite’s associates are satans to him not because they are exotic but because they regularize the distance he has already chosen — distance from the unseen that the mindful affirm, from the prayer that is to be established, from the spending that acknowledges provision. To be “with” such associates (*maʿakum*) is therefore to be *with* distance itself, and to describe that distance as mockery of those who are *upon guidance* (2:5). The next verse will show that the distance is not left unanswered; it is met with a corresponding leaving — *“leaving them to wander blindly”* — so that the company the hypocrite chose becomes the only company he is left with.
