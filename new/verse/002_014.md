## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:14

> When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Rooms, Two Confessions**

The verse puts one group of people in two settings and lets the difference between them speak. In the presence of the believers they recite the creed of the community; in the company of their own kind they withdraw it and describe the earlier performance as theatre. Nothing in the report suggests that the retraction was dragged out of them or that the first statement was sincere and the second a slip. The order is the other way round: the private confession explains the public one.

That arrangement is what separates this verse from a description of weakness. A person who fails under pressure tells one story and regrets it; the men described here keep two and choose between them by audience. The revelation records the exchange without commentary, because the two sentences placed beside each other are the commentary. What follows in the next verse supplies the verdict, and the verse itself supplies the evidence.

**Who the "Evil Associates" Are**

The Arabic word is *shayāṭīn*, the plural of *shayṭān*, and it does not always name a creature of smokeless fire. The Qur'an uses the same word for human beings who lead others into ruin: *“And so We have made for every prophet enemies—devilish humans and jinn—whispering to one another with elegant words of deception.”* (6:112). The verse before the reader follows that usage, and the men to whom these hypocrites speak are their own allies and leaders.

Reading the word this way removes a difficulty. If the verse placed the hypocrites in conversation with unseen devils, the scene would describe something the community could never observe; if it places them with human confidants, the whole report becomes the kind of thing witnesses would bring back. The Qur'an is describing a conversation held in a room, and it names the men in that room by their function rather than their species.

**Them Mocking Whom, and What They Were Doing**

Their own words specify the object of the mockery. The phrase *we were only mocking* comes after a declaration of solidarity with the other side, so the thing mocked is the faith they had just professed to the believers. The Qur'an preserves the word in their own mouths and does not soften it into a misunderstanding. A later sūrah gives the same men the same activity and shows them unsettled by the risk: *“The hypocrites fear that a sûrah should be revealed about them, exposing what is in their hearts. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Keep mocking! Allah will definitely bring to light what you fear.’”* (9:64).

The Book then treats mockery of the believers as mockery directed higher. The same chapter records a taunt aimed at those who gave charity in small amounts and answers it as a financial matter between the mocker and God: *“Allah will throw their mockery back at them, and they will suffer a painful punishment.”* (9:79). Derision in this Book is never a private amusement. It is a position taken on a question that does not belong to the person taking it.

**Loyalty Measured by the Private Sentence**

The verse establishes a criterion, and the criterion is not what a person announces where he is heard. Loyalty is measured by what is said where no one is watching, because the private room is where a person invests nothing and risks nothing. Sūrah 63 describes men who used their oaths as cover: *“They have made their ˹false˺ oaths as a shield, hindering ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah.”* (63:2).

The Qur'an keeps this measure for the same people in other passages: it reports prayers performed *“only to be seen by people”* (4:142), and it describes those who conceal the very knowledge they have been given. What all these portraits share is a gap between the outer and the inner man, and the gap is never detected by argument. It is detected by watching where a person does not expect to be reported on.

**Nothing Hidden From the One Who Hears Both Rooms**

The verse works because the reader has been shown both conversations, which no human observer could have. The Qur'an is not reporting a rumour that reached the Prophet ﷺ; it is making public what was said in private, and the act of publication is itself the point being made to the men involved. What they took to be the safe half of their lives is now in the Book, recited in prayer, memorised by children.

The Book states the principle elsewhere in the plainest terms: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah. For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.’”* (3:29). The sentence removes the last hiding place available to a person who divides his life into an audible half and an inaudible one, and it does so without needing to accuse anyone of anything. The two rooms in this verse are finally one room.

**Faith Reduced to a Membership Card**

What the private sentence reveals is what the public one was for. These men were not seeking guidance or appealing to God; they were managing their position in a community whose protection had become worth having and whose commitments had become inconvenient. Faith, in their hands, was a status that could be worn and removed, and the mocking tone is the sound of a man holding a position he does not believe in.

The Book answers this without inventing an inspection. It does not authorise the community to interrogate confessions or to test loyalties by force; it publishes the conversation and leaves the judgement where it belongs. The verse's function is to end the assumption that a sufficiently careful performance can be sustained indefinitely — and the sentence that follows shows what happens to the performance when its author is the one being addressed.
