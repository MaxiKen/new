## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:253

> We have chosen some of those messengers above others. Allah spoke directly to some, and raised some high in rank. To Jesus, son of Mary, We gave clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. If Allah had willed, succeeding generations would not have fought ˹among themselves˺ after receiving the clear proofs. But they differed—some believed while others disbelieved. Yet if Allah had willed, they would not have fought one another. But Allah does what He wills.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Preferment and the No-Distinction Creed**

The verse opens on a claim that sounds, to the creed-trained ear, like a contradiction: *We have chosen some of those messengers above others*. The sūrah's own closing confession will have the believers say *We make no distinction between any of His messengers* (2:285), and the same no-distinction formula governs belief elsewhere in the Book (2:136, 3:84). The tradition's resolution, which the exegetes state as settled doctrine, distinguishes two objects: belief makes no distinction among the messengers in their truthfulness, their prophethood, and their protection from sin — every one of them maʿṣūm, every one of them to be believed in without exception — while God's preferment ranks their missions, not their persons. The sūrah of ʿImrān supplies the ranking's formula: *They each have varying degrees in the sight of Allah* (3:163). To believe all and to acknowledge the degrees is not double-bookkeeping but one creed with two clauses: the messengers are equal in what they are, and differentiated in what they were sent with and given.

**The Three Axes of Difference**

The commentators enumerate the axes along which the missions differ, and the enumeration fills the verse's general claim with content. First, earthly power: some messengers were given kingdoms — Solomon's dominion over men, jinn, and wind — while others, Jesus among them, were given none; the office's worldly reach varies without the office's worth varying. Second, the kinds of signs: Moses' staff and sea, Solomon's command of the wind and the speech of birds, Jesus' healings and the table sent down — each messenger's miracles are his own, different in kind and not merely in count. Third, scope of sending: in Islamic belief the earlier prophets were sent to their own peoples, while the Prophet Muhammad was sent to humanity entire — a difference of mission-width the tradition reads as the ground of his own rank. The verse's two named examples map onto the axes: Moses, spoken to directly, carries the sign-privilege; Jesus, given proofs and the Spirit, carries the support-privilege; and the raised-in-ranks clause, left unnamed, is where the tradition places the widest mission of all.

**The One Spoken To**

*Allah spoke directly to some* — the kalām-privilege, and the tradition identifies its bearer without hesitation: Moses, the kalīm Allāh, the one to whom God spoke. The identification rests on the Sinai accounts — *When Moses came at the appointed time and his Lord spoke to him* (7:143) — where the speaking is unmediated, without the angelic embassy that carries revelation to every other prophet. The exegetes mark what the privilege does and does not imply: it does not make Moses the highest-ranked (the tradition's ranking places the highest elsewhere), and it does not imply a divine mode of speech like human voices — the theology of the attribute is contested ground the verse does not enter. What the verse's syntax does is place the speech among the preferments: it is one of the things God gave, a gift in the list of gifts, and its listing answers the communities who disputed it — the claim's fact is asserted here as calmly as a ledger-entry. The direct speech, in the Qur'an's economy, is not a metaphysical curiosity but a mission-endowment: the law-giver of Israel received his law mouth-to-ear, and the verse records the receipt.

**Raised High in Ranks**

*And raised some high in rank* — the clause is deliberately unspecified, and the tradition has read the specification two ways. The general reading takes it as the verse's own summary principle: beyond the named privileges — the speech, the proofs, the Spirit — God elevates some messengers by degrees whose content the verse withholds, the ranking being His to assign and His to know. The specific reading, dominant in the commentaries, identifies the highest of the raised: the Prophet to whom the sūrah is being recited, raised above the ranks by the widest mission, the complete Book, and the intercession the tradition attaches to his station — and the verse's placement immediately after his credential-seal (*you are truly one of the messengers*, 2:252) is read as pointing the unnamed clause at the named addressee. Both readings keep the verse's discipline: the preferments are divine acts, listed without envy and without appeal, and the ranks are assigned by the One the verse's final clause will name as doing what He wills. The unspecified elevation is the creed's safeguard — the ranking is believed because it is revealed, and its details are known where they are known and left where they are left.

**The Proofs and the Spirit**

The verse's one named messenger after Moses is Jesus: *To Jesus, son of Mary, We gave clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit*. The proofs — the bayyināt — are the visible signs catalogued elsewhere in the sūrah and in the Table: the healings of the blind and the leper, the quickening of the dead by God's leave, the bird of clay breathed into life, the table sent down (5:110). The support is the same pairing the sūrah stated in its address to Israel — *We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit* (2:87) — and the Spirit's identity is the exegetes' question. Al-Rāzī records the three identifications the tradition carries: the angel Gabriel, the Spirit who brought the revelation and strengthened the prophets; the spirit God breathed into human being (15:29), the life-principle in its divine origin; and the Gospel itself, the revelation given to Jesus. The three, he notes, share one quality — each is life-giving, whether the quickening of bodies or the nourishment of hearts and intellects — and the sharing is the identification's real content: whatever the Spirit is, its function in Jesus' mission is the same, the divine reinforcement carried alongside the proofs. The word's root, qudus, carries purity as well as holiness: the support named here is the clean, uncreated side of every miracle the verse's Jesus performs.

**Had God So Willed**

The verse's second half turns from the messengers to their aftermath, and its refrain begins: *If Allah had willed, succeeding generations would not have fought among themselves after receiving the clear proofs*. The subject is precise — not the pagans but the generations after the messengers, the communities of revelation, fighting each other *after* the bayyināt arrived: the very proofs the verse just named as gifts became the occasion of war, because clarity divides as surely as it guides. The refrain has its network, and the network is the doctrine: *If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given each of you* (5:48); *Had your Lord so willed, He would have certainly made humanity one single community, but they will always choose to differ* (11:118); and the sūrah's own earlier statement that humanity was one community before it differed, the messengers sent with the Book to judge between them (2:213). The verse adds nothing to the mechanism and removes nothing from its hardness: difference after revelation is willed — not approved, not authored in the sense of commanded, but permitted inside a will whose alternative (one undiffering community) is named and declined.

**Some Believed, Others Disbelieved**

The division's shape is stated in the verse's shortest sentence: *But they differed—some believed while others disbelieved*. The two parties exhaust the field, and the commentators read the exhaustiveness as diagnosis: after the clear proofs, there is no third position — no neutral ground between belief and disbelief once the bayyināt have arrived, which is why the aftermath of every mission is conflict rather than indifference. The fighting named twice in the verse is this bifurcation carried into history: communities of the same revelation, holding the same proofs, turning them against each other. The second *had God so willed* — repeating the first almost verbatim — has been read as emphasis and as escalation: emphasis, because the refrain doubled insists that the difference itself, and not merely its violence, stands inside the divine permission; escalation, because between the two refrain-clauses the verse inserts the belief-disbelief split, so that the second clause covers not just the fighting's possibility but the division's. The tradition holds both readings without choosing, and the verse's structure rewards both: the willed difference and the willed-against fighting are two layers of one permission.

**But Allah Does What He Wills**

The verse's final sentence is the refrain's resolution and the whole preferment-doctrine's ground: *But Allah does what He wills*. The clause answers the objection the verse's first half invites — if some messengers are raised and some are not, if the speech is given to one and the Spirit to another, if the proofs arrive and the communities fight anyway — who adjudicates the arrangement? The answer declines adjudication: the will is the last term, and the verse places it last. The commentators are careful about what the clause does not say: it is not arbitrariness dressed as sovereignty, since the same verse names what the will has actually willed — gifts, proofs, support, clarity — and the Qur'an elsewhere attaches the willing to wisdom (2:269, 3:74 in the tradition's citation). It is, instead, the closing of every appeal: the ranks are not negotiable by the ranked, the difference is not litigable by the differers, and the fighting after the proofs is not excusable by its participants, since the proofs they fought over were given. The verse ends where the story-sequence began — with a divine administration whose dispositions are seen, named, and not explained away — and leaves its hearer with the only response the administration has ever asked for: belief on one side of the difference, and the deeds that follow.
