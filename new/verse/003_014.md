## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:14

> The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people. These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A List Built to Be Recognised**

The verse names seven attractions, and the order is not random. It begins with two that a person loves — women and children — then moves to what he accumulates — gold and silver — then to what he uses to display and defend that wealth — horses — then to what feeds him — cattle — then to the ground he holds, described as sown land. The list travels from the heart outward to the estate, and every reader finds himself somewhere along it.

The ancient commentators noticed that the sequence follows the stages of a settled life: attachment, then family, then property, then the means of protecting property. That is also why the list has never aged. Horses are not the modern measure of status, but the category — the vehicle that advertises a man's standing — has kept its place in every economy since, and the verse's final item, productive land, remains the object of the oldest form of competition.

**"Made Appealing" — the Passive Voice and Its Meaning**

*Zuyyina* is passive: the appeal was made, laid on, arranged. The verse does not say that people chose to desire these things from a neutral starting point; it says the desire was placed in them, like decoration on a surface. The same verb is used of the world's attraction for those who reject the message: *“The life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers, and they mock the believers.”* (2:212).

Nothing in the sentence calls the objects evil. A wife and children, gold and silver, horses, herds, and farmland are all described elsewhere in the Book as good things, and gratitude for them is commanded. What the verse establishes is that the appeal is an arrangement rather than a verdict, that the world is designed to be wanted. A person who understands the design is not fooled by his own appetite, and a person who mistakes appetite for evidence will spend his life measuring his worth by what he happens to want.

**"Pleasures of This Worldly Life" — the Word matāʿ**

The noun rendered "pleasures" is *matāʿ*, which carries the sense of goods, provisions, and the gear a traveller takes for a journey. Arabic uses it for luggage and for the merchandise of a market. It is not the word for a treasure that constitutes a person's wealth in himself; it is the word for what serves a purpose and then stops.

The Qur'an uses the term this way throughout its descriptions of the world. This life is *“no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children”*, like crops that flourish and then dry up (57:20), and like rain whose harvest the earth presents beautifully until it is mown down (10:24). The sūrah of Competition states the same with two lines of extraordinary brevity: *“Competition for more ˹gains˺ diverts you ˹from Allah˺, until you end up in ˹your graves˺.”* (102:1–2). The word *matāʿ* is doing the work at the end of this verse, and it is a quiet word for a serious idea: what looks like a destination is equipment.

**The Diction That Punctuates the List**

Two details in the Arabic repay attention. Gold and silver are gathered in *qanāṭīr*, a plural of *qinṭār*, a measure the grammarians disputed — some described it as a weight of gold, others as a quantity that could not be counted. The translation's "treasures" preserves the amplification without inventing a figure. The commentators mostly agree that in this context the number is beside the point; the word is there to sound large, and it does.

The horses are *al-khayl al-musawwama*, which the lexicographers explained either as animals marked with a brand or as animals kept at pasture and left to graze freely. "Fine horses" captures the sense without choosing between the two readings; both describe a herd of value, whether the value lies in the brand on its flank or in the freedom of its pasture. The sentence is written to make the reader feel the weight of possessions, then to ask what they weigh.

**The Comparison, Not the Condemnation**

The verse ends by turning, and it turns without a rebuke: *but with Allah is the finest destination*. The word *maʾāb* is a place of return, from the root that gives "return" and "resort". The sentence does not say the world is worthless; it says the world is not the end of the road, and that the road's end is better.

The sūrah's ethics follow from that comparison. The man who owns all seven items on the list and knows where he is going has goods. The man who needs them to tell him who he is has a master. Nothing in the Book commands the abandonment of the list — the Prophet's companions owned wives, children, gold, horses, and lands — but everything in it refuses to let the list be the answer to the question of a man's worth.

**Where the Danger Actually Lies**

A reader could take this verse as an attack on desire itself, and the sūrah will not support that reading. What it attacks is the scaling of desire above everything else, and the Book states the danger in a form that leaves no room for ambiguity: *“If your parents and children and siblings and spouses and extended family and the wealth you have acquired and the trade you fear will decline and the homes you cherish—˹if all these˺ are more beloved to you than Allah and His Messenger and struggling in His Way, then wait until Allah brings about His Will.”* (9:24).

The list in that verse is this list, expanded and given names. The issue is never the ownership of goods. It is the ranking of loves, and the ranking is settled by the question of what a person would sacrifice. The next verse answers the comparison with what the Book says is better.
