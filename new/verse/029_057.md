## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:57

> Every soul will taste death, then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fact That Makes the Command Reasonable**

The sentence supplies the reason for the instruction that precedes it. A believer told to leave his city for the sake of his worship might reasonably ask what remains of the life he is leaving behind, and the verse answers before the objection is formed: the life is leaving by itself. Death is stated as a universal fact rather than as a threat, and the Book repeats the formulation in several chapters with almost the same words, so that it functions as a fixed element of the reader's mental furniture rather than as a warning directed at particular people. What the sentence establishes is a level floor. The migrant and the man who stayed, the persecutor and the persecuted, the king in his tower and the slave in the quarry all arrive at the same appointment.

**"Taste," Not Merely Undergo**

The verb chosen is sensory, and it describes death as something a person experiences rather than as an event that happens in his vicinity. Arabic uses the language of tasting for punishment and for the consequences of deeds throughout the Book, and its application to death gives the sentence a personal and unavoidable quality. Death is not a statistic; it is a cup that everyone drinks, and the Book's descriptions of it elsewhere involve the angel of death, the soul's departure, and the moment at which a person's capacity to act ends. The metaphor also implies that the experience varies without the event varying. Every soul tastes; what each soul tastes depends on what it brings to the table.

The image also keeps death from being described as a full stop. What the soul does at that moment is taste something, which means the person remains a subject with an experience rather than becoming an object that simply ends, and the Book uses the same figure when it speaks of what the Hereafter holds.

**No Exemption and No Bargain**

The universality is stated without qualification, and the Book reinforces it elsewhere in terms that leave no room for negotiation: every being on earth is bound to perish, and only the Lord, full of majesty and honour, remains (55:26–27). The sūrah has already addressed a community that placed its confidence in wealth, patrons, and power, and it has described their arrangements as a spider's web. The sentence now removes the one asset that all those arrangements were designed to protect. A person who trusted in his own longevity is told that his longevity is a fixed quantity, and no fortress alters the schedule: wherever a person may be, death will overcome him even if he is inside a fortified tower (4:78).

**The Return That Follows the Tasting**

The second half of the verse moves from the event to its consequence, and the wording keeps the two connected. The soul does not simply stop; it returns to the One who made it, and the return is stated in the plural address that gathers every person into one destination. The Book's full formula elsewhere attaches the reward to this return: every soul will taste death, and each will receive its full payment on the Day of Judgment, and whoever is spared the fire and admitted to the garden has prospered, while the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment (3:185). The verse's brevity assumes that formula, and it compresses the whole of the hereafter into two clauses so that a person can carry them.

The return also settles the question the sūrah has been arguing about. A community told that the dead are finished has no reason to weigh its conduct, and the verse closes that position by placing a destination at the end of every life, including the lives of those who were driven from their homes.

**What the Sentence Does for a Persecuted Believer**

Read in its place in the sūrah, the sentence performs a specific service for people whose lives were being made difficult for their faith. It removes the enemy's ultimate leverage. A persecutor can take property, freedom, and life, and the Book's answer is that the last of these was never in the persecutor's gift: the appointment is set, the tasting is certain, and the return is to God rather than to the men who thought they were in charge. The sūrah has been building this argument since it told its audience that no one escapes God on earth or in heaven. The sentence about death applies the same principle to the one event that human power seems able to inflict, and it takes that power away.

The sentence also removes the leverage that persecution depends on. Threats that stop at death lose their finality once death is described as a stage rather than a terminus, and the Book's habit of stating this fact in the middle of a chapter about pressure suggests the audience needed to hear it more than once.

**Why the Verse Is Addressed to Everyone**

The first clause names every soul, and the second addresses the reader with the plural pronoun. The Book uses this shift deliberately in its warnings, moving from the general to the direct so that no hearer can shelter inside the abstraction. The message is not that death happens, which everyone already knows, but that death happens to the person reading. The Book's frequent insistence on this point, in chapter after chapter and with nearly identical phrasing, indicates how quickly the knowledge of mortality becomes a piece of information rather than a fact about oneself. The sūrah has just instructed the believer to reorganize his life around the possibility of worship; the sentence that follows makes the scale of the reorganization clear.

The universal address also prevents a reader from filing the verse under other people's troubles. Every person will taste death, so the choice the sūrah discusses belongs to the comfortable as much as to the hunted, and the wording refuses to let anyone stand outside the sentence.

**Death as the Appointment Nobody Skips**

The Book presents death as an appointment that cannot be anticipated or postponed by the person it concerns, and it makes the point by rejecting the fantasy of escape: say that the death being fled will inevitably meet its subject, after which he is returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen and informed of what he used to do (62:8). The wording of this verse sits in that same register. Every soul is to taste death, and the sentence is stated as a fact of creation rather than a threat aimed at anyone. What the Book adds elsewhere is the destination that follows the appointment, and it is that sequel which gives the verse its function in the sūrah: it tells a community weighing the cost of emigration that the one thing they cannot bargain about with anyone is the date being written down for them.

**A Verse the Community Kept Reciting**

The sentence has functioned in the community's life as a formula for funerals and for every occasion of loss, and its pairing of death with the return to God is one reason for that use. What it offers a grieving person is not consolation in the sense of comfort, but a frame in which the loss is a stage rather than an ending. The Book habitually describes death as a gate and the return as the meeting, and the sūrah has already told its readers that whoever hopes for that meeting will find the appointment certain. The next verse describes what awaits the believing servants at that meeting, and it does so in the same concrete terms the sūrah has used for everything else. The Book's habit of attaching a reward to the same event that carries the warning is what makes the pairing of this life and the next intelligible to a reader who has just been told to leave home for the sake of his worship.
