## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:89

> Although they used to pray for victory ˹by means of the Prophet˺ over the polytheists, when there came to them a Book from Allah which they recognized, confirming the Scripture they had ˹in their hands˺, they rejected it. So may Allah’s condemnation be upon the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Victory They Used to Pray For**

The verse opens on a fact from the recent past of Madina, and the fact is the community's own testimony against itself. Before the prophethood arrived, the exegetes record, the Jewish tribes of the city used to tell their idolatrous neighbors that a prophet was coming — one whose banner would carry the Jews to victory over them (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī). The prayer ran with it: send the prophet we find described in our book, that we may wreak punishment on the idolaters and cut them down. *To pray for victory* here carries the sense of seeking help, of invoking the coming prophet as the instrument of a long-expected settling of scores — the idolatrous Arabs of Yathrib heard the promise repeatedly, in terms specific enough that they could repeat it back. After the Hijrah, some of those same Arabs — now Muslims, formerly the promised victims — used exactly that memory in their daʿwah to the Jewish tribes: fear Allah and accept Islam, they would say; you sought victory through Muhammad against us back when we were the idolaters, telling us he was coming and describing his marks to us (al-Ṭabarī's report). The verse files the whole sequence as its first clause: *they used to pray for victory over the polytheists* — a prayer on record, spoken to witnesses, invoked for decades.

**A Book from Allah, Confirming**

What arrived is described with two qualifiers, and each removes a possible excuse. First: *a Book from Allah* — not a claimant, not a manifesto, but revelation in the same category as the Scripture the prayer was based on. Second: *confirming the Scripture they had in their hands* — the Qur'an's standing relationship to what preceded it, which the Book states as its own charter: *We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ this Book with the truth, as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority on them* (5:48). Confirmation — *taṣdīq* — is not mere agreement; it is the arriving truth testifying to the truth already present, vindicating the Torah's own claims, fulfilling its own expectations. The qualifier does decisive work in the verse's argument. A new book contradicting the old could be refused as a foreign imposition; a new book confirming the old cannot be refused without refusing the old — the confirmation binds the two together, so that the Scripture they held in their hands was itself, in effect, a standing witness for the Book that arrived. The recital had already quoted this dynamic from the other side: the community prayed *by means of* the coming prophet, which means their own Scripture had made him the instrument of their hoped-for victory. When the instrument arrived carrying the same Scripture's confirmation, the prayer's own logic demanded recognition.

**What They Recognized**

The verse's central fact is stated flatly: the Book that came was one *which they recognized*. The exegetes examine the recognition's degree. Al-Rāzī notes that recognition here can mean knowing or being acquainted with — the knowledge need not have come from a detailed physical description matched item by item, but from the general attributes of prophethood and truth that the Messenger ﷺ displayed: the character, the message's content, the confirmation of what they held. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī's reports push further: the tribes had been describing his attributes to their neighbors for years, which is why the former idolaters could quote those descriptions back. Either way, the verse establishes recognition as fact, not possibility — the arrival was identified. The recognition is the verse's evidentiary center because it eliminates the last defense of honest error. A rejection following ignorance is a tragedy; a rejection following recognition is a decision. The community the verse addresses did not lack information — it lacked willingness, and the recital has been building to this distinction since verse 75, where distortion followed understanding: the same sequence, knowing and then refusing, now filed at the community's founding encounter with the Prophet ﷺ himself.

**Recognition, Then Rejection — in One Sentence**

The verse's grammar performs the paradox it reports: *when there came to them a Book from Allah which they recognized, confirming the Scripture they had in their hands, they rejected it*. The subordinate clauses stack the grounds for acceptance — divine origin, recognition, confirmation — and the main clause delivers the single response: rejection. The sentence is built so that every step toward belief is answered by one step away from it, and the contrast is the verse's whole indictment. The exegetes supply the pivot: the rejection turned on the prophet's lineage — he was not of the Israelites (a reading the tradition links to the motive the next verses unfold). What the verse itself states is the sequence, and the sequence is diagnostic of a specific failure the recital has now documented three times: the word distorted after understanding (75), the Scripture rationed by appetite (85), the messenger rejected because his message was disliked (87). Recognition without acceptance is not a neutral state; it is refusal with knowledge, and the verse's final clause pronounces on it.

**“So May Allah's Condemnation Be upon the Disbelievers”**

The sentence closes with a curse in the optative — *so may Allah's condemnation be upon the disbelievers* — and three features of the clause carry weight. The word is *laʿnah*: expulsion from mercy, the opposite of the nearness the sūrah's verse of supplication promises (2:186) — a sentence rather than an insult, the judicial form of divine distance. The object is general: *the disbelievers*, not merely these rejecters — the clause names a class and locates this rejection inside it, so that the verse's history becomes case law: whoever recognizes and then rejects stands where they stood. And the form is optative — *may* the condemnation be — the divine sentence phrased as invocation, which is the recital's characteristic manner when a verdict is being handed to the community to hear rather than merely to witness: the believers are being taught what to say about this rejection, given the words in which the Book itself judges it. The verse thereby does double duty: it convicts the historical rejection and trains the hearing community's judgment, so that recognition-then-rejection is never again met with neutrality by people who have recited this sentence.

**The Prayed-For Reform**

The verse's transfer runs through the prayer itself. The tribes of Madina prayed for a victory whose instrument they had specified: the prophet of their own expectation, arriving for their own vindication, against their own named enemies. When the prayer was answered on terms they had not chosen — a prophet outside their lineage, a Book confirming rather than flattering, a victory that would belong to whoever accepted rather than to whoever had asked — they rejected the answer while keeping the prayer. The structure is permanent, and every reforming movement eventually meets it: communities pray for revival, then refuse the revival that comes, because the actual answer never arrives wearing the exact costume the prayer designed for it. The lesson the recital files is about the honesty of petition: a prayer that has already decided the shape of its answer is not petition but demand, and the demand's test arrives when the answer comes in its own shape. The former idolaters' reminder — *you used to ask for victory through him, and you described him to us* — is the verse's method for every later reader: when the community you belong to rejects what it once prayed for, the record of its own prayer is the sharpest argument available, and the Qur'an preserves that record here so precisely because prayers prayed and then betrayed are the most common betrayal there is. The condemnation-clause stands over all of it: recognition is a mercy, rejection of recognized truth is its forfeiture, and the recital's audience is hearing both facts about a people who heard them first about themselves.
