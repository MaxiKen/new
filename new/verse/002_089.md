## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:89

> Although they used to pray for victory ˹by means of the Prophet˺ over the polytheists, when there came to them a Book from Allah which they recognized, confirming the Scripture they had ˹in their hands˺, they rejected it. So may Allah’s condemnation be upon the disbelievers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Book They Recognized**

The verse adds a circumstance that makes the refusal harder to explain. The community had not been ignorant of what was coming. Its members had asked for help against their enemies through the arrival of a prophet, and when the Book came confirming the scripture already in their hands, they recognized it and turned away.

The Book states the recognition as a fact rather than a possibility, and it will do so again later in the sūrah when it compares their knowledge of the revelation to a man's knowledge of his own children. The refusal being described is therefore not a failure of identification. It is a decision made after identification. That the community's expectation had circulated among its neighbours is preserved in the sūrah's account of the tribes of Madinah, who had heard the report of a coming prophet and were told what it signified. The verse's opening clause rests on knowledge that was public in the region before it became an accusation.

**The Prayer That Preceded the Revelation**

The first clause refers to a practice the community had maintained before the Prophet ﷺ came: they would ask God for victory and relief against the polytheists of Arabia, and they did so appealing to the messenger they expected. Commentators explained that the tribes of Madinah had long lived alongside Jewish communities and had received from them the report that a prophet was to appear in the region.

The Qur'an records the same expectation elsewhere when it describes a community that had announced the coming of a prophet and then declined to accept the one who came. The verse's first clause is therefore not a decoration. It establishes that the community's own position, before the event, was that the arrival of this messenger would settle the dispute in their favour.

**The Criterion of Recognition**

The middle clause states that the Book confirmed the scripture already in their hands, and that they recognized it. The Book's argument here does not rest on the Prophet's lineage or on political considerations; it rests on a criterion the community itself accepted, which is that a scripture which confirms the earlier revelation and agrees with it must be from God.

The verse therefore treats the community's own standard as the ground of its indictment. If their scripture was the measure, the new revelation matched it, and the sūrah's opening passages made the same claim when they described the revelation as confirming what had been sent before it and as a guidance for people who were conscious of God (2:2, 2:4, 2:41).

**The Rejection and Its Motive**

The verse does not spell out why the community rejected the Book. Its members had reasons they kept to themselves, and the next verse names one of them: envy that the grace of God had been given to another people. What this verse establishes is the fact of the rejection, and it leaves the motive to be reported by the sentence that follows.

That order is characteristic of the sūrah's argument. It establishes the recognition first, then the rejection, then the motive, and the reader assembles the case. The result is an account in which the community's conduct is condemning on its own terms before any judgment is pronounced. That the motive is withheld here is part of the construction. The Book withholds the reason for one verse and then reports it as envy, which obliges the hearer to weigh the rejection on its own terms before learning what drove it. The effect is that no explanation can rescue the decision.

**The Curse at the End of the Sentence**

The verse closes with a statement that God's condemnation rests on the disbelievers, and the placement of that clause at the end of an account of recognized but refused truth gives it a specific weight. The Book uses the language of removal from mercy in passages where people have been given knowledge and then acted against it.

The sūrah will use the same vocabulary in the verses that follow, where the community's rejection is described again and the curse is restated. Repetition of this kind serves the passage's purpose: the reader is not allowed to pass over the community's position as a minor episode in the history of the region. In the Book's usage the removal of divine favour describes the condition of a people rather than a sentence announced by a judge, and it follows conduct instead of producing it. Here it closes an account in which the community recognised a book, and the placement ties the verdict to the recognition.

**What the Verse Establishes About Knowledge**

The most important implication of the sentence is that knowing the truth and accepting it are separate events. A person or a community can recognize a revelation as genuine and still refuse it, and the Book treats such refusal as a heavier matter than the unbelief of people who never encountered the message at all.

That distinction runs through the sūrah's account of this people from its opening verses onward. The community had been given more than its neighbours, had been told what to expect, and had asked for the event itself. The Book's portrait of its refusal is designed to make the reader uncomfortable about the possibility of inherited knowledge without submission. The sūrah draws the same distinction later when it describes the learned among this people who accepted the revelation and those who turned away with the same material in their hands. What divides them in the Book's account is willingness rather than information, and this verse states the division in its simplest form.

**Recognition Before Rejection**

The order of events in this verse is what gives it its force. The community asked for the messenger, the Book arrived confirming what they already held, they recognised it, and only then did they reject it. Each stage removes a possible excuse, and the Book states them in the order in which they occurred rather than assembling a case after the fact. That structure is why the sūrah can appeal to the community's own scriptures throughout this section without seeming to argue from an outside authority. The evidence being used against them is theirs, and the recognition the verse describes was their own act before it became their problem. The sequence also explains why the sūrah keeps addressing this community as one that possesses knowledge. The passage treats them as people who understood what they were declining, and the rest of the section argues from their learning rather than from their ignorance, which is a harder case to answer.

**What the Verse Leaves for Its Own Hearers**

The sentence is addressed to a community that had asked for relief, received guidance, and then protected its position instead of accepting the answer. The sūrah keeps the account in the Book for later audiences, and what it carries is not a fact about one people but a warning about the moment when a hoped-for truth actually arrives. The Book describes the same hazard for its own followers when it warns them against measuring revelation by what it costs them, and the sūrah's later addresses to the believers return to the possibility that they might do what this community did. The verses are remembered because the sequence they describe is repeatable in any generation.

**A Book That Was Expected**

Communities that wait for something often prepare for its arrival in ways that make acceptance harder. The expectation in this verse had been voiced for generations, and the report of a coming prophet had become part of the community's standing among its neighbours. When the Book arrived, acceptance meant revising the position that expectation had built. The sūrah's account of the refusal names recognition as the step before rejection, which means the community's difficulty was not identifying the messenger but adjusting to what his arrival required. That is why the verse belongs in a chapter addressed to people who have already accepted the revelation, and why the warning it carries is aimed at the same hazard in a different community: the truth that arrives is rarely the one that was expected.
