## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:51

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures yet believe in idols and false gods and reassure the disbelievers that they are better guided than the believers?

### Expanded Commentary

**When scripture is joined to false worship**

The verse expresses astonishment at people given a portion of Scripture who nevertheless believe in idols and false authorities, then tell disbelievers that their path is better guided than that of the believers. The contradiction is not that a person knows something and still sins—human beings are weak—but that religious knowledge is used to validate a rejection of the truth it should have prepared them to recognize.

**The historical dispute and its wider application**

Exegetes connect the passage with figures who supported the pagan opponents of the Prophet, perhaps because of rivalry with the new Muslim community. The historical setting matters, but the moral pattern is broader. A religious authority can prefer political alliance, ethnic loyalty, or institutional advantage to fidelity to revelation. When that happens, scripture becomes a credential rather than a guide.

**False gods include false standards**

Idols are not only carved images. Anything treated as an ultimate source of allegiance and moral permission can function as a false god: status, nation, party, wealth, or the approval of a powerful patron. A person who invokes scripture while letting such a standard determine truth repeats the contradiction in the verse.

The passage is a warning to Muslims as much as to those it first addressed. Do not assume that possessing a Book protects against betrayal of its guidance. Test claims by tawḥīd, justice, and obedience, and do not call a path well guided merely because its advocates possess religious language.

**The allure of political religion**

Religious language can be attached to a powerful ally because the alliance promises security. The verse asks whether that security has made truth negotiable. The test is not which group offers the greatest advantage but whether the relationship permits justice, worship, and truthful speech to survive.
