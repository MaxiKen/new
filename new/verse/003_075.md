## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:75

> There are some among the People of the Book who, if entrusted with a stack of gold, will readily return it. Yet there are others who, if entrusted with a single coin, will not repay it unless you constantly demand it. This is because they say, “We are not accountable for ˹exploiting˺ the Gentiles.” And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Test of a Trust**

The verse describes a community by its behaviour with property, and it uses a comparison to make the description exact. There are those among the People of the Book who return a large deposit without being pursued, and there are those who refuse to return even a small sum unless the creditor stays at their door.

The choice of subject is deliberate. A community's religion is most visible not in its arguments but in its handling of what belongs to other people, and the sūrah puts the test where no doctrine can obscure it. The same Book repeatedly ties the truth of a person's religion to the fulfilment of obligations: the covenant that God made with the prophets concerned what they would do with what they were given, and around it the Book places warnings about trading the covenant for a small gain.

**The Small Sum and the Large Deposit**

The contrast between the two cases is not between honesty and theft but between honesty that costs nothing and honesty that requires effort. Returning a great deposit to a man who trusts you costs you nothing if you were never going to keep it; returning a single coin to a man who has been asking for it costs you a decision.

The commentators noted the practical dimension of the second case, which is that the creditor has to persist. The behaviour described is therefore not a single refusal but a policy of resistance that wears down the person who is owed. The verse's picture is of a debtor who calculates that the effort required to recover a small amount will eventually exceed its value and who is relying on that calculation. That is a description of a certain kind of dealings, and the Book presents it as a religious matter rather than a commercial one.

**"We Are Not Accountable for the Gentiles"**

The clause the verse reports is the justification the community offered, and the Book reports it as the reason for the conduct rather than as an excuse. The claim is that the obligations of the religion do not extend to people outside it, so that property belonging to an outsider may be treated differently.

The Book's answer to the claim comes in the next verse, where it states that those who honour their trusts and are mindful of God are the ones God loves. But the claim itself deserves attention, because it is the most dangerous position a religious community can reach. A rule of conduct that applies only inside a boundary turns the religion into a loyalty and dissolves the very obligations that the earlier scriptures had been given to establish. The Book elsewhere addresses this directly when it asks the community why they believe in some of the scripture and reject the rest, and the question applies to the position quoted here with exactness.

**"They Attribute Lies to Allah Knowingly"**

The verse's last clause attaches the conduct to a claim about God. The community had justified its policy by reference to what God required, and the sūrah says that what they attributed to Him was not His.

The charge is the same one the sūrah made two verses earlier about concealing and mixing, and it is worded in the same way, with the adverb marking the deliberate character of the act. What the community has done is not to reason wrongly about God but to invent a position and present it as His, and the Book treats this as the gravest form of the offence it has been describing. Everything in the passage about the groups' stratagems, concealment, and fears comes to its sharpest point in this clause, which concerns a statement made about God's own requirements.

**Trusts and the Book's Treatment of Them**

The vocabulary of trusts appears throughout the Book and is applied to several kinds of possession. Property entrusted to another person is one; time, knowledge, and the obligations of the covenant are others. The Book commands the believers to return what is held in trust to its owners, and its warnings about the misuse of entrusted goods extend to the inheritance of scripture itself.

Placing property in that framework is what makes this verse about religion rather than about commerce. A community that holds a book holds a trust, and the Book's argument is that a person's handling of a coin entrusted to him is an indication of what he will do with a text entrusted to him. The two are the same test applied at different scales, which is why the verse can move from a debt to a lie about God within three clauses. The reader is meant to see one habit of mind behind both.

**The Answer That Follows**

The verse ends with the false claim stated and attributed, and the next verse begins with the answer: those who fulfil their trusts and are mindful of God are the ones whom God loves.

The sūrah's arrangement leaves no gap for argument. The claim about Gentiles is answered by a statement about God's love, which is the currency the whole discussion has been conducted in since the passage opened. What a reader takes from the pair of verses is that the boundary of a community does not bound its obligations, and that the obligations themselves are the place where God's approval is decided.
