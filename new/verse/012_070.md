## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:70

> When Joseph had provided them with supplies, he slipped the royal cup into his brother’s bag. Then a herald cried, “O people of the caravan! You must be thieves!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Provisions First, Again**

The verse repeats the order established in verse 59. Joseph prepares their provisions, loads them, and only then acts. The brothers have received what they travelled for before the cup is placed and before the accusation is made. The repetition is not a narrative habit but a consistent feature of Joseph's conduct in this section: whatever he does to his brothers, he does after supplying them. The pattern matters for how the reader is meant to judge the coming events. A man who starves his guests and then accuses them is acting from malice. A man who feeds them fully and then arranges an accusation is acting toward an end the chapter will disclose two verses later. The repetition of the sequence matters. As before, the brothers are loaded, supplied and sent on their way before the device is sprung, so the accusation cannot be read as a pretext for withholding grain. Joseph has already given them everything they came for; what he arranges afterwards is a reason for one of them to stay, and he arranges it at his own expense.

**The Cup Is Both a Drinking Vessel and a Measure**

The commentators describe the object in two functions. It was the king's goblet, used for drinking, and it also served as a measuring vessel for food — which is why the translation of verse 72 can call it a measuring cup. One account adds that it was made of gold and set with jewels. Both functions matter to the plot. A drinking vessel is a personal possession of the ruler, so its loss is a serious matter; a measuring vessel belongs to the granary's operation, so its absence is noticed administratively as well as personally. The object Joseph chooses is therefore conspicuous in two registers at once, which is what makes the public outcry that follows credible. The dual function also explains the penalty attached to it. A stolen measure is a theft against the state's own accounting, which in a rationing economy is a public offence rather than a private one, and the law the brothers invoke attaches the thief's own person to the crime. The object was chosen so that its loss would carry exactly that consequence.

**Placed in the Brother's Saddlebag**

The verse specifies the location: his brother's saddlebag, the bag containing Benjamin's own belongings. The commentators note that the bag was filled with Benjamin's things, so the cup is hidden among a traveller's ordinary possessions rather than left in plain sight. The choice of bag is the point of the whole operation. Joseph has ten bags available and one brother he wants to keep, and the cup goes into the one that will produce the result he needs under the law the brothers themselves are about to state (12:75). Nothing in the verse explains the reasoning; the explanation arrives in verse 76, where the narrator says God inspired the plan and that Joseph could not have taken his brother under the king's law.

**Why a Theft Charge**

The commentators supply the mechanism. Jacob's household had a rule about stealing: whoever was caught stealing would be handed over or distanced, given to the person he had stolen from. Joseph knew that rule, and he arranged a situation in which the rule would apply to Benjamin. The charge of theft is therefore not an accusation Joseph believes; it is a device for bringing Benjamin under a law that will place him in Joseph's own custody. The brothers' answer in verse 75 states the law exactly as it was known to them, and the chapter shows the device working precisely because the law invoked is theirs, not Egypt's. That choice is what makes the detention lawful in the brothers' own eyes. A charge under Egyptian procedure could have been resisted or appealed to the king; a charge governed by their own law binds them, since they cannot dispute a penalty they would themselves impose. Joseph builds the situation so that the only authority available to object to it is one they have already accepted.

**The Herald's Public Accusation**

*Yā ayyatuhā al-ʿīr, innakum la-sāriqūn* — O people of the caravan, you are indeed thieves. The cry is addressed to the whole party, not to one man, and it is delivered with emphasis. The commentators identify the speaker as the herald who called out after them, and the charge as collective. That collectivity is what forces the exchange of verses 71 to 75: the brothers must answer for the group, deny the group's guilt, and then state what should happen to the individual if the denial is false. A charge against one man could be answered by producing the innocent man's bag. A charge against a caravan requires a general statement of law, and a general statement is what Joseph needs. The generality is also what makes the reward acceptable. A charge naming a suspect would have invited denial; a charge offering a camel-load to whoever produces the object invites cooperation and places every man in the caravan under the same suspicion at once. Joseph needs the brothers to consent to a search, and consent is what a general announcement produces.

**The Accusation Is False and the Verse Does Not Soften It**

Joseph knows the cup is where he put it. The herald is announcing a theft that did not occur, in a public place, about a party that has been fed and sent on its way. The verse reports the placement and the cry in the same breath and offers no moral comment on either. The chapter is not embarrassed by the device and does not defend it here. Verse 76 supplies the only justification the text gives — God inspired the plan, and without it Joseph could not have kept his brother under the king's law. The reader is left with a false accusation used for a lawful end within a divine arrangement, stated without apology and without elaboration. That refusal to soften is the chapter's method throughout. It records Joseph's device in the same flat register as his generosity, with no clause explaining that the accusation was merely apparent or that the end justified the planting. The reader must hold both facts — that a falsehood was used, and that it served a purpose larger than itself — without being told how to reconcile them.

**The Engine of the Next Thirteen Verses**

Everything from verse 71 to verse 82 follows from the cry. The brothers turn back and ask what is missing. The herald names the king's cup and offers a camel-load for its return. The brothers swear they came to do no harm. They are asked what penalty applies if they are lying, and they answer with their own family's law. The bags are searched in order, the cup is produced, and Benjamin is retained. The eldest invokes the oath of verse 66 and refuses to go home. The remaining brothers return to a father who has now lost two sons. The whole sequence is generated by an object placed in a bag, and the chapter gives that placement its own verse.

**What the Accusation Costs**

The cry is not free. Eleven men who entered Egypt under a father's oath, who were fed at full measure, whose money was returned, and who have just received their youngest brother's company are now publicly called thieves in a foreign capital during a famine. The reputational cost is real and the brothers will feel it — verse 73 opens with an oath and verse 77 shows them defending the family's honour against a charge that touches Joseph himself. Joseph has weighed that cost against the object he wants, and the chapter reports the weighing in verse 76 rather than here. Verse 70 gives only the act, which is the sūrah's habit when it describes something that will be justified later rather than at the moment of doing.
