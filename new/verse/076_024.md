## Sūrah al-Insān 76:24

> So be patient with your Lord’s decree, and do not yield to any evildoer or ˹staunch˺ disbeliever from among them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Follows the Promise**

Having established the source of the revelation, the sūrah gives the Prophet two instructions: be patient, and do not yield. The order is significant. Patience comes first because it is the standing condition; not yielding is the specific application of it under pressure. The two together describe the posture of a man who has been told something true by a trustworthy source and is living among people who do not want to hear it, and the sūrah's closing movement is addressed to anyone in that position.

The sequence is worth stating plainly, because it is the shape of the whole Qur'anic address. Promise first, then command. The sūrah does not begin by telling the Prophet what to do and offering a reward for compliance; it tells him what is coming, and then tells him how to live in the interval. Obedience in this framework is a response to what has been shown rather than a purchase of it.

**Patience With the Decree**

The phrasing is precise: patience is owed *with the judgement of your Lord* rather than merely with people. The object of the patience is God's decree — what He has decided, permitted, and timed — and not simply the annoyance of opponents. The same construction appears in the sūrah of al-Qalam — *“So be patient with your Lord’s decree, and do not be like ˹Jonah,˺ the Man of the Whale”* (68:48) — and in al-Ṭūr: *“So be patient with your Lord’s decree, for you are truly under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes.”* (52:48). Patience is thus rooted in a belief about who is governing events, which is why the Qur'an pairs it so constantly with the reminder that God sees.

**Do Not Obey a Sinner or a Denier**

The second instruction names two categories of person, and the commentators read them both ways at once: as particular individuals among the Quraysh who approached the Prophet with proposals, and as types. The reports name ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah and al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah as having urged him to abandon the affair. [UNVERIFIED: the identification of these two men as the specific referents was not located this session in a chain-carrying source; it belongs to the asbāb material of the tafsīr tradition.] The general reading is unaffected. The instruction is not to yield to anyone whose position is defined by sin or by rejection of the truth.

The two categories are worth distinguishing. A sinner is someone whose conduct is corrupt, and a denier is someone who has rejected the truth; the first describes behaviour and the second describes a position. The instruction covers both, which means the Prophet was not to follow someone merely because that person was well disposed toward him, nor merely because he was respectable in conduct while rejecting the message.

**What Yielding Would Have Meant**

The Qur'an describes the pressure being applied, and it was not persecution but negotiation. *“They definitely ˹thought they˺ were about to lure you away from what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺, hoping that you would attribute something else to Us falsely—and then they would have certainly taken you as a close friend.”* (17:73). The offer was accommodation in exchange for status, and the sūrah of al-Qalam states the mechanism in a line: *“They wish you would compromise so they would yield ˹to you˺.”* (68:9). The temptation was to adjust the message by a small amount in exchange for being treated well.

The danger in such an offer is precisely that it looks reasonable. Nothing in the proposal required the Prophet to deny anything; it asked only for a small adjustment, in exchange for which he would have been received. The Qur'an's severity here is a measure of how seriously it takes the message's integrity. A truth that can be negotiated by a fraction is not a truth that was revealed.

**Where the Line Is Drawn**

A distinction has to be made, because the Qur'an makes it. Not yielding in the message is not unkindness to people. The same revelation that commands the Prophet not to obey the deniers commands kindness and fairness toward those who have not fought the community (60:8), and it restricts the prohibition to those who have (60:9). The sūrah's own earlier verses commanded feeding a captive taken in war (76:8). The line runs between accommodating people in matters of conduct and yielding to them in matters of truth, and the two are frequently confused.

The two instructions also guard each other. Patience without the prohibition can collapse into endless accommodation, and refusal without patience can become abrasiveness. The Qur'an gives both to the same man in the same verse, and the combination is what it means to hold a position without holding it against anyone.

**Patience as the Thread of the Sūrah**

The command also completes a circle the sūrah has been drawing. Its inhabitants were patient (76:12) — patient in keeping vows, in giving while hungry, in wanting nothing back — and the Prophet is now told to be patient in the same way and for the same reason. The sūrah has one piece of practical advice, and it gives it twice: hold the course, because the One whose decree is being resisted is the One who promised the reward described in the previous eighteen verses.

The command is also the sūrah's last word on the relation between conduct and outcome. Patience is not praised here because it is noble but because it is what the situation requires: the promise has been made, the timetable has not been given, and the interval belongs to God. The instruction to wait is therefore the practical form of believing the promise.
