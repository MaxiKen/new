## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:89

> As for those who repent afterwards and mend their ways, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Interrupts the Passage**

The verse opens with a single word that turns the preceding sentences around. After the description of an unrelieved condition and a punishment that is neither lightened nor delayed, the Book admits an exception, and the exception covers those who repent and make amends.

The interruption is deliberately abrupt. The reader has just been told what cannot be changed, and the next sentence begins with the word for exclusion, so that the severity of the previous verses is measured by the narrowness of the door that opens here. The Book's arrangement is characteristic: it states the rule for those who persist and then states the provision for those who turn. Neither cancels the other, and the two statements are meant to be read together.

**Repentance and Making Amends**

Two conditions are named, and the second is what distinguishes this verse's description from a mere expression of regret. Repentance describes the return; making amends describes the repair of what the return left behind, whether in belief, in conduct, or in the relations a person had cut.

The Book pairs the two terms in several places where it describes the acceptance of those who had gone far astray, and it never leaves the second element out. A person who returns to a position he had abandoned owes something for the damage caused while he was away, and the Book's language treats the repair as part of the return rather than as an optional extra. The verse above does not specify what the amendment consists of, which is appropriate for a sentence that has to cover different kinds of harm; what it does is to name the two things that must be present and stop.

**The Two Names of God at the End**

The verse closes with the declaration that God is Forgiving and Merciful, and the pairing of the two names recurs throughout the Book at the end of sentences about the acceptance of the repentant.

The first name describes the covering and removal of the offence, and the second describes the kindness that accompanies it. The order matters: forgiveness is stated before mercy, so that the reader does not conclude that the return is accepted grudgingly or that its acceptance depends on the person's own adequacy. The Book attaches this pair to statements about the most serious offences it describes, including the offences the previous verses of this passage have just detailed, and its placement here makes the offer apply to exactly the people whose conduct has been described at length.

**The Report the Commentators Attached to the Verse**

The classical commentaries connect this verse with a Companion who had left the community and then wrote to his family asking whether a return was still possible, and they report that the verse was revealed in answer.

The report, whatever its status, describes the situation the verse is addressed to: a person who knows he has done something serious and wants to know whether the door is shut. The Book's answer is the sentence above, and the answer's form is itself instructive. It does not ask how far the person has gone, and it does not weigh the seriousness of the departure. It names the two conditions and the two divine names and leaves the matter there.

**Why the Exception Comes Here and Not Earlier**

The placement of the exception after the verse about the permanence of the punishment is not careless. Placing it before would have softened the warning while the reader was still reading it; placing it after shows the warning in its full weight and then shows the way out.

The Book uses this order at the points where it wants a reader to feel both the seriousness of the matter and the availability of the remedy. A person who has been told that the condition lasts and that its terms will not be revised has a reason to consider his situation, and the immediate offer supplies the direction to move in. The passage is not an invitation to speculate about the fate of the community described in the previous verses; it shows that the same door stands open to everyone still in a position to walk through it.

**What the Verse Does Not Do**

The sentence does not describe how long the repentant person must wait, what penalty he owes, or what the community's response to him should be. It states God's acceptance of the return and leaves the practical arrangements to other parts of the Book.

The Book deals with the repair of the relations a person has broken in its legal passages and in its instruction about reconciling people with one another, and this verse does not pre-empt those. What it establishes is the underlying fact on which they all rest: the return is accepted, and the amendment is part of it. The verses that follow describe a different case, that of the person who does not return but goes further in the direction he has chosen.

**The Word for Returning and What It Carries**

The Book's term for repentance describes a turning back, and it is the same root the sūrah has used for the reversal of a course in its earlier verses. The vocabulary keeps the two movements in a single frame: the person who left a position is described as turning, and the turning is measured against the distance he has travelled.

That is why the verse's two conditions belong together. A return that leaves the consequences of the departure in place is not a return in the Book's sense, and the sentence names the repair as a separate requirement. The pairing appears throughout the Book wherever it describes the acceptance of those who had gone far in the wrong direction, and its consistency is itself part of the answer the verse gives: the terms of the return are known, and they do not change with the size of the person's offence.
