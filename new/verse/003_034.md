## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:34

> They are descendants of one another. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Line That Binds a List Into a Whole**

The previous verse named four parties, and this one joins them: they are descendants of one another. The sentence prevents the list from being read as four separate honours handed out to four unrelated families. What has been described is one continuous line of chosen service, running from the first human being through the generations of Abraham to the house of ʿImrān.

The joining word is *dhurriyyah*, which means offspring and descendants, and it is used in the Book for physical descent and for a carried inheritance. A line in this sense is not a straight genealogy but a chain of custody: each generation holds what the previous ones held and passes it on, and the chain can be described as a thing itself.

**The Two Readings the Commentators Recorded**

Commentators discussed what kind of descent is meant here, and two answers are recorded. One takes the phrase to describe blood: the house of ʿImrān traces back through Israel to Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham, and through them to Noah and Adam. The other takes it to describe spiritual kinship, arguing that these houses are linked by their worship and obedience rather than by a documented pedigree.

Both readings have support. The physical reading fits the list's construction of named genealogies and the Book's habit of naming lineages for the prophets. The spiritual reading fits the sūrah's insistence elsewhere that standing before God is not settled by ancestry, and it is supported by a parallel use of the same phrasing at 4:25, where *one of you is as the other* applies to people who are not related at all. Reading them together gives the fuller sense: descent here is descent within a mission, which a family carries both by blood and by belief.

**Why the Verse Insists on a Single Chain**

The Book takes pains to present revelation as one project rather than a series of unrelated interventions. The verse's joining phrase belongs to that effort. Adam, Noah, Abraham, and ʿImrān are not four answers to the human problem; they are stages in one answer.

The effect on the reader is to shift attention from individuals to the work. A person who reads a list of names may compare the figures; a person who reads a chain considers what was being carried along it. The sūrah's own narrative then shows what one link in the chain looked like from inside: a household praying for a child and dedicating it to service before it was born.

**A Sentence That Refuses Tribal Comparison**

There is a further edge to the phrase. The sūrah is being recited in a city where families measured themselves against one another and where pride of descent was an organising principle of politics and religion.

Saying that the chosen houses are all descendants of one another defuses that comparison at its root. Every claim of special lineage in the audience is set beside a chain of chosen families whose election was an act of God rather than an achievement of their own, and the equality implied is explicit elsewhere in the Book where the same construction appears in a legal context. The verse does not tell anyone that his family is unimportant; it tells him what a family is for.

**"All-Hearing, All-Knowing" — the Two Names Chosen for This Place**

The verse closes with two divine names, and their choice is connected to what follows. *Samīʿ* describes hearing, especially the hearing of prayer; *ʿalīm* describes knowledge, especially of what is concealed. The next verses will report a woman's vow and a prayer, and the two names answer the two difficulties a person bringing a private request to God might have: whether he is heard at all, and whether the intention behind the words is understood.

The names are also a quiet answer to the pride of lineage. What a person inherits is visible to everyone; what he intends is visible to God alone. Closing a genealogical verse with a reference to knowledge of the hidden relocates the measure of a person from what he came from to what he brings, and that relocation is one of the sūrah's central concerns.

**The Names Elsewhere in the Book**

The pairing occurs repeatedly in the Qur'an, often at the end of verses about worship, sacrifice, and prayer. It accompanies the account of Abraham's building of the sanctuary and his request for acceptance, and it closes injunctions about obedience and about what a person conceals.

The repetition is a form of instruction. The Book attaches the same two names to the acts that no audience witnesses — a private vow, a matter between a servant and God — and to acts performed in public, so that the reader learns to treat the two categories as equally observed. Whoever prays is heard, and whoever intends is known.

**What a Line of Worshippers Looks Like**

The verse leaves the reader with an image rather than an argument. A chain of families, each receiving what the previous generation carried and handing it on, sustained by a God who hears the prayers that begin each generation.

There is a practical consequence in that image for the community reciting the sūrah. It is being told that religion is transmitted through households and that a house can be a link in a chain of service rather than a private arrangement for comfort. The verse's chosen families are not admired for what they accumulated; they are named for what they kept passing on, and the same standard is available to any household that reads the verse.

The narrative that follows will show the pattern in operation through a vow made before a child is born, and the verse above provides the frame for it: the same hearing that answered Abraham and Zachariah is present for a mother speaking quietly about her unborn child.
