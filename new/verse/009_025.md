## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:25

> Indeed Allah has given you ˹believers˺ victory on many battlefields, even at the Battle of Ḥunain when you took pride in your great numbers, but they proved of no advantage to you. The earth, despite its vastness, seemed to close in on you, then you turned back in retreat.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Victory Can Become a Test of the Victor**

Verse 25 recalls Ḥunain not simply as a battle report but as a correction to the believers' confidence. Allah had already granted victory on many battlefields. The word many establishes a history of help. Then the verse singles out Ḥunain, where the believers were impressed by their great number. The problem was not having a large army. The problem was allowing the number to become the source of confidence.

The Muslim force after the conquest of Makkah was larger than any force the community had previously fielded. New Muslims from Makkah joined the veterans of Madinah, and the size of the army created a feeling that defeat was no longer possible. The valley of Ḥunain exposed that assumption quickly. A numerical advantage can be useful, but it cannot replace dependence on Allah. When a gift becomes an object of trust, the gift itself becomes a trial.

**The Valley Made Inner Overconfidence Visible**

The verse says the numbers did not avail the believers. This does not mean numbers have no military value. It means that material means have no independent power. The army still needed formation, courage, knowledge of terrain, and obedience. Yet none of these could guarantee victory apart from Allah. The opening of the battle showed what happens when a force relies on size without sufficient readiness and composure.

The earth then seemed to close in despite its vastness. The physical valley had not literally become smaller, but retreating soldiers experienced a loss of room. Narrow ground, surprise, noise, and confusion turned breadth into constriction. The image also describes panic. Fear can make a person feel trapped inside a world that has not changed. Qur'an 8:45 links steadfastness and remembrance with strength in battle; Ḥunain showed the opposite when confidence broke.

**The Qur’an Preserves the Community’s Failure**

The verse speaks to the believers in the second person and does not hide their retreat. A weaker religious history might record only the eventual victory, protecting the community's honor by removing its moment of collapse. Revelation does the opposite. It preserves the failure because a community that remembers only success will mistake success for entitlement.

This honesty is a form of mercy. The believers can study what happened without turning the defeat into despair. The retreat did not prove that Allah had abandoned them forever. It proved that their reliance needed correction. The Qur'an's willingness to name the community's error also gives later believers permission to examine their own failures truthfully. A mistake admitted before Allah can become instruction. A mistake concealed to preserve reputation becomes a repeated foundation.

**Ḥunain Followed a Major Success**

The timing intensifies the lesson. The conquest of Makkah had appeared to confirm the community's strength and the success of its mission. That success was real, but it created a new danger: people might confuse the victory Allah granted with an automatic guarantee attached to their numbers. Ḥunain came soon afterward and showed that the community could be shaken even after a dramatic triumph.

Success can make a person less careful than hardship does. In hardship, dependence is obvious. In abundance, the means look self-sufficient. The verse places the many earlier victories beside Ḥunain so that the believers can identify the pattern. Allah had been the giver in both conditions. The difference was the community's awareness. The event corrected an attitude before the next great demands of the chapter, including the difficult march toward Tabūk.

**The Meaning of “You Turned Back”**

The final clause is brief: then you turned your backs in retreat. It does not soften the image with excuses. Some believers lost formation, fled from the first attack, or were swept along by the panic of the larger mass. The Prophet remained present, and the later verse will describe the restoration of calm, but verse 25 is concerned with the moment when the army's confidence failed.

This separation of verses matters. A later recovery does not erase an earlier mistake, and an earlier mistake does not cancel the possibility of recovery. The believer needs both truths. Denial prevents repentance; despair prevents return. Verse 25 supplies the first truth so that verse 26 can supply the second. Honest memory is the bridge between them.

**The Modern Number We Trust**

The battle's spiritual application reaches beyond armies. A business may trust its capital, a charity its membership, a leader his audience, or a person his education and connections. None of these is wrong to possess. The danger begins when the number becomes the explanation for success and the reason to stop seeking Allah's help. The community can be large and still be fragile if its people assume that size makes error impossible.

The remedy is not to reject planning or strength. It is to place means in their proper position. Prepare well, count resources accurately, and remember who can make them useful or useless. Ḥunain teaches the believer to say after success, Allah granted this, rather than, We were too large to fail. The first sentence produces gratitude and vigilance. The second creates the conditions for a fall. Gratitude keeps a victory in the hands of its Giver, where it can remain a blessing rather than becoming evidence for the ego. Remembering weakness after success is a protection against repeating the valley's lesson. The army must carry the memory of its dependence into its next success. Otherwise gratitude fades, and the same gift that once helped the believers begins to persuade them that they no longer need help.
