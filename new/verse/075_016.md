## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:16

> Do not rush your tongue trying to memorize ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sudden Change of Addressee**

Twelve verses of eschatology, and then the sūrah turns without transition to the Prophet and instructs him about his own tongue. The shift is abrupt by design. The passage that follows — vv. 16 through 19 — is a parenthesis inside the argument about the Day, and its placement is its meaning: the revelation being received is itself part of the case the sūrah is making. The God who will raise the dead and reckon with them is the God who is speaking now, and the manner in which His speech is received is therefore not an incidental matter. Reciting, listening, and being explained to are acts due to that Speaker, and the sūrah interrupts its own theme to establish how His words are to be handled.

**What Prompted the Instruction**

The circumstance is preserved in the most reliable collection of the Prophet's sayings. Ibn ʿAbbās reported that when revelation came down, the Prophet used to move his tongue and lips along with it, hurrying to fix the words before they escaped him, and this was hard for him; so God revealed *“Move not your tongue concerning the Quran to make haste therewith.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4927). A second narration in the same collection adds the explanation of what the following verses promise: that God would gather it in his chest and enable him to recite it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5, 4928). The wording of the instruction is therefore precise: the thing prohibited is not effort but haste, and the haste came from fear of loss. God's answer addresses the fear rather than the diligence.

**The Fear Behind the Haste**

The anxiety is entirely intelligible. A man receiving scripture in an oral culture, with no written text in front of him and no means of checking what he had heard, would reasonably press to secure every syllable before it slipped. The Qur'an answers that anxiety elsewhere with a promise rather than a technique: *“We will have you recite ˹the Quran, O Prophet,˺ so you will not forget ˹any of it˺, unless Allah wills otherwise.”* (87:6). The guarantee is attached to the Giver, not to the recipient's precautions. The Prophet's haste was not disobedience; it was the last remnant of relying on his own management, and the verse removes it. The report carries a further detail. The sub-narrator demonstrated the movement with his own lips so that his students could see it, and the chain — Mūsā ibn Abī ʿĀʾisha from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr from Ibn ʿAbbās — transmitted the gesture along with the words. Al-Bukhārī placed a version of this report at the head of his Book of Revelation, which indicates how the community read it: not as an anecdote about the Prophet's habits but as the charter for how scripture is received. The instruction belongs to the first moment of the revelation's descent and governs every recitation after it.

**Revelation Is Received, Not Seized**

There is a principle here that reaches beyond the historical moment. The Qur'an repeatedly presents revelation as something given at a pace and in an order chosen by its Author, and it treats the recipient's desire to accelerate as a category error. The same instruction is given in Sūrah Ṭā Hā in almost identical terms: *“Do not rush to recite ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran ˹O Prophet˺ before it is ˹properly˺ conveyed to you, and pray, ‘My Lord! Increase me in knowledge.’”* (20:114). The prescribed response to not knowing is not to grab but to ask for more. That ordering — listen, then ask — is the discipline being installed, and it applies wherever a person stands before something he did not compose and cannot control.

**The Reciter's Manner**

The sūrah's instruction also settles a question about how the Qur'an is to be recited. Haste of the tongue is prohibited, and the opposite is prescribed: *“or a little more—and recite the Quran ˹properly˺ in a measured way.”* (73:4). The word there is *tartīl*, recitation that is distinct, unhurried, and given its full measure of sound. A text that is recited slowly is a text the reciter is attending to, and the attention is part of the act. The opposite of rushing is not slowness for its own sake; it is allowing the words to arrive in the order and at the speed at which they were sent. Since the Qur'an describes itself as revealed in stages for exactly this reason — *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace.”* (17:106) — the manner of recitation reproduces the manner of revelation.

**The Student's Discipline**

The early commentators drew out a further application that the wording supports. One who hurries to master the words before their meaning has been settled will form conclusions on partial evidence, and a text revealed over twenty-three years could not be understood in any other way than by waiting for the whole. The instruction is therefore a rule for anyone reading: hold the question open until the passage is complete. The Qur'an makes the same point about itself in several places, insisting that it is a Book whose parts interpret one another and whose verses were perfected and then explained in detail (11:1). The rule reaches the listener as well. If the Prophet was told not to move his tongue ahead of the revelation, the obligation on those who hear it is no lighter: attend while it is recited, and let it finish before drawing conclusions. The Qur'an names the opposite practice and complains about it — *“The Messenger has cried, ‘O my Lord! My people have indeed received this Quran with neglect.’”* (25:30) — and it describes the proper response elsewhere as attending silence. Understanding is promised in the following verses as God's own work, which means the hearer's task is not to outpace the text but to stay with it. Haste in the tongue is only the visible form of a deeper impatience, and the cure for both is the same: let the Speaker finish.
