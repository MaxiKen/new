## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:97

> When the angels seize the souls of those who have wronged themselves—scolding them, “What do you think you were doing?” they will reply, “We were oppressed in the land.” The angels will respond, “Was Allah’s earth not spacious enough for you to emigrate?” It is they who will have Hell as their home—what an evil destination!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Those Who Wronged Themselves — At the Taking of Souls**

The verse opens *inna alladhīna tawaffāhumu al-malāʾikatu ẓālimī anfusihim* — when the angels seize, take fully, the souls of those who have wronged themselves. *Tawaffā* — to take in full — was explained as the angels’ taking at death, the same taking that will gather all upon a Day about which there is no doubt (4:87). *Ẓālimī anfusihim* — wrongers of themselves — was read as those who abandoned what preserved them, not those wronged by another; as the sūrah said *“whoever does evil or wrongs themselves then seeks forgiveness will find God All-Forgiving”* (4:110), wronging the self is the description of sin that remains with the doer, not imposed from outside. The image is the sūrah’s moral audit at the border: the angels — the same who record schemes by night (4:81) and who bring witness against communities (4:41) — seize the wronger while his claim is still *“we were oppressed.”* The taking is the moment where claims are tested, as the sūrah has shown that God knows what is in hearts (4:63) and that He is Reckoner of all things (4:86).

**What Were You Doing — Fī ma Kuntum**

The question *qālū fī ma kuntum* — scolding them, “What do you think you were doing? What were you in?” — was explained as *“in what state were you? In what affair?”* — not a request for information but a rebuke. *Fī ma* — in what — was read as asking about the religion they were upon, the land they remained in, the company they kept. The answer *qālū kunnā mustaḍʿafīna fī al-arḍ* — they will reply, We were oppressed, deemed weak in the land — was explained as the claim that remaining among the hostile was not choice but oppression, that they could not go forth or declare. The same word *mustaḍʿaf* — deemed weak, oppressed — was used where the oppressed men, women, and children cry for a deliverer (4:75); there the cry is for a guardian and helper from grace and is answered by the command to fight for them, while here the claim is used to justify staying where the way to emigrate was open. The angels’ question exposes the difference between being truly oppressed — the helpless for whom pardon is hoped (4:98) — and displaying oppression as excuse.

**Was God’s Earth Not Spacious Enough to Emigrate**

The reply *qālū a lam takun arḍu Allāhi wāsiʿatan fa tuhājirū fīhā* — Was God’s earth not spacious enough for you to emigrate? — was read as the sūrah’s definition of the remedy. *Arḍ Allāh wāsiʿah* — God’s earth spacious — was explained as His earth made wide for movement, as emigrants find many refuges and abundant resources (4:100) and as the single soul’s creation spread countless men and women across lands (4:1). *Fa tuhājirū fīhā* — so that you might emigrate therein — recalls the test *“do not take them as allies unless they emigrate in God’s cause”* (4:89) and the warning that to sacrifice selves or abandon homes few would obey had it been commanded (4:66), yet here the same abandonment is shown as the exit from wronging oneself. The sūrah has said that God wills to lighten burdens because humanity is weak (4:28); emigration is not presented as an unbearable weight but as the spaciousness that makes remaining in hostility unnecessary. Early reports placed the verse concerning Meccans who stayed among the idolaters, were marched to Badr under compulsion, and were killed among the hostile ranks while claiming compulsion; the angels’ answer declares that the earth’s spaciousness was itself the answer to compulsion where the heart could still choose.

**Their Home Is Hell — What an Evil Destination**

The verdict *fa ulāʾika maʾwāhum Jahannam wa sāʾat maṣīrā* — It is they who will have Hell as their home, refuge, what an evil destination, becoming — was read with *maʾwā* — refuge, shelter — inverted: the refuge they sought among the hostile becomes Hell as refuge. *Sāʾat maṣīrā* — evil as destination, as becoming — was explained as the sūrah’s closing formula for the abode of those who remain where belief cannot be lived, matching *“Hell is sufficient as torment”* (4:55) and the threat for intentional killing and for siding with wrong (4:93, 4:109). The contrast with the previous tier is exact: those who strive with wealth and lives are given ranks, forgiveness, and mercy (4:96); those who stay claiming oppression while the earth is wide are given Hell as home. The distinction is not that every stayer is condemned — the next verse will exempt the truly helpless who cannot find a way (4:98) — but that the claim of weakness is audited at soul-taking. Where the sūrah earlier absolved those with valid excuse who stay behind from striving (4:95), here it condemns those whose excuse was a wide earth left unused.

**A Verse That Turns Geography Into Moral Responsibility**

Placed after ranks and mercy for strivers (4:96), this verse turns geography into responsibility. The sūrah that began with a single soul spread across lands (4:1), that promised earth spacious for emigration (4:100), and that taught that good and evil are both destined while evil from the self as means (4:79), here makes the earth’s width the measure of the excuse. To wrong oneself by remaining where the oppressed cry for a helper (4:75) when the earth is wide is not weakness but choice hidden as weakness. The angels’ question — *fī ma kuntum* — and the reply — was not God’s earth spacious — will be heard again where the community is asked why it does not fight for the oppressed; the answer in both is movement. For the weak for whom the sūrah lightens, the next verses lighten further: the helpless who truly cannot afford a way are excepted, and it is right to hope God will pardon them (4:98–99), while the one who does emigrate finds refuge and abundance and, if death overtakes him on the way, his reward secured with God, the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful (4:100).
