## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:99

> it is right to hope that Allah will pardon them. For Allah is Ever-Pardoning, AllForgiving.

**Expanded Commentary**

**It Is Right to Hope That God Will Pardon Them**

The verse states *fa ulāʾika ʿasā Allāhu an yaʿfuwa ʿanhum* — for those, it is right to hope, perhaps, maybe, that God will pardon them, will efface their sin. *ʿAsā* — to hope, to be expected, perhaps — was explained with attention because it follows an exception for the truly helpless (4:98) after a threat of Hell for those who wronged themselves while earth was wide (4:97). When *ʿasā* is from God about Himself, commentators said it carries certainty — His hope is not doubt but a gracious promise phrased as hope to keep both fear and longing alive. The sūrah has sealed other pardons with certainty: *“if you avoid major sins We absolve”* (4:31), *“whoever does evil then seeks forgiveness will find God All-Forgiving”* (4:110), and the striver’s forgiveness as ranks (4:96). Here the phrasing is softer, as befits the helpless whose case is known to God and who remain under His mercy. *Yaʿfuwa ʿanhum* — to pardon them, to wipe away, to efface — was read as *ʿafw* — effacement, removal of trace — distinct from *maghfirah* — covering; *ʿafw* removes the record as if it were not written, while *maghfirah* covers it from consequence. The pronoun *ulāʾika* — those — refers precisely to the men, women, and children who cannot manage stratagem nor find a way (4:98), not to those who could and did not emigrate.

**For God Is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving**

The seal *wa kāna Allāhu ʿafuwwan ghafūrā* — For God is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving, ever effacing, ever covering — was explained as the suitability of the names to the case. *ʿAfuww* — Ever-Pardoning, much effacing — recurs where an error might be held: where approaching prayer in impurity is taught (4:43) it is sealed *ʿafuwwun ghafūr*, and here where helplessness might still be judged as shortcoming. *Ghafūr* — All-Forgiving — adds covering beyond effacement. The sūrah has paired *Ghafūr Raḥīm* for strivers (4:96) and for error requiring fasting (4:92); here the pair is *ʿAfuww Ghafūr*, emphasising that pardon for the helpless is not merely not punishing but wiping the sin of staying. The addition *kāna* — was, is ever — was read as timeless attribute: He was ever Pardoning before they were helpless, and remains so. The two names together show that the hope — *ʿasā* — rests not on the helplessness itself as merit, but on the Pardoner whose nature is to pardon. Classical notes added that the sequence *mustaḍʿafīn* (4:98) → *ʿasā yaʿfuwa* (4:99) → spacious earth for emigrant (4:100) teaches conduct: the helpless hope, the able move, and the Pardoner covers both according to what was possible.

**A Verse That Keeps Fear and Hope Together**

Placed between the evil destination for those who wronged themselves while able (4:97) and the spacious earth promised to the emigrant (4:100), this verse keeps fear and hope together without confusing them. It does not declare the helpless forgiven as a flat verdict, lest staying become light; it does not leave them under threat, lest mercy be despaired. The phrasing *ʿasā* was understood to move the heart to keep seeking means — *ḥīlah* and *sabīl* — even while hoping, and to keep the community fighting for the oppressed who cry for a helper (4:75) rather than abandoning the helpless to their excuse. The sūrah elsewhere says *“God wills to lighten burdens, humankind was created weak”* (4:28) and *“God does not wrong even an atom”* (4:40); hope for pardon here is that lightening applied to geography — where earth is wide yet unreachable for these, pardon is hoped wide as well. The sūrah has also shown that doing what was advised would have been better and would have brought great reward (4:66–67): for the able that advice was emigration, for the helpless the advice is to remain under hope and to seek means where means appear.

**The Helpless Whose Pardon Is Hoped — Not Declared Indifferent**

The verse was read to define its subject precisely so that no able person borrows it. The helpless are men, women, and children who cannot afford device nor find a way (4:98) — the aged who cannot ride, the mother who cannot walk with infants, the blind who cannot see the pass, the one who does not know a contact among treaty people (4:90) to secure passage. Their helplessness is double — no *ḥīlah* to devise and no *sabīl* to follow — and it is real, not claimed. For them *ʿasā* is not uncertainty in God but the Book’s pedagogy: the believer is taught to hope and to keep purifying intention, while the verdict is left to the Ever-Pardoning. The sūrah has warned against taking disbelievers as allies for honour (4:139) and against envying God’s bounties (4:54); the helpless who remain are not those allies nor envious; they are those left behind whom the community must still remember. The next verse will set the other face: whoever does find device and way and emigrates finds many refuges and abundance, and if death meets him on the way his reward is secured, for the same God who is Ever-Pardoning here is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful there (4:100).

**A Short Verse That Anchors the Sūrah’s Mercy in Geography**

Though only *“it is right to hope God will pardon them, for God is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving,”* the verse anchors mercy in geography. The sūrah that began from a single soul spread across earth (4:1), that asks *“was not God’s earth spacious enough?”* (4:97), and that promises spaciousness to the emigrant (4:100), here says that where earth’s spaciousness could not be reached, pardon’s spaciousness is hoped. The law that counts the sitting excused as promised *ḥusnā* though not equal to strivers (4:95), that counts the mistaken killer as expiating by freeing or fasting (4:92), here counts the truly helpless as under hoped effacement. For the weak this is the lightest sentence: not a burden added, but a hope placed — that the One whose earth was not yet reachable will, being Ever-Pardoning, efface the sin of not reaching it.
