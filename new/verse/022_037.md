## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:37

> Neither their meat nor blood reaches Allah. Rather, it is your piety that reaches Him. This is how He has subjected them to you so that you may proclaim the greatness of Allah for what He has guided you to, and give good news to the good-doers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Was Revealed to Stop a Practice**

Al-Qurṭubī preserves the occasion. The idolaters used to smear the blood of their sacrifices on the Kaʿbah, and some of the Muslims wanted to do the same — presumably reasoning that if the gesture was respectable for the other side, it was more so for them. This verse was revealed in response.

The correction is total, and it is aimed at a piece of reasoning rather than at a ritual detail. The assumption behind smearing the blood was that the offering travels: that what a person gives has to get to God, and that the physical stuff is the vehicle. The verse denies the premise. Nothing physical arrives, because the Recipient is not the sort of being that receives parcels.

That is why the sūrah places this verse where it does. It has spent three verses on the sacrificial animals — their benefits, their dedication, the invocation, the distribution of the meat — and it now states what all of that is for, before anyone mistakes the mechanism for the point.

**Two Negations, Then the Positive**

*Lan yanāla Allāha luḥūmuhā wa-lā dimāʾuhā* — neither their meat will reach God, nor their blood.

The two nouns are the two parts of a slaughtered animal: the solid and the liquid, the flesh and what runs out of it. Between them there is nothing left of the carcass. The verse is not saying that a better-quality offering would reach Him; it is saying that the category is wrong. Flesh and blood are the whole material of the sacrifice, and neither arrives.

Then the positive clause, and it is a single word: *wa-lākin yanāluhu al-taqwā minkum* — but reverence from you reaches Him. *Taqwā* is the same word the sūrah used in verse 32, where it was said to come from the hearts. The circle is closed deliberately: the rite's value was located in the heart six verses ago, and now the sūrah says that the heart is the only thing about the rite that arrives.

**The Verb "Reaches" Carries an Assumption**

*Yanāla* means to attain, to get hold of, to arrive at something one was reaching for. It is the verb of effort meeting its object.

The verse uses it twice, and the second use is the surprising one. Ordinarily the word describes a person attaining what he sought — a reward, a goal, a thing he wanted. Here the subject is reverence, and the object is God. What attains God is not what the worshipper shipped but what he *was*.

That is the assumption embedded in the verb and the thing the verse overturns about the practice it corrects. The people smearing blood on the Kaʿbah were operating with a physical theory of transfer: the offering has to arrive, so put it where God is. The verse keeps their verb and replaces the cargo. Something does reach Him; it is not a thing.

**God Is Not Fed**

The theological claim underneath the verse is the one the Qur'an makes with unusual bluntness elsewhere: God has no needs. A sacrifice that feeds the deity belongs to a religion of a different kind, and the Qur'an's insistence on this point is what makes its sacrificial law coherent.

If God were fed, the quality and quantity of the meat would be the variable that mattered, and the poor would be in competition with the altar. Because He is not, the meat has somewhere else to go, which is why the verse immediately before this one commanded that it be eaten and given away, and why the commentators ruled that feeding others is the binding part of the rite while eating is merely permitted.

The sacrifice is therefore not a transfer of goods upward. It is a distribution sideways, with the invocation as the thing directed upward.

**The Address Switches to the Singular**

The last sentence of the verse changes grammatical person. Everything before has been plural — *your* piety, *He has subjected them to you* — and then the closing command, *give good news*, is addressed to a single person: the Prophet ﷺ.

The switch is not a slip and the commentators noted it. The ruling in the first half of the verse is for the community; the announcement in the second half is the Prophet's office. He is the one who gives good news, and the sūrah marks that by changing the grammar rather than by naming him.

The same shift happened in verse 34, which also ended with *give good news*, and the two are a pair: good news to the humble, and good news to the good-doers.

**"That You May Magnify God for Guiding You"**

*Li-tukabbirū Allāha ʿalā mā hadākum* — so that you magnify God for the guidance He gave you. The purpose of the whole arrangement is stated here, and it is not the transfer of anything.

The object of gratitude is *hudā*, guidance. The animal, the subjugation, the slaughter and the meal are all secondary to the fact that these people were shown what to do. A religion that begins with being guided will always owe its central act to the guidance rather than to the offering.

The verb is *kabbirū*, to declare great — the same root as the *takbīr* that opens the prayer and accompanies the slaughter. Magnification is not praise for a benefit received; it is the acknowledgment of the size of the One who gave it.

**Good News to the Good-Doers**

*Wa-bashshir al-muḥsinīna* — and give good news to the good-doers. *Iḥsān* is the word for doing a thing well, for excellence that exceeds the requirement.

The sūrah's last word on the sacrifice is thus addressed to a quality of character rather than to a rite. The people who are promised good news are not the ones who slaughtered, since the sūrah has just said the slaughter itself arrives nowhere. They are the ones for whom the slaughter was an act of *iḥsān* — done properly, for the right reason, with the meat reaching the people it was meant for.
