## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:108

> Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? But whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Caution Addressed to the Believers**

The verse turns to the Muslims with a warning, and it compares their situation to one that had already occurred in the history of an earlier community. The question is put in the form of a doubt about their intention, and the comparison supplies the answer before the command is even given.

The sūrah has been addressing the People of the Book for several verses, and the change of addressee here indicates that the lesson of the passage applies to the community now holding the revelation. The Book's practice of warning its own readers through the accounts of earlier communities is at work in this sentence. Warning its own readers through the accounts of earlier communities is one of the Book's standard methods.

**How Moses Was Questioned**

The Book describes the requests made of Moses in several places. The people asked to see God directly, demanded a god like the gods of a neighbouring people (7:138), and called for signs that would settle the matter on their terms. The chapter of the women summarizes the demand to see God and the punishment that followed it, and it notes that the calf was taken up after clear signs had been given (4:153).

The point of the comparison is not that all questions are forbidden but that a community had turned the messenger into a supplier of proofs on demand. What had been given was treated as insufficient, and the requests were made in a spirit of testing rather than of learning.

**The Requests of the Quraysh**

Commentators connected the verse to demands made of the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah, including requests that he produce a book that could be seen, turn a hill into gold, or provide a tree for their rituals. The reports illustrate what the verse warns against, since the requests were not aimed at understanding but at putting the messenger in a position where he either produced a spectacle or was dismissed.

The Book elsewhere records the demands of those who asked for a different revelation or for changes in the one they had, and it answers each request with a statement about the messenger's role. He delivers what he has been given; he does not manufacture signs at the request of his audience.

**Questions That Are Not Condemned**

The Book's practice shows that inquiries of a different kind were welcomed. Many verses of the Qur'an came down in response to questions the companions asked about orphans, inheritance, charity, and the moon, and the sūrah's own discussions of intoxicants and charity are among them. What distinguished these from the requests being warned against was the purpose: the companions asked in order to act, and their questions produced legislation.

The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to avoid the multiplying of questions that had ruined earlier peoples, and he instructed them to do what they could of what they were commanded and to abstain from what they were forbidden. The instruction is not a prohibition on seeking knowledge; it is a caution against turning the messenger's mission into a debate and against asking about matters that create obligation without benefit.

**The Exchange Named**

The sentence states that whoever trades belief for disbelief has strayed from the level path, and the wording returns to the commercial image the sūrah has used throughout this passage. The community that demands proofs on its own terms is described as giving up one thing to obtain another, and the exchange rate is stated to be ruinous.

The word for the path describes a road that is straight and even, and the Book uses it elsewhere for the middle course that avoids both excess and neglect. Straying from it, in this sentence, is not a matter of a single misstep; it is the consequence of a disposition that treats revelation as something to be negotiated. The term for the path pictures a route that runs level and unbroken, and the Book applies it elsewhere to the balanced course between excess and neglect. Straying from it, in this sentence, is the consequence of a disposition rather than of a single misstep.

**What the Warning Preserves**

The caution protects two things at once. It guards the messenger's authority, since a prophet who answers to his audience's demands is no longer delivering anything, and it guards the community's own faith, since the habit of testing is what the verse identifies as the road to losing it.

The next verse returns to the People of the Book and describes their wish to turn the believers back, and the sequence shows what the sūrah is doing. It warns the Muslims against adopting the manner of those who had opposed the messengers, and then it describes the opposition they should expect. The two verses belong to a single instruction about how a community that holds revelation should behave. **A Discipline Rather Than a Prohibition**

Read carefully, the verse does not forbid asking the Prophet ﷺ anything. It forbids the manner of asking that treats the messenger as an object of experiment, and the Book's own record of the companions' questions shows the alternative. What the sentence asks for is the trust that receives what has been given and seeks understanding within it, rather than the demand for evidence that would render trust unnecessary. The disposition the verse commends receives what has been given and studies it, instead of demanding proof that would make trust unnecessary.

**The Difference Between Inquiry and Testing**

The Book does not forbid questions, and its own pages contain many of them. The companions asked about orphans, inheritance, charity, and the moon, and legislation came down in response, which shows that an inquiry made in order to act is welcome. What the verse warns against is a different use of the same act: a request designed to put the messenger in a position where he must produce a spectacle or be dismissed. The distinction lies in what the questioner intends to do with the answer, and the Book's account of the earlier community's requests makes that clear, since the demands made of Moses were not followed by obedience when they were met. The sentence therefore protects the community from a habit that consumes the trust on which faith depends, and the next verse describes the opposition the believers should expect from outside.
