## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:69

> Relate to them ˹O Prophet˺ the story of Abraham,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet Is Told to Make Abraham's Story Public**

The command to relate Abraham's story changes the scene from the sea to a family and a community debating worship. The Prophet is not asked to invent a new argument for monotheism. He is told to bring an earlier prophetic encounter before his audience. Abraham's history becomes a living proof that the call to worship God alone is not a recent demand tied to one nation or one messenger.

The verb relate carries the sense of reciting and conveying an account so that it can be heard and considered. Story here does not mean fiction or entertainment. It is a truthful account selected for guidance. The Prophet's task is to present the event in a way that exposes the same moral issue facing his own people: will inherited worship be examined, or will it be protected from questions by custom?

**Abraham Follows Moses as a Different Model of Confrontation**

Moses confronted a ruler who claimed divine authority and enslaved a people. Abraham will confront idolatry in his family and society. The settings differ, but the central question remains who deserves worship and who has the right to define truth. The transition from one story to the other shows that false lordship can appear as political tyranny or as inherited religious devotion.

This sequence broadens the lesson of the sea. Deliverance from a tyrant is not the only form of monotheistic struggle. A person may live without Pharaoh's army and still give ultimate loyalty to objects, ancestors, or social habits. Abraham's story brings the call into the intimate space of family and community, where obedience to custom can be as strong as fear of a ruler.

**A Story Can Reopen a Settled Question**

The audience may think its worship is too old to question. Abraham's story interrupts that confidence by showing a revered ancestor who questioned his own people. The past is not presented as a wall against reasoning. It becomes a field in which a prophet asks what the community has accepted and why.

That makes storytelling a method of reform. A direct accusation can make people defensive, while a remembered encounter lets them see their own habits in another setting. The story still carries judgment, but it allows the hearer to approach the question through a person whose courage and clarity can be examined. The Prophet is told to relate, not merely to declare, because the shape of the history itself teaches.

**Abraham's Example Reaches Beyond Sectarian Ownership**

Abraham is claimed by different religious communities, but the Qur'anic presentation places his essential identity before later labels. He stands as a ḥanīf, one who turns away from false worship toward primordial monotheism. This retelling therefore brings a common ancestor into the argument and asks every audience to consider whether its current practice matches his devotion.

This does not erase historical differences among communities. It identifies a criterion deeper than group pride. Abraham's worth lies in his submission to God and his refusal to make created things lords. A community cannot claim him while preserving the very idolatry he opposed. His story belongs to truth before it belongs to a faction's self-description.

**The Story Is Directed at Present Hearers**

The command says relate to them. The audience is not invited to admire Abraham as a distant hero without examining itself. The story is selected because it can address the people now hearing the Prophet. Their idols, customs, and arguments must be placed beside Abraham's questions.

This gives revelation a living relation to history. Past events are not museum pieces. They become mirrors in which present conduct can be judged. The audience may say that Abraham's people were mistaken while missing the same structure in its own worship. The command to relate forces the story across that distance.

**Truthful Narrative Preserves the Messenger's Role**

The Prophet is a conveyor of the story, not its owner. He receives the account and recites it under God's authority. This keeps the narrative from becoming a literary invention shaped to win an audience. The story's power lies in its revealed truth and in the lesson its events disclose.

That role also links the Prophet with Abraham without making them identical. Both call people away from false worship, but each serves in a different historical setting. The Prophet's authority is strengthened by continuity with earlier messengers. The same God who guided Abraham now gives Muhammad a story through which his people can be warned.

**The Transition Protects Against a Narrow Reading of Moses**

If the chapter stopped with Pharaoh, a reader might treat its message as only a lesson about political oppression. Abraham's story prevents that narrowing. The Lord who saves a people from an external tyrant also asks individuals and communities to examine what they worship inwardly. Freedom from Pharaoh must be joined to freedom from false objects of devotion.

The command to relate Abraham therefore opens the next movement with purpose. It carries forward the theme of lordship while changing the form of the challenge. The question will move from how a ruler treats a people to what a father and community call a god. The story begins before the question is spoken, because the instruction to tell it has already made the question relevant.

A truthful story can therefore expose a present error without naming the present audience in every sentence. The hearer recognizes the pattern and must decide whether the inherited practice deserves the same examination Abraham gave it.

The command to relate is thus an act of warning, not a change of subject. Abraham's story is chosen because the audience needs its question now.
