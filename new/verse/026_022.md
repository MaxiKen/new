## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:22

> How can that be a ‘favour,’ of which you remind me, when ˹it was only because˺ you ˹have˺ enslaved the Children of Israel?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses Rejects a Benefit Built Beside Oppression**

Pharaoh has presented Moses' upbringing as a favor that should purchase loyalty. Moses answers by exposing the moral cost hidden behind that claim. How can the palace's care for one child be called a favor when the same ruler has enslaved an entire people? The question does not deny that Moses received shelter or food. It denies that those private benefits can be weighed without the public wrong that made Pharaoh's rule what it was.

The contrast is deliberate. Pharaoh remembers the years he gave Moses; Moses remembers the Israelites whom Pharaoh held. One view narrows gratitude to a personal relationship with the ruler. The other asks how a ruler treats a whole community. Justice requires the wider view. A kindness toward an individual cannot cleanse a system that degrades many others.

**Mann Can Become a Debt Used for Control**

The verb translated remind has a broad Arabic range. Mann can mean to give a benefit, to count or recount it, and to press it upon the recipient as a reason for submission. The last sense is especially sharp here. Pharaoh is not simply recalling history. He is counting the favor in public so that Moses will feel indebted and the court will see his opposition as betrayal.

That is how benefaction becomes political control. The ruler gives something, records it, and later demands a return far beyond what was given. Gratitude is transformed into silence about wrongdoing. The Qur'an warns elsewhere against charity spoiled by reminders and injury, and the same moral danger appears in Pharaoh's speech. A gift ceases to be generous when it is used to own the conscience of the person who received it.

**A Private Benefit Cannot Cancel a Public Crime**

Pharaoh's argument would be persuasive only if personal care erased communal harm. It does not. Moses can acknowledge that he grew up in the palace and still say that the palace's rule was unjust. These truths do not contradict each other. The household may have preserved one child's life while the state oppressed the Children of Israel. Both facts must be judged, not forced into a single flattering story.

This principle applies to every institution that points to its good works while asking people to ignore its injuries. A government may build roads and still persecute a minority. An employer may offer benefits and still exploit workers. A family may provide care and still demand complicity in abuse. Good received should be recognized, but it cannot purchase permission to call evil good. Moses' question restores the victims to a discussion that Pharaoh tried to make personal.

**The Enslaved Are the Measure of the Ruler's Claim**

Pharaoh's title to gratitude is tested by the condition of the Israelites. If he were a just guardian, their safety and freedom would be part of the evidence for his rule. Instead, their enslavement reveals that the palace's language of care is selective. A ruler can call himself beneficent while the people outside his household bear the cost of his power.

Moses therefore refuses to let the court define blessing by proximity to privilege. The palace may feel generous to the person inside it, but a society must be judged from the position of those made vulnerable by its order. This is not envy of legitimate care. It is the demand that care be extended according to justice rather than reserved for the ruler's chosen dependents.

**Gratitude Belongs Under God's Moral Order**

Moses' reply does not teach people to despise every favor or to deny the good done by others. It teaches that gratitude has a limit set by God. A created benefactor may be thanked, but he cannot demand obedience in oppression. The first debt is to the Creator, and that debt includes refusing to help a human being violate the rights of another.

This order protects both gratitude and freedom. Without gratitude, people become arrogant and forget the help through which their lives were sustained. Without moral limits, gratitude becomes servitude. Pharaoh wants the second condition: he wants Moses to treat a palace upbringing as a title over his conscience. Moses' answer says that no human favor can make slavery just.

**The Question Exposes the Ruler's Evasion**

Pharaoh's words were meant to shift attention from the Israelites to Moses' childhood. Moses returns the focus to the very people whose condition prompted the mission. He does not allow the court to decide that the messenger's personal history is the only matter worth hearing. The question about a favor is therefore also a question about political accountability: what is the value of a ruler's kindness if his rule is built on another people's suffering?

The answer is not that every good act becomes worthless because the actor has done evil. It is that good acts cannot be used as a shield against judgment. Pharaoh may have cared for Moses in one setting and oppressed Israel in another. The Lord judges the whole pattern. Moses' sentence keeps the whole pattern in view.

**A Ruler Cannot Make Oppression Look Like Generosity**

The most dangerous feature of Pharaoh's argument is its change of names. He calls control a favor, obligation a debt, and resistance ingratitude. Moses refuses those substitutions. Naming matters because a community can be trained to accept injustice when the language around it is softened. Enslavement remains enslavement even when the ruler points to a benefit he once gave.

The verse thus gives the oppressed a vocabulary with which to answer power. Do not allow a selective kindness to erase a whole people's suffering. Do not let a benefactor's reminder become a command to abandon truth. Gratitude can be sincere only when it remains inside justice, and justice begins by refusing to make another person's captivity the price of one's comfort.
