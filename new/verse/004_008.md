## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:8

> If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it and speak to them kindly.

### Expanded Commentary

**The distribution is a public moral moment**

After identifying the lawful heirs, the verse turns to people who may be present at the division but have no fixed legal share: relatives outside the prescribed classes, orphans, and the needy. Their presence tests whether the family regards inheritance as an occasion for gratitude and humane generosity or as a scramble for private gain. The instruction is to offer them something and address them with kindness.

The precise legal status of the gift was discussed by the jurists. Some regarded it as an enforceable duty, others as a strongly encouraged practice, and some connected it with the portion a person may bequeath. Those differences should not erase the ethical core: the completion of legal shares does not exhaust the heirs’ moral responsibility toward vulnerable people standing before them.

**Fixed shares and voluntary provision**

The verse cannot be used to override the fractions established later in the chapter. A non-heir cannot be promised the property of a person who is legally entitled to it, and the heirs cannot give away another heir’s share. The appropriate provision comes from what the recipients may lawfully offer—before distribution through a valid bequest, or afterward from their own portions. Law supplies the floor; charity raises the community above mere compliance.

**Speech can either prevent or deepen injury**

The command to speak kindly is as important as the material gift. A small amount offered with contempt may wound; a modest provision accompanied by clear, respectful words can preserve dignity. Conversely, when there is little to give, gracious speech remains possible. This principle echoes the Qur’an’s wider teaching that a kind word and forgiveness are better than charity followed by harm (2:263).

**A lesson for estate practice**

Inheritance meetings can expose old grievances, poverty, and unequal power. A just process should therefore identify all claimants, explain the shares, provide a private avenue for questions, and ensure that widows, minors, and estranged relatives are not silenced. The verse asks the family to see those whom the arithmetic does not automatically include. The estate is not only an inventory of things; it is a final opportunity to turn loss into care.

**Dignity at the distribution table**

An estate meeting should not become a public ranking of whose life was valuable. Offering provision to non-heirs and speaking kindly can prevent a bereaved gathering from turning into humiliation. The heirs who give from their own shares are not losing the law; they are showing that the law has made them trustees of a wider social mercy.
