## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:199

> Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims. And seek Allah’s forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Surge Onward With the People**

The verse legislates the pilgrimage's great collective movement: then surge onward from where the people surge onward. The reference, the exegetes agree, is the pilgrims' flow from ʿArafāt to Muzdalifah — the ifāḍah the previous verse described at its arrival, now commanded at its joining. The word for the movement carries the image of water: a pouring, a streaming, a crowd moving as one body down the plain's western edge in the sunset. And the command's emphasis falls not on the movement but on its company — *from where the people* surge. The pilgrim is to go with the going multitude, from the place of its going, in its manner and its measure. Al-Qurṭubī notes a grammatical point that sharpens the emphasis: the *then* of the verse does not denote temporal succession — the going-forth is not simply the next item after Muzdalifah's remembrance — but functions as a logical connector, "so" or "and so," tying the command to the whole preceding legislation. The surge is thus the summed conclusion of the rites so far: the months kept, the iḥrām guarded, the bounty sought without blame, the plain stood upon, the monument remembered at — and now, with all of it done in the individual's own person, the person dissolved back into the people.

**The Ḥums and the Aristocracy of the Sanctuary**

The command was revealed against a specific practice of pre-Islamic exclusivism. The Ḥums — a confederation of Quraysh and the tribes linked to them by professed devotion to the Kaʿbah and its ḥaram — held themselves too sacred for the plain. Since ʿArafāt lies outside the ḥaram's boundary while Muzdalifah lies within it, the Ḥums would not stand with the other people at ʿArafāt and would not surge onward from there with them; they remained at Muzdalifah, reasoning that the entire hajj should take place inside the sacred precinct, and treating the other Arabs' station on the plain as beneath their rank. The verse abolishes the distinction by commanding the Ḥums — through the whole community — to surge from where the people surge: the aristocracy of the sanctuary is overruled, and the Quraysh's claimed holiness is required to stand, walk, and flow exactly where the herdsman of Najd stands, walks, and flows. The same group is identified by the commentators as the one addressed by the sūrah's earlier ruling on entering houses by their doors rather than their backs (2:189) — a pattern of sanctimonious custom corrected by plain obedience. The hajj this verse legislates is, at this point, an enacted equality: one stream, one station, one departure, no precedence of lineage in the plain's geography.

**Ask Forgiveness**

At the summit of the rite — the movement the whole pilgrimage exists for — the verse commands a second act, and it is not praise but istighfār: ask God for forgiveness. The placement is the command's theology. The pilgrim at this point has done everything the legislation required: he has stood on the plain in the day that the tradition calls the hajj itself, he has remembered God at the sacred monument through the night, and he is now flowing onward with the people. The natural movement of the heart at such a summit is self-congratulation — the completed rite, the earned merit, the pilgrimage accomplished — and the verse interrupts that movement with a petition for pardon. The pilgrim is to ask forgiveness at the moment of his greatest achievement: for the shortcomings inside the achievement, for the rafath and fusūq and jidāl that iḥrām forbade and the weeks of road perhaps carried, for the intention that wavered between God and the world the whole journey long. The tradition's own practice confirms the placement — the great gathering at ʿArafāt is in its substance a mass of supplication and seeking of pardon, the ummah standing before God with its needs and its faults rather than its resume. The verse legislates the posture: at the top of the rite, the pilgrim stands at the bottom of himself.

**Forgiving, Merciful**

The two names that close the verse are the istighfār's answer, and their pairing is the Qur'an's standard formula of comprehensive pardon. The pilgrim who is commanded to ask forgiveness is told in the same breath who is being asked: the Forgiving — the ghafūr whose forgiveness covers the faults just enumerated, the rite's small corruptions, the road's quarrels, the heart's mixed intentions; the Merciful — the raḥīm whose mercy exceeds the asking, so that the petition arrives before One already disposed to grant it. The names do structural work across the whole passage: the hajj-legislation has moved from severity (know that God is severe in retribution, 2:196) through knowledge (whatever good you do, God knows it, 2:197) to this pardon — the arc of a legislation that arms itself with awe and then opens into mercy, exactly as the pilgrimage itself moves from iḥrām's restrictions to the ʿīd's release. And the names answer the Ḥums' error at its root: the confederation that trusted its own sanctity is commanded to trust God's forgiveness instead — the aristocracy of lineage replaced by the democracy of pardon, in which the Qurayshī and the Bedouin ask in the same words and are answered by the same names.

**The Stream as the Ummah's Portrait**

The verse's central image — the people surging as one — has carried weight in the tradition far beyond its legal content. The ifāḍah from ʿArafāt is the largest single gathering the Muslim world produces: every nation, every rank, every school of law moving in one current at one hour from one plain, dressed in the same two white cloths, answering the same call. The verse that commands joining that current is read as legislating the ummah's visible form — the community as stream rather than hierarchy, in which the going-forth is defined by the people's going-forth and no one departs from a private station. The Ḥums' sin, in this light, was not merely a ritual error but an ecclesiology: the claim to a separate departure, a holier exit, a sanctuary-aristocracy that flows apart. The verse's answer is total — surge *from where the people* surge — and its istighfār-clause completes the leveling: the stream that flows as one body asks forgiveness in one petition, since no member of it arrives at the plain without faults and none leaves it except by pardon. The pilgrimage this verse completes is thus the community rehearsing both its equality and its dependence: one current, one asking, one Forgiving and Merciful at the current's end.

**Between the Plain and the Days**

Within the passage's architecture the verse is the hinge between the hajj's peak and its coda. Before it: the months, the prohibitions, the provisions, ʿArafāt, Muzdalifah (2:196–198). After it: the rites' completion and the remembrance that outdoes the fathers (2:200), the two supplications and their portions (2:201–202), the numbered days at Minā with their easy departures (2:203). This verse stands at the turn — the surge onward, the forgiveness sought — and its two commands set the tone for everything that follows: the collectivity that the numbered days will keep (the takbīr recited by pilgrims and non-pilgrims alike) and the pardon that the completion will presume (the rites remembered before a Forgiving God). The pilgrim who has surged with the people and asked forgiveness has, in the verse's own logic, already received the hajj's inner substance; what remains is the working-out — the stones, the sacrifice, the days, the farewell circuit. The verse therefore closes the mountain-pass of the legislation and opens its descent: onward with the people, asking pardon, under the two names — and the plain behind, the valley ahead, and the gathered community streaming between them toward home.
