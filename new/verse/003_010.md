## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:10

> Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Things Everyone Trusted**

Wealth and children were the whole portfolio of the ancient world, and much of the modern one. Money answered emergencies and bought influence; sons carried a man's name, defended his household, and kept his memory alive after death. To have both was to be secure by the standards of the market and the standards of the tribe at the same time.

The verse does not say that these goods are evil, and it does not say they are useless. It says something narrower and harder: they will not help their owners *against Allah*. The restriction is the whole point. In this life, a fortune can postpone a hundred miseries; a powerful family can shelter a man from his neighbours. Neither can be carried into a courtroom where the judge is the one who gave them, and neither can be argued with as evidence.

**"Against Allah" — the Preposition That Decides the Sentence**

The Qur'an uses the same construction in a verse that closes the loop: *“Neither their wealth nor children will be of any help to them against Allah whatsoever. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.”* (58:17). The phrase *against Allah* presupposes that the crisis a person will actually face is a reckoning with God, and it measures everything a person owns by its usefulness for that moment.

Once the measure is set, the Book's scattered statements about prosperity fall into one doctrine. Wealth and children are *adornment* of this life while good deeds outlast it (18:46). They can be a *test*, not a certificate (8:28; 64:15). They can turn from support into diversion: *“O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.”* (63:9). The Book never denies that these things are good in themselves; it refuses to let them be evidence of anything.

**Fuel for the Fire: the Image and Where It Recurs**

The image is deliberately physical. A fire needs something to eat, and the verse says the deniers themselves become its food. It is not a picture of punishment imposed from outside on an innocent object; it is the transformation of the person into the material that keeps the punishment going.

The same image anchors the Book's earliest warning to its opponents: *“then fear the Fire fuelled with people and stones, which is prepared for the disbelievers.”* (2:24), and the believers are taught to protect their households from that same fire (66:6). Polytheists and their objects of worship are named together as its fuel (21:98), which is worth noticing: in this image nobody's status survives. The king and the stone are consumed by the same appetite.

Notice also where the fuel comes from. The verse does not say the deniers will be thrown into a fire that burns other things; it says they themselves are the fuel, which means the fire's continuance is drawn from what they are. The Qur'an describes the reciprocal case in the punishment of hoarders, whose own treasure is heated until it marks them (9:35). What a person has accumulated, whether metal or self, is turned into the substance of his penalty. That is the reversal the image carries: the security a man spent his life assembling becomes the material of the thing he feared.

**When a Blessing Becomes Evidence Against You**

The Qur'an is willing to follow the doctrine to a conclusion that unsettles comfortable readers: the very gifts can be a means of punishment. *“So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life, then their souls will depart while they are disbelievers.”* (9:55).

A believer reading that sentence has to ask what he is doing with what he has been given. And the Book elsewhere shows the same goods being hoarded into a specific kind of condemnation: treasures heated until they brand the skin of the one who accumulated them, with the words, *“This is the treasure you hoarded for yourselves. Now taste what you hoarded!”* (9:35). Nothing in either passage makes a rich man a bad man. Both passages insist that property is an instrument with a direction, and that its direction is the only thing that survives its loss.

**What the Verse Says to a Community in Trouble**

The first readers of this sūrah were a small community in Madinah facing a rich and confident opposition. The natural fear was demoralisation by comparison: our enemies have numbers, funds, and heirs to run their business when they are gone; we have conviction.

The verse answers that fear by dismantling the comparison at its base. What the enemy possesses has no value in the transaction that will decide everything, and the question is not who holds what but who will stand when the reckoning opens. Followed by a sentence about a fire these goods cannot extinguish, it gives the believers an argument that cannot be taken from them by the arithmetic of power.

There is a discipline in the verse for the believers as well, and the sūrah states it plainly a few lines later: do not be impressed by the prosperity of those who reject the message, because it is brief and its end is known (3:196–197). A community that has been told the outcome has no reason to measure itself against the assets of its opponents, and no reason to soften its message in the hope of sharing them. The sentence about wealth and children is therefore not only a warning to the wealthy. It is a release for the anxious.
