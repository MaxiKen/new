## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:51

> Surely Allah is my Lord and your Lord. So worship Him ˹alone˺. This is the Straight Path.’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Closes a Prophet's Speech**

The verse ends a speech that began four verses earlier, and it ends on a creed rather than on a wonder. The list of signs has been given — the bird of clay, the healing of the blind and the leper, the raising of the dead, the knowledge of what is hidden in houses — and the speech closes with a single declaration about God.

That arrangement is the sūrah's argument in miniature. A messenger who performs signs that no human being can perform, and who then says nothing about his own status, has answered the question his signs raise before anyone asks it. The sentence he chooses to end on is not "I am" but "He is", and the conclusion he draws from his own miracles is an instruction to worship God.

**"My Lord and Your Lord" — One Lordship, Two Relations**

The words name a shared relationship without flattening it. God is the speaker's Lord and the hearers' Lord, which means both parties stand in the same position of dependence, and the difference between them lies in the mission, not in the nature.

The Book puts this phrase in the mouth of the same prophet on several occasions, and each time it answers a different error. In the sūrah of Maryam, Jesus declares that God is his Lord and theirs and that this is the Straight Path (19:36). In the sūrah of Ornaments of Gold the words are again his, followed by the same call to worship God alone (43:64). In the sūrah of the Table, the declaration is quoted as the answer to the claim that God is the Messiah: the Messiah himself said, worship Allah, my Lord and your Lord (5:72). The verse under discussion is the sūrah's own statement of the same formula, and its placement inside the list of miracles is deliberate.

**Other Prophets Said the Same Words**

The phrase is not unique to Jesus, and every reader of the sūrah has heard it before. Moses uses the same pair of possessives when he seeks refuge: I seek refuge in my Lord and your Lord from every arrogant person who does not believe in the Day of Reckoning (40:27), and again when he asks not to be stoned (44:20). Hūd uses the form in the sūrah of Hūd, and the formula recurs throughout the missions recorded in the Book.

The uniformity is the point of revelation's presentation of the prophets. What every messenger announces is that God is the Lord of both speaker and audience, which makes the messenger a servant of the same Lord as the people he addresses. A prophet who stood outside that relation would be a different kind of being, and the Book does not have such a category.

**Worship as the Only Conclusion**

The instruction that follows the declaration is *so worship Him alone*. The particle connects the two sentences logically: because He is the Lord of both, worship is owed to Him by both. The sign and the command are bound together.

This is where the sūrah's treatment of miracles differs from a reader's expectations. The signs are not presented as proof of the messenger's elevation; they are presented as reasons to worship the one who permitted them. Everything a prophet does in the Book points away from himself, and a wonder that pointed toward the wonder-worker would be a different sort of event entirely. The healings and the raising of the dead in the previous verse lead to an imperative about God, and the transition is so natural that the reader may pass over how unusual it is.

**"This Is the Straight Path"**

The speech closes with a description of what it has just said. The Straight Path is the Book's term for the way of God, used in the opening sūrah where the worshipper asks to be guided into it, and used repeatedly for the religion of the prophets.

Applying the phrase to the statement that God alone is Lord makes the doctrine of God the content of the path rather than a preliminary to it. A person who has grasped that God is one and has worship to Him alone is already walking the road, which is why the Book can identify the path with the message rather than describing it as the reward for keeping laws. The verse ends by naming the destination and the road in the same breath, and both of them are the same fact about God.

**What the Declaration Refuses**

Read against the doctrines the sūrah is answering, the sentence excludes two positions at once. It refuses the claim that the messenger is divine, since he names a Lord over himself; and it refuses the claim that the people are free of any lordly claim, since he names that Lord over them too.

The symmetry is exact and it leaves no room for the middle position that a reader might wish for. Nobody in this verse is exempt from the relation; the messenger is included in it as a servant, and the audience is included in it as dependents. What Jesus offers his people is not the sight of a man above them but the identification of a God before them both.

**A Creed Spoken by the Person at Its Centre**

The final observation this verse invites concerns the placement of the words. The declaration about God comes from the mouth of the man whose birth is the sūrah's greatest wonder, and he speaks it after performing works that the Book itself describes as extraordinary.

The sūrah is thus building its case about Jesus out of Jesus' own statements. Whatever a reader of the surrounding narrative concludes about the virgin birth, the miracles, or the raising up of the prophet, the figure at the centre of it has already told him what to conclude: worship God, my Lord and your Lord. The next verse leaves the creed and returns to a moment of crisis in the prophet's mission.
