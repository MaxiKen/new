## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:19

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who is the best witness?” Say, “Allah is! He is a Witness between me and you. And this Quran has been revealed to me so that, with it, I may warn you and whoever it reaches. Do you ˹pagans˺ testify that there are other gods besides Allah?” ˹Then˺ say, “I will never testify ˹to this˺!” ˹And˺ say, “There is only One God. And I totally reject whatever ˹idols˺ you associate with Him.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question About Testimony Instead of Power**

The Makkans had been asking for evidence, and the sūrah now asks them a question about evidence: who is the best witness? The dispute between the Prophet and his people is being treated as a legal matter between two parties, and the verse goes to the largest possible authority, the one whose testimony cannot be doubted because nothing is outside his knowledge. In the preceding verse God is described as All-Aware; here that awareness becomes testimony in a case.

The move is shrewd in a society that ran on witnesses. Contracts, marriages, and blood-money settlements all rested on testimony, and a man's word was a public asset. By framing the argument this way, the verse puts the audience on familiar ground and then names a witness they had not been considering.

**The Witness Between Two Parties**

God is called Witness in several places, and the Book uses the title precisely where a dispute needs closing. The Prophet is told to say that God is sufficient as a witness between him and those who deny (17:96), and the title also appears in the account of the Day when witnesses testifies for every community (22:17; 85:9). The Book never offers this witness as a mere formality.

In this sūrah the parties are the messenger and his own people, standing in one marketplace with incompatible accounts of what is true. The audience had spent years asking the Prophet to produce a witness of their choosing — an angel, a book, a treasure — and the verse answers the demand for a witness by naming one that no party can cross-examine and no faction can bribe. In a city where a purchased testimony could decide a case, the choice of witness is the argument. Neither can produce an ordinary witness who saw revelation arrive, and that is the point. The only being present at the giving of the message, at the intention of the messenger, and at the secret reasoning of the deniers is the One who is now named as the witness between them.

**The Warning That Extends to Whoever It Reaches**

The verse then states why the revelation was given: so that with it the Prophet may warn you and whoever it reaches. The wording places everyone who hears the Book on the same footing as the first audience, which is how the sūrah's message becomes universal rather than a local announcement. Commentators read the phrase as evidence that the Qur'an's warning is addressed to whoever the text arrives at, generation after generation, not only to the Arabs of the Prophet's lifetime.

That universality changes the tone of the verse. If the warning stopped with the Quraysh, the argument about witnesses would be a closed historical dispute. Because it travels, the same question travels with it, and every later hearer is placed in the position of those who heard it first.

**The Turn: Do You Testify to This?**

Having established the witness and the warning, the verse pivots and asks the disbelievers whether they testify that there are other gods besides Allah. It is a carefully chosen question, because it forces the audience to move from practice to claim. Makkans performed rites for their deities; being made to say aloud, as testimony, that these were gods besides God was another matter.

The answer is supplied before they can speak. The Prophet is commanded to declare that he will never testify to it. That refusal is not silence, and it is not neutrality between two accounts. He has been asked to lend his word to the central claim of the society he lives in, and he declines to give it.

**"I Am Quit of What You Associate"**

The verse ends with the formula of dissociation that belongs to the messengers in the Book. Abraham, watching the sun set, tells his people that he totally rejects whatever they associate with Allah (6:78), and Hūd calls on God and the people together to witness that he rejects whatever they associate (11:54). The declaration is not merely that the associates are false; it is that the speaker has separated himself from the practice of associating them.

That separation is the difference between argument and allegiance. A man can debate whether a stone is a god; a man who declares himself quit of the association has told his tribe where he stands in the dispute that governs its life. The verse therefore ends with two statements held together: there is only One God, and the messenger has removed himself from the alternative.

**Why the Verse Replaces a Request With a Witness**

Everything the Makkans demanded — an angel, a written book, signs, a treasure — was a request for something visible. The answer they receive is a witness they cannot see but cannot avoid, and a Book that will serve as that witness in their own language for as long as it is recited. The sūrah does not deny them evidence; it redefines where the decisive evidence lives.

The last words leave the hearer with a choice that is also a testimony. In this verse nobody is asked to produce a miracle. They are asked what they say, and on what authority they would say it, which is a question that follows every reader of the passage out of the text and into his own life.
