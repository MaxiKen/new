## Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah 69:4

> ˹Both˺ Thamûd and ’Ȃd denied the Striking Disaster.

**Expanded Commentary**

**History Arrives Immediately After the Warning**

Dread opens the sūrah, and now evidence marches in. After three verses of escalating awe, verse 4 pivots from the unnamed Hour to named peoples who denied it and paid for that denial. The movement from cosmic threat to dated precedent follows a fixed Qur'anic logic: the future Day gets proven by past days, the greater judgment by lesser ones already visible in ruins. ʿĀd and Thamūd lead the procession because Arabian hearers knew their stories the way later peoples know fallen empires — through tales, landmarks, and traded proverbs. Their placement here also answers the overture's unanswered questions obliquely. What is the Inevitable Hour? Look first at what befell those who mocked the idea, and tremble by analogy. Doctrine will follow in the trumpet scenes; documentation comes first. The sūrah argues like a prosecutor, establishing pattern before announcing sentence.

**Two Tribes the Hearers Knew by Name**

ʿĀd and Thamūd were no abstractions to Makkah. ʿĀd, the mighty builders addressed by the prophet Hūd, had flourished in the southern windswept lands until prosperity rotted into arrogance (7:65-72; 11:50-60). Thamūd, the carvers of mountain homes addressed by Ṣāliḥ, had hamstrung God's she-camel and sealed their doom (7:73-79; 11:61-68). Caravans passing al-Ḥijr could still see Thamūd's hollowed cliffs, and poets invoked ʿĀd's vanished palaces as proverbs for pride laid low. By naming these two first, the verse summons witnesses the audience could almost touch. The order — Thamūd before ʿĀd here, though ʿĀd came earlier in time — likely follows rhetorical weight rather than chronology, placing the nearer ruin first. Familiarity is the point. Judgment stops being theory when its exhibits stand beside the trade road, and the sūrah exploits that geography without needing to describe it.

**What the Striking Disaster Strikes**

The object of denial gets its own dread-name: al-qāriʿah, "the Striking Disaster." The root q-r-ʿ means to strike violently, as one object slams against another, and the noun denotes a calamity that pounds its victims like a hammer. Commentators connect it with the Day that strikes hearts with terror and ears with the trumpet's blast; some link it to the stunning noise of resurrection itself. The same title heads Sūrah 101, where the triple overture mirrors this sūrah's own (101:1-3), confirming that both names — Ḥāqqah and Qāriʿah — circle one Day from different sides. One stresses certainty, the other violence. What ʿĀd and Thamūd denied was therefore not merely their prophets' persons but the very idea of a striking Day. They treated warning as noise and discovered, too late, that the noise was a hammer. Vocabulary here does double work, naming both the eschatological terror and the historical catastrophes that previewed it.

**Denial Meant the Day, Not Only the Prophet**

Precision matters: the verse says the two peoples denied the Qāriʿah, not simply Hūd or Ṣāliḥ. Rejecting a messenger and rejecting the Resurrection intertwine, yet the sūrah names the deeper crime. Messengers can be dismissed as men; the Day claims to be destiny. By locating denial at the level of the Hour, the verse universalizes the warning beyond seventh-century Arabia. Every age produces its own sophisticated reasons for doubting reckoning, and every such doubt re-enacts Thamūd's sneer. The exegetes note this scope when they gloss the verse as admonition for Makkah: the charge against the Quraysh was not ignorance of ruins but repetition of ruin's causes. To deny the Striking Disaster is to declare the moral order unenforced, the universe unpresided, and cruelty costless. ʿĀd's towers and Thamūd's carvings embodied that declaration in stone. Their rubble now embodies the reply.

**Makkah Hears Its Own Story**

Admonition aims at resemblance, and the resemblance was uncomfortable. ʿĀd's prosperity had bred decadence — strength mistaken for self-sufficiency, exactly the disease of Makkah's merchant elite. Thamūd's crime added sacrilege to arrogance: a miracle-beast slaughtered in defiance, mercy answered with a knife. Quraysh hearers prided themselves on custodianship of the sanctuary, yet their persecution of the Prophet replayed both patterns at once. The verse therefore functions as a mirror held up discreetly; it never says "you are they," but the stories it is about to retell make the identification unavoidable. Prophetic preaching often used this indirection, letting history accuse where direct charge would only harden hearts. Ruins persuade the persuadable and condemn the rest. For those with ears, verse 4 already contains the whole sermon: peoples greater than you denied and vanished, so measure your own denial.

**Ruin Displays What Denial Conceals**

Every ruin is an argument, and ʿĀd's desolation argued daily to passing caravans. Stones cannot speak, yet abandoned terraces and hollowed cliffs preached louder than any orator: greatness passed this way and did not save its owners. The sūrah counts on that mute testimony, summoning two peoples whose wreckage its hearers could verify with their own eyes. Denial always conceals consequences, presenting rebellion as costless and consequences as mythical; ruin rips the concealment away. Fallen powers litter history's roadside in every century, and each warns the powerful of the present. Thamūd's carved facades must have seemed eternal to their carvers, as all monuments do. Their emptiness now testifies that eternity belongs to Another. The verse needs no footnote because the desert supplied one, written in collapsed masonry across the trade routes.

**The Verdict Before the Evidence**

Notice the verse states the crime before narrating the punishment. Denial comes first as verdict; the howling wind and the overwhelming blast will follow as exhibits in verses 5-8. This order — charge, then proof — shapes the entire passage through verse 12, where Pharaoh, Noah's flood, and the Ark complete the gallery. Each exhibit will match its people: wind for the towering ʿĀd, a sudden blast for the entrenched Thamūd, water for Pharaoh's hosts. Variety in punishment underscores unity in principle: however the blow falls, it falls. The verse's compactness also serves memory; one line holds two nations and one crime, easy for reciters to carry and hard for hearers to dodge. With the charge recorded, the sūrah now calls its first witness in detail, describing exactly how Thamūd's denial ended.
