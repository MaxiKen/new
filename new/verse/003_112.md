## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:112

> They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go, unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people. They have invited the displeasure of Allah and have been branded with misery for rejecting Allah’s revelations and murdering ˹His˺ prophets unjustly. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Condition Described as Lasting Abasement**

The verse states that the people in view will be struck with humiliation wherever they are found, unless they are protected by a covenant from God or a treaty with people, and it explains the condition by naming their offences. The sentence is about the circumstances in which such a community lives.

The Book describes the same condition in an earlier passage about the Israelites, where they are said to have been struck with disgrace and misery for rejecting God's signs and unjustly killing the prophets (2:61). The wording of the two verses is close, and the repetition shows that the Book treats the state it describes as the working out of a long-standing pattern rather than as an isolated event.

**The Two Means of Protection**

The exception in the sentence names two things that lift the condition: a covenant from God and a treaty with people. The commentators explained the first as the protection that comes from a divine obligation, whether through accepting the religion or through the pledge that a Muslim ruler grants, and the second as an agreement with a community or a state.

Ibn Kathīr read the same phrase in the command to hold firmly to the rope of God as a reference to a pact, which is the sense it carries here. The sentence therefore acknowledges that a people can live in safety among others, and it locates that safety in a formal relationship rather than in strength.

**The Reading That Connects the Verse With the Tax and Protected Status**

Commentators who lived in later centuries connected the abasement with the indemnity paid by non-Muslim communities under Muslim rule and with the protected status that accompanied it, since the Book's instructions about such agreements appear elsewhere.

Al-Ṭabarī took a different view of the same phrase, arguing that the protection cannot be what removes the abasement, because the abasement and the protection are described as alternatives in the sentence itself. The disagreement is about how a legal institution relates to a scriptural description, and the difference is worth noting because it shows the commentators distinguishing between the text's statement and the arrangements later societies built around it.

**The Offences the Verse Names**

The verse gives two reasons for the condition: rejecting God's signs, and killing the prophets without right. Both are acts committed by communities that had received messengers, and the Book attaches them to the history of the Israelites in particular.

The phrase about killing the prophets without justification is the Book's way of marking a crime that has no excuse available. The prophets were sent to the people as their own, and the Book's account of their rejection includes violence in several cases. The accusation here is not addressed to a foreign enemy; it is addressed to the descendants of those who received the message, and the sūrah's argument with them has been conducted on that footing throughout.

**The Closing Word About Disobedience and Transgression**

The verse ends by describing the outcome as a recompense for disobedience and for going beyond limits. The two words describe the failure to do what was required and the decision to exceed what was permitted, so that the description covers both leaving a duty and invading a prohibition.

The Book uses the pair when it wants to state that a people's condition is earned rather than imposed. That is the point of the sentence in this context: the abasement is not a gratuitous humiliation of one community by another but the result of a course of conduct that the Book has been tracing through the passage. The next verse then prevents the judgment from being applied indiscriminately.

**Why the Verse Does Not Authorise Contempt**

The sentence describes what happens to a people whose conduct has been as described, and it does not instruct the believers to humiliate anyone. The distinction is essential, and the sūrah makes it in the verse that follows by stating that the People of the Book are not all alike.

A community reading this passage is being told how to understand the condition of the people around it, and the explanation offered is moral rather than racial. The Book's own statements elsewhere about believers among the People of the Book and about the faithful remnant prevent the description from hardening into a verdict on a group defined by descent, and the sūrah keeps those statements close to this one.

**The Conditions Under Which a Community Lives Among Others**

The verse's practical content concerns the terms on which communities coexist. It says that safety is available through agreement, and it names the agreement as coming either from the obligations of the religion or from a treaty concluded between peoples.

That is the framework the sūrah's later verses assume when they address the believers about their dealings with others. The Book permits the ordering of relations by contract and by covenant, and it treats the resulting security as real. The sentence before the reader states both sides: the condition of a people that has neither, and the two instruments by which the condition is lifted.

**A Judgment With a Qualifier Attached**

The verse reads differently once the next sentence is in view, and the sūrah's arrangement is deliberate: the judgment comes first, and the qualification follows it immediately. A reader who stops at the abasement has read half of the passage.

The Book uses this pattern at several places where it describes a community's failure. It states what the conduct produced, then names the portion that did not share the conduct, and it does so with enough detail for the distinction to be usable. The purpose is to make the judgment a description of a history rather than a licence for contempt, and the passage in this sūrah carries the point through to the verses that describe the upright group in specific terms.
