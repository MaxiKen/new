## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:73

> Or can they benefit or harm you?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham Tests the Idols' Power Over Consequence**

The second question moves from hearing to effect. Can the idols benefit or harm their worshippers? The issue is not whether an image can influence feelings or whether a shrine can organize social life. It is whether the object has power over the goods and dangers that shape human existence. If people fear an idol's anger or hope for its favor, the idol must possess more than symbolic value in their theology.

Benefit and harm form a broad pair. They include protection and danger, provision and loss, healing and affliction, success and failure. Abraham asks whether the idols have any real agency in these matters. The question brings the community's religious claims into ordinary life, where people know that an inert object cannot independently alter the world around it.

**Worship Is Tied to Dependence on Power**

People do not usually worship an object only because it is beautiful. They worship because they believe it can secure something or avert something. Abraham's question identifies that dependence. If the idols cannot benefit or harm, then the fear and hope directed toward them lack a worthy object.

This reasoning also clarifies the difference between respect and worship. A symbol may be honored for what it represents, and a created person may be thanked for help he actually provides. Worship belongs to the One whose power is independent and comprehensive. An idol that cannot affect events cannot be treated as the source of ultimate safety or danger.

**The Pair Benefit and Harm Covers the Whole Moral Life**

The question does not ask whether idols can perform one spectacular act. It asks about benefit and harm in general. The pair covers the direction of life: what helps a person flourish, what brings loss, and what determines final security. A god who has no power in either direction cannot be a god in the meaningful sense.

This breadth prevents the community from escaping through minor examples. It cannot say that the idol does not control everything but perhaps controls one small blessing. If the object is created and dependent, its supposed power remains borrowed or imagined. Abraham is asking for a consistent account of what the idol is capable of, not an anecdote that preserves the shrine.

**The Question Separates Divine Permission From Idolatrous Agency**

A believer can recognize that created things may be means through which benefit or harm occurs. Medicine can help, fire can burn, and people can assist or injure one another. None of these means acts independently of God. Abraham's question concerns the idols' claimed agency as objects of worship. The issue is not whether events have causes, but whether a created image can be treated as an ultimate cause.

This distinction is important because idolatry can hide behind the language of intermediaries. A person may admit that God is supreme while attributing sacred control to objects. Abraham's question asks whether the intermediary has any power of its own. If not, why address it as a lord? The worshipper must decide whether he trusts God's real governance or an imagined power assigned to the image.

**Fear of Harm Can Make Falsehood Feel Practical**

Some people remain devoted to idols not because they can prove their power but because they fear what might happen if they abandon them. Habit, family pressure, and stories of punishment create a practical anxiety. Abraham's question cuts through that anxiety by returning to capacity. Fear is not evidence that the feared object can harm.

A community may feel safer maintaining a ritual, yet felt safety can be produced by conformity. The idol's supposed power is protected by the fear of questioning it. Abraham invites people to ask whether the object has ever acted independently or whether the community is frightening itself into devotion. A false power often survives because people fear leaving it more than they examine it.

**A God Who Cannot Benefit Cannot Receive Ultimate Trust**

Trust in God is not blind dependence on an object. It is reliance on a Lord who creates, sustains, hears, guides, and judges. The idols fail the basic test Abraham gives them. They cannot offer a benefit and cannot impose a harm. Their worshippers are giving away fear and hope without receiving a true relation in return.

This does not mean that every benefit or harm is immediately visible. Divine wisdom may delay an answer or permit a trial. The difference is that God remains the real disposer of affairs even when His wisdom is hidden. An idol has no such agency at all. Abraham's question exposes the gap between trusting a wise Lord and fearing a powerless object.

**The Test Is Simple Enough to Resist Sophistry**

A priest may supply elaborate stories about an idol's hidden role, but the question remains. Can the idol benefit or harm? The issue is plain because the object is plain. The community cannot solve it by adding titles or ceremonies. It must show a real power or admit that devotion has outrun evidence.

Abraham's method is therefore both rational and pastoral. He does not confuse complexity with truth, and he does not humiliate the people before they have answered. He gives them a test that can free them from fear if they are willing to follow it. The next response will show whether they answer the test or retreat to inherited custom. A true power does not need a community to invent capacities for it. If an object needs worshippers to speak on its behalf, its supposed authority is already dependent on the people it claims to govern. Power is not created by assigning a title to what has no power.
