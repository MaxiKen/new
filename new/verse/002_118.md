## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:118

> Those who have no knowledge say, “If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!” The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike. Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Demands**

The verse quotes a sentence from the demanders, and the sentence contains the whole program of spectacular religion: *if only Allah would speak to us, or a sign would come to us!* Two wishes, covering both channels of divine contact — the unmediated address and the visible wonder — and the conditional mood, *if only*, marks the wish's posture: not a question seeking information but a longing performed for an audience. The speakers are identified as *those who have no knowledge*, and the exegetes divide on the reference — the Christians in some readings, the pagan Arabs in others — while agreeing on the description's function: the absence named is not missing information but missing certainty, the organ the verse's final clause will name. *Those who came before* are read with the same spread: the Jews, or the Christians, or the Arabs, or all three together, or — in al-Ṭabarī's widest gloss — anyone for whom the description is true, which converts the verse from report into standing category. The taunt-reading the tradition prefers gives the sentence its edge: this is not sincere inquiry misdirected but mockery in the interrogative mood, the demand designed not to be met but to be repeated. The recital has met the posture at close range already — the questioners of verse 108 warned against asking their Messenger as Moses was asked — and verse 118 files the demand itself, quoted, classified, and answered in four sentences.

**Hearts All Alike**

The verse's central claim is its shortest sentence and its deepest: *their hearts are all alike*. The similarity asserted is not of arguments — the demanders across the generations never coordinated their wording — but of the organ producing the arguments. The recital's heart-taxonomy has been building toward this law: hearts hardened like stone after the signs (74), hearts sheathed and claiming their own sheath (88), hearts made to drink the calf's love (93) — and now the diachronic clause: across communities and centuries, the demanding heart is one heart. The verse's method here is the Qur'an's *tashābuh*-principle — likeness as evidence of common origin: identical speech from separated generations proves identical interior, the way identical handwriting proves a single hand. The claim is empirically checkable in the recital's own files. The Israelites at Sinai asked to see Allah openly and were struck by the thunderbolt for their wrongdoing (4:153's record of the demand: *they demanded what is even greater — "Make Allah visible to us!"*); the Quraysh before the Prophet ﷺ itemized their conditions in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ — a spring gushing from the earth, gardens with rivers beneath, the sky falling in pieces, Allah and the angels brought face to face, a house of gold, ascent into heaven with a readable book carried down (17:90–93). The two catalogs, centuries apart, are the same list: direct address demanded, spectacular sign demanded, belief suspended until delivery. The hearts behind them are, in the verse's flat statement, alike — and the alikeness is the recital's answer to every later generation that believes its skepticism is original.

**The File of Those Before**

The verse's middle sentences function as citation, and the citations deserve their full quotation because the archive is the argument. To Moses, the demand was vision itself: *make Allah visible to us* — and the answer was not a vision but a thunderbolt, and after the thunderbolt, the same generation took the calf (4:153). To the Prophet ﷺ, the demand came as an itemized contract of belief, each clause more extravagant: springs, gardens, falling sky, face-to-face audience with Allah and the angels, gold architecture, celestial ascent with documentary proof — and the recital records the answer given to the whole catalog in one sentence of the Messenger's mouth: *glory be to my Lord! Am I not only a human messenger?* (17:93). The pattern the verse draws from the archive is exact and its moral is doubled. First, the demands were met on their own terms once — Moses's people received direct speech at the mount, the only community in the archive granted the first demand — and the granted demand produced not faith but the calf within forty days, which is the archive's standing proof that direct address does not create the organ that receives it. Second, the demanders never proposed their lists as satisfiable: the Quraysh's final clause — *even then we will not believe* — confesses the list's function, and the verse classifies the confession before it is made. *The same was said by those who came before* is therefore not merely a historical observation but a predictive law: the wording will recur, because the heart recurs; and the heart's recurrence means every generation's demanders are the archive's demanders, speaking its sentences, awaiting its answers.

**Signs Already Clear**

The verse's closing sentence answers the demand by pointing at its satisfied condition: *indeed, We have made the signs clear — for people of sure faith*. The sentence's structure is the answer's whole precision. The signs are not promised; they are declared made — the clearing has occurred, the bayyināt of verse 99 stand delivered, the revelation in hand is itself the sign-cluster the demanders ask to be shown. And the clearing is qualified by its recipients: *for a people of sure faith* — clarity realized in certainty's people. The qualification is not a restriction of the signs but a description of their reception: the same sign is clear to the certain and insufficient to the demander, not because the sign differs but because the organs differ. The verse thereby locates the demand's real object. *If only a sign would come to us* is spoken by people already holding signs, which means the sign demanded is not evidence but spectacle — not something that clears the truth but something that overwhelms the demand to be convinced. The recital's epistemology states the difference as a law: signs address the heart of certainty and are legible there; spectacle addresses the appetite and feeds it without satisfying it, which is why the Quraysh's list ends with *even then we will not believe* and why Sinai's direct-address generation ended at the calf. Sure faith — *yaqīn* — is the verse's named organ of sight, and its absence is what *those who have no knowledge* describes: not ignorance of facts but the unsettled heart that no fact settles, demanding the next sign from the one it has not used.

**The Answer to Both Demands**

Read as a pair, the two demands receive a paired answer inside the recital's own record, and the pairing completes the verse's case. *If only Allah would speak to us* — He spoke: at Sinai, to Moses directly, an event the sūrah itself files and the demanding community's own ancestors attended; and the speaking produced, in the archive's plainest datum, the demand for a visible god within the speaker's absence. *If only a sign would come to us* — signs came: the staff, the hand, the sea, the springs, the provision from above, and in the final register the revelation itself, the bayyināt made clear. Both channels were opened; both audiences failed at the receiving end, which is where the verse locates the problem: not in the silence of heaven but in the likeness of hearts. The demand, on this diagnosis, is not a request awaiting fulfillment but a posture avoiding fulfillment — and the posture's comfort is that an unsent sign can always be awaited, while a sent sign must be answered. The recital leaves the verse's audience with the audit running inward: every *if only* the heart forms — if only guidance were clearer, if only a miracle confirmed my choice, if only Allah spoke to me directly — is the archived sentence, spoken by the alike heart, and the answer is the same: the signs are made clear, the speech has descended, and the only missing term in every demand is the certainty the demanders refuse to begin with.
