## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:118

> Those who have no knowledge say, “If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!” The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike. Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand Reported**

The verse reports a request made of God rather than of the Prophet ﷺ, and the request concerns direct speech and a visible sign. The sūrah has already dealt with demands for proofs in the earlier passage, and this sentence gives the subject a sharper form by quoting the demand itself.

The Book's response does not consist in refusing the request on grounds of policy; it places the demand in a lineage of similar demands made by earlier peoples. What the speakers took to be an original objection is shown to be an old one, and the sentence states that the hearts producing it are of the same kind. The Book's account of the demand also reveals what the speakers believed about the messenger. A request for God to address them directly treats the messenger as an intermediary to be bypassed, and the sūrah's answer reasserts the office he holds. The same presumption appears in the requests reported of earlier communities, where a prophet was asked to produce what only God could supply.

**Who the Speakers Were**

Commentators identified those who have no knowledge in more than one way. Some took them to be Christians, others the pagan Arabs, and a few read the phrase as applying to anyone for whom the description is true, whatever his community. The people referred to as those who came before were identified correspondingly, as the Jews, the Christians, or the Arabs, and some commentators noted that the criterion matters more than the label.

The Book's phrase about the absence of knowledge is therefore not a statement about literacy or education. It describes a disposition that produces demands, and the sūrah applies it to whoever makes those demands, whatever the community he belongs to.

**A Taunt Rather Than a Question**

Several commentators understood the request as an expression of contempt rather than of genuine inquiry. The demand that God speak to a group directly, or that a sign descend among them, presumes that the message already delivered was insufficient, and the Book treats that presumption as the real subject of the sentence.

The Qur'an records a similar series of challenges elsewhere, where the disbelievers proposed conditions for belief involving springs, gardens, gold, and ascents into the sky. The Book's answer to that kind of proposal is not a counter-offer; it states that the messenger is a warner and that the signs are what God provides, not what the audience auctions for.

**"Their Hearts Are All Alike"**

The sentence joins the present speakers to their predecessors by describing their hearts as alike, and the comparison is exact: the same demand has appeared wherever a community has decided in advance what would satisfy it. The Book's phrase does not say the two groups shared a doctrine, only that they shared the disposition that produced the demand.

This diagnosis recurs in the sūrah, which has described people whose hearts hardened after receiving signs and people whose request for proofs was described as a step away from belief. What the Book consistently identifies as the obstacle is not the quantity of evidence but the state of the one evaluating it. The comparison also prepares the reader for the sūrah's later material about the same community's heart, which was described as unreceptive. The Book's account of unbelief in this passage consistently traces conduct to a condition that precedes it. The demand is the symptom, and the sentence identifies its source without offering an explanation of how the condition arose.

**The Signs Already Made Clear**

The middle of the sentence states that the signs have been made clear, and the phrase carries the force of a completed provision. The sūrah has narrated the sea, the mountain, the springs, the manna, the raising of the dead, and the revelation itself, and the statement here sums them up as sufficient.

The Book's position is therefore not that no signs were given but that the signs supplied were the ones God chose, and that the demand for different ones is a claim to authority over the terms of revelation. When the Qur'an states elsewhere that its verses are clear signs, it is making the same argument in a positive form.

**People of Sure Faith**

The sentence ends with a description of those who profit from the clear signs: people of certain conviction. The Arabic term for certainty describes knowledge that has settled rather than knowledge that is still being tested, and the Book uses it for the condition of the believers who act on what they know.

The phrase implies its opposite as well, since the speakers in the first half of the verse are described as lacking knowledge, and their demand is the expression of that lack. The contrast is between a community that receives what has been given and asks for understanding, and a community that proposes conditions. The sūrah's later verses describe the same two groups in other terms, and the passage here supplies the criterion the Book applies throughout.

**What the Verse Teaches About Sign-Seeking**

Read with the surrounding passages, the sentence establishes that the demand for miraculous verification is not a neutral request. The Book treats it as evidence of a settled position, and it answers it by pointing to what has already been provided. The verses that follow address the outlook of those who make the demand: they will never be satisfied with anything short of religious submission on their own terms, and the messenger is not responsible for their decision. The Book's treatment of the demand also protects the messenger from being turned into a performer. If signs were produced at the request of audiences, the message would become a spectacle and acceptance would become a transaction, and the Book consistently refuses that arrangement. What remains for the hearer is the revelation already given and the responsibility to consider it.

**Signs and Their Recipients**

The Book's discussions of signs usually turn on the recipient rather than on the sign, and this verse is a clear example. The demand recorded here could have been met in principle, since the Book describes God speaking to Moses and sending down signs for earlier peoples, and yet the sentence treats the request as evidence of a settled condition. What the Qur'an provides is not a shortage of evidence but an account of why evidence alone does not settle belief. The sūrah has recorded people who saw the sea divided and then worshiped a calf, and it will describe people who recognise a book and then reject it, and in each case the problem lies with the receiving heart. The verse's closing phrase about people of sure faith states the alternative: a settled conviction that acts on what it receives, and the Book's signs are said to be clear for that kind of reader.
