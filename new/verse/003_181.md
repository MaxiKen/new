## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:181

> Indeed, Allah has heard those ˹among the Jews˺ who said, “Allah is poor; we are rich!” We have certainly recorded their slurs and their killing of prophets unjustly. Then We will say, “Taste the torment of burning!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Saying That Was Heard**

The verse opens with the declaration that God has heard what a group said about Him, and it reports the taunt itself: that God is poor while they are rich. The announcement that the words were heard is the first move in the sentence, and it places the speakers in a position they had not considered.

The Book records many statements of its opponents, and it frequently introduces them with a notice that the words have been registered. The formula is a reminder that the speech of human beings about God is not private, and the verse uses it before it states what the words will bring.

**What Prompted the Taunt**

The commentators explain what occasioned the remark. The Book had told the believers that whoever lends to God a goodly loan will have it multiplied, and the same invitation appears in another sūrah as a question addressed to those who might give. Some of the group seized on the wording and turned it into a mockery, saying that the invitation showed that God was in need and they were wealthy.

The sūrah about the Table reports a similar taunt from the same quarter, in which a group among the People of the Book said that God's hand was tied. The Book's answer in that passage is a statement of His generosity, and the verse before the reader answers the taunt in a different way.

**The Intention Behind the Words**

Al-Qurṭūbī records that the group did not believe what they said. Their purpose was to stir up unease among the weaker members of the community, who might be disturbed by ridicule aimed at the revelation.

The explanation fits the pattern of the sūrah's earlier passages about the community's opponents, whose object was to shake the believers rather than to win an argument. The Book elsewhere states the relation between the two groups plainly, telling the believers that God is the rich one while they are the poor, and the verse before the reader supplies the answer to those who turned that statement into a joke.

**The Record Kept of What They Said**

The verse states that God will record what the group said, and the Book describes the record elsewhere as a register that will be produced for each person on the Day of Judgment. The sūrah of the Night Journey describes a record that will be brought out and read, and other passages describe books given to people in their right hands or behind their backs.

The sentence places the mocking words in that register alongside the deeds the same people had committed. The verse joins the two in a single clause, and the joining is the answer to anyone who supposes that speech about God is a lesser matter than conduct.

**The Slaying of the Prophets**

The verse adds the killing of the prophets to what will be recorded, and the phrase about killing without right appears in the sūrah's earlier description of those who deny God's signs. One passage describes the people who were arrogant enough to reject some messengers and kill others, and another describes a community that denied a messenger whenever he brought what they did not want.

The acts referred to belong to the history of the earlier community, and the verse includes them in the reckoning of the people who were speaking in the present. The Book treats the two as connected, and the connection is explained in the comment tradition.

**Approval as a Form of Participation**

Al-Qurṭūbī explains the mention of the earlier killings by observing that agreement with an act is treated as a share in it. The group in question had not committed the killings themselves, and the verse's inclusion of the deed in their record indicates that their approval of what their ancestors did had put them in the same position.

The reasoning is supported by a report in the tradition about the responsibility of those who witness an evil and those who are absent from it but approve of it. The principle is severe, and the verse applies it to a case in which the approval was of the killing of messengers.

**The Punishment Named**

The sentence closes with a command addressed to the group on the Day of Judgment: taste the punishment of the burning. The Book uses the imperative form in its descriptions of the treatment of those who denied, and the command is spoken by the one whose words they had mocked.

The word for the punishment describes the fire's effect, and the sūrah had used the same vocabulary in its warnings about the destination of those who reject the truth. The verse leaves the group with the outcome stated in their own language, since the taunt they had made concerned a matter of wealth and the answer concerns what they will carry away from everything else.


The verse gives the community a lesson about the treatment of revelation by people who have no intention of accepting it. The mocking words of a group that did not believe what it said are recorded as though they had been meant, and the verse teaches the believers to take such speech seriously.

The community at Madinah had been subjected to a steady stream of taunts about its message and its poverty. The sentence assures it that the taunts were heard and would be answered, and the next verse adds the reason for the treatment that has been announced.
