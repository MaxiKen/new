## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:161

> taking interest despite its prohibition, and consuming people’s wealth unjustly. We have prepared for the disbelievers among them a painful punishment.

### Expanded Commentary

**Two economic violations**

The verse continues the list of wrongdoing by naming interest despite prohibition and consuming people’s wealth unjustly. These are not merely technical financial errors. Interest in the Qur’anic sense can turn need into an instrument of compounding extraction, while unjust consumption removes property through fraud, bribery, seizure, and abuse of trust.

The prohibition of *ribā* belongs beside the chapter’s inheritance and orphan laws: wealth must not flow upward simply because one party has more leverage. Islamic jurists distinguish prohibited increase from legitimate trade, investment, rent, and profit-bearing risk, and the exact application of modern instruments requires careful expertise. The moral principle is clear even when a product’s legal classification is complex: do not guarantee gain by transferring all risk to the vulnerable.

The painful punishment is for those who persist in disbelief and wrongdoing. It should motivate reform of markets, not merely fear of a private penalty. A just economy records debt honestly, provides relief to the distressed, and refuses to make poverty a source of endless profit.

**Finance and moral asymmetry**

A profitable contract can still be unjust when one party has no realistic ability to refuse or recover. The prohibition of interest and wrongful consumption asks financial institutions to examine who bears risk, who receives relief, and whether the debt grows while the person’s capacity shrinks. Ethical finance is measured by human consequences, not elegant paperwork.
