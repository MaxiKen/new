## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:70

> Have they not received the stories of those ˹destroyed˺ before them: the people of Noah, ’Âd, and Thamûd, the people of Abraham, the residents of Midian, and the overturned cities ˹of Lot˺? Their messengers came to them with clear proofs. Allah would have never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves.

**Expanded Commentary**

**History Is a Moral Witness**

The verse asks whether the hypocrites have received the accounts of earlier peoples. These stories are not offered as decoration or as a list of ancient names. They are evidence that communities can possess strength, culture, and time yet still destroy themselves by refusing clear guidance. History becomes a witness against the claim that consequences are too distant to matter.

The named peoples include Noah's community, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Abraham's people, Midian, and the overturned cities commonly associated with Lot. The range of stories prevents the listener from reducing judgment to one kind of sin or one region. Different societies received messengers, faced different tests, and shared the choice to reject clear proof. Their variety makes the warning harder to dismiss as someone else's unique problem.

**Clear Proof Makes Responsibility Concrete**

The messengers brought clear proofs. The communities were not destroyed because Allah wanted to trap them in ignorance. They received signs, instruction, and a chance to respond. A clear proof does not force every person to believe, but it removes the excuse that no guidance was available.

This principle applies to the believer's life. He should seek knowledge and ask for evidence, but when the truth becomes clear he cannot remain permanently neutral. Understanding creates responsibility. The Qur'an also teaches that Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity, so clarity must be considered alongside ability and circumstance. The verse condemns people who had the means to recognize and obey, then chose injustice instead.

**The Overturned Cities Carry a Double Image**

The expression “overturned cities” points to a destruction that changed the order of a place. Some interpreters connect it specifically with the cities of Lot; others see the phrase as broader. Both readings preserve the central lesson: a society can be turned from apparent stability to ruin when its moral corruption is defended as normal.

The image also warns against trusting permanence. A city appears solid because houses, roads, and institutions remain in place. Yet social order depends on choices that can be reversed. When injustice becomes a custom and warnings are mocked, the outside may still look strong while the inside is collapsing. The believer asks not only whether a society functions, but whether it functions justly.

**Allah Does Not Wrong the Wrongdoer**

The closing principle is explicit: Allah did not wrong them; they wronged themselves. This rejects the complaint that punishment is arbitrary. Allah sent messengers, presented proof, and judged according to what people chose. Their destruction was not an injustice inflicted on innocent communities.

The statement also describes sin as self-harm. A person may imagine that disobedience injures only another person or violates only an external rule. In reality, the wrong reshapes his own heart, relationships, and future. Allah's command protects the servant, so rejecting it damages the one who rejects it even when he thinks he is gaining freedom.

**Receiving Stories Is Not the Same as Learning**

The hypocrites may have heard these stories many times, yet the question asks whether the accounts have truly reached them. Information reaches the ears without reaching the conscience. A person can recite the names of destroyed peoples and still imitate their pride, mockery, and refusal of guidance.

Learning occurs when history changes a choice. The believer asks which habit in the story resembles his own, which warning he has been postponing, and what repair must begin now. He does not use the destruction of earlier people to feel superior. He uses it to fear becoming another example for those who come after him.

**Mercy and Warning Stand Together**

The messengers came with proof before judgment. This sequence shows mercy. Allah gave the people a chance to recognize truth, and the warning gave them a chance to change. Mercy does not mean withholding every consequence forever. It means that the consequence is preceded by guidance and that the door of repentance remains open before the final end.

A believer should carry this balance into teaching and reform. He explains clearly, warns honestly, and leaves room for return. He does not assume that a person's past makes change impossible. He also does not call endless delay mercy when delay is only allowing harm to spread. The stories become guidance when they produce humility, justice, and a prompt return to Allah.

The list of peoples also teaches that no community is protected by ancestry. Being connected to a prophet, a land, or a respected history does not replace present obedience. Each generation receives the record and must answer for its own response. The stories are not tools for boasting that one's group is better than the destroyed. They are warnings that clear proof can be resisted by people who still possess wealth, intelligence, and social confidence. The believer receives the account with humility: Allah's justice is perfect, the evidence is real, and the choice to continue in wrong belongs to the person who makes it.

A clear proof also creates a duty to teach with clarity. The believer should not hide behind complex language when the moral issue is plain, and he should not turn a complex issue into a false certainty merely to win an argument. The messengers brought proof suited to their people, and the people were still responsible for how they received it. Modern readers receive the accounts through scripture and reflection; they should allow those accounts to challenge inherited pride. Allah's justice means that no person is judged by another person's response. Each soul receives warning, ability, and the chance to return.
