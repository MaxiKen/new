## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:28

> O believers! Indeed, the polytheists are ˹spiritually˺ impure, so they should not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year. If you fear poverty, Allah will enrich you out of His bounty, if He wills. Surely, Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Impurity Here Is a Religious Description**

The verse calls the polytheists spiritually impure and then gives a practical ruling concerning the Sacred Mosque. The wording does not simply describe dirt on the body. Classical jurists discussed whether najasah here means a ritual condition, the pollution of polytheistic belief, or a status that requires distance from the sanctuary. The translation's addition of spiritually captures the main point: the verse is addressing the contradiction between the worship of many gods and a sanctuary dedicated to Allah's oneness.

This distinction matters because the verse has been used to make claims about the physical worth of every non-Muslim person. Its immediate concern is not that human bodies become untouchable. The context is the end of pagan pilgrimage practices at the Sacred Mosque. A person is not reduced to filth as a human being. The religious system of shirk is described as impure in relation to the house whose purpose is tawḥīd.

**The Sanctuary Has a Defined Religious Identity**

The Sacred Mosque was not merely a public marketplace with a religious corner. It was the central sanctuary of the worship of Allah. Pagan rites had continued around it even after the political balance changed. The verse establishes that the sanctuary's public function must now match its theological foundation. The prohibition concerns approach to that sacred center after the announced year, not a command to mistreat every polytheist encountered elsewhere.

Jurists differed over the geographical scope. Some restricted the ruling to the Sacred Mosque and the boundaries of the ḥaram around it. Others extended restrictions to mosques generally, while acknowledging disagreement. The most immediate reading follows the verse's own named place. It is safer to keep the text's focus than to turn a specific sanctuary rule into a general license for hostility toward non-Muslims in ordinary life.

**The End of Pagan Pilgrimage Changes the Economy**

The verse anticipates a practical fear: if polytheists no longer approach the sanctuary, will Makkah lose income? Pilgrims brought trade, gifts, and commercial activity. The believers may have wondered whether religious purification would damage the city's material life. Allah answers that He can enrich them from His bounty. The promise does not deny the possibility of a short-term economic loss. It denies that obedience leaves Allah unable to provide.

This is a recurring Qur'anic test. A community may be asked to abandon a profitable practice because the practice is tied to false worship or injustice. The immediate ledger shows a loss, while faith asks the believers to trust a larger provision. The verse does not instruct them to neglect planning. It asks them not to treat forbidden income as irreplaceable. A market built on compromising worship is not the only market Allah can sustain.

**Purification Is Not Contempt**

The believer must hold the ruling without adopting contempt for people. The Qur'an commands justice and kindness toward those who do not fight the Muslims (60:8). Verse 6 of this chapter protects an idolater who asks to hear the Word and requires safe escort. These commands stand beside verse 28. The sanctuary can be protected from a religious practice while the person outside it is treated with fairness.

That distinction is especially important in religious communities. Sacred boundaries are not a reason to insult visitors, deny them food, or violate their safety. A Muslim who enforces a mosque's religious rules while behaving unjustly has preserved the outer line and broken the ethical purpose. Spiritual purity includes truthful conduct toward the person who does not share the faith.

**Allah Knows the Need and the Remedy**

The verse ends with Allah's knowledge and wisdom. His knowledge includes the material fears of the believers, the religious meaning of the sanctuary, and the consequences of changing a long-standing custom. His wisdom joins the ruling to the promise of provision. The community is not asked to choose between worship and survival as though Allah were absent from the economy.

These names also restrain human application. A ruler may not invent restrictions in the name of purity without knowledge of the law and wisdom about its purpose. A person may not use the verse to justify social exclusion beyond the sanctuary's ruling. Allah knows the scope of the command, and human beings must not claim more than He has stated. Wisdom prevents zeal from becoming disorder.

**When Revenue Depends on Compromise**

Modern believers can apply the provision clause to businesses, institutions, and personal income. A source of money may look essential because a household, charity, or city has built its plans around it. If the income requires idolatry, fraud, exploitation, or another clear wrong, the verse supplies courage to leave it. The loss may be real, and responsible planning may be necessary, but dependence on forbidden gain is not security.

The promise of enrichment is not a guarantee that every person becomes wealthy immediately. It is a guarantee that Allah is not made poor by obedience. Provision can come through lawful work, community support, unexpected opportunity, or contentment that changes what the person needs. The verse's final word is wisdom. Trusting Allah does not mean refusing means; it means refusing to make a forbidden means into a god. A lawful alternative may begin small, but it carries a dignity that unlawful abundance cannot provide. Provision is not measured only by quantity; it is also measured by blessing, safety, and freedom from shame. A smaller lawful income can carry a household farther than a larger income that trains it to ignore Allah. The believer asks not only how much enters, but what the wealth is doing to worship.
