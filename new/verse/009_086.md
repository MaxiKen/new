## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:86

> Whenever a sûrah is revealed stating, “Believe in Allah and struggle along with His Messenger,” the rich among them would ask to be exempt, saying, “Leave us with those who remain behind.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Reaches the Able First**

When revelation calls people to belief and struggle, the affluent among the hypocrites ask to be left behind. Their wealth matters because they possess the means to prepare and participate. The request is not the plea of people whose bodies or resources make the task impossible. It is the refusal of those who have ability but do not want the cost.

This does not make wealth a cause of guilt. Wealth can equip a person to serve, support dependents, and relieve hardship. The verse shows how wealth can become an excuse when it creates a sense of entitlement to comfort. The affluent expect their resources to protect them from the duties that those resources make possible. Ability increases responsibility when Allah places a serious task before the servant.

**Revelation Interrupts Comfortable Religion**

The revealed command does not allow them to keep a faith made only of words. Belief in Allah is joined to action with the Messenger. A person may prefer a religious identity that costs nothing, but revelation repeatedly connects conviction to sacrifice, prayer, justice, and care for people.

This remains a test for every believer. He may gladly affirm the parts of religion that comfort him while resisting the parts that claim his time, money, or status. The command exposes selective faith. A servant should ask not only what he believes in theory but what obedience has become costly enough to prove.

**The Request to Stay Behind Is a Moral Choice**

“Leave us with those who remain behind” (9:86) sounds modest, but it contains a decision. They do not ask how they can support the mission from another place. They ask to be placed among the people whose absence has already become a sign of refusal. The location they seek carries a meaning, and they choose that meaning willingly.

A lawful exemption can be accepted without shame when Allah grants it. The issue here is the desire to take the exempt position while being able to serve. The believer therefore distinguishes between asking what is permitted and asking permission to ignore a duty. The first can be obedience; the second can be a decorated refusal.

**Affluence Can Hide Dependence on Comfort**

The rich may imagine that they are independent because they can purchase safety, transport, and influence. The verse reveals a deeper dependence: they are unable to surrender the comfort their wealth has made familiar. Possession has trained them to interpret inconvenience as an unacceptable threat.

The cure is not to despise wealth. It is to practice spending and service before crisis arrives. A person who has never used wealth for Allah will find every sacrifice shocking. A person who regularly gives, prepares, and supports others has already learned that property is a trust. Affluence becomes useful when it makes obedience easier rather than making disobedience more comfortable.

**A Community Should Not Let Wealth Buy Exemption**

Leaders must also be careful not to excuse the wealthy because their money is useful. Resources matter, but money cannot purchase immunity from every duty. If a person is physically able and the task requires his presence, a donation may not replace the required action. In another situation, money may be the person's best contribution. The category must be decided by the actual command and the person's ability.

Fair leadership applies one principle without ignoring different capacities. It does not force the poor to give what they do not have, and it does not let the rich hide behind what they do have. Trust grows when people see that status does not rewrite obligation.

**The Heart Is Tested by Its First Response**

The first response to revelation reveals much. A believer may feel fear, then ask Allah for courage and prepare. A hypocrite hears the command and immediately searches for a place among those excused. Feelings can be overcome, but the chosen response shows which feeling is being obeyed.

The servant should watch his first excuse without assuming it is his final identity. He can name the fear, seek counsel, and take one concrete step. Allah does not ask him to pretend that sacrifice is easy. He asks him not to let wealth, comfort, or status decide whether revelation will be obeyed.

The phrase about staying behind also reveals how people use group identity. They want to be counted among those who are exempt, even though their wealth places them among those who can contribute. A person may seek the protection of a category without accepting the truth of the category. This is unfair to the genuinely unable, whose exemption was established for mercy rather than convenience. A sound community asks what each person can do and does not let status rewrite the answer. The rich can fund transport, care for families, equip the work, or participate directly. They cannot assume that money always buys a substitute, nor can they assume that money always creates the same duty. Allah's command and the person's actual capacity decide.

The affluent person's first response should therefore be practical self-examination. What can he provide? What can he personally endure? Which dependents need care, and which comforts are merely habits? He may discover that his best contribution is equipment, another's is travel, and a third person's is skilled leadership. The verse does not flatten these differences. It condemns the person who uses difference as a pretext for doing nothing. If Allah gave him wealth and strength, he should expect those gifts to appear in service. A request for exemption can be truthful, but it cannot be the automatic response of everyone who values comfort more than the command.
