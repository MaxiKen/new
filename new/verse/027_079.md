## Sūrah an-Naml 27:79

> So put your trust in Allah, for you are surely upon the ˹Path of˺ clear truth.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instruction That Follows a Deferral**

The previous verse placed the verdict in the Hereafter. This one says what to do in the meantime: *tawakkal ʿalā Allāh* — rely on God. The connective *fa* at the start makes it a conclusion drawn from what preceded.

The logic is worth tracing. If the judgment is not yours to deliver, and the timing is not yours to know, and the outcome is not yours to secure, then the appropriate posture is reliance. Trust here is not a mood cultivated for its own sake; it is what remains once the things outside a person's control have been correctly identified. The Qur'an commands *tawakkul* in many places, and it is almost always attached to a situation in which the addressee has done what he can and cannot do more.

**Trust Is Not Passivity**

The word could be misread as an instruction to stop working, and the chapter itself blocks that reading. Everything an-Naml admires involves action. Sulaymān verified a report, wrote a letter, and altered a throne. The queen consulted, tested and travelled. The hoopoe flew two thousand kilometres and came back with evidence.

None of that is quietism, and none of it is presented as a failure of trust. What the Qur'an pairs *tawakkul* with elsewhere makes the balance explicit: the Prophet is told to consult his companions on the matter and then, having resolved, to rely on God. Consultation first, decision second, reliance third — in that order. Reliance is what a person does about the part of the outcome that was never his, and it becomes an excuse only when it is applied to the part that was.

**"You Are Upon Clear Truth"**

The second half gives the ground for the first. *Innaka ʿalā al-ḥaqq al-mubīn* — you are upon the manifest truth. The preposition is spatial: he is *on* it, standing on it, as one stands on solid ground.

The commentators note that the phrase can also be rendered as following it or being based upon it, and all three senses share the image of support from below. That is the reason reliance is possible. A man is told to trust not because trusting is a virtue in itself but because what he is standing on will hold. The adjective is the chapter's own signature word, *mubīn*, clear — the same one applied to the Book in verse 1, to the sorcery charge in verse 13, to the favour Sulaymān named in verse 16, and to the record of hidden things in verse 75. Each time it marks something unambiguous.

**Consolation Addressed to a Specific Pressure**

This verse belongs with verse 70, where the Prophet was told not to grieve and not to feel constricted by plots. The two together form the chapter's treatment of his position, and they work differently.

Verse 70 named the feelings and set a limit on them. This one supplies what replaces them. A man told only to stop grieving has been given a prohibition; a man told to rely on God because he stands on clear truth has been given something to do instead. The order is also right. The chapter first records the distress, then argues at length — six questions, a command to travel and look, a statement about the record of hidden things — and only then instructs him to trust. The reliance is placed after the evidence rather than in place of it.

**What He Is Not Told**

Notice what the instruction omits. He is not promised success, not told his people will believe, not given a timeline, and not assured of protection. The chapter has already withheld all of that.

What he is given is an assessment of his position — you are on clear truth — and a direction for his confidence. That is a narrower consolation than a promise of victory and a more durable one, because it does not depend on how the following years turn out. The next verses will state plainly that some of the people he is addressing cannot be reached at all, comparing them to the dead and the deaf. Being told to rely on God immediately before being told that his audience includes people beyond persuasion is the sequence the chapter has chosen, and the two verses are meant to be held together.

**Reliance Recommended Across the Book**

The command appears in many other chapters and usually in the same position: after a difficulty has been described and before it has been resolved. Yaʿqūb sends his sons to Egypt with precautions and then says the decision rests with God. The believers under threat are told that God suffices them. Mūsā tells his people to rely if they have submitted.

The pattern in every case is the same three-part structure — a real danger, an action taken, and reliance for the remainder. None of these passages uses *tawakkul* to replace the action, and several of them describe the precaution in some detail before naming the trust. That consistency is the best available guide to what the word means in the Qur'an. It is the disposition a person holds toward the part of the outcome that was never within his reach, and the Book is careful to show the reach being used first.

**The Root of the Word**

*Tawakkul* comes from *w-k-l*, to appoint an agent or to entrust a matter to someone else. The related noun *wakīl* means a representative who acts on another's behalf, and it is used in ordinary Arabic for a legal proxy.

That commercial and legal background sharpens the sense. A man who appoints an agent has not stopped caring about the outcome; he has transferred the handling of it to someone better placed, and the transfer is deliberate and specific. What the Qur'an asks for is that structure applied to the matters no human agent could take on — the timing of the Hour, the response of a hostile city, the verdict on ancient disputes. The Prophet is being told to appoint the one party who actually has jurisdiction over those things, which is the same division of responsibility the previous verse established.
