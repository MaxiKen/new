## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:7

> But when the sight is stunned,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Begins With the Eye**

The sūrah's reply to the scoffer starts in an unexpected place: not with the earth splitting or the Trumpet sounding, but with a faculty failing. *Baṣar* is sight, and more narrowly the act of looking with comprehension. On that Day, the first thing to go is the organ by which a human being normally takes hold of his surroundings. The choice is deliberate. Throughout the Qur'an, seeing is the master-metaphor for certainty — a thing is known when it is seen, and the person who will not accept a truth is described as one who does not see. So the collapse of sight at the opening of the Hour is not one catastrophe among many. It is the undoing of the instrument the denier had been relying on to judge what was real.

**Dazzled, or Confounded**

The verb *bariqa* carries two related senses, and the commentators divided between them. The first is being dazzled, struck by a flash so that the eye is blinded and cannot function; the root belongs to *barq*, lightning, and the picture is of an organ overwhelmed by light. The second is being confounded — the eye open and moving but unable to settle on anything, because terror will not let it rest. Ibn Kathīr took the verse in the sense that the disbelievers, at the terror of that Day, cannot fix their gaze on anything at all; al-Jalālayn read it as amazement at seeing the things they used to deny. The two are not exclusive. A dazzled eye and a panicked eye produce the same symptom from different causes, and the verse may be taken to hold both: light too great to bear, and a scene too frightening to take in.

**The Stare Recorded Elsewhere**

The same fixed gaze is described in several passages, and the details agree. In Ibrāhīm the guilty come forward *“rushing forth, heads raised, never blinking, hearts void.”* (14:43). In al-Qamar, *“With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts, rushing towards the caller. The disbelievers will cry, ‘This is a difficult Day!’”* (54:7–8). In al-Anbiyāʾ the disbelievers are portrayed as *“staring ˹in horror, crying,˺ ‘Oh, woe to us! We have truly been heedless of this.’”* (21:97). The consistency of the detail is instructive. These are not people looking for a way out or scanning the horizon for help; sight has stopped being a tool of action and has become pure reception. What they refused to look at in life is now the only thing in front of them.

**Why the Eye Fails First**

There is a logic in the sequence. The Qur'an's complaint against the deniers was never that they lacked evidence but that they had eyes and did not use them: *“They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with.”* (7:179). The faculty was intact; its employment was refused. The Hour accordingly begins by disabling the instrument that was misused, and the reversal is exact. Sight that would not read the signs is left with nothing to read but the thing it denied, and this time it cannot look away. The punishment fits the fault without any need for a separate machinery of retribution: the organ that was made for recognising the truth is simply handed the truth at a moment when recognition no longer helps.

**A List Without Connectors**

The sūrah strings these signs together with almost no connectors. The clauses fall one after another with the barest of connectors, giving the passage the quality of a list delivered quickly rather than a narrative unfolding in order. The effect is compression: the events are piled rather than sequenced, and the reader is left with the impression of a world coming apart faster than it can be described. Other sūrahs use the same device for the same subject — *“When the sky splits open, and when the stars fall away, and when the seas burst forth, and when the graves spill out.”* (82:1–4). The rhetorical point of the piling is that the distinction between first and next has stopped mattering. For the person living through it, catastrophe is not a series; it is a single event with many edges.

**The Collapse Comes Before the Answer**

The scoffer had asked when. The sūrah answers by describing the state of the man who asked, which is a way of saying that the question has been overtaken by its subject. By the time the date is known, the knowing is no longer information but experience, and the eyes that demanded proof have been rendered incapable of examining anything. The same principle governs what happens to the other faculties on that Day. Hearing, speech and the power to argue are not removed; they are left intact and turned against their owner, because a person who would not use an instrument for its purpose must eventually meet the truth the instrument was built to receive. The Qur'an describes mouths sealed while hands and feet speak (36:65), and ears, eyes and skin testifying to what was done through them (41:20). Sight is handled in exactly this way: not destroyed but overloaded, handed at last the full object it spent a life avoiding, and left without the capacity to process what it receives. Nothing in the description is cruelty for its own sake. The correspondence between the fault and its consequence is the argument, and the sūrah will go on from here to the moon, the sun, and the cry for escape. It begins with the eye because the eye is where the refusal began.
