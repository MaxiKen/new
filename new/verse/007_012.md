## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:12

> Allah asked, “What prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you?” He replied, “I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put to Iblīs**

The verse reports the question put to Iblīs about what prevented him from prostrating when he was commanded, and the question exposes the gap between the command and the answer. The Book puts the same question in its other accounts of the scene, and the sūrah of Ṣād records the words that God would ask him whether he was too proud or had become one of the exalted (38:75). The question in front of us is not an inquiry after information; it is the setting out of the charge.

The account's arrangement places the command first and the explanation second. The Book does not present Iblīs as ignorant of what was required of him, and his answer confirms that he understood the command exactly. What the verse establishes is that the refusal was deliberate, and that the reasoning behind it is offered as an excuse.

**I Am Better Than Him**

The answer Iblīs gives is a claim of superiority, and the Book's accounts of the scene present it as the source of the refusal. He measures himself against Adam by the substance from which each was created, and he draws the conclusion that the command does not apply to him. The Book's ethical teaching treats such self-measurement as a form of pride, and the sūrah states in the next verse that the Garden is no place for arrogance.

The claim is stated without any reference to the command that preceded it. The reasoning runs from his own assessment of the two natures to a conclusion about his obligation, and the Book's account gives the reader the anatomy of an excuse that many later people repeat. The sūrah's warnings about following the customs of the ancestors and about the protectors taken in place of God are versions of the same operation.

**Fire and Clay**

The verse states the comparison on which the claim rests: Iblīs was created from fire and Adam from clay. The Book's account of the creation of the jinn from smokeless fire appears in the sūrah of the Merciful (55:15), and the accounts of Adam's creation from clay, dust, and sounding mud appear in several sūrahs. The materials are named by Iblīs himself, and the tradition of commentary develops the contrast between them.

The qualities the commentators attach to the two substances turn Iblīs's argument against him. Fire is described as luminous and subtle but also as restless, fickle, and destructive; clay is described as low and heavy but also as patient, stable, and humble. The sūrah's narrative shows which of the two qualities leads to repentance: Adam admits his wrong and asks forgiveness, while Iblīs argues about his own merit. The verse in front of us supplies the reasoning that produced the difference.

**The Nobility That Iblīs Ignored**

The commentary tradition notes that Iblīs's argument fixed on one aspect of the matter and ignored the rest. The Book states that Adam was created with God's two hands (38:75), that the divine spirit was breathed into him (15:29; 32:9), and that he was taught the names of all things (2:31). Each of these is a mark of honour that has nothing to do with the substance of his body, and Iblīs's comparison takes account of none of them.

The Book's treatment of human nobility elsewhere rests on the same facts, and the sūrah of the Night Journey states that God has honoured the children of Adam and carried them by land and sea. The verse in front of us reports the reasoning that sets all of that aside, and the narrative then states the consequence.

**Reasoning Against a Command**

The Book's narrative shows Iblīs using an argument in the face of an explicit instruction, and the tradition of commentary notices the shape of the operation. The argument is drawn from analogy: fire is nobler than clay, and therefore the one made of fire should not bow to the one made of clay. Its defect lies not in analogical reasoning as such but in its use against a direct command, and the commentary treats the scene as a warning about the order in which arguments and commandments are ranked.

The lesson is drawn out by the sūrah's audience as well, since the people of Makkah had their own arguments for retaining their practices. The claim that the forefathers did a thing, the claim that God commanded it, and the claim that a practice is honourable in itself all appear in the sūrah's later passages, and each of them is answered on the same ground. The verse in front of us supplies the first and most famous example of the reasoning that sets itself above the revelation.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands twelfth in the chapter, and it follows the report of the prostration and the refusal. The verse that comes after it states the sentence passed on Iblīs, and the passage then continues with his request for respite and his vow to mislead. The exchange in front of us is the turning point of the narrative, since it converts a single act of disobedience into a declared position.

The reader of the sūrah is given the command, the refusal, and the excuse in three short verses. The pattern is then repeated in the sūrah's accounts of the prophets and their peoples, where the message is delivered, the people refuse, and the refusal is supported by an argument about ancestry, custom, or status.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the command given to Iblīs to descend from the Garden, and it states that arrogance has no place there and that he is among the humbled (7:13). The excuse offered in front of us is answered not by refuting the argument but by removing the one who made it.

The two verses together state the consequence of pride in the presence of a command. The reader who has heard the argument about fire and clay is shown where it leads, and the sūrah's account then proceeds to the request for delay that Iblīs makes next.
