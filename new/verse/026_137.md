## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:137

> This is simply the tradition of our predecessors.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The People Appeal to Precedent**

ʿĀd explains its indifference by appealing to the tradition of its predecessors. The statement shifts responsibility from the present generation to those who came before. What they do feels safe because it is inherited. Yet age and familiarity do not turn a practice into truth, and an ancestor's decision cannot answer for a descendant's judgment.

The appeal is powerful because communities receive language, rituals, social rules, and stories through generations. Inheritance gives people identity, but it can also make examination feel like betrayal. Hūd's call requires ʿĀd to ask whether loyalty to its past has become resistance to God.


The Arabic expression can carry the sense of an inherited way, custom, religion, or claim repeated from earlier people. The ambiguity itself is instructive. Quran 25:5 records a related dismissal in which opponents reduce revelation to tales of the past, showing how an appeal to antiquity can be used to evade the truth being heard now. ʿĀd may be defending its worship, its conduct, or the stories that make its conduct appear respectable. In each case, the appeal to the past is being used as a shield against correction.

A tradition deserves respect when it preserves truth and wisdom, but it does not become authoritative merely by being old. The people must distinguish between memory that helps them obey God and habit that asks God to approve whatever they already do.

**Ancestors Are Not the Final Measure**

Honoring ancestors can include gratitude for their sacrifices and care for their good example. It cannot mean assuming that they were incapable of error. If every predecessor were beyond question, no generation could ever repent of inherited injustice or recover a truth that its elders neglected.

Hūd does not ask ʿĀd to despise its forebears. He asks the living to answer the Lord with their own conscience. The dead cannot carry the responsibility of the present people, and their existence in the past cannot cancel the warning now being delivered.


A practice may survive because it is true, but it may also survive because power protects it, fear repeats it, or people never examine it. Longevity is evidence of transmission, not proof of righteousness. ʿĀd mistakes continuity for confirmation.

This error appears whenever a society says, “We have always done this,” as though history itself were a revelation. The statement avoids the real questions: Does the practice honor God? Does it protect justice? Does it withstand truthful examination? Hūd's message brings those questions back into view.

**The Past Can Be Used to Silence the Present**

Those who benefit from an established order often invoke ancestors to prevent reform. A person who questions the order is labeled disloyal, even when the order has become cruel. The past then functions as a political tool rather than a source of wisdom.

The people of ʿĀd have already shown their readiness to use force against others. Their appeal to predecessors may therefore protect more than worship; it may protect a whole arrangement of prestige and domination. Hūd's warning threatens the arrangement because it says that no custom stands above the Lord's judgment.


Divine guidance does not require people to forget every earlier generation. It gives them a criterion by which inherited practices can be kept, corrected, or abandoned. A beneficial custom can remain beneficial after being submitted to truth, while a corrupt custom loses its claim even if it is ancient.

This balance avoids two extremes. Blind traditionalism treats every inheritance as sacred, while blind novelty assumes that anything new is better. Hūd calls for neither. He calls ʿĀd to obey God, which means honoring what is true and relinquishing what opposes Him.


The people do not say that their predecessors were right for a particular reason. They simply point to the fact of inheritance. That brevity reveals how little examination they have performed. The custom is defended by its origin rather than by its moral content.

A serious believer can state why a practice accords with revelation and justice. He does not need to rely on ancestry as a substitute for evidence. When a person cannot explain the good of an inherited way but refuses to leave it, attachment has replaced conviction.


Every generation receives something and passes something on. The question is whether it transmits wisdom or merely repeats appetite. A responsible heir examines what he has received so that he does not pass harm to his children under the name of heritage.

ʿĀd's example warns that a people can inherit strength and still inherit a blindness about strength. Its predecessors may have begun a custom, but the present generation chooses to continue it. The response to Hūd therefore belongs to them, not to the ancestors behind whom they hide.

**The Reader Must Test Familiar Claims**

The verse is especially challenging because inherited language often feels natural. A reader may accept a belief, social ranking, or habit simply because it was present before he could question it. Hūd's encounter invites a pause: is this truly guidance, or only the oldest explanation available?

The right response is not contempt for one's roots. It is gratitude joined to discernment. Keep what leads to God, correct what departs from justice, and refuse to make the past an idol. Tradition can be a vessel of truth, but only God is its final authority.

This discernment is an act of loyalty, not betrayal. A community honors its worthy inheritance more faithfully when it removes what contradicts justice and preserves what leads to God. Otherwise, it passes an unexamined burden to children and calls the burden identity.

The appeal to predecessors should therefore prompt investigation rather than surrender. Ask what the earlier practice was meant to protect, whom it served, and whether its present form still serves that good. Honest examination can recover the wisdom of the past without becoming captive to its errors.
