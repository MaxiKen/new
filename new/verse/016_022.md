## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:22

> **Your God is ˹only˺ One God. As for those who do not believe in the Hereafter, their hearts are in denial, and they are too proud.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Affirmation After Negation**

Ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid — your God is One God. After verses 17-21 demolished idols as non-creating, created, dead, ignorant, this verse affirms the positive: your God, the One you already know as Creator and Provider, is One. Ilāh means god, object of worship, one to whom devotion is directed. Wāḥid means one, unique, without partner. The phrase ilāhukum, your God, is possessive and personal: the God who created you from a drop, who gave cattle, rain, crops, sea, mountains, is your God, not a distant deity. The repetition ilāhun wāḥid emphasizes oneness in essence, attributes, and right to worship. Al-Ṭabarī explains that this is the core message that angels brought in verse 2: *Warn that there is no god except Me*. Now it is stated directly to the audience. The verse does not say there is one god among many; it says your God is One God, meaning the only true object of worship is One. This is tawḥīd in its simplest form, accessible to all.

**Hearts In Denial And Pride**

Fa-al-ladhīna lā yu'minūna bi-l-ākhirati qulūbuhum munkiratun wa-hum mustakbirūn — as for those who do not believe in Hereafter, their hearts are in denial and they are arrogant. Munkiratun from n-k-r means denying, refusing to recognize, strange, unrecognizing. Their hearts do not recognize truth when presented. Mustakbirūn from k-b-r means arrogant, seeking to be big, refusing to submit. Al-Zamakhsharī notes the order: first hearts deny, then selves show arrogance. Denial is internal, arrogance is external behavior. The two feed each other: a heart that denies Hereafter sees no need to humble itself, and arrogance prevents heart from accepting Hereafter because acceptance would require humility. Al-Rāzī links denial of Hereafter to denial of tawḥīd: if there is no return, then creation's purpose is void, and then there is no need for One God to hold accountable. So denial of Hereafter leads to denial of oneness, and vice versa. The verse thus diagnoses the root of shirk not as lack of intelligence but as moral disease: denial and pride.

**Why Hereafter Is Mentioned Here**

The sūrah opened with *The command of Allah is at hand* (16:1), referring to Hour. It then listed blessings that prove power to resurrect. Now it says those who do not believe in Hereafter have hearts that deny and are arrogant. The connection is that belief in Hereafter is necessary for gratitude to be complete. If this life is all, then blessings can be seen as mere chance to be enjoyed without thanks. If there is return, then blessings become trust to be accounted for. Al-Qurṭubī notes that Makkan idolaters did believe in Allah as Creator but denied resurrection, as 23:82 says: *When we have died and become dust and bones, shall we be resurrected?* (23:82). Their denial was not of Allah's existence but of return, and that denial allowed them to continue injustice without fear. The verse says their hearts are munkiratun — they find truth strange, unfamiliar, because their hearts are accustomed to denial. The cure, implied, is dhikr and tawāḍuʿ, remembrance and humility.

**The Link Between Tawḥīd And Humility**

Your God is One God — this statement demands humility. If God is One, then no human can claim partnership in divinity, no tribe can claim its idol is equal, no self can claim independence. Arrogance, istikbār, is the opposite of tawḥīd because arrogance is claiming greatness for self that belongs to Allah alone. The Qur'an says *He does not like the arrogant* (16:23). The sūrah will later say *So enter gates of Hell to abide therein; evil indeed is abode of arrogant* (16:29). The sequence shows that arrogance is not a minor fault but a barrier to accepting oneness. Al-Rāzī explains that mustakbirūn are those who deem themselves too great to worship Allah alone or to follow a messenger who is a human like them. Their pride says, how can we follow a man, how can we abandon what our fathers worshipped? Tawḥīd requires humility to say, I am servant, not partner.

**A Verse That Summarizes The Makkan Conflict**

This verse condenses the whole Makkan conflict into two sentences. One sentence states truth: your God is One. Second sentence describes opposition: those who deny Hereafter have denying hearts and are arrogant. The conflict is not about evidence — evidence of creation, blessings, and prophethood has been given — but about heart attitude. The Qur'an often describes disbelief as disease of heart, not defect of mind, as in 2:10: *In their hearts is disease, so Allah increased their disease* (2:10). Here disease is described as munkiratun and mustakbirūn — denial and arrogance. The remedy is not more arguments alone but softening of heart through remembrance of blessings and awareness of return. This is why Sūrat al-Niʿam spends so much time listing blessings: to soften hearts that have become hard through denial and pride.

**Translation Note On Only**

The translators add ˹only˺ in brackets before One God to emphasize exclusivity: your God is only One God, not one among many. The Arabic ilāhun wāḥid already implies exclusivity, but English *One God* could be misread as one god among many. The bracket clarifies that wāḥid here means unique, alone, without partner. The phrase ilāhukum, your God, is also significant: it addresses listeners directly, making tawḥīd personal. It is not *God is one* in abstract, but *your God*, the One who created you, sustains you, knows what you conceal and reveal, is One. The personal possessive invites relationship, not only doctrine.

**How Oneness Reshapes Daily Life**

When a person accepts that his God is One, daily decisions change. He no longer seeks to please many masters. He does not fear idols, does not offer to them, does not ask them for help. His worship, supplication, reliance, and hope are directed to One. Al-Qurṭubī notes that ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid is not only creedal but practical: it means your worship, your love, your fear, your hope should be for One. The Makkans who heard this understood its social cost: to say God is One meant to abandon tribal idols that brought pilgrims and trade. Yet the verse presents it as simple fact, not as negotiation. Your God is One. Whether you accept or deny, whether your heart is open or arrogant, the reality remains. The question is whether your heart will recognize what is already true.
