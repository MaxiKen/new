## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:8

> How ˹can they have a treaty˺? If they were to have the upper hand over you, they would have no respect for kinship or treaty. They only flatter you with their tongues, but their hearts are in denial, and most of them are rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Power Reveals Whether a Treaty Is Real**

Verse 8 answers the question raised in the previous verse by proposing a test: what would these people do if they gained the upper hand? A treaty that exists only while one side lacks the strength to break it is not secure. The verse does not praise weakness, and it does not condemn every strong party. It exposes a specific character: the party that uses polite words while waiting for the chance to disregard every obligation.

This test reaches beyond the original tribes. Many people appear honest when external pressure makes honesty easy. The deeper question is what they do when enforcement disappears. A promise honored only because betrayal would be costly reveals fear of consequence, not necessarily faithfulness. The verse asks the reader to look at a pattern of conduct under advantage, because power exposes the values that polite circumstances can conceal.

**Kinship and Treaty Cover the Natural and the Chosen**

The two bonds named in the verse cover different kinds of obligation. The first term can refer to kinship or an alliance that creates a bond of mutual protection. The second, dhimmah, is an undertaking that places responsibility upon a person or group. Together they indicate that the hostile party would disregard both inherited ties and formal promises. It would not protect a relative because of blood, nor a treaty partner because of a pledged duty.

The breadth of the charge matters. A person who breaks one promise may have failed in a particular matter. A party that discards every kind of bond has made disregard itself into a policy. Such a party cannot be trusted merely because it speaks the language of agreement. Trust is not built from a vocabulary of loyalty; it is built from conduct toward people whom one could safely betray.

**Mouth and Heart Are Put in Conflict**

The verse describes flattery with the tongue and denial in the heart. It does not teach that all pleasant speech is false, or that inner intention is irrelevant to a contract. Rather, it identifies deliberate speech that is used to conceal refusal. The tongue performs agreement while the heart has already rejected the obligation. This split is the signature of hypocrisy: language is made to reassure the other party while intention prepares to violate him.

The Qur'an often treats the heart as the center of settled purpose, not as a private organ that excuses public behavior. Words still matter because they create promises; what makes these words blameworthy is that they are used without the intention to honor them. The verse thus warns believers not to evaluate trust by charm alone. Pleasant speech can be a sign of good manners, but it can also be a tool of manipulation. Conduct over time reveals which one it is.

**“Most” Refuses an Unjust Generalization**

The verse concludes that most of them are rebellious, not that every individual without exception is identical. That small word matters. The chapter has already protected those who keep their treaties, and the qualification remains in force here. The Qur'an can make a severe judgment about a dominant pattern without erasing the people who do not share it. The exception is not a weakness in the accusation; it is evidence of careful judgment.

This restraint also guards against the believer's tendency to make an enemy morally uniform. Collective language can be necessary when a group acts through a common political structure, but it must not become a license to ignore individual conduct. Some members of a tribe may be guilty leaders, some reluctant followers, and some faithful treaty partners. The Qur'anic description remains tied to what the evidence shows. Even in a passage of confrontation, justice refuses to say more than the case supports.

**The Upper Hand Is a Moral Examination**

The phrase about prevailing supplies a general spiritual lesson. Every advantage is an examination of the person who receives it. A manager with power over an employee, a creditor over a debtor, an adult over a child, or a majority over a minority faces the same question: will strength be used to honor the relationship or to escape it? The verse's hostile parties fail because victory would expose the absence of kinship and treaty in their moral order.

Power also tests the believer. It is easy to read verse 8 as a description of someone else and miss the question directed inward. If a promise can be broken without consequence, would I still keep it? If a weaker party cannot answer me, do I still protect his claim? Allah's standard is not limited to agreements between states. The person who honors a warranty, repays a loan, or keeps a confidence when no one can force him is resisting the very pathology this verse names.

**A Just Response Requires Verification**

The warning does not tell the believers to become suspicious of every word or to answer possible hypocrisy with immediate violence. It tells them to recognize that a treaty has no moral security when one party has shown that it will discard it at the first opportunity. That conclusion should follow a record of behavior, not a feeling. Verse 7's command to remain true as long as the other side remains true still governs the faithful case.

For public leaders, this means distinguishing negotiation from deception and disagreement from treachery. For private believers, it means refusing both gullibility and cynicism. The believer neither worships appearances nor invents betrayal. He watches what happens when power changes hands. The verse's enduring test is simple: when someone has the upper hand, does he become more just because he fears Allah, or more treacherous because he fears no one else? That answer tells you whether the treaty is being honored in the heart as well as on the tongue.
