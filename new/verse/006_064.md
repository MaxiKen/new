## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:64

> Say, “˹Only˺ Allah rescues you from this and any other distress, yet you associate others with Him ˹in worship˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Stated as a Verdict**

The previous verse asked who rescues from the dangers of land and sea, and this one answers without waiting for the audience's reply. The answer is Allah alone, and the wording makes the exclusion total: no partner, no intercessor, no shrine receives credit for the rescue. The Book frequently supplies the answer to its own questions in this way, and it does so because the audience did not need information about the identity of their rescuer. They needed to hear it named in public, in the words of the man they were resisting.

The sūrah of Yūnus contains a similar question about who provides from heaven and earth, who owns hearing and sight, and who conducts every affair, and it records that the answer people give is Allah, followed immediately by the question of whether they will not then be mindful (10:31). The Book treats the audience's own admissions as the strongest evidence available, and it produces them for the record.

**"And Any Other Distress"**

The sentence widens the scope deliberately. The rescue described in the previous verse concerned the most desperate situation conceivable, but the answer here states that the same God rescues from that and from every other hardship as well. The Book makes the same point where it says that whatever blessings people have are from God, and that whenever hardship touches them it is to Him alone they cry for help (16:53).

The widening removes the last defence available to a person who prayed at sea. Some might say that the extremity of the situation produced an instinctive cry rather than an acknowledgment, which would leave the normal conditions of life in the hands of the gods. The verse rejects the separation. The power that answered the cry in the waves is the power that runs everything, which is what makes the next clause a charge rather than an observation.

**What the Prayer Did Not Include**

The verse also exposes the difference between what the audience said and what they practised. The prayer described a person who calls on God alone, who asks God alone for rescue, and who promises God alone his gratitude. Nothing in the moment of crisis left room for the associate-gods, and the verse notes that they were added afterwards, when the danger had gone and the story was being told.

The Book describes the same sequence with a sharper image when it says that a person touched by hardship cries out to God lying on his side, sitting, or standing, and then returns to his old ways once the hardship is relieved, as though he had never called at all (10:12). The Book's conclusion is that the misdeeds of such people were made to look attractive to them, which is a statement about how a habit of ingratitude becomes a settled way of life.

**The Meaning of Associating Others With Him**

The charge in this verse is that they associate others with Him in worship, and the sūrah has been dismantling that practice from every angle. The associated beings were created things like the worshippers (7:194), they could neither harm nor benefit anyone (5:76), they could not respond or protect (20:89), and they had been given names by their own ancestors with no authority from God (12:40).

What this verse adds is the specific context of gratitude. The Book treats the blessing and the rescue as coming through channels that would be entirely the work of God even if the idols were real, and it treats the assignment of a share of thanks to a created thing as a falsification of the record. The person who thanks a stone for a rescue he received from God has made a mistake about what happened to him.

**The Book's Pattern of Testing Its Audience With Their Own Words**

The Book's practice of using the audience's confessions is a feature of its method, not an accident of this passage. It asks who created the heavens and the earth, and the answer recorded is Allah (29:61); it asks who sends down rain to revive the dead earth, and the answer is Allah (29:63); and in each case the question is followed by a rebuke that turns on the gap between the admission and the practice.

That method leaves no exit. A person who answers Allah to these questions has already accepted that the beings he invokes alongside God have no share in the matters he is asking about, and the verse in front of us closes the discussion by naming the inconsistency directly rather than by arguing about the idols' nature.

**What Gratitude Would Have Required**

The verse's charge also states what the correct response would have been. The rescue and every other deliverance come from one source, and the acknowledgment belongs with it. The Book describes gratitude as a form of recognition that produces conduct, and it pairs the ungrateful with those who reject the signs, as it does in the sūrah of Luqmān, where the Book says that none rejects the signs except the treacherous and ungrateful (31:32).

The sūrah then moves from this rebuke to a warning about the forms the punishment can take, and the transition is not sudden. A people who attribute their rescue to the wrong source have placed themselves in the position the next verse describes, in which the same power that saved them is the power that can reach them without any possibility of escape.

The Book states the rebuke in a compact form in the sūrah of the Bee, where it says that every blessing people have comes from God and that whenever hardship touches them it is to Him alone that they cry for help, and then asks why they turn away from the One who gave them what they have (16:53–54). The movement from gratitude to association is described there and here in the same order, which shows that the charge is a general diagnosis of human conduct rather than a complaint about one city.
