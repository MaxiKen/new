## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:25

> Indeed, those who persist in disbelief and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah and from the Sacred Mosque—which We have appointed for all people, residents and visitors alike—along with whoever intends to deviate by doing wrong in it, We will cause them to taste a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Charges, and the Second One Is Active**

The verse names two offences joined by a conjunction: disbelief, and turning people away from the way of God and from the Sacred Mosque.

The first is a state. The second is an activity, and the Arabic for it, *ṣadda ʿan*, carries an ambiguity the commentators noticed. It can mean that a person is himself diverted, or that he diverts others. Both senses fit the Makkan situation, and both are probably meant: the Quraysh were themselves turned from the revelation and worked to turn others from it, and the same verb covers the refusal and the campaign.

The sūrah has met this pattern before. Verse 9 described a man who turned his side away in order to lead others astray. The sin in that verse, as here, was not the wrong belief as such; it was the propagation of it, and the doubling of the sentence matches the doubling of the harm.

**"Which We Made Equal for All People"**

The parenthesis is the verse's most consequential clause: the Sacred Mosque, *which We appointed for mankind, equal are the resident in it and the visitor*.

This is a claim about ownership, and in seventh-century Makkah it was a radical one. Quraysh controlled the sanctuary, kept the keys, distributed the water of Zamzam, and derived both income and prestige from the traffic of pilgrims. The verse says that none of that gives them a title. The House is God's, and the man who lives next to it has no more right in it than the man who walked six hundred miles to reach it.

The sentence also does something subtler: it removes the custodians' standing to expel anyone. If the resident and the visitor are equal in the precinct, then barring the visitor is not an exercise of a right but a usurpation, and that is exactly the grievance the sūrah will name in verse 40.

**"Whoever Intends Deviation Therein with Wrongdoing"**

The third clause extends the offence from acts to intentions: *wa-man yurid fīhi bi-ilḥādin bi-ẓulmin* — and whoever intends, in it, deviation through wrongdoing.

The commentators differed over what the deviation is. Many read it as idolatry and the worship of anything other than God inside the precinct. Others took it as certain kinds of dishonest commercial dealing within the sanctuary. Both readings are credible, and the wording is general enough to hold both: *ilḥād* is a swerving, a leaning away from what is straight, and *ẓulm* is the wrong that results.

The striking feature is the verb. What is punished is *intending*. A religion of outward conformity would have required the act; this verse names the will. Theological and legal consequences were drawn from it in both directions — some holding that a resolved intention formed in the sanctuary is itself the offence, whether or not it is carried out — and the dispute turns on how literally the verb is pressed. **Why the Sanctuary Makes the Offence Worse**

The logic of a *ḥaram* — a precinct set apart — is that it is a place of guaranteed safety. Hunting is forbidden inside it, fighting is forbidden inside it, and a man who has blood on his hands is supposed to be secure there.

Offences committed in such a place are therefore not merely offences with an unfortunate address. They are offences against the institution of safety itself, and the Qur'an treats them on that footing. Committing idolatry anywhere is the gravest wrong; committing it in the House that was purified for the worship of one God (22:26) is a different order of thing, because it uses the sanctuary for the precise purpose the sanctuary exists to exclude.

That is why the verse names the Sacred Mosque as an object of the hindrance rather than merely its location. The people being turned away are being turned away from a place as well as from a path.

**Who Was Listening When This Was Revealed**

The verse's threat lands differently once the setting is recalled. The believers had been expelled from Makkah and barred from the Sacred Mosque; the pilgrimage they were commanded to perform was being performed by their enemies at a sanctuary filled with idols.

A verse that says the precinct was made equal for resident and visitor alike, and that those who bar people from it will taste a painful punishment, is not a general maxim in that context. It is a verdict on the specific arrangement, and it contains an unstated promise: the people being barred will be the ones who return.

That is why the sūrah can move from this verse to the pilgrimage rites without a transition. The hearers were being told, in effect, that the House they could not reach was going to be theirs to purify.

**The Punishment Tasted**

The verse closes with *nudhiqhu min ʿadhābin alīm* — We will make him taste a painful punishment. The same verb the sūrah used in verse 9 for the man who turned aside to mislead others, and it will be repeated for the disbelievers in verse 57.

The consistency is the point. The sūrah has now named, in three separate places, the same offence of active diversion, and each time it answers with the same verb. Tasting, as noted earlier, is the sense that cannot be declined: this is a punishment that will be fully registered by the person it is administered to.

And the adjective is *alīm*, painful — the Qur'an's most frequent description of punishment, and one it applies to the Hereafter rather than to this life. The verse's threats are real in history — the sanctuary was cleared — but the sentence it announces is not a political forecast. It is the verdict that comes after.
