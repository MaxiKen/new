## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:102

> They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved. They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon. The two angels never taught anyone without saying, “We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith.” Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah’s Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter. Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew!

**Expanded Commentary**

**What They Followed Instead**

The previous verse described a book cast behind the back, and this sentence names what replaced it. Instead of the scripture they had been given, the community followed practices attributed to the devils in the time of Solomon, and the contrast is deliberate: the text that had been set aside was replaced by material that promised power.

The Book's account of the exchange is a study in what a community turns to when it has declined what it knows. The practices described here were not a fresh revelation; they belonged to a body of lore the community claimed to have inherited, and the verse begins by naming their real source. What the sentence measures is the distance between a text that was available and a substitute that was preferred.

**Solomon Cleared of the Accusation**

The sentence states in the strongest terms that Solomon did not disbelieve, and the clarification answers a charge the community circulated. The Book elsewhere describes the powers he was given, including command over the wind and over the jinn who served him (21:81–82), and those powers were being used by later generations as evidence that his kingdom rested on magic.

The sūrah's correction is exact and brief. The one who was given authority over created things did not obtain it by sorcery, and the Book attributes the practice to the devils instead. Commentators noticed the pattern in the Book's treatment of its prophets: whenever an earlier figure is credited with what he did not do, the text restores his name before it proceeds with the argument.

**The Two Angels and the City of Babylon**

The verse next names Hārūt and Mārūt, two angels associated with Babylon, and reports that what was revealed to them was taught to people along with magic. The commentary tradition around these names contains a large amount of material of very uneven reliability, and the verse's own words are the safest guide to the passage.

Some classical commentators, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī, rejected a number of the elaborate stories attached to the verse, among them the account of angels tempted by a woman they identified with Venus. What the sūrah itself states is that a teaching reached people, that it came with a warning, and that the warning was ignored. The Book's interest lies in the misuse rather than in the folklore that gathered around it.

**"We Are Only a Test"**

The angels' warning is the sentence's moral centre. They taught nothing without announcing that the matter was a trial and instructing their students not to abandon their faith, so that the harm was never in the knowledge but in the purpose to which it was put.

That structure removes an excuse a person might otherwise claim, since the warning was part of the teaching itself and the learners proceeded anyway. Responsibility is placed exactly where the conduct occurred, and the sūrah's description of what people did with what they learned confirms where the fault lay.

**What Magic Can and Cannot Do**

The verse states that the practices people learned could separate a husband from his wife, and immediately adds that they could harm no one except by God's permission. The Book acknowledges the real effects of such practices in about as small a compass as possible while attributing their efficacy to the divine will rather than to the objects and words involved.

The commentators discussed the varieties of magic in the Arabic usage, noting that the term covers illusion and deception as well as claims about hidden knowledge, and that the Book elsewhere reports the disbelievers describing the revelation itself as magic. Under every sense the point holds: the harm occurs within limits God has set, and no practitioner commands the outcome.

**The Price and the Warning to Later Readers**

The sentence closes by stating that those who bought into this practice had no share in the Hereafter, and it calls the price miserable. The sūrah's language of buying and selling, used earlier for the trade of the Afterlife for this world, reappears here, and the verdict is the same.

The next verse states what the community could have had instead, and the contrast the sūrah draws is between a practice that harms the person who uses it and a reward given freely by God. The reader is left with a comparison the surrounding passage has already prepared.

**Knowledge With a Warning Attached**

The angels' instruction is unusual among the Book's accounts of forbidden knowledge because it arrives with its own caution. The teachers announce what the matter is before teaching it, and they name the risk to the person who takes it up. That detail places the responsibility on the learner, and the verse's description of what people did with what they learned confirms where the fault lay.

The sūrah's treatment of knowledge in this passage is consistent: it is not the subject itself but the use to which it is put and the price accepted for it that determines the outcome. What the verse leaves the reader with is not a curiosity about Babylon but a warning about the purchase, and the next sentence states the alternative that was available all along.
