## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:102

> They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon. Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved. They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon. The two angels never taught anyone without saying, “We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith.” Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife; although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah’s Will. They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter. Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew!

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Devils Recited**

The recital turns from the cast Book to what filled the vacancy, and the verse's opening names both the substitute and its authors: *they followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon*. The devils' activity is described with the same word the Qur'an uses for revelation's recitation — they *recited* against Solomon's kingdom, or, in the variant reading the exegetes preserve, recited regarding it or within its time — and the verbal echo is deliberate: against the Book cast behind the back stands a counter-recitation, a corpus taught, transmitted, followed. The setting is Solomon's reign, and the tradition's accounts of how the corpus entered circulation cluster around the king's death: writings describing the arts by which Solomon ruled, interred beneath his throne-platform, exhumed after his death and put to misuse — documents of power turned into manuals of manipulation, spread among people given over to their passions, circulating until the Prophet's ﷺ own time. The commentators are candid about the reliability gradient here — this verse's exegetical literature, more than any other's, gathers material startlingly incongruous with the tradition's standards, and the major commentators, Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī among them, reject the legends' speculative elaborations while retaining the verse's plain architecture. What the verse itself asserts is narrower and firmer: there was a devil-authored tradition of magic associated with Solomon's era, the people of this verse followed it, and the following was itself the disbelief the sentence structure implies — they traded a sent-down Book for a recited-up corruption.

**“Never Did Solomon Disbelieve”**

The verse interrupts its own narrative to clear a prophet's name, and the interruption's vehemence — *never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved* — marks what was at stake. In the surrounding traditions, Jewish and later popular accounts had woven Solomon's unmatched dominion into a magician's biography: the king who commanded jinn and wind rendered as a king who dealt in sorcery. The Qur'an's counter-testimony is absolute. Solomon's powers were conferred — the raging winds subjected to him, blowing by his command (21:81), the jinn and the devils in service, the kingdom none after him would hold — and conferred power is the opposite of usurped art: it arrives with its Source named, which is why its holder never disbelieved. The distinction the verse installs runs through every case of extraordinary capacity: powers granted by Allah point back to Allah and keep their holder inside faith; powers sought from the devils' recitation point back to the self and their price is the soul. Solomon is thus not merely exonerated but exhibited — the maximum case of divinely given power, held without a gram of disbelief, against which the magic-buyers' minimum purchase stands condemned. The clause's placement does further work: by clearing Solomon at the moment the devils are named, the verse assigns the disbelief precisely — *rather the devils disbelieved* — and makes the following of their recitation a participation in their disbelief rather than a neutral technical acquisition.

**Hārūt and Mārūt, and the Warning Built Into the Teaching**

The verse's most discussed element is its second curriculum: *what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon*. The exegetical literature around the two angels includes the legends the tradition itself flags as incongruous — angels sent down after boasting of human frailty, tempted by a woman of beauty (Venus in the legend's astrology), intoxicated into sin — and the major commentators reject these elaborations; al-Rāzī at one point even identifies the two with Gabriel and Michael. The sober reading the verse's own wording supports is cleaner and more instructive: the angels taught, and their teaching carried its warning inside it — *"We are only a test for you, so do not abandon your faith"* (2:102). No one was taught without first being told what the knowledge was: a trial, a double-edged instrument, dangerous in proportion to its power. The structure is the Qur'an's permanent law of hazardous knowledge. The hidden dimensions of the cosmos can be read — for wisdom, for healing, for good — and the same reading can be turned to severance and harm; the difference is not in the knowledge but in the receiver, which is why the knowledge is delivered with the test declared. Babylon as the setting is exact rather than incidental: the esoteric tradition associates this revealed-and-warned knowledge with the correspondence-sciences that originated in Babylon — the web of sympathies joining stars, minerals, plants, and human affairs, the foundation on which astrology, alchemy, and divination were built. The angels taught the science with its warning; the people kept the science and discarded the warning, which is the verse's account of how a test becomes a trap.

**Siḥr: The Range of the Word**

The verse's subject-term deserves its lexical range stated, because the range explains the verse's severity. *Siḥr* covers, in Arabic usage and in the exegetes' accounting, more than stage-magic: it includes sleight of hand and illusion; the manipulation of hidden forces toward a chosen effect; the attainment of knowledge of absent things — distant in space or time — by occult means; and, at the word's furthest reach, a persuasive eloquence so captivating it binds its hearers, which is why the Qur'an's opponents could accuse the recitation itself of being *siḥr mubīn*, manifest magic (10:2). The range gathers the verse's elements into one category: the devils' recitation (a counter-revelation binding hearts), the angels' warned science (hidden forces legible and steerable), the separation of spouses (effects in the world wrought by unseen means). What unifies the range is the bypass: siḥr in every sense reaches its ends around the ordained channels — knowledge without revelation, power without permission, persuasion without truth. The verse's judgment on the bypass is written into its economics, which arrive in the second half: the buyer of magic purchases harm, spends his soul, and forfeits his share in the Hereafter. The tradition's esoteric reading runs the same judgment inward — the magic here is the soul's defection from the heart and spirit to the ego's lower instruments, the turning from the *Solomon of the spirit* to the devils of the soul — and the inward reading keeps the verse's law portable: every age has its correspondence-sciences, its techniques for steering hidden forces past their warned limits, and the purchase-price is unchanged.

**The Rift Between Husband and Wife**

The verse names magic's signature effect, and the naming is a social diagnosis: *they learned from them what caused a rift even between husband and wife*. The marriage bond is the target because it is the load-bearing wall of human society — the first covenant between persons, the structure under every household, market, and generation — and an art whose showcase achievement is its severance is an art aimed at the social order's foundation. The commentators note how common this commission was in premodern societies, and the Qur'an's final sūrahs will keep their refuge-language trained on the same threat (113's introduction in the tradition). The verse's limiting clause then delivers its theology in a sentence that dismantles the art from inside: *their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah's Will*. The permission-principle is total: no technique, however ancient, however warned-about, however purchased, carries independent causal power — every effect runs through the same divine Will that runs every event, and the magician's real transaction is not with hidden forces but with the devils' recitation, buying a participation in disbelief at the price of a power he does not actually possess. The clause protects the verse's audience in both directions: against terror of the art (it harms only by the Will that also protects) and against temptation toward it (its exercise is disbelief's purchase, whatever its effects). Between husband and wife, the bond the recital's covenant-theology most resembles, the art's greatest recorded success is also its greatest recorded crime — and the Will that permits the rift is the same Will verse 186 will describe as answering the prayer of whoever calls.

**The Purchase and Its Price**

The verse closes with the buyer's ledger, in the sūrah's transaction-grammar: *they learned what harmed them and did not benefit them — although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter*. The learning verbs stack deliberately: they learned the art, they learned its harm, and they already knew its cost — knowledge at every level, purchase anyway, which is the recital's fixed portrait of informed self-destruction. The trade-clause then pronounces in the formula this passage has already established as the sūrah's verdict-idiom: *miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls, if only they knew!* — the same exclamation filed at verse 90 over the souls sold in envy, here over souls sold for severance-power. The commentators mark the verse's Faust-structure by name: the bargainer who trades the soul for unworthy worldly gain, the Western tradition's crystallization of the purchase this verse describes with its warning-clause intact. And the final *if only they knew* holds the verse's cruelest precision against its own premise — they already knew. The knowledge that should have blocked the purchase was present at the purchase, which locates the failure exactly where verse 75 located the distortion and verse 101 located the casting: not in the intellect's supply but in the will's direction. The devils' recitation found its followers among people fully informed about the Hereafter-share they were paying away — informed, and buying — and the verse's ledger stands as the recital's complete account of what informed purchase of the forbidden costs: harm without benefit in this world, no share in the next, and a soul spent against a knowledge that watched the spending.
