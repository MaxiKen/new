## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:15

> Whoever does good, it is to their own benefit. And whoever does evil, it is to their own loss. Then to your Lord you will ˹all˺ be returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Account Belongs to the One Who Acts**

The verse states the most basic rule of the Qur'anic moral order: a person's deeds are credited to that person, in both directions. Good done by one person cannot be charged to another, and harm done by one person cannot be collected from another. The same sentence opens another chapter almost word for word, where it is completed by a statement that God is never unjust to his creation (41:46). The Book repeats the rule because human beings resist it in both directions, sometimes claiming credit for what others did and sometimes hoping that someone else's merit will cover their own deficit. Two short verses near the end of the Qur'an state the accounting with unmatched brevity: whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (99:7–8). Nothing is too small for the ledger, and nothing is filed under the wrong name.

**Why This Sentence Follows the Command to Forgive**

The placement of the verse is part of its meaning. The previous command asked the believers to forgive without demanding justice first, and a reader might reasonably ask what an unpunished wrongdoer will face. The answer is given before the question can be put: the damage an evildoer does lands on himself. That reorders the whole dispute. The Muslim who restrains himself is not permitting a wrong to go unanswered; he is declining to compete for a debt that the debtor already owes to himself, and the loss is stated plainly as the evildoer's own. A community acting on this rule can afford patience, because it does not need the offender's punishment in order to be satisfied. The verse supplies the accounting that the previous verse had referred to, and it does so with a symmetry that leaves no room for the injured party to feel cheated.

**Loss That Is Not a Metaphor**

The Book's word for the second half of the equation is loss, and it uses it the way a merchant would. The Qur'an describes the people who trade their guidance for error as those who have ruined themselves, and it will say later in this very sūrah that on the day the Hour arrives, those who made false claims will have lost. The clearest statement of what this involves comes earlier: the true losers are those who will lose themselves and their families on Judgment Day, and that is called the clearest loss (39:15). The phrasing is precise. The loss is not a penalty added to an otherwise intact life; it is the life itself, and in some cases the household along with it. The verse therefore does not tell the reader that evil has unpleasant consequences. It tells him that evil spends the capital, and the account is his own.

**No One Carries Another's Burden**

Underneath the sentence lies a doctrine the Qur'an states many times: no soul bears the burden of another. The doctrine cuts against almost every instinct people bring to religion, because the alternative — that merit can be pooled, inherited, or traded — is convenient and socially comfortable. It was the claim of those who said they would be saved by the merit of their ancestors, and it is the assumption of anyone who counts on family, nation, or community standing in place of his own record. The verse's possessive language leaves no gap for that assumption: the benefit and the loss are each attached to the person who acted. What a believer can do for another, the Qur'an says elsewhere, is pray for him, advise him, forgive him, and ask mercy for him. What he cannot do is finish another person's account after that person has closed it.

**Return as the Reason for the Accounting**

The verse's final clause explains why the ledger exists: everyone is going back to his Lord. The return gives the sentence its seriousness and also its comfort, because it means that the judge at the end of the process knows the case from the inside. The Qur'an closes ethical instructions with a reference to the meeting in this way so often that the pattern becomes a signature: act, then answer for the act, then meet the one who kept the record. Almost every chapter establishes the same movement, and one of them states the reckoning itself in a single sentence: on that Day no soul will be wronged in the least, nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do (36:54). The verse before us is that rule stated in advance, for a reader who is still able to change what is written next.

**Good Done in the Dark Is Still Recorded**

There is one more consequence that the wording leaves open. The verse does not say who will notice, only that the benefit returns to the doer. A generous act performed where nobody saw it can feel like a thing lost, and a corrupt act performed where nobody noticed can feel like a gain, because in this world the account often seems closed by the absence of witnesses. The verse says the second half of the transaction has not happened yet, and that it will happen under different conditions. The person who did good in obscurity will receive what he could not see when he did it, and the person who did harm in obscurity will be handed the same bill. Between now and then, the only thing being tested is whether a person believes that the account exists at all.
