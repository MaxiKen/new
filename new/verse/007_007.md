## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:7

> Then We will give them a full account with sure knowledge—for We were never absent.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Account Given With Knowledge**

The verse states that God will recount to the people what they did with knowledge, and the phrase names the ground of the whole reckoning. The Book describes the day of judgment as the day on which every person is informed of what he did, and the sūrah of the Cattle states that people will return to their Lord, who will inform them of what they differed about (6:164). The informing in the verse in front of us is described as proceeding from knowledge, which means that the account is complete and accurate in every particular.

The Book's language about the day includes the giving of a written record to every person. The sūrah of the Night Journey states that every person will be called with his record, and the sūrah of the Cave describes the record that is placed open, from which nothing great or small is omitted (18:49). The sūrah of the Inevitable describes the one who receives his record in his right hand, and the one who is given his record from behind his back (69:19; 84:10). The account in front of us is the reading and the explanation of that record.

**The Deeds That Are Recounted**

What is recounted is the whole conduct of a life, and the Book's description of the book of deeds states that it leaves out nothing. The sūrah of the Cattle says that God does not forget the weight of an atom, and the sūrah of the Bee states that God knows what is in every breast. The chapter of the Emigration describes the day when people will be shown their deeds, and the Book's repeated statements that God knows the hidden and the open appear in the sūrah of the Cattle itself.

The tradition of commentary notes that the recounting serves to establish the justice of the outcome rather than to supply information to God. The deed is named, its circumstances are stated, and the one who did it is left with no ground for denial. The verse in front of us states the character of that recounting: it is made with knowledge, and it is therefore complete.

**The Direct Address in the Sound Reports**

The sound reports describe the reckoning as a private conversation between the Lord and His servant, without an intermediary. The Prophet is reported to have said that there will be none among you but his Lord will speak to him, and that there will be no interpreter between them and no screen to screen Him (al-Bukhārī 7443); and in another narration, that each person will meet God without needing an interpreter between him and his Lord, who will ask him whether a messenger was sent to teach him and whether he was given wealth and favours (al-Bukhārī 3595). The reports place the recounting of deeds in the context of a face-to-face accounting.

The same reports describe the person looking to his right and his left and seeing only his own deeds and the fire before him, and they end with the instruction to guard against the fire even by giving half a date in charity. The verse in front of us states the principle that these reports illustrate, since the account proceeds from a Lord who knows and who addresses each person with what he knows.

**Never Absent**

The verse's closing clause states that God was never absent, and the phrase asserts the presence of the One who recounts. The sūrah of Iron says that God is with people wherever they are and that He sees all that they do (57:4), and the sūrah of the Cattle states that no leaf falls without His knowledge. The Book's use of the attribute in its discussions of the reckoning ties the exactness of the account to the constant presence of the One who keeps it.

The commentary tradition reads the clause as naming both God's knowledge of all things and His presence with His creation. The verse that precedes it describes the recounting as proceeding from knowledge, and the clause in front of us names the presence from which nothing escapes. The two attributes together make the day of judgment a settling of accounts rather than a reconstruction of events.

**What the Verse Contributes to the Warning**

The verse follows the questioning of the recipients of the message and of the messengers, and it supplies the reason the questioning can be conclusive. The sūrah's opening has described the destruction of the communities in the world and the confession they made when it was too late; the verse in front of us moves the reckoning to the day when nothing can be concealed and no excuse can be invented.

The Book's use of the same theme appears in its warnings to the audience of the Prophet. The chapter of the Bee says that God will ask the recipients of the message about their response, and the sūrah of the Cattle says that the people who forget God will be informed of what they did. The verse in front of us states the basis of those warnings.

**The Reckoning and the Record**

The Book's presentation of the record gives weight to the sūrah's insistence on the revelation that has been sent down. The community is told to follow what has come to it, and the verse in front of us states that the same revelation's audience will have to give an account of what it did with the message. The knowledge of God and the record of deeds meet in the verse, and the audience is left with the plain statement that its conduct is written.

The Book's descriptions of the day include the testimony of the limbs and the tongues of the condemned, and the sūrah of the Heights itself will describe the dialogue between the people of Paradise and the people of the Fire. This verse supplies the foundation of those scenes, since a reckoning conducted with knowledge requires no witnesses beyond the Lord who speaks.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands seventh in the chapter's opening section, and it completes the pair formed with the verse before it. The questioning is stated in the sixth verse, the recounting in the seventh, and the scales are set in the eighth and ninth. The opening section of the sūrah thus presents the full sequence of the last judgment in four verses.

The reader who has been told to follow what has been sent down is now given the account he will face if he does not. The sūrah's warning does not rest on the ruins of the earlier cities alone; it rests on the knowledge of the Lord who has sent the message and who will recount what each community did with it.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that the weighing on that day will be just, and that those whose scales are heavy will be the successful (7:8). The passage in front of us has stated that the deeds are recounted with knowledge; the verse that follows states the standard by which they are weighed.

The two verses together describe the reckoning in its parts: the account is complete and the measure is just. The sūrah then proceeds to the fate of the two groups, and the reader is left with the plain bookkeeping of the day on which no one is wronged.
