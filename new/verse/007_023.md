## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:23

> They replied, “Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Words of the Pair**

The verse reports the answer that Adam and his wife gave to the divine question, and their words combine an admission, an appeal, and a statement of what is at stake. They acknowledge that they have wronged themselves, they ask for forgiveness and mercy, and they state that without both they will be among the losers. The Book's account in the sūrah of the Cow reports that Adam received words from his Lord and that God turned to him in forgiveness (2:37), and the sūrah of Ṭā Hā reports the same sequence of disobedience followed by forgiveness (20:122).

The confession in front of us is the model of the response that the sūrah commends, and the commentary tradition contrasts it with the answer that Iblīs gave to the same kind of questioning. The enemy argued about the comparative nobility of fire and clay and then asked for time; the pair admit their fault and ask for what will save them. The two answers stand at the head of the sūrah's teaching about repentance.

**The Admission of Wrong Against Oneself**

The pair's first words state that they have wronged themselves, and the phrase is the Book's standard description of the harm that a person brings on himself by disobedience. The sūrah of Yūnus states that God does not wrong people in the least but that people wrong themselves, and the sūrah of the Family of Imran repeats the statement in its accounts of the battles and the losses of the community. The confession in front of us applies the description to the first human act of disobedience.

The commentary tradition draws attention to the fact that the admission is made before any sentence is pronounced on the pair. They do not wait for the outcome to be announced; they name their own fault at the first question. The Book's treatment of the confession in the sūrah of the Women states that if the wrongdoers had come to the Prophet and sought forgiveness and he had asked it for them, they would have found God relenting and merciful (4:64), and the scene in front of us presents the original instance of that response.

**The Appeal for Forgiveness and Mercy**

The pair ask for two things, and the commentary tradition distinguishes them. Forgiveness covers the offence and removes its consequence, and mercy supplies what the offender needs beyond the removal of the penalty. The Book pairs the two terms in its accounts of divine response throughout, and the sūrah of the Troops tells those who have exceeded all limits against their own souls not to despair of the mercy of God, since He forgives all sins (39:53). The pair's prayer in front of us stands at the head of that tradition.

The Book's instructions to the community about seeking forgiveness often include the acknowledgment of the offence before the petition, and the sūrah of the Heights itself will present the community's confession in a later verse. The words in front of us supply the pattern for both parts of the prayer.

**What Is at Stake in the Appeal**

The pair state that without forgiveness and mercy they will be among the losers, and the term connects their situation with the account of the scales that the sūrah's opening has given. The loss they name is the loss of the self that the sūrah attributes to those whose balance is light (7:9), and their language ties the first fall to the general reckoning that the chapter describes. The pair know the consequence of the wrong they have done.

The Book's use of the word for loss appears in its descriptions of the condemned in the Hereafter, and the sūrah of the Believers states that those whose scales are light will lose themselves. The confession in front of us is therefore placed within the framework the sūrah has established, and the mercy they request is the means by which the loss is avoided.

**The Contrast With the Adversary**

The Book's narrative places the two answers to the divine questioning in the same passage, and the contrast is deliberate. The adversary claimed a reason for his act and asked for time; the pair admitted the act and asked for forgiveness. The sūrah's later accounts of the prophets and their peoples repeat the contrast, since the peoples who are destroyed refuse the message and answer the warnings with arguments while the prophets answer their own failures with repentance.

The commentary tradition reads the scene as the founding example of the difference between the proud and the humble, and the sūrah's opening verses on the weighing of deeds supply the framework in which the example is set. The pair's confession is brief, and its brevity is part of its force; the Book records no defence from them and no request that the consequence be lifted apart from the appeal to mercy.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands twenty-third in the chapter, and it answers the divine question of the previous verse. The narrative then reports the command to descend and the terms of life on the earth, and the sūrah's instruction to the children of Adam about clothing, food, and worship follows that. The confession in front of us closes the account of the fall and opens the account of life in the world.

The reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening has been given the revelation, the command, the warning, the reckoning, the adversary's story, and the story of the first pair. The verse in front of us records the answer that the chapter holds up to its own audience, and the instruction that follows is addressed to people who have heard both answers.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse reports the command given to the pair and to the adversary to descend, with each as an enemy to the other, and it states that the earth will provide them with a dwelling and a provision for a time (7:24). The forgiveness requested in front of us is not presented as a return to the Garden, and the narrative proceeds to the conditions of life in the world.

The two verses together state what followed the fall. The confession was accepted, the descent was commanded, and the earth became the place of dwelling until the term appointed for it. The sūrah then states the cycle of life, death, and resurrection that the earth contains.
