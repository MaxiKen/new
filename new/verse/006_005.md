## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:5

> They have indeed rejected the truth when it came to them, so they will soon face the consequences of their ridicule.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial of Something Present, Not Something Absent**

The truth came to them, and the coming is part of the charge. This is the standard Qur'anic description of rejection: not a verdict reached in a library after weighing evidence, but a decision made in the presence of the thing denied. When the messengers came with clear proofs, the ones who had been proud of their own knowledge were the ones destroyed (40:83), and the Book gives the same sequence twice over when it reports people turning from every new reminder and then being overtaken by what they mocked (26:5–6).

The word order matters for the reader who thinks unbelief is a matter of insufficient data. The verse knows no such category. The reminder was delivered, understood as a reminder, and refused as one, and the sūrah has already treated that refusal as a choice made with the shoulder rather than with the mind.

**"What They Used to Mock" Comes Back as Its Own Answer**

A formula runs through the Book: the mockers are overwhelmed by the very thing they used to mock. It appears when punishment is expected to be delayed (11:8), it appears as a consolation to the mocked Prophet (21:41), and it appears twice in the account of what happens when the consequences of deeds unfold before their eyes (39:48; 45:33). The repetition is not rhetorical padding; it is a doctrine about the relationship between contempt and consequence.

Mockery works by naming a thing as too small to matter. The Book reverses the operation: the object of contempt becomes the matter that cannot be avoided. The people who laughed at the warning about a Day will be the people who meet the Day, and their laughing will have bought them nothing in the interval. The verse's closing clause is therefore not a threat attached to the denial; it is the natural outcome of it, stated in the register of a debt falling due.

**What They Were Mocking Was Resurrection**

The specific joke in Makkah is recorded elsewhere in the Book. They ask, once we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected (23:82)? They say mockingly that bones and ashes will be raised as a new creation (17:49), and they argue about who will give life to decayed bones (36:78). The tone in each report is the tone of people who think the claim is not merely unlikely but absurd, and the subject of the joke is the one event that would end it.

Mocking the resurrection has a peculiar self-sealing quality, because the evidence for it arrives only at the moment when the joke is no longer useful. That is the shape of the danger this verse warns of. Not that mockery is punished for its tone, but that mockery has chosen the side of the question that cannot be revisited.

**The Tidings Read as Badr**

Commentators took the promised tidings to be a disaster inside the mockers' own lifetime, and the reading with the widest support among them points to the defeat of the Quraysh at Badr in the second year of the migration, 2/624. The leading figures of the mockery were the leading figures of that march, and the caravan dispute that drew them out ended with the most powerful of them in the field instead of the market.

The reading fits the Makkan setting of the verse. A warning delivered in a city that had heard nothing but its own laughter would be heard first as a claim about this world, and the audience itself would live to see the claim tested. That is what makes the verse's brevity so heavy: the tidings were not filed away for the end of history.

**Mockery Was the Standing Response to Every Messenger**

The Prophet is not being handed a unique hardship. A line repeated with small variations across the Book says that no messenger came to a people without being mocked (15:11; 36:30; 43:7). The sūrah of the Heights puts the complaint in the mouths of the mockers themselves, taunting whether this is the one who speaks ill of their gods (21:36), and the earliest Makkan reaction to revelation was to call the messenger a clear magician (10:2). Mockery is the oldest costume in the audience's wardrobe, and every generation has found that it fits.

The repetition serves two purposes here. It supplies evidence for the charge of rejection, since the treatment received by this Prophet matches the treatment recorded of messengers before him, and it locates the fault in the response rather than in the message. The Book says as much in its own voice: it is not your honesty that is being questioned, but the signs of God that the wrongdoers deny (6:33). The joke is aimed at the messenger and lands on the message, which is why the next clause of the verse treats ridicule as a debt with a due date.

**Two Readings of the Punishment**

Not everyone restricted the tidings to a battlefield. Some early commentators held that the punishment in view is an earthly one, since the line of the verse concerns the affairs of its hearers, while others read it as covering either this world or the next, since nothing in the wording limits where the account is settled. The second position rests on the repeated use of the same idiom for the reversal that unfolds in the Hereafter (39:48; 45:33).

The first is stronger for this verse's situation. Addressing an audience that denies the very assembly the Hereafter assumes, the sūrah reaches for a proof they can still witness, and the reports of Badr show that the Quraysh themselves came to read that defeat as something more than a commercial setback. The general reading is not wrong; it is simply broader than the moment demands. Both agree on the essential point: the mocking does not travel free.
