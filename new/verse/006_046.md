## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:46

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Imagine if Allah were to take away your hearing or sight, or seal your hearts—who else other than Allah could restore it?” See ˹O Prophet˺ how We vary the signs, yet they still turn away.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Faculties Named One by One**

The verse takes three gifts the audience used every hour and asks who could return them if they were withdrawn. Hearing, sight, and the heart are named because they are the whole apparatus of understanding: the ear receives, the eye observes, and the heart is where a person decides what the information means. The Book elsewhere treats the heart as the seat of comprehension rather than of emotion, and the sealing of hearts appears in several places as the end of that comprehension (2:7; 16:108; 45:23).

The question is put as a hypothesis rather than as a threat. Nobody is being told that the faculties have been removed; they are being asked to imagine the situation and to name a candidate who could undo it. The Book has already answered that question about harm and benefit in general a few verses earlier, where it said that none can undo harm except Him (6:17), and it repeats the answer elsewhere with the same firmness (10:107).

**The Question the Sūrah Has Asked Before**

This is the third time in the sūrah that an appeal of this shape has been made, and the repetition builds a case rather than padding one. Earlier, the audience was asked who rescues them from the darkest times on land and at sea (6:63), and a few verses before this one they were asked about being overwhelmed by the punishment (6:40). Each question uses something the audience already knows, and each one strips away another layer of the claim that the idols had standing.

The pattern in this verse is more exacting, because the loss described is not an external danger. It is the loss of the means by which a person could notice the danger at all. A man who cannot hear, cannot see, and cannot understand has no resource left, and the verse leaves him with a question that no shrine in Makkah could answer.

**The "Varying" of the Signs**

The second half of the verse turns to the method of revelation itself: see how We vary the signs, yet they still turn away. The Book uses this vocabulary repeatedly, and the sūrah names the practice in more than one place. To vary the signs means to bring the same truth through different arguments, images, and parables, so that an audience cannot claim it was offered only one kind of proof, or that the message depended on a single demonstration.

The idea appears where God says that every kind of example has been set out for people in this recitation, though most of them persist in disbelief (17:89), and where the sūrah says the parables and warnings are presented in various ways (18:54; 20:113). The verse therefore presents the diversity of the message as an act of care by God, and the audience's turning away as a response to a generosity they have no ground to complain about.

**Two Readings of What the Signs Are**

Commentators observe that the word for signs can refer both to the recited verses and to the created order, since both are called by the same term. On that reading, the varied signs include the alternation of day and night, the revived earth, and the ordinary facts of human dependence, alongside the arguments presented in the revelation itself. The sūrah of Fussilat supports the wider reading when it says that God will show people His signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this message is the truth (41:53).

The two readings do not compete. A person who has heard the recitation has already seen the signs in his own body, and the verse's closing complaint is that he turns away from both at once, which is the reason the sūrah's descriptions of denial keep returning to the use a person makes of what he already possesses.

**The Emphasis on Sight at the End**

The verse closes with the Prophet as the observer of the turning away: see how they still turn away. The same instruction was given earlier in the sūrah, where the command to travel through the land and look at the fate of the deniers was followed by the observation that it is not the eyes that go blind but the hearts inside the chests (22:46). Here the object of sight is not a ruin but a living refusal that the Prophet encounters daily.

That emphasis carries a message for him. The refusal is visible and it is habitual, and the verse treats it as something to be looked at rather than something to be argued away. The sūrah then continues with the subjects of punishment, warning, and the treatment of the poor, as though to show the Prophet what the argument looks like when it is translated into the practical decisions of a community.

**What the Question Does to the Audience**

A sentence that asks who could restore your hearing if it were taken is difficult to answer with the name of a stone. It puts the audience in the position of a person imagining the loss of everything he uses to make sense of the world, and it leaves him with only one possible reply. The Book does not require the reply to be spoken; it supplies the conclusion through the question mark.

The verse therefore functions as a demonstration rather than as a threat, and the demonstration lands on a matter of fact. Whatever else the Makkans disputed with the Prophet, they had no story about who gave them their eyes, and the sūrah records their answer to a similar question in another passage, where they concede that it is Allah who created the heavens and the earth (29:61). The verse simply brings that admission home to their own bodies.
