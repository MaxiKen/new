## Sūrah an-Naml 27:74

> And surely your Lord knows what their hearts conceal and what they reveal.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter's Recurring Claim, Stated Plainly**

This assertion has appeared three times in an-Naml now. The hoopoe used it against Sheba's idolatry, describing God as knowing what is concealed and what is disclosed. Verse 50 demonstrated it in narrative, with nine conspirators inside a plan they could not see. Here it is stated flatly about the present audience.

The repetition tracks the chapter's structure. First the principle is voiced by a character, then it is shown operating in history, and now it is applied to the people listening. That is the same movement the whole sūrah makes — argument, demonstration, application — and this verse is where the third stage arrives for this particular claim. Nothing new is being asserted. What changes is who is being described.

**"What Their Breasts Conceal"**

The Arabic locates the concealment in the *ṣudūr*, the chests, and the Qur'an uses this term dozens of times for the seat of hidden thought and intention. What a person keeps in the chest is what nobody else can reach.

The physical metaphor is worth taking seriously because of what it implies. A chest is a container with a lid; things are kept in it deliberately, and keeping them there takes effort. The Qur'an's language for the inner life is consistently of this kind — things are held, covered, sealed, or brought out — rather than the language of a transparent interior that simply happens to be invisible. That framing makes concealment an act rather than a condition, which is why the verse pairs it with disclosure. Both are things people do, and the claim is that the two are equally available to God.

**Concealment Is Where Sincerity Is Decided**

Why does the Qur'an return to this attribute so often? Because the gap between what is held and what is shown is where the moral reality of a person is located.

Public conduct can be produced by pressure, habit or calculation, and a community can be full of people performing correctly for reasons that have nothing to do with belief. The Qur'an addresses this repeatedly, warning about those who say with their mouths what is not in their hearts. What this verse asserts is that the performance is not the record. It also cuts in the other direction, and the chapter has shown that side too — Sheba's queen worshipped the sun in public while, on one reading of verse 42, already knowing something she had not yet declared. The attribute is not only a threat.

**Placed Immediately After a Statement of Bounty**

The previous verse said God is bountiful to mankind and most are ungrateful. This one says He knows what is hidden and what is shown. The order matters.

Ingratitude is often an interior matter. A person can receive a benefit, say the right thing about it, and privately regard it as his due — and no observer could tell the difference between him and someone genuinely thankful. Verse 73 names the failure; verse 74 says it is visible. Together they close the gap that gratitude most easily slips through. Sulaymān's reaction to the throne is the counter-example the chapter has already supplied: his first words on receiving an extraordinary gift concerned whether he would be thankful, which is a man examining exactly the interior this verse describes.

**Knowledge Without a Verdict Attached**

The verse states what God knows and stops. No punishment is named, no reward, no consequence at all. That restraint is characteristic of how the Qur'an deploys this attribute.

A statement of omniscience does not need a threat attached to do its work, and adding one would narrow it. As it stands, the sentence applies equally to the mockers demanding a date, the believers enduring them, and every reader since. What each of those parties has hidden differs, and the verse addresses all of them with the same words. The Qur'an makes a nearly identical statement in Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ, and in both places the effect is the same: a fact is asserted, and the hearer supplies the application from his own knowledge of what he is holding. The next verse widens the claim from human interiors to everything hidden anywhere.

**Knowing Both, Not Only the Hidden**

The verse names two things and not one: what they conceal *and* what they reveal. A claim about knowing secrets alone would have been the expected form, and the addition of the second term changes the sense.

What it rules out is a picture in which the public life is unimportant and only the interior counts. Both are known, and both are therefore part of the record. That matters for a religion with an extensive outward practice — prayer at fixed times, alms at fixed rates, obligations discharged in front of witnesses. The chapter's third verse marked the believers by exactly such visible acts. If only the hidden were weighed, those would be theatre; if only the visible, the hypocrite would be safe. The pairing keeps both on the ledger, which is the arrangement the chapter's opening description of the believers already assumed.

**An Attribute That Requires No Enforcement**

There is a practical consequence to this claim that the Qur'an relies on throughout its ethics. A community that genuinely holds it needs less policing, because the most effective constraint on conduct is not the likelihood of being caught but the conviction that there is nothing to catch.

Islamic law developed with famously high evidential standards for its severest penalties — requirements so demanding that conviction was rare by design. That is workable only alongside a belief of the kind this verse states. Where the record is complete regardless of what any court establishes, the legal system is not the primary mechanism of restraint and does not need to be. The chapter has already illustrated the failure case in Thamūd's nine, who calculated precisely what their local system could prove and arranged their testimony accordingly. They had understood the law and missed the verse.
