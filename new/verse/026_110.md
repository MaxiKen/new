## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:110

> So fear Allah, and obey me.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah Repeats the Essential Command**

Noah repeats the call to revere God and obey him. The repetition is not a failure of expression. It gives the command emphasis after he has established his trustworthiness and freedom from personal payment. The people have been told who he is and what he does not seek; now the required response is stated again without distraction.

Repetition can be necessary when a community hears a message but refuses to act on it. The command is short because its substance is already clear. Noah is not asking for admiration of his character or compensation for his work. He is asking for taqwa toward God and obedience to the guidance he carries.

**Taqwa Remains the First Allegiance**

The order again begins with reverence for Allah. Noah's authority cannot be understood apart from God's authority. The people are not being transferred from one idol to a human master. They are being called out of false worship into conscious accountability to the Lord.

Taqwa gives obedience its limit and its purpose. If Noah's instruction is accepted as God's guidance, following him is an act of worship. If any human demand contradicts God, it cannot claim the same obedience. The repeated order therefore protects both submission and discernment.

**Obedience Makes the Call Concrete**

A person may say that he reveres God while refusing every instruction that challenges his habits. Noah's command prevents such a vague claim. Reverence must take form in obedience. The people cannot preserve their idols and customs while speaking as though inward fear alone makes them faithful.

Obedience also includes listening before acting. The messenger's command requires the community to give his warning a fair hearing and to let it judge the direction of their lives. The response is not a public gesture performed for a moment. It is a change in the authority under which the people live.

**The Repetition Counters Social Resistance**

A community can turn a simple call into a debate about status, numbers, and influence. Repetition returns the issue to its center. Fear Allah and obey me. Noah does not allow the audience to hide the spiritual question behind a dispute about his popularity or the social position of his followers.

This economy is a form of strength. The messenger does not need a new argument every time the people change their objection. The command remains true even when the excuses multiply. A clear duty does not become unclear because a powerful group refuses to accept it.

The account of Noah in 71:2–3 joins clear warning with worship of Allah, reverence, and obedience. The parallel confirms that this verse summarizes the structure of his mission. He warns because he wants the people to return to God, not because he enjoys announcing their danger.

The combination also guards against a one-sided religious message. Warning without worship can produce fear without direction. Obedience without reverence can produce empty conformity. Noah joins both to the Lord, so the people know whom they fear and why they should follow the messenger.

Noah's repetition gives the people another chance to hear the central demand. The message has not changed, but their response can still change while life continues. Repeated counsel is not necessarily pressure; it can be patience with a community that has not yet understood the danger of its direction.

Mercy does not mean that Noah will conceal consequences. It means he continues to call before judgment arrives. The repeated command keeps the door open for a person who was distracted by custom, pride, or fear and now becomes ready to listen.

Noah does not qualify his command by saying that only chiefs or respected families must obey. The command reaches the whole people. Taqwa is available to the poor and the powerful, and obedience is not made more valuable by social position. The community's later objections will reveal how strongly it wants to make rank a condition of faith.

This verse already rejects that arrangement. God is the One to be revered, and Noah is the messenger to be obeyed. Neither command is limited to an elite. A person with little worldly influence can be nearer to truth than a chief, while a chief's status cannot excuse refusal.

The line ends with a quotation mark because Noah's speech continues as part of one coherent appeal. He does not change into a ruler making a political decree. He remains a messenger addressing his people and directing them to God. The repetition keeps his role visible.

A trustworthy caller repeats the essential truth without making himself the center. Noah's sentence has two objects in proper order: Allah receives reverence, and the messenger receives obedience as God's representative. The people are left with no ambiguity about the response required by the warning.

**The Command Is Still a Test of the Hearer**

The wording is simple enough that the issue cannot be hidden behind complexity. Do they fear God, and will they obey the messenger? A person may understand the sentence and still refuse because obedience threatens comfort or status. The difficulty is not always intellectual; it can be moral.

Noah's repeated call therefore tests the heart's priorities. The believer accepts correction, follows reliable guidance, and keeps God as the final object of fear. The rejecter searches for another excuse. The verse preserves the command in its simplest form so that no one can claim that the central demand was unclear. Its simplicity leaves the responsibility where it belongs: with the hearer who must decide whom to fear and follow. The answer is shown in obedience, not in a claim made only with the tongue. A living response is the proof that the repeated command has been heard.
