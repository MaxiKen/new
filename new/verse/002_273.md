## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:273

> ˹Charity is˺ for the needy who are too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land ˹for work˺. Those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need ˹of charity˺ because they do not beg. You can recognize them by their appearance. They do not beg people persistently. Whatever you give in charity is certainly well known to Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Constrained Poor**

The verse describes the section's most delicate category of recipient: the poor whom poverty has made invisible. The opening participle — *too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land* — carries, the commentators explain, the sense of a confinement: the verb connotes what hinders a traveler or prevents a setting-out. These are people whose commitment to the cause has cost them their mobility — and therefore their livelihood, since the working poor of the verse's world earned by moving: trade routes, day labor, caravan work. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī record the identification the tradition settles on: the People of the Veranda — ahl al-ṣuffah — the poor, homeless Companions who lived in the shaded portico attached to the Prophet's Mosque in Medina, known for their piety, devotion, and asceticism. They could not travel the earth, the commentators give two reasons, because they were too poor to equip a journey or because the roads outside Medina ran hostile — and their confinement *in the way of God* is read both ways: the ṣuffah-people held to the community's life when leaving would have meant safety or subsistence, and the verse honors the holding as the cause's own cost.

**Supposed Rich**

The verse's second sentence states the category's problem with unnerving precision: *those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need, because they do not beg*. The taʿaffuf — the self-restraint, the keeping-oneself-away-from-asking — is the mechanism of the invisibility: the dignified poor are misread by every casual observer, and the misreading runs in the direction that starves them. The commentators draw out the social mechanics: charity in a community flows toward the visible claimant — the asker at the door, the voice in the market — and the restraint that makes a person worthy of admiration makes him absent from the distribution. The verse legislates against this flow with a reversal of evidentiary burden: the silence is not evidence of sufficiency but evidence of the specific need the community is commanded to seek out. The Prophetic ḥadīth the exegetes quote here is the category's definition: the truly indigent person is not the one who goes around receiving morsel after morsel from people — the visible beggar the community's charity reflexively feeds — but the one who has not enough, restrains himself, and does not ask people importunately (in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections). The verse and the ḥadīth together install a second poor behind the first: the fed asker and the unfed silent, and the community's duty to find the second.

**Known by Their Mark**

The verse's third sentence gives the finding its instrument: *you can recognize them by their appearance* — their sīmā, their mark. The commentators read the mark at two levels. The physical: the pallor of hunger, the traces of exertion and hardship on bodies whose need they will not voice — the signs the tradition elsewhere attaches to the righteous community, the mark on the faces from the trace of prostration (48:29), the sīmā-language repeated at the Day's recognition-scenes. And the level the commentators press: al-Rāzī records the reading of the mark as the manifestation of the soul in the body, especially the face — the dignity itself made visible, the restraint readable by the trained eye as exactly what it is. The ḥadīth the exegetes attach here is the tradition of spiritual discernment: beware the believer's firāsah, for he sees by the light of God — firāsah being, in the commentators' gloss, the perceptual skill by which the qualities of a soul are read through the form of the body. The mark of the truly poor, on this reading, is their humility and self-effacement made perceptible — present in the body but not of it — and the verse's instruction to the community is a training command: learn to see the need that presents as its opposite, because the presentation is the need's disguise and the seeing is the charity's first installment.

**Not Asking Importunately**

The verse's fourth sentence returns to the asking, and the tradition reads it as the category's legal boundary: *they do not beg people persistently* — the ilḥāf, the importunity, the pressure of repeated asking. The commentators supply the jurisprudence: the question of when asking becomes ilḥāf was measured by the fiqh tradition against a threshold of possession — a sum, forty dirhams in one transmitted measure, beyond which the asker's request is counted as importunate and blameworthy. The verse's silence-people stand at the far remove from that boundary: their restraint is not a refusal of asking at need but a refusal of the persistence that converts need into pressure. The two sentence-halves of the verse thus describe one ethics of the poor in its two registers — the dignity that does not ask, and the need that, when it asks, does not press — and the community's correlative duty in two registers: to see the unasked need (the mark), and to answer the asked need without making the asker pay in dignity (the 2:263 legislation of the honorable word). The verse is the section's complete circuit: the giving that begins in perception ends in the protection of the perceived.

**Whatever You Give, Allah Knows**

The closing sentence returns the verse to the section's ledger-line — *whatever you give in charity is certainly well known to Allah* — and the repetition from the audit-verse (2:270) is deliberate at just this point. The recipients the verse describes have no publicist: their need is unvoiced, their receipt unwitnessed, their gratitude — in the section's own legislation — not to be collected by the giver in any case. The charity that reaches them is thus doubly invisible: hidden by the giver's secrecy (2:271's better case) and hidden by the recipient's silence. The knowledge-clause answers both invisibilities with one verb: the transaction is known where it occurs, in full, with the mark's reading and the need's reality and the gift's arrival all registered. The commentators connect the clause to the verse's opening identification as well: the ṣuffah-people's confinement *in the way of God* was itself an unseen transaction — a poverty accepted for the cause's sake, invisible as charity and known as sacrifice — and the verse's closing assures both parties of the same record. The section's recipient-legislation ends where its whole ethics began: the giving is between the Knower and the known, the poor are to be sought rather than awaited, and the dignity the silent keep is the dignity the seeing are commanded to keep with them.

**The Verse and the Community's Eye**

Read as the section's closing movement before its final verse, this āyah completes the education the spending-legislation has been running. The parables taught the giver where his gift goes (the grain, the rock, the garden, the burnt orchard); the condition-verses taught what may not follow it (reminders, injury, annulment); the ranking-verses taught where to give it (the good things, the secret hand, past every boundary of creed); and this verse teaches whom to look for — the poor the community's own structures of visibility hide. The ahl al-ṣuffah identification gives the lesson its historical body: the portico's residents were the Prophet's own neighbors, fed from the gifts hung in his mosque (the date-clusters of 2:267's occasion), sustained by a community trained by exactly these verses to read the mark. The verse's permanent instruction is the training itself — that a community's charity is measured not by what it gives to the hand extended but by what it carries to the door not knocked, and that the carrying begins in the eye: the firāsah that sees by God's light, the mark read through the body, the silence recognized as speech. The section's final verse will describe the givers of night and day, secret and open; this verse has described the people their giving must find.
