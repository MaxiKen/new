## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:19

> Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder, and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death. And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Parable for One Condition**

The *or* at the head of the verse signals that this is not a new subject. The fire parable and the storm parable describe the same people from two angles, and the two together cover more than either does alone. The fire was about light lost. The storm is about a person who has not lost the light but cannot use it, because everything around him is moving.

The pairing is deliberate. A man who has lost his fire is in a stable darkness and can, in principle, stop and think. A man in a storm is in a darkness that keeps changing, subjected to noise he cannot shut out and flashes that show him just enough to be frightened. The first parable describes the hypocrite's destination; the second describes his life.

**The Scene**

Everything in the verse is sensory and nothing in it is comfortable. A rainstorm from the sky, and inside it darkness, thunder, and lightning. Rain in the Quran is predominantly a mercy — water sent down, the dead ground brought back — and the sūrah will use it that way three verses later. Here the same rain arrives as an ordeal, which is the first thing the parable wants a reader to notice: the thing that should have been deliverance is the thing doing the damage.

The description gives the three elements in the order a person experiences them. There is the darkness, which is the condition. There is the thunder, which cannot be escaped. And there is the lightning, which is the only help on offer and which arrives as a threat.

**Fingers in the Ears**

The gesture is the most human detail in the sūrah so far. They press their fingers into their ears *at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death* (2:19). Nobody does that about a noise he considers harmless. The panic is real, the danger is understood, and the response is the only one available: block the input.

Two things follow, and both are unflattering. The first is that these people know perfectly well that they are in danger — the charge of unawareness levelled earlier in the passage applies to their condition, not to their experience of it. The second is that their response is aimed at the symptom. Fingers in the ears do nothing about the storm. They block the sound of the thing that is killing them and change nothing else, which is an exact picture of how the hypocrites have been handling every warning they have received.

**The Dispute About the Thunder**

A tradition attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās has the Prophet saying that *al-raʿd*, thunder, is the name of an angel among the clouds. [UNVERIFIED: reported by some commentators and rejected by others as a gratuitous attribution; I could not verify a chain.] Others rejected it outright, on the ground that the word's ordinary sense was well known to the Arabs and that the Quran would not use a common noun for an angel without making it clear.

The disagreement is worth keeping because the rejected reading makes a real point even if its chain fails: the noise in the parable is not meaningless. Another sūrah says so in plain words — *The thunder glorifies His praises, as do the angels in awe of Him* (13:13). What the hypocrites are plugging their ears against is, on the Quran's own account, a voice doing what it was made to do.

**Encompassed**

The verse closes with four words that reframe everything: *Allah encompasses the disbelievers* (2:19). They are running from the thunder, and what they are running through is the One who sent it. The word for encompasses is the one used for a wall around a city; there is no direction left in which to flee.

The irony is exact and the Quran uses it elsewhere. These people have spent the passage managing their position, keeping options open, refusing to commit to either side — and the closing line of the parable states that there was never any outside to escape to. A person who will not take refuge in God is not thereby in neutral ground.

**What the Storm Describes**

Read as a picture of a life, the parable is uncomfortably precise. The hypocrite is not calm; the sūrah's earlier verses and the next one both describe him as terrified and unstable. He is not in darkness because nothing was shown to him — the lightning keeps coming, and each flash shows him the ground for a moment. What he lacks is any interval long enough to walk by.

That is the condition the sūrah has been diagnosing from the beginning: not ignorance, but clarity that arrives in bursts, is unusable, and leaves the person more frightened than he was before. The storm is what happens to someone who has been given light and will not hold it.

The parable also explains why these people are so difficult to reach, and it does so more honestly than a moral verdict would. A man in a storm is not indifferent to his danger — he is preoccupied with it. Every resource he has is going into getting through the next thunderclap. There is no attention left for the question of how he came to be out in it, or which direction leads to shelter. The sūrah's diagnosis of the hypocrites is not finally that they do not care; it is that the life they chose has consumed the capacity to care about anything else.
