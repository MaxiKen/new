## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:19

> Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder, and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death. And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Likeness Introduced by a Choice of Word**

The verse begins with the Arabic *aw*, or, which marks an alternative rather than a new topic. The Book's custom is to place two comparisons where one would do, and the two here describe the same people under different pressures. The first likeness showed a light withdrawn; this one shows a storm that will not stop, and the men caught under it are handling it by covering their ears.

The change of scene is a change of problem. In the first parable the question was what the man had and lost. In the second, the question is what a person does when the sky is loud, the ground is dark, and something dangerous is suddenly visible in flashes. Both images come from a landscape where rain is an event and lightning is a threat, and both take the weather seriously as a way of describing spiritual exposure.

**The Storm and Each of Its Elements**

The Arabic word rendered rainstorm, *ṣayyib*, names a cloud heavy with rain, and the verse then lists its contents: darkness, thunder, lightning. Rain that arrives in the desert is a gift, and this verse turns the gift into the setting of a crisis, because the men in it experience the sky only as noise and danger. The Book elsewhere describes the same sky in terms of its beauty and mercy: *“Have they not then looked at the sky above them: how We built it and adorned it ˹with stars˺, leaving it flawless?”* (50:6).

The elements are not decorative. Thunder and lightning appear together in the Qur'an as signs that produce fear in the observer: *“He is the One Who shows you lightning, inspiring ˹you with˺ hope and fear, and produces heavy clouds.”* (13:12), and the verse that follows reports that the thunder itself glorifies God's praises along with the angels (13:13). Two verses later in this sūrah, the same flash is described as nearly destroying the eyesight of the men who depend on it (2:20), and elsewhere as almost taking away eyes: *“The flash of the clouds' lightning nearly takes away eyesight.”* (24:43).

**Fingers in the Ears at Every Clap**

The gesture is described with an unusual amount of physical detail: the fingers are pressed into the ears, and they are pressed at each thunderclap rather than once for the duration of the storm. The Book is showing men reacting repeatedly, and the repetition is the point — a person who has decided how to handle a danger has to keep on handling it.

What the gesture states is the absence of any other response. The men do not flee, argue with the sky or help each other; they stop their ears and wait for the sound to pass. The Qur'an's portraits of the hypocrites elsewhere show the same pattern of minimal self-protection, and one verse says it plainly: *“The hypocrites, both men and women, are all alike: they encourage what is evil, forbid what is good, and withhold ˹what is in˺ their hands.”* (9:67). The detail of the fingers fits a people whose whole method is damage limitation.

**Fearing Death in a Storm**

The verse names the motive: fear of death. That phrase is what lifts the description out of the weather and into the sūrah's argument. What these men are protecting is their lives, and the storm is only the occasion on which that priority becomes visible. Given a threat to their safety, they shut out the thing they cannot control and keep their attention on what they can.

The Book treats fear of death as a diagnostic. It tells the Prophet that among these people he will find those who cling to life, and it records the same priority in their refusal to risk anything for the community they publicly belong to. The storm in the parable is a small laboratory: it applies pressure for a few minutes and shows what a man's loyalty is anchored to. Nothing about the sky is unusual, and nothing about the response would surprise anyone who had watched these men in a week of ordinary politics.

**Encompassed, Which Leaves No Route Out**

The verse closes on God: He encompasses the disbelievers. The Arabic participle describes a circle drawn completely around something, so that no gap remains on any side. The Book uses the same root for the divine throne: *“His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him.”* (2:255), and it uses it when it tells the Prophet that the community's enemies cannot outmanoeuvre the one who holds the whole field in view.

That closing clause is what the men in the story do not know. They are concentrating on the two ways the weather can hurt them, hearing and sight, and the sentence states that neither of those is the real situation. The Book's answer to their panic is not reassurance about the storm; it is the information that they were never outside the circle to begin with, and that the one who drew it is measuring the same thunder and the same darkness against them.

**What the Second Parable Adds**

The first likeness took a man's light away, and the second leaves its subjects in the dark with the lights still flashing. In the first, the crisis is that something has been removed; here the crisis is that something keeps arriving and cannot be absorbed. Taken together, the two parables describe the same spiritual position from two sides, and the sūrah's portrait of hypocrisy closes with an image of people whose environment is beyond their control and who have no plan except to cover their ears.
