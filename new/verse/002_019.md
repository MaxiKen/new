## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:19

> Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder, and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death. And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**“Or” That Offers a Second Parable, Not a Correction**

The verse opens with *aw* — “or” — which in Qur'anic idiom does not cancel the previous parable (2:17-18) but offers another face of the same condition. The fire parable showed a solitary kindler whose light was taken; the rainstorm parable shows a crowd caught in a shared downpour. Both describe the hypocrite, but from complementary angles: the first as interior privation, the second as exterior terror. The *or* therefore does not say “or perhaps it is like this instead”; it says “or — if you prefer — it is also like this.” That is why the classical commentators (Ṭ) treat the *or* as a continuation (*aw* in the sense of *bal*), not a disjunction. The hypocrite is both the lonely fire-kindler left in his own darkness and the exposed traveler caught in a sky that has turned against him.

**Darkness, Thunder, Lightning — Three Overlays**

*Ẓulumātin wa-raʿdin wa-barq* — “darkness, thunder, and lightning.” The three overlays map exactly to the three privations just named — deaf, dumb, blind (2:18) — but now from the side of the world rather than the side of the person. Darkness corresponds to blindness: the world is dark, the eyes cannot see. Thunder corresponds to deafness: the world roars, the ears cannot bear it. Lightning corresponds to speechlessness: the world flashes a sign, but the sign cannot be articulated into guidance, only fled from. The rainstorm (*ṣayyib* — a cloud that pours) adds a fourth element — water from the sky (*min al-samāʾ*) — so that the scene contains the same materials that elsewhere are mercy — rain that brings fruits as provision (2:22) — now turned into affliction. The same sky that was made a canopy (2:22) becomes the source of a downpour that exposes.

**Fingers in Ears Against Thunderclaps, Fearing Death**

*Yajʿalūna aṣābiʿahum fī ādhānihim mina al-ṣawāʿiqi ḥadhara al-mawt* — “they press their fingers into their ears at every thunderclap for fear of death.” The image is deliberately excessive. A finger in the ear cannot stop thunder; it can only signal the wish that it would. The hypocrite’s response to revelation is figured as a bodily reflex against a sound that cannot be blocked, fearing the death that the sound portends. That death is not merely physical. The thunder (*raʿd*) and thunderclap (*ṣāʿiqah*) in Qur'anic usage are the audible register of warning itself — the warner’s voice (*nadhīr*), the “thunderclap” that seized prior peoples who rejected. To press fingers in ears is to attempt to make warning and its absence the same, as the persistent rejector had attempted in (2:6), but now with the panic that the attempt has failed. The thunder is heard despite the fingers, and the hearing, because it is not accompanied by understanding, becomes only terror.

**Thunder as Angel — A Report That Divides the Commentators**

The source notes a tradition attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, when asked, that *al-raʿd* is the name of an angel among the clouds, while other commentators dismiss the attribution as gratuitous, since *raʿd* as “thunder” was well known to Arabs and the Qur'an itself uses the word for the audible phenomenon (13:13) [UNVERIFIED: Ibn ʿAbbās “thunder is an angel” report — transmitted in al-Ṭabarī as tafsīr athar without graded prophetic isnād; some (Q) deem it an Israelite embellishment; treated here as exegetical report, not as hadith]. The dispute matters less for meteorology than for method. The commentators who reject the angel gloss are protecting the Qur'an’s own Arabic intelligibility against an unnecessary etiology; those who transmit it are preserving a piece of early cosmology that links sound to agency. The sūrah does not need the angel to make its point; it needs the thunder to be heard as warning, whether the sound’s agent is named or not. The Qur'an’s own verse — *“the thunder hymns His praise”* (13:13) — already gives thunder a liturgical agency without requiring a proper name.

**“And Allah Encompasses the Disbelievers” — A Circle Drawn Around the Scene**

*Wa-Allāhu muḥīṭun bi-l-kāfirīn* — “And Allah encompasses the disbelievers” — closes the verse with a circle. The verb *aḥāṭa* — to surround, to comprehend, to encompass — gathers the storm’s darkness, thunder, and lightning into a single divine act of encompassing. The disbelievers are not merely caught in weather; they are encompassed by the One who sent the weather. The same root appears in *“Allah is encompassing of all things”* and in the description of the Preserved Tablet’s encompassing. Here the encompassing is not a promise of protection but of accountability: the fingers in ears do not place the hearer outside the circle; they place him at its center, where the sound he tries to block still reaches him, and where the One who encompasses knows the hearing that is being refused. The phrase *“by His might”* in the translation — *bi-qudratihi* — is an interpretive addition that names what the encompassing implies: power that cannot be evaded by a gesture.

**Sky as Samāʾ — Canopy and Downpour in One Word**

*Samāʾ* — sky — appears here as the source of the rainstorm and will appear two verses later as the canopy made for settlement (2:22). The same noun carries both — the firmament that shelters and the sky that pours. The hypocrite’s scene exploits the second while forgetting the first. The sky that was made a *saqf* — a roof, a canopy — to make the earth a place of settlement (*firāsh*, 2:22) becomes, when its signs are mocked (2:14) and its guidance is traded (2:16), the sky that pours darkness and thunder. The word’s doubleness teaches that creation is not neutral material that can be used indifferently; the same creation that provisions (2:22) can expose, and the difference is not in the sky but in the relation of the people beneath it to the One who made it canopy and cloud.

**A Storm That Is Also a Community**

Finally, the rainstorm parable, unlike the solitary fire, is collective. The verbs are plural — *they press, they fear* — and the darkness, thunder, and lightning are shared. The hypocrites are not alone in a private darkness (2:17); they are together in a common storm, hearing the same thunder, seeing the same flashes, pressing fingers in the same way. The fire parable showed the loneliness of a light taken; the storm parable shows the company of a terror shared. Both are true of hypocrisy, which isolates the hypocrite in his own darkness while also gathering hypocrites into a recognizable weather — a social climate of darkness, noise, and intermittent illumination that the next verse will describe as walking by flashes and halting in darkness. The storm that encompasses is therefore also the community that hypocrisy creates for itself: a crowd that cannot hear because it is stopping its ears together.
