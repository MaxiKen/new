## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:19

> Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder, and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap for fear of death. And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Parable Widens the Scene**

The particle *aw* — "or" — links this image to the fire of verse 17 as a second exposure of the same subject: another parable, or their case is like the case of people caught in a storm. The scale change is total. The first image was domestic — one man, one flame, one plain; this one is cosmological — a sky, a downpour, a weather system with the victims inside it. The bracketed *˹those caught in˺* in the translation states the grammar's implication: the hypocrites are no longer standing beside a fire they lit but enclosed in a rainstorm they did not make and cannot leave. Commentators have read the two parables as complementary states — the extinguished flame depicting the end of borrowed light, the storm depicting the daily weather of a life lived under revelation one refuses. Together they cover the type in both tenses: what hypocrisy ends in, and what it feels like from inside while it lasts.

**One Sky, Three Weathers**

The storm carries darkness, thunder, and lightning together — *ẓulumāt wa-raʿd wa-barq* — and the traditional mapping of the three is consistent: the darkness is the doubt and denial the hypocrites live in; the thunder is the warning, the menace of the message that makes them flinch; the lightning is its proof and clarity, the flashes of truth their eyes cannot deny. The image's precision lies in the simultaneity. This is not darkness relieved by occasional light, but one sky producing all three at once — exactly as one revelation reaches the hypocrites as guidance and threat and dazzle in a single text. The plural darkness matches verse 17's layered dark; the storm now gives it weather — moving, loud, active. Revelation, in this reading, is not a lamp offered in a neutral room. It is a storm system: the same event that waters the valley of the God-conscious (13:17) deafens and blinds the men standing against it.

**Fingers in Ears: The Posture of the Verse**

The middle of the parable is pure body language: *yajʿalūna aṣābiʿahum fī ādhānihim* — they put their *fingers* into their ears. Not hands over ears, but fingers driven in — the gesture of panic precision, the anatomy of someone trying to seal a passage that cannot be sealed. The reason given is *mina al-ṣawāʿiq*, from the thunderclaps, literally the stunning bolts; and the motive beneath the reason is *ḥadhara al-mawt* — fear of death. The verse has captured the hypocrites' entire religion in one reflex: their relationship to revelation is not assessment but survival. They do not argue with the thunder; they plug against it. Fear of death, in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary, is not piety but the engine of every compromised creed — the attachment to a life whose terms are set by the world explains the lie of verse 8, the deception of verse 9, and the mockery of verse 14 alike. Men who stuff their ears at the truth are not refuting it; they are surviving it, thunderclap by thunderclap.

**Raʿd: A Sound, and a Question About an Angel**

The exegetical tradition attaches one curious debate to the word for thunder. A report from Ibn ʿAbbās has the Prophet ﷺ, asked about *al-raʿd*, describing it as the name of an angel who drives the clouds; other scholars rejected the attribution as gratuitous, since the Arabs knew *raʿd* perfectly well as the sound of thunder, and the Qur'an's usage needs no celestial bureaucrat behind it. The debate is worth less than what both sides share: the Qur'an made thunder a named presence in revelation — Sūrah 13 takes its title from it, and that sūrah's verse describes the sound's true status: *"The thunder glorifies His praises, as do the angels in awe of Him"* (13:13). Whether *raʿd* is an angel or an acoustics, the thunder in the Qur'an's universe is a worshipper — a voice engaged in *tasbīḥ*, the same act the hypocrites' creed lacked. That is the parable's quiet irony at full depth: the men stuffing their ears are not shutting out noise but doxology. The sky over their heads is praying; their fingers are pressed against a hymn.

**Encompassed: The Clause That Closes the Sky**

The verse ends by stepping out of the image to state the doctrine the image performs: *wa-Allāhu muḥīṭun bi-l-kāfirīn* — Allah encompasses the disbelievers. The word *muḥīṭ* carries encirclement on every side, the way an ocean surrounds an island or a siege surrounds a city. Inside the parable the meaning is spatial: there is no edge to the storm, no horizon they can run for, because the weather is not local but total. Outside the parable the meaning is the divine attribute: knowledge and power that leave no exterior, no back room, no exit — the same encompassment that heard the mockery of verse 14 now stated as cosmology. The men in the storm fear death from above while the verse informs them that what is above is not merely lethal but enveloping. Death they might outrun; an encompassing God is the condition of every direction they could run. The clause is the parable's wall of sky made explicit — and it names its occupants not as hypocrites here but as disbelievers, filing them under the genus their private creed always held.

**Samāʾ: The Word Above the Scene**

One lexical note sharpens the whole image. The storm comes *mina al-samāʾ* — from the *samāʾ*, a word whose range runs from sky to heaven to firmament to simply "above," chosen by context. The commentators flag the range, and the parable exploits it: what descends on these men is meteorological and revelatory at once. In the Qur'an's consistent vertical grammar, guidance comes down — water from the sky, books sent down, angels descending — and the storm obeys the grammar while inverting its mercy: the descent that waters the believer's valley arrives at the hypocrites as darkness, concussion, and dazzle. Their response completes the inversion. Instead of receiving what comes down, they press inward and downward, fingers into ears, the only motion available to people who have refused to look up. The verse's geography is therefore moral: a sky full of revelation, a ground full of men whose entire posture is defined by what they will not take from it.
