## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94

> Then the idols will be hurled headlong into Hell, along with the deviant

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idols Are Thrown Into the Fire**

The verse describes the idols being hurled headlong into Hell together with the deviant. The scene reverses the honor given to them in worldly life. They were lifted, decorated, protected, and placed at the center of ritual. Now they are toppled and gathered into punishment. Their treatment reveals what they were worth before God all along.

The action is not the punishment of a true deity. The idols have no divine power and no independent life. Their presence in the Fire is part of the exposure of those who worshipped them. The objects become evidence against the false system that assigned them a sacred status. What people treated as protectors is shown as incapable of protecting even the role they were given.

**Headlong Captures a Violent Reversal**

The translation headlong evokes a fall without dignity or control. The Arabic verb kabkaba also carries the sense of tumbling and being gathered together. The word makes the image physical. The idols are not escorted as honored guests; they are overturned and thrown down. Their public elevation has become a humiliating collapse.

That reversal answers the pride surrounding idolatry. A shrine may be placed high, a statue may be polished, and a priesthood may guard its authority. None of those arrangements can prevent the object from being brought low. The final movement of the idol shows that created things cannot retain an honor that belongs only to God.

**The Idols and the Deviant Share a Destination**

The verse places the idols and the deviant together. This does not mean that a stone bears moral responsibility in the same way as a human being. The people chose worship; the idols were the objects through which that false choice was organized. Their shared placement makes the relationship visible: the worshippers are brought face to face with what they served.

The scene also shows the emptiness of partnership. The idols cannot separate themselves from the punishment, advocate for their worshippers, or offer an alternative refuge. The deviant cannot appeal to them as gods because their own position has been exposed. The relation that seemed to promise protection now unites both sides in humiliation.

**Alternative Readings Still Preserve the Core Warning**

The source tradition records different interpretations of who is included with the idols. Some understand the deviant to be the idolaters themselves; others connect the phrase with leaders or objects of false allegiance. These readings differ over the referent, but they share the central point: the false system and those devoted to it are gathered for judgment.

The interpretation that takes the idols as the immediate object fits the context of the question about what they worshipped. Another reading can illuminate the human leaders who were treated as authorities beside God. Both expose the same transfer of worship. What is created becomes a rival, and those who follow the rival discover that it cannot save them.

**The Punishment Does Not Make the Idols Powerful**

Someone might misunderstand the scene and think that the idols are being punished because they possess real divine agency. The verse does not grant them that status. Their inclusion is a sign of disgrace and exposure. They are objects that people served, not gods who ruled independently.

This distinction protects the doctrine of divine oneness. The Fire is not a battlefield where God defeats rival deities. It is the place where the false claim is displayed as false. The idols are thrown because human beings made them sacred, and their fall shows the futility of giving worship to what cannot hear, help, or save.

**The Fall Answers the Earlier Appeal to Custom**

The idolaters defended their worship by pointing to their forefathers. Generations of practice made the idols seem permanent. The headlong fall shows that inherited permanence is an illusion. A custom can survive centuries in society and still collapse in one moment before God's judgment.

This is a warning for every community that confuses age with authority. Ancestors can transmit wisdom, but their repetition cannot transform a created object into a lord. The fate of the idols makes the distinction visible. They are gathered with those who followed them, not honored because many earlier people had bowed before them.

**The Scene Is a Verdict on Misplaced Reverence**

Reverence belongs to God, while created things may be respected within their proper limits. The idols received sacrifices, fear, and hope that should have been directed to the Lord. Their fall is therefore a verdict on a disordered heart. The objects themselves are not the source of the harm; the worship attached to them is.

A person may avoid physical statues and still elevate created things through unquestioned obedience or absolute trust. The verse asks what receives sacred treatment in practice. Anything that demands worshipful allegiance will ultimately fail its worshippers. The headlong fall is the image of a false center collapsing under the weight of the devotion placed upon it.

**Divine Judgment Restores the True Order**

The verse does more than announce punishment. It restores the distinction between Creator and creation. The Lord remains the Judge, while the idols are objects under judgment. The deviant remains accountable, while the false objects reveal the foolishness of the worship offered to them.

That restoration gives the warning its moral clarity. The proper response is not to fear idols more carefully but to abandon their worship and seek God alone. The ones people treated as protectors are thrown down; the Lord whose authority they denied remains sovereign. The scene ends the illusion that false worship can survive the final unveiling. What seemed powerful in the marketplace is shown to be helpless before the command of resurrection.
