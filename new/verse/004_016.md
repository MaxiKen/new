## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:16

> And the two among you who commit this sin—discipline them. If they repent and mend their ways, relieve them. Surely Allah is ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Discipline for the Pair**

After addressing women who commit the foul act with a four-witness procedure and confinement (4:15), this verse broadens the scope to the pair: *wa alladhāni yaʾtiyānihā minkum fa ādhūhumā* — and the two among you who commit this sin, discipline them. The dual pronoun *alladhāni* and the dual verb *yaʾtiyānihā* make clear that the act involves two persons, and the command to discipline — *ādhūhumā* — is directed to both. Early exegetes differed on whether this verse concerns two men, a man and a woman, or specifically those not covered by the previous verse’s marital context, but all agreed that the principle of shared accountability is central. The Qur’an does not treat unlawful intercourse as a fault borne by one party alone; it addresses both participants as responsible agents. The type of discipline — *adhā* — was understood as harm through rebuke, exposure, and shaming, rather than a fixed corporal penalty at this stage, because the sūrah immediately adds the exit that later legislation would detail. As with verse 15, this ruling sits in a sequence where final penalties arrive later (24:2 and Prophetic clarification), so the immediate sanction is corrective and social rather than final.

**What Discipline Meant at This Stage**

Commentators described *ādhūhumā* as verbal reproach, harsh words, striking with sandals, or other forms of public disapproval that make the offenders feel the community’s rejection without exceeding what the law authorizes. The purpose was deterrence and moral signalling: the household and neighbours see that the act is not treated as private taste but as *fāḥishah* that damages lineage and trust. At the same time, the discipline was not meant to become a second injustice. The sūrah’s broader ethic of *qawlan maʿrūfā* — speaking kindly where due (4:5, 4:8) — remains the norm for ordinary relations; here, a different register — *adhā* — applies because the act itself breached the trust those kind words normally protect. The distinction matters. The community is not asked to be harsh generally; it is asked to be clearly disapproving where a boundary that guards families has been crossed. The Qur’an elsewhere commands *“do not kill yourselves”* (4:29) and elsewhere describes God as ever Merciful (4:29), so even this disciplinary moment is framed as protection, not cruelty.

**If They Repent and Mend Their Ways, Relieve Them**

The pivot of the verse is *fa in tābā wa aṣlaḥā fa aʿriḍū ʿanhumā* — if they repent and mend their ways, relieve them. Repentance — *tawbah* — is not only regret in the heart; it is accompanied by *iṣlāḥ*, mending, repairing what was broken. That mending was understood to include ceasing the relationship, separating from the partner, showing remorse, and returning to behaviour that guards chastity. When those signs appear, the community is commanded to turn away — *fa aʿriḍū* — to stop the discipline, to cease pursuit, to leave them alone. That turning away is itself an act of worship. The verse does not say continue to remind them or keep them marked; it says relieve them. The sūrah will soon define what counts as accepted repentance — done ignorantly or recklessly and then soon after (4:17) — and what does not count — delay until death is at hand (4:18). This verse supplies the bridge between the two: where repentance is shown with amendment, the community’s role ends and divine acceptance begins. The discipline was never meant to brand a person permanently.

**Ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful**

The closing *inn Allāha kāna tawwāban raḥīmā* — surely Allah is ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful — gives the reason for relieving. *Tawwāb* stresses repetition: the One who accepts repentance again and again, not once. *Raḥīm* stresses the mercy that follows acceptance. The pair appears together where the Qur’an wants to emphasize that God’s mercy is not reluctant. The sūrah elsewhere says *“Allah wishes to turn to you in mercy, but those who follow desires wish you to deviate entirely”* (4:27), and *“Allah wishes to lighten your burdens, for humankind was created weak”* (4:28). Those wishes frame this verse’s command to relieve. To continue harshness after repentance would align the community with the deviaters who want people to fall, not with the turn toward mercy that God desires. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse therefore teaches two kinds of mercy: God’s mercy in accepting the return, and the community’s mercy in expressing that acceptance by ceasing harm. Both are needed; divine pardon without social relief leaves the offender isolated, while social relief without divine pardon would be empty.

**A Ruling Held Until a Way Was Ordained**

As with the previous verse’s *“until Allah ordains a different way for them”* (4:15), this verse’s disciplinary measure was understood as an interim stage. Later revelation specified *“As for the fornicatress and the fornicator, lash each of them with a hundred lashes”* (24:2), and Prophetic practice clarified the penalty for the married adulterer, showing that the community had been held in a preparatory discipline until the final way — *sabīl* — was given. Recognizing that progression prevents two misreadings: that the early verse’s confinement or the second verse’s *adhā* remains the final law, or that the later penalties negate the mercy that these early verses displayed. The progression is additive: the early verses taught procedural caution, shared accountability, measured social discipline, and an open door to return; the later verses kept that caution and added precise penalties where proof and conditions were met. The through line is consistent. The law guards lineage from the act, guards the accused from unproven accusation, and guards the repentant from endless shaming.

**Shared Fault and Shared Mercy**

Read together, verses 15 and 16 form a careful balance. One verse sets a high evidentiary bar and a custodial measure for women, with a future way awaited; the next sets shared discipline for the pair and a clear exit when repentance and mending appear. Both verses refuse to treat illicit intercourse as trivial, and both refuse to treat the offender as beyond return. The sūrah’s surrounding chapters on wealth and marriage show why that balance matters here. Family property is guarded by witnesses and reckoning (4:6); family honour is guarded by witnesses as well (4:15); both guards exist because trust is easy to break in private. Yet trust can also be restored. The command to relieve those who have repented and mended teaches that restoration has a form: stop the harm. That form becomes a habit the sūrah will extend beyond this case, into speaking equitably (4:9), trading by mutual consent (4:29), and appointing mediators when a split is feared (4:35). In each, the law asks not only for justice against wrong but for a path back when wrong is amended, because the One who judges is ever Accepting, Most Merciful.
