## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:31

> If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds and admit you into a place of honour.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Avoiding the Major to Be Forgiven the Lesser**

The verse offers a precise bargain: *in tajtanibū kabāʾira mā tunhawna ʿanhu nukaffir ʿankum sayyiʾātikum wa nudkhilkum mudkhalan karīmā* — if you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your lesser misdeeds and admit you into a place of honour. The language was explained as God’s undertaking to handle the small faults for those who guard against the great ones. *Kabāʾir* — major sins — were listed by early authorities with variations, but a core was consistent: associating partners with God, killing a soul, consuming orphan wealth (4:10), devouring wealth illegally and killing deliberately (4:29–30), disobedience to parents, false testimony, and the *ḥudūd* violations the sūrah has detailed such as illicit intercourse (4:15–16), exceeding inheritance limits (4:13–14), and reclaiming dowries by slander (4:20). *Sayyiʾāt* — lesser misdeeds, evil deeds that stain even if smaller — are the slips that weakness (4:28) makes inevitable. The sūrah has already said humankind was created weak and that God wills to lighten and turn toward you (4:26–28); here it gives the mechanism of that lightening: guard the great, and the small is effaced. The promise does not trivialize the small; it says the small is ankles covered when the great dam holds.

**What Counts as Major**

The sūrah itself supplies many examples without needing an exhaustive list. Eating orphan wealth is named as a great sin — *ḥūban kabīrā* (4:2) — and as eating fire (4:10); devouring wealth by falsehood and killing without right is met with burning where *that is easy for Allah* (4:30); exceeding God’s limits in shares is met with abiding humiliation (4:14). Illicit intercourse was described as *fāḥishah* with a high bar of four witnesses to prevent slander (4:15). Inheriting women against their will and pressuring them to surrender dowries is called not permissible (4:19) and then slander and clear sin (4:20). Each of those is a candidate for *kabāʾir* because each betrays a trust that God named as a limit. The sūrah elsewhere warns *“do not kill yourselves”* (4:29) and describes God as ever Merciful to you (4:29), so the major sins are those that, if done *ʿudwānan wa ẓulman* — sinfully and unjustly (4:30) — show a hardened intent rather than a moment of ignorance followed by soon return (4:17). That distinction matters because not every slip is major, and the verse invites the believer to learn the difference. A community that can name its major boundaries is more likely to keep them.

**We Will Absolve You of Your Lesser Misdeeds**

The phrase *nukaffir ʿankum sayyiʾātikum* — We will absolve you of your lesser misdeeds — uses *kaffara* — to cover, to efface — as though the small faults are stains that can be wiped. The covering is God’s act, not a self-erasure through ritual alone, though prayer, charity, and seeking forgiveness were mentioned as means that invite that covering. The Qur’an elsewhere says that good deeds remove evil ones and that seeking forgiveness immediately after a lapse is the mark of the mindful (3:135). This verse adds a structural covering: avoidance of the great itself becomes a cause for wiping the small. That structure keeps the believer from two extremes. One extreme counts small faults as nothing and therefore drifts into the great; the other counts small faults as unforgivable and despairs. The promise addresses both: take the great seriously enough to avoid it, and know that the small, while still needing to be turned from, will be handled with mercy. The sūrah has already promised that for those who repent soon after acting ignorantly, God will pardon (4:17); this verse extends that pardoning logic to the many lesser missteps that never reached the level of the major but still weigh on the heart.

**And Admit You Into a Place of Honour**

The reward *wa nudkhilkum mudkhalan karīmā* — and admit you into a place of honour — was read as entry into the Garden described earlier as *Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, that is the ultimate triumph* (4:13), now named by its quality. *Karīm* — noble, generous, honourable — suggests not only a pleasant location but a reception that honours the entrant. The one who avoided eating orphan wealth, refusing to devour by falsehood, and keeping inheritance and marriage within limits is welcomed as an honoured guest, not as a tolerated entrant. The word *mudkhal* — place of entry, way in — implies that the path itself is honourable, not only the destination. That matches the sūrah’s earlier promise to make things clear and guide to noble ways (4:26). The honourable entry is therefore the counterpart to the humiliating punishment named for exceeding limits (4:14) and to the burning that is easy for God for deliberate devouring and killing (4:30). The sūrah keeps the two ends visible so that the middle path — avoidance of the major — feels both urgent and inviting. Honour becomes the name for the recompense that mercy prepares when the great boundaries are kept.

**A Verse That Lightens Without Loosening**

Read with the verses on weakness and lightening, this verse shows how burden is lifted without law being loosened. *“Humankind was created weak”* (4:28) explains why lesser faults will occur; *“We will absolve you of lesser misdeeds”* explains how they are handled when the great is avoided. The law remains firm where it must — the orphan’s wealth with witnesses (4:6), the fixed shares after debts (4:11–12), the bar on reclaiming a heap of gold (4:20), the bar on killing and devouring (4:29) — but the moral economy around the law is merciful. The believer is not asked to be flawless; he is asked to be serious about the great and quick to return from the small. Prophetic tradition listed seven destructive sins and taught that avoiding them and maintaining prayer, paying alms, and seeking forgiveness secures that covering, which shows that the verse was heard as both comfort and discipline. Comfort, because perfection is not demanded; discipline, because the major is not negotiable. Between those poles the weak person can walk without despair and without complacency, knowing that the One who is All-Knowing, All-Wise (4:26) knows which faults are major, and that the One who is ever Merciful (4:29) has promised to cover the rest when the great is avoided, and to admit to honour those He covers.
