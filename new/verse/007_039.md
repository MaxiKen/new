## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:39

> Then the leaders will say to their followers, “You were no better than us! So taste the torment for what you used to commit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply of the Earlier to the Later**

The verse reports the answer that the first of the communities give to the last, and the answer rejects the claim that the later generations were led astray without responsibility. The leaders state that the followers were no better than they were, and they call on them to taste the punishment for what they earned. The verse completes the exchange that the previous verse began, and it closes the attempt to transfer the burden from one group to another.

The Book's treatment of the mutual accusations of the Hereafter appears in several passages, and the sūrah of the Confederates presents the followers addressing their leaders with the request that they be given a double punishment (33:68). The reply in front of us is the answer of the accused leaders, and it consists in the observation that the followers made the same choice. The sūrah's teaching about responsibility is stated in the exchange: each group bears what it earned.

**You Were No Better Than Us**

The phrase states the equality of the two groups in the matter that produced their condition. The followers had claimed that they were led astray, and the reply points out that they followed willingly and shared the same denial. The Book's descriptions of the followers in the world include their eagerness to imitate their leaders and their refusal to consider the message on its own terms, and the sūrah's passage on the practices of the forefathers has already described the same willingness.

The observation does not excuse the leaders, since the previous verse has stated that the punishment is multiplied for both. What it does is remove the distinction on which the followers' request depended. Neither group can claim to have been carried into error, and the verse in front of us states the equality in plain words.

**Tasting the Punishment for What Was Earned**

The second half of the reply calls on the followers to taste the punishment for what they earned, and the phrase assigns the punishment to the deeds of those who receive it. The Book's language about earning appears in its accounts of the reward and the punishment, and the sūrah of the Cattle states that no one reaps except what he sows (6:164). The reply in front of us applies the principle to the quarrel of the condemned, and it leaves both groups with the record of their own acts.

The verb of tasting is one of the Book's standing expressions for the experience of punishment, and it appears in its descriptions of the destroyed communities and of the Hereafter alike. The verse in front of us uses it in the dialogue of the Fire, and the sūrah's accounts of the peoples who were destroyed in the world have already used it in the same sense.

**The Rancour and Its End**

The verse closes the exchange between the two groups, and the sūrah does not report any further reply from the followers. The scene is left with the recriminations spoken and the punishment shared, and the passage then turns to those who deny the signs and the gates that will not open for them. The Book's descriptions of the Fire include the quarrelling of its inhabitants as one of the features of their condition, and the verse in front of us supplies the last speech of that quarrel.

The commentary tradition reads the passage as a warning against the excuses that the audience used in the world. The claim to have followed the ancestors and the leaders is answered at the reckoning by the leaders themselves, and the answer removes the ground on which the claim rested. The sūrah's reader who recognizes the excuse in his own community is given its end in the dialogue in front of him.

**The Book's Account of Responsibility**

The Book's statements about responsibility appear throughout the revelation, and the passage in front of us belongs with those that place the burden on the individual. The sūrah has already stated that no soul carries another's burden and that every person reaps what he sows (6:164), and the Book's descriptions of the day include the giving of the record to each person separately. The reply of the leaders in front of us applies that arrangement to the relations between generations and between classes.

The Book's warnings about following others without examination appear in its instructions to the community to use the faculties of hearing, sight, and reason, and the sūrah of the Night Journey states that the hearing, the sight, and the heart will all be questioned. The verse in front of us states the outcome for those who did not use them, and the sūrah's teaching about the revelation as the guide returns in the exchange of the condemned.

**Where the Verse Sits**

The verse stands thirty-ninth in the chapter, and it completes the dialogue of the previous verse. The verses that follow turn to those who deny the signs and are too proud to accept them, and the passage describes the gates of the Garden and the comparison with the camel and the needle. The reply in front of us closes the scene of the Fire's recriminations.

The reader has been given the entry of the communities, the cursing, the blaming, the request, the doubling, and the reply. The sūrah then moves from the assembly of the condemned to the final statement of their exclusion from the Garden.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse states that the gates of heaven will not be opened for those who deny the signs and treat them with disdain, and that they will not enter the Garden until a camel passes through the eye of a needle, and it closes with the statement that this is how the wicked are recompensed (7:40). The dialogue of the condemned in front of us is followed by the declaration of their exclusion, and the sūrah's account of their end is complete.

The two verses together state the end of the path that the sūrah's opening verses have described. The community that fabricated and denied is gathered, quarrels, and is excluded, and the reader is left with the image of the needle's eye that the Book uses for the impossibility of the outcome.
