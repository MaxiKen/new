## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:10

> Indeed, We granted David a ˹great˺ privilege from Us, ˹commanding:˺ “O mountains! Echo his hymns! And the birds as well.” We made iron mouldable for him,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Gift Named Before It Is Described**

The verse opens with *ātaynā Dāwūda minnā faḍlā* — We gave David a bounty from Us. The word *faḍl* means surplus, what exceeds the necessary; it is favour beyond entitlement rather than payment for service. Two details in the phrasing matter. The gift is qualified by *minnā*, from Us, which locates its source before naming its content and rules out the reading that David's powers were talents he developed. And the content is withheld for a clause, so that the reader meets the fact of privilege before learning what the privilege was. Commentators have read *faḍl* here as prophethood and revealed scripture, pointing to *"And to David We gave the Psalms"* (4:163), or as the judgment and knowledge granted to him and his son together: *"Indeed, We granted knowledge to David and Solomon"* (27:15). Both readings are compatible with the verse, which names no single item. What the sūrah needs at this point in its argument is the category itself. It has just been dealing with men who measured worth by wealth, and it now introduces a figure whose distinction came from elsewhere.

**Mountains Told to Answer a Human Voice**

The command is addressed to the landscape: *yā jibālu awwibī maʿahu* — O mountains, return the praise with him. The verb *awwaba* is from the root *ʾ-w-b*, to return, the same root as *maʾāb*, a place of return. It carries the sense of repeating, coming back again and again, which is why the translation reaches for *echo*. The image is precise and physical. A voice raised in a mountain valley comes back, and the Qur'an describes that returning sound as the mountains joining in. The birds are added without a new verb, folded into the same command. Parallel passages fill out the scene: al-Anbiyāʾ says the mountains and birds were subjected *"to hymn ˹Our praises˺ along with David"* (21:79), and Ṣād supplies the timing — *"in the evening and after sunrise"* (38:18) — with the birds *"flocking together. All turned to him ˹echoing his hymns˺"* (38:19). Morning and evening are the hours when a herdsman or a king would be out on the hills. The miracle is placed inside the working day rather than in a sanctuary.

**Why the Non-Human World Would Join a Man at All**

The obvious question is what makes David's voice worth answering. The Qur'an's frame for this is stated in the same sūrah where David appears as king: *"O David! We have surely made you an authority in the land, so judge between people with truth"* (38:26). The word there is *khalīfah*, a deputy or successor, the term used at the creation of the first human. A creature appointed to represent the whole order on earth is, on this understanding, a being in whom the rest of the order is summed up — so when he praises, what he sums up praises with him. There is a second reading worth keeping alongside it. The Qur'an elsewhere asserts that everything already glorifies its Maker: *"Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth ˹constantly˺ glorifies Allah"* (62:1). On that reading David's gift was not that the mountains began praising but that his praise fell into audible step with a chorus already running. Nothing in the verse decides between these, and the two are not rivals.

**Iron Made Soft: A Miracle Placed in a Workshop**

The last clause changes register completely: *wa alannā lahu al-ḥadīd*, We made iron soft for him. The verb *alāna* is from *layyin*, soft or pliant, the word used of a supple branch or a gentle manner. Iron is the Qur'an's standing example of hardness and force — al-Ḥadīd names a whole sūrah and describes the metal as sent down *"with its great might, benefits for humanity"* (57:25). Making the hardest available material behave like clay in one man's hands is a striking gift, but the striking thing is what it was for. Not a weapon of legend, not an invincible sword; the next verse will show him making body armour. The tradition has read this softening in two ways: as a suspension of the metal's nature in his hands, or as the teaching of smithcraft — the knowledge of heat and hammer by which iron does in fact become workable. The second reading has the advantage of matching how the Qur'an describes the outcome elsewhere: *"We taught him the art of making body armour to protect you in battle"* (21:80). Taught, not simply enabled.

**Prophet, King, Singer, Smith**

What the verse assembles is a portrait unusual among the prophets. David holds political authority, receives revelation, sings, and works metal with his hands, and the Qur'an presents no tension between these. The combination is not incidental to the sūrah's argument. Sabaʾ is speaking to men who believed that God's favour showed itself in wealth and status and who will shortly say so out loud (34:35). David had wealth and status in abundance, and the verse mentions neither. It mentions a voice the hills answered and a craft learned at a forge. The Prophet Muḥammad is reported to have singled out that manual labour for praise, saying that nobody has eaten better food than what he earns with his own hands, and that the prophet of God David used to eat from the earnings of his hand (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2072, reported from al-Miqdām; the point is repeated at Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2073 from Abū Hurayrah). A king who could have lived off the treasury ate from the anvil.

**The Word That Ties This Story to the Rest of the Sūrah**

*Faḍl* is a keyword here, and its reappearance is worth tracking. Solomon's inheritance is described with the same idea when he says he and his father were *"privileged"* over many believing servants (27:15), and the whole David-and-Solomon section of this sūrah is framed as gift rather than achievement. That framing is why the section sits where it does. Between the mockery of the resurrection deniers and the ruin of Sheba, the sūrah places two men who received extraordinary favour and handled it correctly — and Sheba, which received a smaller favour and did not. The comparison is structural rather than stated. Read alone, this verse is about echoing mountains and soft iron; read in position, it is the opening term of an argument about what favour is for. The instruction attached to the iron follows in the next verse.
