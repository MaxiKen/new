## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:97

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah’s Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse About a Dispute Over an Angel**

The sūrah turns from the community's claims about the Hereafter to a specific objection it had raised about the messenger who brought the revelation. The objection was directed at Gabriel, and the verse answers it by describing what Gabriel actually did rather than by defending his rank.

The Book's method here is characteristic. Instead of arguing about the standing of angels, it states the function of the one being criticised. A person who objected to the carrier of the message is invited to look at the message itself, and the verse supplies its contents in a single line. The Book's method here is characteristic. **The Reported Background**

Commentators preserved accounts of the exchange that prompted the verse. In one report the Prophet ﷺ was asked questions that only a prophet could answer, among them a question about which angel brought him revelation, and when he answered that it was Gabriel, his questioners said they would have followed him had he named Michael. In another account ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb visited a group studying the Torah and heard from them that they believed in the Prophet but were at peace with some angels and not with others, and returned to find the verse already revealed.

The reports are preserved in the commentary literature rather than in the Book itself, and the sūrah does not depend on them. What the reports supply is the shape of the objection, which the verse's opening clause confirms: an enmity directed at one angel by name.

**Why the Angel Mattered**

The angel's identity mattered to the community because its own traditions assigned different functions to different angels, and one of them had come to be associated with severity. The commentators noted that the objection probably disguised a more basic complaint, which was that the revelation had not come to a member of their own people, and that the angel's reputation supplied a respectable way of saying so.

Al-Rāzī made this point directly when he observed that enmity toward the angel is enmity toward the One who sent him, since the carrier does not choose his message. The verse's structure implies the same conclusion, because it moves from the objection about Gabriel straight to a description of what the revelation accomplishes. The reports explain how a dispute about function could become a dispute about identity, and they show the objection working as a respectable form of a complaint the community did not want to state plainly. The verse addresses the stated objection and leaves the underlying one to be exposed by the surrounding passage.

**Revealed to Your Heart**

The verse locates the revelation in the Prophet's heart, and the choice of organ is deliberate. The Book treats the heart as the seat of knowledge and understanding, not merely of sentiment, and it uses the same language in other passages describing the descent of the revelation (26:194; 53:10–11).

The phrase also answers the objection in a second way. A message deposited in the heart of the recipient is not an external imposition on a community; it is the formation of the messenger himself, and the community's objection about the carrier collapses when the effect of the message is considered. What Gabriel brought became the Prophet's inward knowledge, and the revelation was then spoken, recited, and transmitted. The phrase also answers the objection in a second way. **Confirming What Came Before**

The revelation is described as confirming what preceded it, and the sūrah has been making this point from its opening verses. This is the ground on which the argument with the People of the Book stands throughout the chapter: the new book does not contradict the earlier one, it validates it, and a person who honours the earlier scripture has a reason to receive the later one.

The community being addressed in this verse had asked for a confirmation of its own book, and the sentence states that the confirmation has arrived. What the verse does not do is concede that the confirmation required the messenger to come from a particular people or through a particular angel, and the objection it addresses is precisely about that requirement.

**A Guide and Good News**

The sentence closes by describing the revelation as guidance and as good news for the believers. The two terms capture the two directions in which the Book presents itself: it tells its hearers what to do and it tells them what awaits those who respond. Both are attached to the believers rather than to a nation, which is consistent with the sūrah's treatment of the two categories throughout the passage.

The phrase also answers the community's complaint by showing what the message would have given them. The objection in the reports was about the identity of the angel; the answer describes a guidance and a good news that would have been theirs had the objection not stood in the way, and the sūrah leaves that contrast with its hearers.

**What the Verse Establishes About Revelation**

The sentence states three things about how revelation arrives: it comes by God's command, it enters the heart of the one chosen to receive it, and it confirms what preceded it. Nothing in the description leaves room for the objection that a particular angel or a particular people should have been selected. The verse therefore answers the complaint by describing the process rather than by entering the dispute, and it ends with the benefit the process was meant to deliver. **Belief in the Angels as Part of Faith**

The sūrah's opening description of the believers includes faith in what was sent down to the Prophet ﷺ and in what was sent down before him, and the Book elsewhere names the angels among the objects of belief without exception. The objection answered in this verse is therefore a rejection of something the community's own tradition affirmed, since the angels were already part of its understanding of how God communicates. What the verse rules out is a selective relation to the means of revelation, in which one carrier is accepted and another refused on the basis of preferences formed before the message arrived. The Book's treatment of the matter throughout the passage is that the message and its carrier come from the same source, and that accepting the one while rejecting the other is not a position that can be held consistently.
