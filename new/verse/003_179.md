## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:179

> Allah would not leave the believers in the condition you were in, until He distinguished the good from the evil ˹among you˺. Nor would Allah ˹directly˺ reveal to you the unseen, but He chooses whoever He wills as a messenger. So believe in Allah and His messengers. And if you are faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, you will receive a great reward.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Separation Promised**

The verse states that God will not leave the believers in the state they are in until He separates the bad from the good, and it explains that the means of separation is not a disclosure of hidden knowledge. The sentence answers the community's uncertainty about who among it was sincere.

Al-Ṭabarī took the separation to mean that those whose adherence was hollow are singled out from those whose commitment was genuine. Most commentators read the promise as given to the community about itself: its members would not be left in a state where the two could not be told apart.


Some commentators read the opening words as addressed to the disbelievers and hypocrites, so that the sentence means that God would not leave the believers in the same condition as those who deny. The two readings distribute the sentence's parts differently, and they produce the same assurance.

The difference is worth noting because it shows how closely the comment tradition attended to the question of address. The Book's sentences about the community frequently change their addressee without marking the change, and the commentators tracked those movements as part of their work.

**The Book's Teaching About Testing**

The verse's statement that the separation happens is amplified elsewhere in the sūrah. The Book asks the community whether it imagined that it would reach the garden with no one having been shown to have struggled and endured, and the account of the battle shows the sorting that the sentence describes happening in the course of one afternoon.

The two groups at Uḥud were distinguished by conduct rather than by declaration, and the verse before the reader states the principle that the day had demonstrated. Testing, in the Book's teaching, is not an incidental hardship but the mechanism by which the community's composition becomes visible to those who live through it and to those who come after them.

**The Unseen Not Disclosed**

The verse states that God will not inform the community of the unseen, and the commentators explain this as a statement that God does not reveal what lies in a person's heart. The community would not be given a list of names; it would be given occasions on which character showed itself.

Both al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī tie the sentence to the campaign itself, where the pressure of the day brought out what people were. The Book's way of dealing with a community is to let conduct bring to light what a report from the unseen would have settled only by coercion.

**The Occasions Reported for the Verse**

The commentators record two occasions for the revelation of this verse. One report places it after a request from the community for a means of telling who among them was sound and who was not; another has it answering an opponent who demanded that the Prophet name each person's category.

Both reports describe the same desire: a knowledge of people's inner states that would settle the community's uncertainty once and for all. The verse declines to provide it and supplies the community with the method it was going to have anyway.


The verse makes an exception for the messengers, whom God chooses and to whom He grants what He wills of the knowledge of such matters. The Book states elsewhere that God is the Knower of the unseen and discloses it to those He chooses among His messengers.

The sentence therefore keeps the community from expecting the knowledge while acknowledging that God gives it where He decides. The Prophet knew more about the people around him than anyone else, and the verse's exception explains the source of that knowledge without turning it into a general provision for the community. What the community is offered instead is the ordinary means of finding out, which is the conduct that trials bring into the open. **The Command to Believe and Its Reward**

The verse ends with an instruction and a promise: the call to place trust in God and in the men He sends, and the assurance that those who hold to that trust and keep their duty will receive a great reward. The sentence is short, and its position at the end of a passage about tests and knowledge gives it the force of a conclusion.

What the community is told to do is what it can do, and the sentence's brevity keeps the instruction within reach of everyone who hears it. It cannot see into the hearts of its members, and it is not asked to; it is asked to hold to God and to His messengers and to conduct itself with the awareness the sūrah has been teaching. The reward named at the end is the same one the sūrah has attached to the same combination in earlier verses.

**What the Verse Teaches About Certainty**

The verse's teaching about certainty is that it comes through trial rather than through revelation of hidden matters, and the community's own history had just demonstrated the point. The Book does not promise its people a way of knowing who is sincere without the cost of the occasions that reveal sincerity.

The spiritual reading recorded in the commentaries draws the same lesson for the individual, describing the purification of the soul as a gradual sorting of what is sound from what is not, and warning against expecting hidden knowledge to be granted as the fruit of the path. What a person is asked for is conduct, and what the verse promises is the reward that follows it.
