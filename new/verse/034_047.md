## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:47

> Say, “If I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it. My reward is only from Allah. And He is a Witness over all things.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Answering the Charge of Motive**

Four verses earlier the opponents said he was a man who wanted to turn them from their fathers' religion (34:43), which is an accusation about motive. The previous verse answered the charge of madness; this one answers the charge of interest. *Qul mā saʾaltukum min ajrin fa-huwa lakum* — whatever payment I have asked you for, you may keep it. The sentence is constructed so that the hypothetical collapses on inspection. He had asked for nothing, so there is nothing to return, and the offer to hand it back is a way of saying that publicly without appearing to boast about it. This is a shrewder rhetorical form than a flat denial. A man who says "I never took anything from you" invites the reply that he wanted to; a man who tells his opponents to keep whatever he took has invited them to name the sum.

**The Test That Was Available to Them**

The argument works because it is checkable. Motive is normally the hardest thing to establish in a dispute, since it lives inside a person. Payment is not: it leaves traces in a small community where everybody knows what everybody has. After years of preaching, the Prophet's material position had not improved and was in most respects worse — his clan boycotted, his following drawn from people who could contribute nothing, his own trade neglected. The Meccans could have settled the question by producing a single transaction. The verse is confident enough to make the challenge explicit, and the silence that followed it is part of the record. This is why the Qur'an gives the argument to the Prophet as speech rather than stating it as doctrine. Its force depends on being said out loud, in the place where the facts were known.

**A Standing Formula Among the Messengers**

The disclaimer is not unique to this Prophet. The Qur'an puts it in the mouths of several: Noah tells his people he asks no payment and that his reward lies only with God, and the same words are repeated by Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot and Shuʿayb in a single sūrah, each delivering the identical line to his own community (26:109, 127, 145, 164, 180). The Prophet Muḥammad is instructed to say it at al-Anʿām, al-Furqān, Yā-Sīn, Ṣād and al-Shūrā as well as here. The repetition across so many figures marks it as a structural feature of the office rather than a personal virtue. A messenger who charges has entered a transaction, and a transaction gives his audience a way to evaluate him that has nothing to do with whether he is telling the truth. Removing the fee removes the distraction.

**Where the Payment Actually Comes From**

*In ajriya illā ʿalā Allāh* — my reward is only upon God. The preposition *ʿalā* is the one used for a debt owed, which is a striking choice; the Prophet describes his compensation as an obligation resting with God, not a hope or a gift. This does two things at once. It denies his audience any leverage, since a man whose income does not depend on his listeners cannot be influenced by withholding it. And it explains how he can continue under conditions that would have ended a commercial enterprise years earlier. The sūrah has been arguing since its thirty-sixth verse that provision comes from a source the beneficiary does not control (34:36, 34:39), and this verse applies that doctrine to the messenger's own situation. He is the first test case for the teaching he brought.

**The Witness Named at the End**

*Wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd* — He is a Witness over all things. *Shahīd* comes from the root *sh-h-d*, to be present at and to testify to; a *shāhid* is someone who was there and can speak to what happened. The name is precisely fitted to the claim it closes. A dispute about whether money changed hands is a dispute about facts, and facts require a witness. The Prophet has no way to prove a negative to men determined to disbelieve him, so he names a witness who was present at every transaction that never occurred. The Qur'an uses this name in similar evidentiary settings at al-Nisāʾ, al-Māʾidah, al-Ḥajj and al-Aḥzāb, and at Fuṣṣilat it is invoked against those who think their conduct is hidden.

**Why an Unpaid Messenger Is Harder to Dismiss**

The argument has a force that goes beyond clearing the Prophet's name, and it is worth drawing out. Every explanation his opponents had offered required a motive. Fabrication requires one — a forger forges for something. The charge in verse 43 that he wanted to turn them from their fathers' worship requires one, since a man does not undertake years of public hostility for no return. Even the accusation of sorcery, which is about method rather than motive, assumes a practitioner working for a purpose. Remove the payment and every one of these explanations loses its engine. What remains is a man doing something costly, sustained over years, with no visible return in a society where returns were the point of public action — and the only remaining explanation is the one his opponents refused to consider.

**The Shape of the Closing Sequence**

This verse is the second in a run that will carry the sūrah to its end, each opening with *qul* and each answering one specific charge with one short sentence. Madness is answered by a procedure for thinking (34:46). Greed is answered by an offer to return what was never taken. What follows will address the source of the message and the standing of the messenger himself. Read together, the sequence is a defence brief delivered in fragments, and its most notable feature is what it declines to include: no miracle is offered, no threat is repeated, no claim is made about the speaker's virtue beyond the facts his audience could verify. Everything rests on evidence already in their possession, which is the same method the chapter used when it told them to look at the sky and the ground around them (34:9).
