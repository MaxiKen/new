## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:241

> Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women—a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sentence, Every Divorce**

The code's divorce legislation, which has run twenty-odd verses through counts, waiting periods, ransoms, halves, and waivers, is gathered into a single sentence — and the sentence is universal. *Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women*: not the unconsummated wife of 2:236, not the priced-and-released wife of 2:237, not the twice-divorced or the thrice-divorced — every divorced woman. Ibn Kathīr records the verse's import in exactly these terms: where the earlier ruling concerned the marriage not yet consummated, this one carries the general obligation, binding every man who divorces to make provision for the woman he releases. The verse is the code's safety-net clause — the rule that closes every gap the preceding rules' case-by-case structure might leave — and its position after the widow-verses extends the net's reach: by the time the sentence arrives, no dissolution of marriage, by divorce or by death, has left the woman's material claim unlegislated.

**Maʿrūf: The Custom Standard**

The provision is bi'l-maʿrūf — the code's recurring formula, rendered here *reasonable* and elsewhere *honorable* or *fair*. Maʿrūf is the standard of the recognized-good: what the community's decent practice acknowledges without being told. The word does the legislative work that fixed sums cannot do — it scales automatically with wealth, locality, and era, and it imports the community's own best behavior as the law's floor. The same word governed the nursing mother's provision (2:233), the retained-or-released wife's treatment (2:229), the widow's own decisions (2:234), and the wet-nurse's payment. Its recurrence across the code amounts to a drafting philosophy: where amounts cannot be fixed universally, the Qur'an fixes the standard and delegates the number to the honorable — and then, as here, attaches the standard to the conscience rather than to the court. A maʿrūf-provision argued down to its minimum has met the letter and missed the word.

**A Duty on the Mindful**

The verse's second half names its enforcement: *a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺* — ḥaqqan ʿalā'l-muttaqīn. The formula appeared two verses earlier with a different noun: the compensation for the unconsummated marriage was *an obligation on the good-doers* (2:236), assigned to the muḥsinūn. Here the same grammatical structure — a ḥaqq laid ʿalā a named company — assigns the general provision to the muttaqīn, the God-fearers. The exegetes read the pairing as cumulative: the code's financial duties are owed by everyone and claimed by the excellent and the reverent as their specific honor. The phrasing also does precise legal-ethical work: calling the provision a ḥaqq — a right, a claim — makes it enforceable in principle, while attaching it to taqwā locates its real operation in the conscience, since no court can audit the spirit of a settlement. The verse thus legislates at both levels at once, the way the whole code does: the woman has a claim; the man's taqwā is where the claim lives.

**Why the General After the Particular**

The verse's placement has struck readers from the beginning — a universal rule arriving after the specific cases it seems to underlie, and after the widow-interlude that interrupted them. The order is instructive rather than accidental. The particular cases (2:236–237) established the code's method on the easiest instances: the marriage never begun, the price never set, the arithmetic clean. The widow-verses extended the method to death. Only then does the universal sentence arrive, and it arrives as the method's summary rather than its exception — every reader who has followed the cases now knows what *reasonable provision* means in practice, because the cases have priced it. The drafting mirrors the sūrah's wider habit of legislating by accumulation: instances first, principle last, the principle arriving with the instances as its commentary. The one-sentence verse could not carry its weight at the code's start; at its end it carries everything.

**The Provision's Range**

What the provision consists of, the jurists debated within the maʿrūf-standard, and the debate maps the verse onto the code's other rulings. For the unconsummated marriage it was the mutʿah-compensation scaled by capacity (2:236). For the consumed marriage with a named dowry, the retained wife's maintenance during her waiting and the released wife's settlement (2:229, 65:1–7's later legislation). For the nursing mother, provision and clothing (2:233). The general verse gathers these into a single expectation: whatever the configuration, the departing woman leaves with something — a gift, a half, a maintenance, a wage — and the something is measured by his means and the community's honor. The exegetes' note that the obligation falls on all who divorce, not merely on those who divorced well, closes the range's far edge: there is no divorce in this code so clean, so faultless, or so early that it releases the husband from giving.

**The Code's Last Domestic Word**

As the final verse of the family legislation before the sūrah pivots to prayer, this sentence is the code's last domestic word — and it is a word about generosity under obligation. Everything before it has balanced rights: the degree, the reciprocal obligations, the ransom's ceiling, the half's arithmetic. The last clause declines to balance anything; it simply loads the mindful man with the departing woman's provision and calls the load a ḥaqq. The effect on the code's whole shape is retrospective: read backward from this verse, the counts and waivers and halvings appear as the structure that guarantees the woman is never released empty-handed, and the taqwā-formula appears as the guarantee's engine. The commentators connect the verse to the sūrah's opening portrait of the muttaqīn (2:2–5) — the same company named at the Book's start and at the code's close — and the connection is the code's quiet claim: the legislation between those two mentions exists to make the mindful man's mindfulness concrete, one settlement at a time.
