## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:99

> When they entered Joseph’s presence, he received his parents ˹graciously˺ and said, “Enter Egypt, Allah willing, in security.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole Family Arrives**

Verse 93's second command was to bring the family, all together. Verse 99 reports the arrival, and the chapter gives it a single verse. No journey is described, no route is named, and no account is taken of the migration itself beyond the fact of entry. The sūrah's economy at moments of fulfilment is consistent: predictions and instructions receive full treatment, and their realisation is stated in a clause. What matters here is not that a household travelled from Canaan to Egypt during a famine but that the household arrived intact, with the parents alive, the sight restored and the eleven sons present — the terms of the dream in verse 4 at last capable of being met. The capability is the verse's contribution. For ninety-five verses the dream has been a statement about an unseen future and a source of trouble; here the eleven sons and both parents are in one country at one time, and the arithmetic of the vision becomes possible for the first time. The chapter notes the arrival and leaves the fulfilment to the verse that follows.

**He Received His Parents**

The verb denotes taking someone in, giving them a place, drawing them close to oneself. It is the same verb the chapter used when Joseph drew Benjamin aside to disclose himself (12:69), and its use here for the parents marks the reunion as an act of shelter rather than of ceremony. The verse specifies the parents and does not mention the brothers, who have already been received, forgiven and provided for. The order is one of honour: Joseph's first act on the family's arrival is directed at his father and mother, and the greeting given to them precedes anything said to the party as a whole. Verse 100 will continue the sequence by raising them to the throne. The order carries the meaning. Joseph's first act on meeting the family he has not seen for decades is to bring his parents forward, and the honour conferred on them in the next verse follows that gesture rather than preceding it. What the chapter records is a son receiving his parents, with a state ceremony arranged around the reception rather than the other way about.

**"Enter Egypt in Security"**

The word denotes safety, freedom from fear — the condition the chapter has been describing the absence of since verse 8. Joseph's own entry into Egypt was as cargo, hidden in a well and sold for counted coins (12:19–20). His brothers' entries were as anxious purchasers during a famine, once accused of theft publicly and once threatened with exclusion (12:60, 12:70). Jacob's household has lived for decades under a false report and a famine. The invitation names the opposite of all of it in a single word, and it is addressed to the whole family at the moment of arrival rather than after a period of adjustment. The timing makes it a welcome rather than a reassurance. Security announced after a period of adjustment would be a report; announced at the gate it is a gift conferred on arrival, and it answers the particular insecurity this family has lived with — a son sold into this country, a son detained in it, and a household that twice travelled to it afraid of starving.

**The Qur'an Uses the Same Formula Elsewhere**

The combination of entry and security appears in two other contexts worth noting. The angels' greeting to the inhabitants of Paradise is an invitation to enter in peace and security (15:46). And the Qur'an records the fulfilment of a vision given to the Prophet ﷺ in the same terms: *Allah willing, you will surely enter the Sacred Mosque, in security* (48:27). Verse 99 stands inside that pattern — an entry promised, an entry granted, and the granting marked by the removal of fear. The parallel with 48:27 is particularly close, since both concern a vision disclosed in advance and fulfilled at a stated destination, and both attach the divine exception to the promise. The shared formula also imports a shared condition. Wherever security is promised in these terms it is promised by God and held subject to His will, so it cannot be treated as a possession. The family entering Egypt receives something belonging to the category of divine grant rather than political protection, which is how the chapter has treated every other good thing in the story.

**"If God Wills" Attaches to a Promise of Safety**

The exception is not a hedge about whether the family will enter Egypt; they are standing at the entrance as the words are spoken. It attaches to the security. Joseph is a ruler with authority over the country's stores and a personal position with the king (12:54), and he nevertheless qualifies the promise of safety with a reference to divine will. The chapter has shown him doing the same at every point where his own power is greatest: he declines to absolve himself from the condition of the soul (12:53), attributes his preservation to God's turning of a scheme away (12:34), and ascribes his establishment to mercy given according to a will (12:56). The exception here belongs to that pattern rather than to uncertainty.

**Security Is the Chapter's Final Gift**

The sūrah's movement can be described as a passage from fear to security. A boy is afraid of a dream his father warns him not to relate (12:5). He is thrown into a well by people who intend his death (12:9–10). He is sold, imprisoned on a false charge, and forgotten by the one man who owed him a message (12:42). His father fears for a second son, weeps until his eyes fail, and is called deluded for hoping (12:84–95). Verse 99 names the end of that sequence in a single word, and it names it as something given rather than achieved. Nobody in the family secured themselves; they were fed, forgiven, healed and received. The passive runs through the entire sūrah. Joseph was saved from the well, taught, released and elevated; Jacob was given patience, knowledge of his son and the return of his sight; the brothers were fed, spared and forgiven. What arrives at the gate of Egypt is the last item in a long list of things done for this family rather than by it.

**A Migration With Consequences Beyond the Chapter**

The entry of Jacob's household into Egypt is the point at which the sūrah's story becomes the background of a much longer one. The chapter does not mention what follows — the settlement, the generations, the later Pharaoh who did not know Joseph, the exodus. Its interest ends with the reunion and the prayer that follows it in verses 100 and 101. The reader is nevertheless aware that the invitation in verse 99 has a scope larger than the scene: a family enters a country in security, under the protection of one of its own, during a famine that the same person's foresight made survivable. What the chapter records as an arrival is elsewhere in the Qur'an the beginning of a long residence.

**Receiving Before Reuniting**

The verse's sequence is deliberate. Joseph receives his parents, and only then speaks the words of welcome. Nothing in the text reports an embrace, a greeting from the brothers, or a public announcement of the family's identity to the Egyptian court. The first act is shelter given to two elderly arrivals, and the first words are a promise of safety to the whole party. The chapter has been careful all along to describe Joseph's care in terms of acts rather than feelings — food loaded, money returned, a brother taken aside, a shirt sent — and verse 99 follows the pattern. The reunion the reader has waited ninety-nine verses for is reported as something done: he took them in, he gave them a place, he told them they were safe.
