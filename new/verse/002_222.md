## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:222

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about menstruation. Say, “Beware of its harm! So keep away, and do not have intercourse with your wives during their monthly cycles until they are purified. When they purify themselves, then you may approach them in the manner specified by Allah. Surely Allah loves those who always turn to Him in repentance and those who purify themselves.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question and the Hurt**

The question-cluster continues with a matter the Arabian household needed legislated: they ask you about menstruation — and the answer opens with a single word of diagnosis: it is *adhā*, a hurt. The exegetes divided over the word's precise sense, and the division matters. Some read adhā as the discomfort the period causes the woman herself — the answer beginning, on this reading, with acknowledgment of her pain. Many others understood it as uncleanness attaching specifically to the menstrual blood. Still others glossed it simply as the blood, without attaching any notion of defilement to the woman. The three readings generate three different atmospheres for the ruling that follows: a law attentive to the wife's suffering, a law of ritual separation, or a law of practical timing. The legislation's actual content favors the restrained readings — what the verse prohibits is bounded, what it permits is broad, and the defilement it implies attaches to a flow, never to a person. The answer's structure follows the diagnosis: it is a hurt — therefore keep away, therefore do not approach — the ruling derived from the fact rather than from a mythology of pollution.

**Against the Background of Exile**

The ruling's context makes its moderation visible. The commentators record that before Islam, menstruating women in Arabia were not slept with in the same bed and did not eat from the same plate — a separation customs possibly shaped by the neighboring religious law: the prescriptions of Leviticus (15:19–33) and the Zoroastrian Vendidad (16), both of which mandated substantial isolation for menstruating women and ritual purification of objects they touched. Against that background of household exile — and the Arabian custom sitting somewhere inside it — the verse's ruling is a deliberate middle: it prohibits one act and returns everything else. The woman is not exiled from the bed in the sense of isolation, not separated from the table, not treated as a contaminating presence; she is approached in every manner except the one the hurt forbids. The tradition's reports fill in the practice: in many hadiths the Prophet is described as not staying aloof from his wives during their periods but showing them affection — kissing and fondling — short of intercourse. And the ruling on touch is explicit in the commentators' summary: apart from cleaning any item that menstrual blood contacts, Islamic teaching does not deem the touch of a menstruating woman unclean, and does not treat her as rendering anything unclean.

**The Boundaries of the Ruling**

The jurisprudence the verse generated is precise about both edges of the prohibition. Jurists are unanimous in forbidding vaginal intercourse during menstruation, and unanimous in forbidding anal intercourse at any time — the boundaries fixed by this verse read together with the tilth-verse that follows. Within those boundaries the verse's keep-away was understood as specific rather than total: other forms of contact and closeness remain lawful, and the commentators note that most schools allowed affection and fondling during the period. The verse also states the resumption's condition: do not approach until they are purified — a phrase read at two levels. Some interpret the purification as the cessation of the flow; the majority of the schools understand the verse to require, after cessation, the major ablution — ghusl, the ritual washing of the whole body — before intercourse resumes, with the Ḥanafī school allowing, under certain circumstances, resumption after the blood has ceased even before the ghusl. When the purification is complete, the verse restores the marriage's intimacy in full: go in unto them as God has commanded — the resumption itself a commanded good, the return to lawful intimacy within the manner God ordained. There is no formal expiation or punishment legislated for intercourse during menstruation, though some reports mention a monetary payment to the poor — a dinar or half a dinar — and the commentators classify the act as disobedience short of the major kind, from which the pious refrain.

**Your Menses Are Not in Your Hand**

The tradition preserves a scene that fixes the verse's atmosphere with unforgettable economy. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records it (298), from ʿĀʾishah: the Messenger of God said to her — bring me the small mat from the mosque. She answered: I am menstruating. And he said: *your menses are not in your hand*. The exchange is a ruling in miniature: the flow is located in the body, not in the hand that reaches; the woman in her period is not a contamination that spreads by contact but a person whose one bounded restriction leaves her hands — and her service, her presence, her household life — untouched. The report has been read in the legal tradition as addressing the mosque's boundary too: remaining inside the mosque and the retreat of iʿtikāf are restricted for the menstruating woman — restrictions the commentators connect more to protecting the mosque from blood-staining than to any defilement of the woman — while passing through and reaching into it stand. The verse's wider jurisprudence follows the same logic of bounded restriction rather than exile: the canonical prayer and the fast are suspended during the period (the fast made up later), the circumambulation of the Kaʿbah deferred, loud recitation and touching of the Qur'an withheld — suspensions of specific acts, never of the person's standing, her household, her affection, or her nearness to God.

**The Two Beloveds**

The verse ends where its subject most unexpectedly leads — in love: God loves those who repent, and loves those who purify themselves. The double object of divine love is the verse's theological summit. The repentant — the tawwābūn, the ones who turn and keep turning, the intensive form marking repeated return — are loved: the household's failures, the ruling's transgressions, the periodic lapses of a marriage and a life are met not merely with pardon but with love at the turning. And the purifiers — the mutaṭahhirūn — are loved: the ones who wash, who keep the purity the verse legislates, whose scrupulousness about the ghusl and the cleaned garment is itself an object of divine love. The pairing gathers the verse's whole movement: the hurt acknowledged, the boundary drawn, the resumption commanded, the purification required — and over all of it, at both its failure and its keeping, the same love. No other ruling in the passage ends so; the naming of love here transforms the menstrual legislation from a code of restriction into a chapter of intimacy with the Divine — the body's monthly cycle legislated inside a relationship in which both the turning back and the washing up are beloved acts.

**The Verse in the Household Series**

The verse's position in the domestic sequence completes its meaning. The marriage-gate legislated faith as the household's foundation (2:221); this verse legislates the body's cycle within the household; the verse that follows will legislate the marriage's intimacy itself and end in the meeting with God (2:223); and the oaths, the abstention-oath, the divorce, the waiting-periods, and the nursing will follow. Across the whole series the same method runs: a question from real life, an answer that acknowledges the fact (the hurt, the desire, the anger, the womb), a boundary that protects without exiling, a resumption that restores, and names of God — Forgiving, Merciful, Hearing, Knowing, loving the repentant and the pure — presiding over the details. The menstruation-verse is the series' clearest case of the method's genius: a subject the surrounding cultures handled with exile and pollution-mythology, the question-verses of other traditions left largely unaddressed, receives here a ruling of surgical narrowness wrapped in love — one act withheld while the flow lasts, everything else returned, and at the ruling's end the declaration that both the sinners' turning and the washers' purity are, in God's reckoning, objects of love.
