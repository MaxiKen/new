## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:21

> He does not have any authority over them, but ˹Our Will is˺ only to distinguish those who believe in the Hereafter from those who are in doubt about it. And your Lord is a ˹vigilant˺ Keeper over all things.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Excuse Removed Before It Is Offered**

The previous verse said that most people follow Iblīs. The obvious next move for anyone listening is to plead force: if a being of that kind is working on us, what chance did we have? This verse closes that exit in its first clause. *Wa mā kāna lahu ʿalayhim min sulṭān* — he had no *sulṭān* over them at all, with the negation reinforced by *min*, which in Arabic makes the denial absolute rather than partial. Not limited authority, not weak authority; none. *Sulṭān* is a word of real weight, used for governing power and for the force of a decisive proof, and both senses fail here. He cannot compel and he cannot demonstrate. What he has is suggestion, and suggestion is refusable by definition, which is precisely why following him remains a choice attributable to the follower.

**What Was Granted, and What Was Not**

The Qur'an sets out the terms of the arrangement in al-Ḥijr, and reading them alongside this verse settles the question of scope. Iblīs declares he will tempt them all except the chosen (15:39–40), and the reply is a binding statement: *"you will certainly have no authority over My servants, except the deviant who follow you"* (15:42). The exception in that sentence is the whole doctrine in miniature. His power extends to those who have already inclined his way — he does not create the inclination, he exploits it. A man is not turned by an external force into someone who wants what he did not want; he is met, at the point where he already wants it, by an encouragement. This is why the Qur'an can hold both propositions at once without contradiction: the tempter is real and active, and the tempted is fully responsible. He has an audience, not a command.

**The Purpose Clause: Why the Arrangement Exists**

*Illā li-naʿlama man yuʾminu bi-l-ākhirati mimman huwa minhā fī shakk* — except that We may know who believes in the Hereafter from who is in doubt about it. The construction is a purpose clause, so the licence given to the tempter is presented as serving a function rather than as a concession extracted from a reluctant authority. That function is separation. The verb *naʿlam*, that We may know, has troubled readers since it appears to make divine knowledge dependent on an outcome, and the standard answer is the right one: the knowing in question is the making-manifest of what was already known, the difference between a fact and a demonstrated fact. Al-ʿAnkabūt states the principle in the same shape, saying that the testing occurs so that God will *know* the truthful from the liars while affirming that He already knows them. A quality that has never been tested is real but unevidenced. Temptation is what converts an untested disposition into a visible record.

**Belief Against Doubt, Not Belief Against Denial**

The line of separation is drawn in an unexpected place. The verse does not contrast believers with deniers; it contrasts those who believe in the Hereafter with those who are *fī shakk*, in doubt about it. Doubt is the milder condition, and choosing it as the opposite pole is a deliberate raising of the standard. A man who denies the Hereafter outright has at least taken a position. A man in doubt has taken none, and the verse places him on the far side of the line anyway. The reason emerges from the sūrah's own material. Sheba did not deny anything; they turned away (34:16). Doubt is sufficient to produce that turning, because a person unsure whether the accounting is real will behave, in every practical decision, exactly like a person certain that it is not. This is also why the Hereafter is the chosen test item rather than, say, belief in God. Nearly everyone in the sūrah's audience believed in a creator; what divided them was whether anything would be settled later.

**A Keeper Who Misses Nothing**

The closing clause names the guarantor: *wa rabbuka ʿalā kulli shayʾin ḥafīẓ* — your Lord is a Keeper over all things. *Ḥafīẓ* comes from the root *ḥ-f-ẓ*, to preserve, guard and retain; it is the root behind *ḥāfiẓ*, one who has memorised the Qur'an, and behind the preserved Tablet mentioned in (85:22). The name therefore carries two functions at once — watching over, and keeping a record. Both are needed at this point in the argument. If the world is a place of testing, the test is worthless unless someone is invigilating, and worthless again unless the results are retained. The sūrah established the record early, in the verse about the atom's weight and the perfect Record (34:3), and this name reaches back to it. There is also a note of protection in the word that softens the surveillance: the same root gives the sense of guarding something valuable, which is what a Keeper does for those under His care.

**Freedom Described as a Deliberate Design**

Putting the three clauses together yields a compact account of why the world is arranged as it is. A tempter exists but cannot compel; his presence functions as the instrument by which real dispositions become visible; and the results are kept by One who watches everything. Each element is necessary. Remove the tempter and there is no test; give him authority and there is no responsibility; remove the Keeper and the test produces nothing. What the verse refuses, throughout, is the picture of a struggle between two powers with the human being as territory. Iblīs in this sūrah is not an opposing force but a permitted instrument, and his accurate guess about human weakness (34:20) is reported without alarm because the arrangement was never a contest. The sūrah now turns from the hidden influences on worship to its visible objects.
