## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:60

> But if you do not bring him to me ˹next time˺, I will have no grain for you, nor will you ever come close to me again.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Refusals Stated in Advance**

The sanction has two parts and they are not the same. *No measure from me* withdraws the grain; *nor will you come near me* withdraws access. The first is commercial — the brothers will not be served. The second is territorial, and the commentators read it as exclusion from the city itself, since failure to meet the condition would demonstrate that their claim about an eleventh brother was false. Both refusals are announced before the next journey, which means the brothers travel home knowing exactly what is at stake. Nothing is left to be discovered on arrival. The clarity is deliberate: a condition whose consequences are stated in advance can be planned for, argued about at home, and either met or declined with full knowledge.

**The Severity Is Proportionate to the Claim**

A demand that seems arbitrary acquires its logic from what it is testing. Ten men have requested eleven camel-loads of food during a famine on the grounds that a dependant brother remains at home. The minister of the granaries has given them the food and asked to see the dependant. Refusal to produce him is not merely a failure to comply with a personal request; on the administrative reading it confirms that the extra load was obtained on a false statement. The threat of exclusion follows from that. A rationing authority in a famine cannot continue to serve purchasers whose declared household composition it has reason to doubt, and the verse states the rule rather than the resentment behind it. The rule is also a normal instrument of a rationing state. In a famine a government distributing grain has a direct interest in the composition of the households it feeds, because quantities are calculated against numbers. Joseph's threat to withhold supplies is what such a system sounds like from the receiving end, and the chapter does not soften it for his benefit.

**They Know They Must Return**

The condition works because the brothers cannot avoid the second journey. The famine lasts seven years (12:48) and ten camel-loads will not sustain eleven households through it. Verse 65 makes the calculation explicit: the food they already have is described as meagre, and the additional camel-load is described as something easily secured. Joseph is not creating an obligation that would otherwise not exist; he is attaching a term to a return visit the brothers are certain to make. That is what makes the demand effective without being cruel. It costs them nothing to accept in principle, since they were coming back anyway, and the only real difficulty is the conversation they must now have with their father. That difficulty is a specific one and the narrative knows it precisely. They have already lost a brother, produced a bloodied shirt as evidence, and been disbelieved in everything except the bare fact of the loss. Asking Jacob to hand over another son on the word of an Egyptian minister is asking him to repeat the act that broke his household.

**Pressure Without Accusation**

The commentators note that Joseph's method here is a way of obtaining his object without alleging wrongdoing. He does not tell the brothers they are liars. He does not detain one of them as a hostage. He states a condition and a consequence, both of them administrative in form, and leaves the brothers to decide whether the condition can be met. The subtlety is worth naming because the alternative was available to him and was used in the biblical account, where an accusation of spying precedes the demand and one brother is held in prison (Genesis 42:16–19). The Qur'anic Joseph applies no accusation and detains nobody at this stage. What he uses is the brothers' own need, stated plainly. The restraint is tactical and moral at the same time. An accusation would have ended the trade and sent them home as suspects; a statement of need keeps them returning as customers. Joseph holds the single fact that matters — that their household will starve without Egyptian grain — and lets that fact do the persuading his grievance never could.

**The Engine of the Remaining Fifty Verses**

Everything that follows depends on this sentence. Verse 61 records the brothers' promise to persuade their father. Verse 62 has Joseph return their money into their saddlebags to encourage a second journey. Verses 63 to 66 contain the negotiation with Jacob, ending in an oath sworn in God's name. Verse 67 gives Jacob's instruction about entering by separate gates. Verse 69 has Joseph take his brother aside and identify himself. Verse 70 plants the cup. None of these events would occur without a condition attached to the brothers' first visit, and the condition is stated in a single verse. The chapter's second half is generated by the sentence in verse 60, which is why the sūrah gives it its own verse rather than folding it into the demand that precedes it.

**Not Revenge, and Verse 62 Proves It**

A man intending retribution against the brothers who sold him would have begun here, at the first meeting, with them standing helpless in his office during a famine. He does not. He gives them their full measure, and then, after they have left, he orders his servants to put their money back into their bags so that they will find it on the road home and be encouraged to return (12:62). The two acts together — food supplied, price refunded — establish the character of the pressure Joseph applies. It is directed at bringing his brother to Egypt, and it is executed at his own expense. Verse 60 sounds like a threat because a threat is what it is, but the chapter immediately supplies the context that determines its purpose.

**The Verse Also Binds Jacob Into the Plot**

The condition cannot be met by the brothers alone. Their father must release Benjamin, and their father has already lost one son to their custody and has said so in the terms he will use again (12:64). Joseph's demand therefore reaches beyond the ten men standing in front of him and lays an obligation on a household in Canaan that does not know the demander's name. The sūrah is constructing the reunion of verse 100 by means of an administrative condition issued in a granary. Nothing in the verse indicates that Joseph intends this; he wants his brother. The larger design is the chapter's own, and it works through a minister's rationing rule.

That design has a further consequence. Because the demand passes through Jacob, the oath that follows is sworn in God's name and witnessed by God (12:66), and the brothers enter Egypt a second time under a religious obligation rather than a commercial one. When the cup is found in Benjamin's bag the test is no longer about grain. It is about whether ten men will abandon a second son of Jacob in a foreign country, having sworn they would not. Verse 60 is the sentence that makes that test possible, and the chapter spends the next twenty verses administering it.
