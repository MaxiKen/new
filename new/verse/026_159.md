## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:159

> And your Lord is certainly the Almighty, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain Interprets Thamûd's Judgment**

The account of Thamûd closes with the same two divine names that followed the earlier prophetic stories: the Almighty, Most Merciful. The refrain tells the reader how to hold the punishment and the warning together. God has power to end a people's false security, and He showed mercy by sending Ṣāliḥ before the consequence arrived.

The judgment is therefore not outside divine care. It is the act of the Lord who knows what the people heard, what sign they received, and what limit they crossed. Mercy does not mean that the camel's destruction is ignored. Power does not mean that the warning was unnecessary. The two names explain the whole sequence.


Thamûd carved mountains and cultivated a prosperous land. Its skill was impressive but bounded. The Almighty is the One who created the people, the stone, the water, the crops, and the forces that can overturn human plans. No monument can claim equality with the Lord who gives the material and the ability to shape it.

That truth does not require despising craft. It requires placing craft beneath worship. A skilled community can use its ability for protection, food, and beauty, but it cannot use ability as evidence that it owes no obedience. The refrain strips power of its false independence while leaving room for responsible work.

**Mercy Was Present in the Warning**

Ṣāliḥ spoke as a brother, declared his trustworthiness, asked no payment, and repeated the call to fear Allah. Each part of his mission was an opening of mercy. The community had time to listen before the punishment overtook it. Its refusal does not turn the opportunity into cruelty.

The mercy also appears in the clarity of the sign. The she-camel was not a hidden riddle available only to a small elite. It stood within the people's daily life and came with a clear limit. The people were given a practical path to obedience, not an impossible demand to discover an unseen code.

**The Two Names Reject Two Distortions**

One distortion sees only power and imagines God as a force whose judgment leaves no hope. Another sees only mercy and assumes that no wrongdoing can bring a final consequence. The refrain rejects both. God is Mighty enough to judge and Merciful enough to warn, delay, and accept a sincere return.

A believer needs both truths. Fear of the Almighty prevents pride, while hope in the Merciful prevents despair. Thamûd's story especially corrects presumption, the idea that abundance and delay prove safety. The mercy they received was an invitation to repent, not permission to continue violating the command.


The final names direct attention away from the spectacle of a ruined people. A reader who delights in the punishment has not understood the Most Merciful. The event should produce sober recognition that justice is real and that God's care includes protecting the truth from being erased by powerful refusal.

This also disciplines human judgment. People can resist oppression and seek lawful justice, but they should not claim to know every hidden reason behind a calamity. God's power is complete because His knowledge is complete. Human beings must act with evidence, restraint, and humility.


Quran 7:77–78 records the killing of the camel followed by an overwhelming earthquake that left the people lifeless in their homes. That parallel confirms the severity of the judgment described here. It also preserves the order that makes the judgment intelligible: a clear sign and command came before the community's defiance.

The refrain therefore keeps the parallel from becoming a mere disaster report. The Lord's power is shown in the punishment, and His mercy is shown in the warning and sign that preceded it. The event is morally legible because both names remain present.

**Mercy Does Not Cancel the Rights of the Victim**

The she-camel's protected share represented a right assigned by God. To ignore that right in the name of mercy would leave the vulnerable exposed to whoever has the greater appetite. The Most Merciful is not indifferent to the creature and community harmed by transgression.

This gives divine mercy a moral shape. It includes forgiveness and opportunity, but it also includes the restoration of order and the ending of aggression. A society cannot demand mercy for its own pride while refusing mercy to the beings and people its power harms.


The reader is not asked to stand with the crowd because it is large, or with the builders because their work is impressive. Stand under the two names. Trust God's power more than human security, and seek His mercy before a choice becomes irreversible.

This posture is neither passive fear nor confident self-righteousness. It is active obedience. Use gifts carefully, accept limits, repair harm, and keep the heart open to correction. The Lord who judged Thamûd is still Almighty, and the Lord who sent Ṣāliḥ is still Most Merciful.


The refrain closes Thamûd's episode but not the Qur'an's concern. A new community will be mentioned next, and the same pattern will invite comparison. The reader must not treat the refrain as a formula that belongs only to a vanished nation.

Every generation receives signs, provision, and warnings in forms suited to its condition. The question is whether it sees them as mercy from the Almighty or as obstacles to its own authority. Thamûd's end is a sign because it gives the living time to choose differently.

A refrain at the end of judgment is also a safeguard for memory. It prevents later generations from treating the event as fate without a Lord, or as a triumph of one human group over another. The correct memory keeps both divine power and divine mercy in view.
