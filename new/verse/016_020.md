## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:20

> **But those ˹idols˺ they invoke besides Allah cannot create anything—they themselves are created.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idols That Create Nothing**

Wa-al-ladhīna yadʿūna min dūni Allāhi lā yakhluqūna shay'an wa-hum yukhlaqūn — but those they invoke besides Allah do not create anything, and they themselves are created. Yadʿūna means to call upon, invoke, pray to, worship. Min dūni Allāh means besides Allah, apart from Allah, as rivals. Lā yakhluqūna shay'an — they do not create anything, not even a small thing. Shay'an is indefinite, indicating nothing at all, even the smallest. Wa-hum yukhlaqūn — while they themselves are created, made, fashioned. The contrast is absolute: Creator vs created, active vs passive. Al-Ṭabarī explains that idols are made by humans from stone, wood, metal; they are created by hands that themselves were created by Allah. How then can they be gods? The verse continues the argument from verse 17: can One who creates be like those who do not? Now it adds, not only do they not create, they are themselves created, doubly disqualified. A creator cannot be equal to a non-creator who is himself created.

**Why The Verse Emphasizes Creation**

Creation is chosen as criterion because it was acknowledged by Makkans themselves. When asked who created heavens and earth, they said Allah, as 29:61 and 31:25 report. Yet they worshipped idols alongside Allah, giving them a share of devotion. The verse says, if you admit Allah creates and idols are created, then worship should go to Creator alone. This is the Qur'anic definition of tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah leading to tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah: acknowledging Allah as Lord, Creator, leads to worshipping Him alone. Al-Rāzī notes that lā yakhluqūna shay'an includes inability to create even a fly, as 22:73 says, or to create themselves. Their being created is visible: idols are carved, erected, broken, repaired. The Makkans saw idols being made in workshops. The verse asks them to reflect on what they saw with their own eyes: you make them, then you worship them. How can maker worship what he made?

**The Bracketed Idols In Translation**

The translation adds ˹idols˺ in brackets to clarify al-ladhīna yadʿūna min dūni Allāh — those they invoke besides Allah. The Arabic is general, including idols, jinn, humans, desires, anything invoked besides Allah. The bracket specifies that primary referent here is idols, as context of Makkan worship indicates. Classical tafsīr agrees that immediate referent is idols, but principle extends to any false object of worship. The brackets show interpretive clarification, not addition to Qur'anic text. The verse's generality is important: it does not say idols only, but those invoked besides Allah, covering all forms of shirk. This makes the argument timeless: any being that does not create and is itself created cannot be divine, whether stone idol or human leader or ideology.

**A Verse That Prepares For Dead, Not Alive**

Verse 20 says idols do not create and are created. Verse 21 will add they are dead, not alive, not knowing when they will be resurrected. The progression is from inability to create to lack of life to lack of knowledge, each step further disqualifying them from divinity. A god should be living, knowing, creating, sustaining. Idols are opposite: non-creating, created, dead, ignorant. The sequence is logical and cumulative. Al-Qurṭubī notes that yukhlaqūn, they are created, implies they had a beginning, were non-existent then made existent, which is characteristic of creatures, not Creator. Creator is without beginning; created has beginning. Worshipping something with a beginning as if it were without beginning is confusion.

**The Social Reality Behind The Verse**

In Makkah, idols were not abstract symbols; they were economic and social centers. Each tribe had its idol, each idol had custodians who benefited from offerings. To say idols do not create and are themselves created was not only theological but social critique: it exposed that custodians were promoting worship of objects they themselves had made for profit. The verse strips away prestige. It says, those you invoke besides Allah cannot create anything — your trade, your rain, your children, your guidance, none come from them — and they themselves are created — by you, by craftsmen, from materials Allah created. The argument is therefore both rational and empirical: look at idols, you see they are made; look at world, you see they made nothing. How then can they be equal to Allah?

**Linguistic Note On Yadʿūna And Shay'**

Yadʿūna from d-ʿ-w means to call, invoke, pray, from same root as duʿā', supplication. In Qur'anic usage, duʿā' often means worship, because worship is calling upon. Min dūni Allāh means besides, apart from, below Allah in rank. Lā yakhluqūna shay'an — they do not create anything — uses shay', thing, the most general noun, to emphasize absolute inability. Even a thing, any thing, they do not create. Wa-hum yukhlaqūn — while they are created — uses passive, indicating they are acted upon, not actors. The passive vs active contrast is stark: they do not create (active negative), they are created (passive positive). The verse's rhythm — lā yakhluqūna shay'an wa-hum yukhlaqūn — with same root kh-l-q in both active and passive, creates a memorable, almost proverbial statement that would stick in listener's mind.

**Why This Criterion Destroys All Forms Of Shirk**

The test lā yakhluqūna shay'an is universal. It applies not only to stone idols but to any rival set up beside Allah: a saint whose tomb is invoked, a leader whose law is treated as ultimate, wealth that is trusted as provider, desire that is obeyed as god. None of these create anything; all are created. Al-Rāzī extends the argument: even if someone claims an idol represents a star or angel that does have some power, that star or angel itself is created and does not create independently. Therefore worship must terminate at the uncreated Creator. The verse thus provides a simple, portable criterion that any person can apply without philosophical training: does this thing I am relying on create anything? Is it itself created? If yes to second and no to first, it cannot be divine. This simplicity is part of the Qur'an's method: profound theology in plain language.

**From Created To Dependent**

Being created implies dependence. A created thing depends on its creator for existence, sustenance, and continuity. Idols depend on craftsmen to shape them, on worshippers to maintain them, on Allah to sustain the materials they are made from. They cannot sustain themselves, let alone others. In contrast, Allah is al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm, the Living who sustains all. The verse hints at this by using yukhlaqūn, they are created, which implies ongoing need for Creator. The Makkans who polished their idols, clothed them, and repaired them when broken were demonstrating with their own hands that idols are dependent. The verse asks them to notice what their hands already know: you maintain them; they do not maintain you.
