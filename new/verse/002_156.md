## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:156

> who, when faced with a disaster, say, “Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Words Given for the First Blow**

The previous verse promised good news to the patient and defined patience as standing fast at the first blow; this verse supplies what the patient say at that moment. The sentence is short enough to be spoken through shock — *innā lillāhi wa-innā ilayhi rājiʿūn*, surely to Allah we belong and to Him we return — and it became, across the Muslim world, the words uttered on hearing of any death, recited by most Muslims at the moment the news arrives. Its placement in revelation is as precise as its use in life: it is attached to the loss of souls announced in the test (2:155), and by extension to every disaster the verse's word *muṣībah* covers — the strike that befalls, the calamity that finds a person whether or not he was looking. The Qur'an does not leave the grieving to invent their speech. It hands them a formula, and the formula is theology compressed into a breath.

**Two Clauses, Two Truths**

The sentence divides into belonging and returning. The first clause settles the present: *we are Allah's* — His property, His servants, His to dispose of. The second settles the future: *to Him we return* — the disposal is not final at the grave; the trajectory runs back to the Owner. Between the two clauses lies the whole of a believer's biography: from Him, owned by Him, going back to Him. Nothing in the sentence is decorative. The belonging answers the wound of loss — what was taken was never mine to begin with, and the taking was by the One to whom it belonged and to whom I belong too. The returning answers the wound of finality — the separation the disaster effects is temporary, because the direction of travel is toward reunion at the place all accounts are settled. Grief ordinarily protests both points: this was *mine*, and it is *gone*. The formula concedes neither protest and refuses both.

**Belonging: The Verse Behind the Verse**

That human beings belong to God is not a claim this verse makes alone; it stands on the sūrah's repeated declaration of universal ownership — *"to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Will"* (2:116) — a statement the Qur'an restates at every register, from the Throne verse's comprehensiveness to the confession that all matters return to God. Belonging is the creature's permanent condition, and the mourner who recites the formula is not acquiring a new status but acknowledging the standing one. This is what makes the acknowledgment consoling rather than crushing: property language between humans connotes vulnerability — a thing owned can be taken — but the Owner named here is the One whose care of what He owns is the sūrah's constant theme. To say "we are Allah's" in a disaster is to relocate the loss inside the largest safe custody there is. The thing taken did not fall out of the world; it moved within His possession, and so did the person it was taken from.

**Returning: The Qur'an's Standing Theme**

The second clause names the movement the whole Qur'an describes. Human beings are reminded that to God all matters are returned (2:210); they are warned to be mindful of the Day *"when you will ˹all˺ be returned to Allah, then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done"* (2:281); the return is universal and unavoidable — *"all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned"* (3:83) — and collective, all gathered to Him (5:48). The mourner's sentence inserts his private disaster into that public itinerary. The death in his house is not an exit from the system; it is an early arrival at the destination everyone is traveling to, which is why the formula can be spoken with a straight heart: the beloved did not go somewhere the speaker will not follow. The return makes every separation provisional, and provisionality is the exact opposite of despair's claim that loss is forever.

**The Reward Attached to the Words**

The formula carries a promise of its own, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (918) from Umm Salamah: she heard the Messenger of God say that no person struck by a calamity who says what God has commanded him — *we belong to Allah and to Him we return; O Allah, reward me in my affliction and replace it with something better* — but God will reward him in the affliction and replace it with something better. She added the report of its testing: when Abu Salamah died, she said the words, but at "replace it with better" she thought, who could be better than Abu Salamah? She said it anyway — and God replaced him with the Messenger of God himself, who married her. The ḥadīth does two things to this verse. It extends the formula into a supplication with an appended request, and it demonstrates the replacement clause in the most extreme case imaginable: the widow who could not conceive of a better received the best. The recitation is thus not resignation dressed in piety; it is a transaction offered on God's terms — the loss surrendered, the reward requested, the replacement trusted.

**Speech as the Instrument of Patience**

The verse's structure — *who, when faced with a disaster, say* — makes speech the instrument by which patience is performed. The glad tidings of the previous verse go not to those who feel calm but to those who say the sentence, and saying it is possible while feeling everything the disaster deserves. This is the practical wisdom of a formula: grief disorganizes thought, and a person in its first hour cannot compose theology; he can only repeat what he was given. The repetition does the composing for him — the tongue states the belonging, the tongue states the return, and the heart, following the tongue, is led back to both truths it had been shaken from. Al-Qurṭubī's ruling that patience is not the absence of complaint but its direction is realized here in its highest form: the formula is complaint carried all the way to God and converted on arrival into confession. The disaster is not denied; it is answered — in ten Arabic words that hold ownership, destination, and hope in a single breath.
