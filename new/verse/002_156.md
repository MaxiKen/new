## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:156

> who, when faced with a disaster, say, “Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence the Believer Says**

The verse defines the patient by a vocabulary rather than by a temperament. *Innā li-llāh* — we belong to God — states ownership; *wa-innā ilayhi rājiʿūn* — and to Him we are returning — states direction. The two halves are a single argument: what belongs to God has not been taken away from its owner but returned to him, and what is returning has not been annihilated but is on the way to the One who sent it.

The phrase is known in the community by the name *istirjāʿ*, "the returning", taken from the verse's own verb. It is said at bereavement, but also at any loss at which a person must tell himself what he has just heard. The community's teachers note that the sentence is a confession of two facts rather than a consolation offered to the self, and that this is why it works when consolations do not.

**When It Is Said**

The verse attaches the words to a specific moment: "when a calamity strikes them". The Arabic *muṣība* is used for anything that strikes a person — a death, an illness, a loss of property, a disappointment, and the ordinary failures of family life. The community's scholars had available a report in which the Prophet ﷺ uses the same general word and promises the reward to whoever answers it as the verse prescribes.

Al-Nawawī, commenting on the report transmitted from Umm Salama in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (918), makes the extension explicit: the wording of the ḥadīth is *muṣība*, not *mawt*, and therefore the sentence and its accompanying supplication belong to every kind of loss. The community's practice followed him, and the phrase entered the ordinary speech of Muslims to the point that it is recognised long before a person learns where it comes from.

**The Report of Umm Salama**

The best-known account of the verse in practice is the narration of Umm Salama. At the deathbed of her husband Abū Salama she heard the Prophet ﷺ teach the words — the verse's own sentence followed by *Allāhumma'jurnī fī muṣībatī wa-akhlif lī khayran minhā*: O God, reward me for my calamity and replace it with something better — and then, when he died, she recited it. Her narration records what she thought at the time: who among the Muslims was better than Abū Salama, that he should be replaced? The report ends with the replacement she received, in her marriage to the Prophet ﷺ (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 918).

The community's teachers read the account as the worked example of the verse's promise, and they used it when they taught the supplication to the bereaved.

**The Two Facts Worked Out**

The first clause answers the complaint of injustice: the property, the child, the husband were never the person's own in the sense that he supposed. The second answers the complaint of finality: nothing has been destroyed, and everything is in transit to the One who gave it. The two clauses together produce the disposition the sūrah has been describing since verse 153.

Al-Rāzī notes that the order of the clauses is not reversible: the realisation of ownership precedes the acknowledgment of return, because a person who has not accepted that he owns nothing cannot say with any meaning that he is going back. The sentence is short because the argument is complete.

**The Correction of the Old Lament**

The Book's correction of pre-Islamic mourning runs through this passage. The old poetry over the grave measured a loss in the terms of what the living had been deprived of; the verse supplies different terms — ownership and return — and a different speaker, since the sentence is placed in the mouth of the person struck rather than of the visitor.

The community's scholars read the passage as the replacement of a vocabulary rather than as the prohibition of grief. A person may weep; the sūrah's own Prophet ﷺ wept over his son Ibrāhīm. What the Book changes is the account of what has happened.

The community's teachers set the two vocabularies side by side in their teaching. The old lament measured the dead by the loss felt by the living, while the verse measures the event by the ownership of God and the direction of the soul, and the change is a re-description of what has occurred rather than a prohibition of grief. The teachers encouraged the bereaved to say the sentence aloud rather than to repeat it inwardly, since the verse places it among the things that are spoken.

**The Heart That Is Guided**

The Book attaches a further promise to the same moment: no calamity befalls except by the permission of God, and whoever believes in God, He guides his heart (64:11). The commentators read the clause as the inward work that accompanies the sentence of this verse — the words are said with the tongue, and the guidance is granted to the heart that accepts what the tongue has said. Ibn Kathīr records the observation of the early teachers that a person struck by loss either finds in it a lesson or loses himself in it, and that the promise of 64:11 concerns the first response. The two texts together supplied the community's teaching about grief: the event belongs to the decree, the disposition belongs to the person, and the guidance is given to the second.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse establishes the sentence itself, the moment at which it is said, the report of its use in the household of the Prophet ﷺ, and the two truths from which it is built. The verse that follows tells the community what the recitation of this sentence earns, so that the passage concludes its movement from announcement to trial to response and then to reward.
