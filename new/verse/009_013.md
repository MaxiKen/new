## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:13

> Will you not fight those who have broken their oaths, conspired to expel the Messenger ˹from Mecca˺, and attacked you first? Do you fear them? Allah is more deserving of your fear, if you are ˹true˺ believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Supplies the Reasons**

Verse 13 asks whether the believers will fight a people and then lists three reasons: broken oaths, a plan to expel the Messenger, and first aggression. The list is the chapter's clearest explanation of the hostile case introduced earlier. The fighting is not left hanging on a vague charge of difference. The verse presents a record of actions and intentions that have made continued submission to the old treaty arrangement impossible.

The first reason points to the destruction of a pledged bond. The second concerns an attack on the Messenger's presence and leadership. The third establishes who initiated the hostilities. Together they describe a threat to both covenant and community. Even if exegetes differed over whether the attempted expulsion refers to the Hijrah plot, later efforts against the Prophet in Madinah, or both, the principle remains: an intention to remove the Messenger by force is itself part of the case.

**First Aggression Matters**

The phrase attacked you first places the community's response in a moral sequence. The believers are not being asked to pretend that all sides began at the same time. The attack on Banū Khuzāʿah by Banū Bakr, with Quraysh support according to the historical reports, is the immediate example connected with the broken Ḥudaybiyah order. Qur'an 22:39 gives the wider principle that permission to fight was given to those who were fought because they were wronged.

This does not mean that being attacked makes every later action permissible. The Qur'an's rule of proportionality still governs. It means that the original responsibility for opening the conflict must be identified accurately. A community that refuses to distinguish aggressor from defender may call its own retaliation unprovoked, while a community that invents first aggression may use victimhood as a weapon. Verse 13 insists on a factual account before a forceful response.

**Fear Is Reordered, Not Denied**

After listing the reasons, the verse asks, do you fear them? The question does not assume that danger feels unreal. Soldiers can fear an army, families can fear expulsion, and a small community can fear the consequences of confronting a stronger power. The command is to reorder fear, not to pretend that the human emotion does not exist. Allah is more deserving of fear because His judgment is final and because obedience to Him cannot be traded for temporary safety.

The same principle appears in the command, Do not fear them; fear Me (5:44), and in the reassurance that Satan frightens his allies, so do not fear them but fear Allah (3:175). These verses do not praise recklessness. They teach that fear must not become the authority that decides whether a duty is obeyed. A believer may feel afraid and still choose the right action. Faith is shown by which fear governs the choice.

**The Rhetorical Question Addresses Hesitation**

Will you not fight is an urging question. It addresses a community that has reasons to hesitate after repeated conflict, exhaustion, and uncertainty. The command does not ignore the cost of action. It answers the cost of inaction. If a treaty-breaking power can attack allies, expel leaders, and strike first without consequence, then the people under threat are not being protected by passivity. The verse confronts the belief that avoiding a decision is always the safer option.

The surrounding passage keeps this urgency from becoming license. Verse 4 commands faithfulness toward those who have kept their treaty. Verse 6 commands protection for the one seeking an audience. Verse 13 therefore concerns a defined group whose conduct has crossed the boundaries named in the text. The believer is urged to respond to actual aggression, not to replace moral analysis with general hostility.

**Fear of Allah Produces Discipline**

The final condition, if you are true believers, connects courage with faith. A person who fears Allah will not be paralyzed by human power, but neither will he be intoxicated by his own power. Divine fear places the believer under limits even while he confronts an enemy. It forbids treachery, cruelty to noncombatants, and the pursuit of vengeance beyond the stated purpose. Courage without taqwā can become another form of rebellion.

The Prophet's conduct at Ḥudaybiyah shows the balance. He accepted terms that many Companions found humiliating because the agreement served a larger good. When the treaty was later broken, he responded decisively, but the response did not erase the distinction between those who breached and those who remained faithful. Fear of Allah allowed patience when patience was right and firmness when firmness became necessary.

**A Test for Modern Moral Courage**

Most readers will not face the battlefield situation of this verse, but its structure still tests them. Whose displeasure prevents you from stating a fact? Whose power persuades you to call an injustice peace? Do you fear losing status more than you fear betraying a trust? These questions transfer the verse's moral logic without pretending that every personal dispute is a war.

The verse does not authorize believers to dramatize their private conflicts as sacred battles. It teaches them to identify facts, protect the vulnerable, and place final accountability before human intimidation. When the record shows broken promises and first aggression, courage may be required. When the record does not show them, fear of Allah should prevent an invented charge. The believer's answer to the question is therefore not loudness. It is a disciplined readiness to act when Allah's justice requires it. Such readiness begins with truthful fear, not with excitement.
