## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:32

> He said, “But Lot is there!” They responded, “We know best who is there. We will certainly save him and his family—except his wife, who is one of the doomed.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Plea Made With One Man's Name**

Abraham's answer to the announcement is not a protest against the judgment in general; it is a question about a single person. He names Lot, his relative, the man who believed in him and travelled with him, and he leaves the matter of the towns to those who have decreed it. The Book presents this as the natural instinct of a believer who has just been told that a place is about to end: before arguing about the place, find out what happens to the one righteous person inside it. The plea also implies a doctrine that the following sentence confirms — that the presence of the faithful inside a corrupt community is a fact worth raising, and that the fate of the town and the fate of Lot are two different questions.

**"We Know Best Who Is There"**

The reply is a statement about knowledge rather than a refusal of the request. The envoys tell Abraham that they know better than he does who is in the city, and commentators explain the answer as a clarification that they distinguish the wrongdoers from the believers, and that Lot is not numbered among the wrongdoers. The sentence gently corrects Abraham on a point he had not considered: his concern assumed that a general destruction would cover the innocent, and the envoys inform him that the distinction has been made with precision. The Book elsewhere makes the same claim about the towns it destroyed, and the correction is the reason the reader can be confident in the account's justice.

The reply also protects Abraham from a false comfort. He was assured that the identification had been made by the one who could see every person in the town, which meant that no mistake would be made in either direction, and the promise he received was precise rather than general.

**Rescue of a Family as a Unit**

The promise that follows names a household: Lot and his family will be saved. The Book does not treat salvation in the narratives as an individual transaction only, and the family of a prophet is gathered with him where the family has taken his side. Another chapter reports the same night from the city's perspective, saying that the believers were evacuated before the punishment and that only one family was found there that had submitted to God (51:35–36). The phrase is stark. A town that organized itself around shame produced one household that belonged to its prophet, and the rescue operation was designed around that single unit. The Book's account of the rescue therefore answers Abraham's question in the affirmative, with a precision that is greater than he had asked for.

**The Exception Stated Before the Escape**

The sentence also names the exception: his wife will not be saved, and she is counted among the doomed. The Book states the exception in every version of the story, and the repetition is deliberate, since the reader might otherwise assume that a prophet's household is automatically delivered. The woman is identified in the narratives as belonging to the people by her sympathies rather than by her marriage, and the accounts of the night add that she looked back or stayed behind when the others left. Sūrah al-Taḥrīm places her alongside the wife of Noah as an example for those who disbelieve, noting that both were married to righteous servants of God and betrayed them, and that the standing of their husbands was of no benefit to them (66:10).

Mentioning the exception in advance also keeps the rescue from being read as a divine miscalculation. The Book records the terms before the event so that a reader who knows how the story ends will not find a stray person inside the sentence, and the same care is visible in the account of Noah's household.

**The Vocabulary of Those Left Behind**

The Arabic word rendered as doomed carries the sense of being left behind or of being covered over, and commentators connect it with dust, both the dust that buried the towns and the dust on faces that the Book describes as belonging to the disgraced on the Day of Judgment (80:40). Whether the connection is taken as etymology or as a deliberate wordplay, the image is the same: the person who remained with the town when the town was finished. The term appears in the other accounts of this night without change (7:83; 15:60; 26:171), and the consistency of the wording suggests that the Book wants this detail fixed in the reader's memory. Being left behind is the fate that the whole narrative warns against.

The word also carries the sense of abandonment rather than of mere absence. Those described are not people who happened to be elsewhere; they are the ones who remained with the conduct after everyone who objected had gone, and the term places them at the scene of the offence.

**The One Household Found in the City**

Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt gives the outcome in two lines: the believers were brought out of the city before the torment, and only one family was found there that had submitted to God (51:35–36). The two sentences measure the town. A place that had developed a settled practice of violence, had defended it in public, and had mocked its warning produced a single household on the side of the messenger, and that household was taken out before the event. The chapter under study records the promise made in advance to Abraham, and the other chapter records its execution in the dark. Together they show the precision the envoys claimed for themselves when they told a worried old man that they knew better than he did who was inside the city.

The size of the exception also answers a complaint the sūrah anticipates. A community may ask whether a message can be true when so few accept it, and the account of a city with one believing household presents the question in its starkest form and leaves it with the answer that truth is not determined by attendance.

**An Accounting That Misses Nobody**

The reply to Abraham rests on a claim about divine knowledge that the Book states in a single line elsewhere: the peoples of the past will be given a full account with sure knowledge, because God was never absent from them (7:7). The envoys' answer in this verse is that same claim applied to one town on one night. Communities are not dealt with as masses whose individual members are indistinguishable; the distinction between a wrongdoer and a believer is made before the sentence is carried out, and the promise to Abraham about his nephew shows the distinction being made in advance of the event. What the reader is meant to carry away is the precision of the arrangement. The chapter's warnings about nations are never warnings about crowds in the abstract, and the rescue of a single household from a condemned city is the demonstration.

**What the Plea Reveals About the Man Who Made It**

Abraham had just been given the news of a son in his old age, and the first use he makes of his position in the conversation is to speak for a relative in a doomed town. The Book records the plea without disapproval and records the answer to it, which is the pattern it follows whenever a prophet intercedes: the appeal is heard and the justice is not adjusted. Nothing in the episode suggests that Abraham hoped to save the city; his question was about one man. That narrowing is instructive for the reader, since it shows that the intercessor's instinct operated at the scale of a person he could name, and it shows the Book's approval of the instinct even when the answer arrives in a form he had not anticipated.
