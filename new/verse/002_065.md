## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:65

> You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, “Be disgraced apes!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Incident the Audience Already Knew**

The verse opens by attributing knowledge to the audience: they are already aware of the people who broke the Sabbath. The Book does not narrate the episode in this sūrah; it refers to it as something the community knew, and it leaves the details to be filled in from the account preserved elsewhere, where a coastal settlement was tested by fish that appeared on the Sabbath and vanished on other days (7:163).

The phrasing makes the sūrah's address a matter of shared memory rather than of instruction. The community being spoken to in Madinah is told about a failure in its own tradition, and the sentence assumes that the story is familiar. What the Book adds is the outcome, which is the part the reader needs to have stated.

**The Sabbath as a Test of Restraint**

The command to keep the Sabbath is treated in the Book as a specific obligation laid on this community, and it is described as a penalty of a kind: the restriction is named among the burdens placed on the Children of Israel, and the Qur'an notes that the day was appointed for them rather than for the Muslim community, which was given Friday.

The test at the coast made the obligation a matter of daily exposure. Fish came within reach on the one day the community was forbidden to work, and stayed away on the days when fishing was permitted, which meant that obedience depended on watching a source of food swim past. The Book's account of the incident is therefore a study in restraint under temptation rather than in violence or idolatry, and the punishment attached to it underlines how the Book weighs the deliberate crossing of a boundary.

**What the Punishment Language Means**

The command recorded here uses the imperative form, and the commentators discussed whether the transformation was physical or whether the sentence describes a state of disgrace into which the offenders settled. Some held that the community's inner nature was changed while their outward form remained, and they compared the Book's similar comparisons of people to animals elsewhere.

Both readings agree on the substance of the sentence. A people who preferred the catch of the day to the command they had been given were told to be what their conduct made them, and the Book uses the image of an animal for a person who has lost the capacity to weigh a command against an appetite. The verse in this sūrah states the command without narrating the result, and the following verse describes its purpose.

**The Qur'an's Use of Animal Imagery**

The Book compares people to animals in several passages, and each comparison is aimed at a specific failure. The community entrusted with the Torah and then neglecting it is likened to a donkey carrying books (62:5). Those who take protectors other than God are likened to a spider building a house (29:41). A man who follows his appetite whatever happens is likened to a dog panting (7:176).

The comparison in this verse belongs to the same family. What is being described is not stupidity but the loss of the distinction between an appetite and an obligation, which the Book treats as the end point of repeated disobedience. The transformation is therefore presented as a description of a condition rather than as an arbitrary penalty, and its horror lies in the fact that the condition was chosen.

**Why the Incident Is Recalled Here**

The verse sits in the middle of the address to Israel, immediately after a statement that the community turned away and was preserved only by mercy, and immediately before a verse that describes the punishment as a lesson. Its function is to give the reader a concrete instance of the crossing of limits that the previous sentence summarised, and the Book supplies the instance without editorial comment.

The pattern of the address is therefore maintained. Each item in the record is stated, and each one is followed by a statement about its purpose or its result. What the sūrah never does is separate the offences from the gifts and mercies that surround them, and this verse's place in that sequence keeps the reader's attention on the whole account rather than on a single scandal.

**Disgrace as the Book's Term for a Settled Condition**

The word attached to the transformation carries the sense of being driven away and humbled, and the Book uses it for the state of a people who have been excluded from the standing they once held. It appears again in its description of the disfigurement of the community when it records their rejection of the covenant in a later sūrah.

The sentence therefore does more than describe an event. It names a condition that the Book says persisted, and it places that condition inside the history of a community whose descendants the sūrah is addressing. The next verse states why the episode was preserved in the record, which means the Book is treating the incident as instruction rather than as a verdict on a family.

**The Book's Own Telling of the Sabbath Case**

The fuller account appears in the sūrah of the Heights, where the community is described as living by the sea and being tested by fish that surfaced on the day of rest and disappeared on working days. The test is stated there without embellishment, and the outcome is described in the same passage when the transgressors are warned that their conduct would bring ruin. The sūrah's own narrative matches its usual economy: the temptation is described in one clause, and the response of the community follows immediately.

What the two accounts share is the character of the offence. The community was not tempted by wealth, power or violence; it was tempted by food that arrived at the wrong time. The Book's placement of the incident among the addresses to a people with a book suggests that it functions as a standard case of a command tested by convenience, and the phrase used for the resulting punishment reflects that reading.
