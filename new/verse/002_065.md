## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:65

> You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, “Be disgraced apes!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**“You Are Already Aware”**

The verse opens by assuming knowledge: *you have known* — *wa-laqad ʿalimtum* — those of you who transgressed in the matter of the Sabbath. The addressees are not being told a story; they are being reminded of a case in their own communal memory, one their tradition preserved and their scholars recited. The assumption of shared knowledge does rhetorical work no new information could: a charge the accused already knows cannot be met with denial, and the verse proceeds directly to the verdict. The *of you* — *alladhīna iʿtadaw minkum* — keeps the inheritance logic of the whole address: the Sabbath-breakers are filed inside the community's own *you*, ancestors whose case belongs to the descendants as the sea and the calf did. And the word for their act is *iʿtadaw* — from the same root as verse 61's closing charge, violations, the crossing of set bounds. The recital has been accumulating this root: transgression at the table, transgression at the gate, and now transgression in the calendar. The verse names the specific bound — *fī al-sabt*, in the matter of the Sabbath — because the Sabbath was the one commandment written into time itself: a weekly border, recurring, unmissable, equally binding on the rich and the poor. The bound chosen for this case file is the one no member of the community could claim not to see.

**The Test Engineered at the Seaside**

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf preserves the scene in full, and its details explain why the Qur'an calls the event a test: *"Ask them ˹O Prophet˺ about ˹the people of˺ the town which was by the sea, who broke the Sabbath. During the Sabbath, ˹abundant˺ fish would come to them clearly visible, but on other days the fish were never seen. In this way We tested them for their rebelliousness"* (7:163). The arrangement is deliberate provocation by arrangement: a fishing town whose fish appeared, visibly, in abundance, only on the one day work was forbidden — appetite maximized exactly where law interdicted. The exegetes read the visible abundance as the test's precision instrument: the fish were not merely present but *shurraʿan*, surfacing in plain sight, engineered temptation in the town's own harbor. The Sabbath itself carried the covenant's weight — al-Nisāʾ lists its keeping among the terms of the firm pledge taken under the mountain (4:154) — so the weekly surfacing was a weekly audit of the pledge. The verse in al-Baqarah assumes the whole apparatus and states only the breach and the sentence, because for its first audience the apparatus was known: the town by the sea was a case recited in their own houses, and the Qur'an's reminder needed only the case number.

**The Town's Three Companies**

Al-Aʿrāf's filing adds the social anatomy of the breach, and it belongs to any honest reading of the sentence. The town held three groups: the transgressors, who fished; a company that warned and forbade; and a company that questioned the warners — *"Why do you ˹bother to˺ warn those ˹Sabbath-breakers˺ who will either be destroyed or severely punished by Allah?"* — answered by the warners with the sūrah's whole doctrine of public admonition: *"Just to be free from your Lord's blame, and so perhaps they may abstain"* (7:164). The answer defines the warner's two reasons: discharge — the obligation of forbidding evil falls on the one who knows, whatever the target's response — and hope — *perhaps* they will abstain, the same particle that governs the recital's purpose clauses. Verse 65's audience in Madina had just been addressed about this exact obligation: the scholars who preach righteousness and exempt themselves (2:44). The town scene is that verse's case law. When the warning was ignored, the outcome sorted the companies: *"We rescued those who used to warn against evil and overtook the wrongdoers with a dreadful punishment"* (7:165). The rescued are named by their warning — not by their fishing-abstinence alone but by their standing in the breach and speaking. The third company's fate the exegetes debate; the text's emphasis falls where the warners' answer put it: the blame discharged is the blamer's own salvation.

**“Be Disgraced Apes”**

The sentence is four words: *kunū qiradatan khāsiʾīn* — be apes, disgraced. The imperative of being — *kun* — is the Qur'an's creative command, the word that makes rather than requests; the transformation is filed as divine speech act, instantaneous and total. *Khāsiʾūn* adds the condition attached to the new form: driven away, disgraced, banished — the word used elsewhere of a dog slinking off and of the punished cast out from favor. Al-Aʿrāf files the same sentence with its precondition: *"But when they stubbornly persisted in violation, We said to them, 'Be disgraced apes!'"* (7:166) — persistence first, transformation second; the sentence lands after the warning was refused and the violation hardened into custom. The exegetical debate over the transformation's mode is old: the majority reads it bodily and literal — the Qur'an's plain statement, confirmed by al-Māʾidah's mention of people Allah reduced to apes and pigs (5:60); Mujāhid read it as hearts transformed while bodies remained, comparing the metaphor of 62:5 — *"The example of those who were entrusted with ˹observing˺ the Torah but failed to do so, is that of a donkey carrying books"* — where the animal is admittedly likeness. The bodily reading is stronger: the Qur'an distinguishes clearly between its *mathal*-parables, which announce themselves as likenesses, and its *masakh*-transformations, which are stated as events; 5:60's phrasing — reduced to apes and pigs — describes a fate, not a figure. The metaphorical reading keeps the moral truth the literal one contains: the form followed the nature. A people whose humanity had become purely appetitive — surfacing fish as the only object of thought — received the body of the appetite they lived by.

**No Progeny: The Line That Ends**

The sentence raises the question every generation asks of it, and the Prophet ﷺ answered the question directly. When the metamorphosed peoples were mentioned before him — the monkeys, and as one narrator recalls, the pigs — he said: *"Verily, Allah did not cause the race of those which suffered metamorphosis to grow or they were not survived by young ones. Monkeys and swine had been in existence even before"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2663b). The ruling has two clauses, and each does theological work. The metamorphosed left no offspring: the transformation was terminal for the transformed, a dead end rather than a new species — which answers the naturalist objection that today's apes would then be human descendants, and preserves the punishment's character as extinction-of-a-people rather than origin-of-a-kind. And monkeys and swine already existed: the animal forms were not created at the transformation but entered by it. The ḥadīth thereby fixes the punishment's semantics: *masakh* is not evolution but verdict — a soul's removal from the human register, complete with the loss of what makes human life continue, posterity. The transformed did not become ancestors; they became a case, which is exactly how verse 65's continuation files them. For the town's story the clause adds its final severity: the Sabbath-breakers did not merely change shape. Their line ended with them, and the fish they surfaced for found no heirs.

**The Sin's Shape: Law Kept, Intent Broken**

The exegetical tradition preserves the mechanism of the breach, and the mechanism is the verse's permanent warning. The townsfolk, the reports relate, did not simply cast nets on the Sabbath: they engineered around it — channels dug and pools dammed before the holy day, so the surfacing fish would be trapped by Saturday's tide and gathered on Sunday. The letter stood; the intent was gutted. The Qur'an's own text supports the reading of engineered breach: the fish came *on* the Sabbath visibly and not on other days (7:163), and the response was *iʿtidāʾ* — transgression, working the bound rather than keeping it. Islamic jurisprudence built its doctrine of *ḥiyal* — legal fictions, contrivances that preserve a form while defeating its purpose — in the shadow of this case: the jurists cite the Sabbath-town as the archetype of the sin that keeps the words and kills the ruling, and the transformation as its verdict. The shape matters beyond its history because it is the most portable sin in the catalogue. Table-complaints and mocked gates are sins of appetite and insolence, visible in the act; the contrived breach is a sin of jurisprudence — committed by people who know the law precisely enough to route around it, and who are therefore the hardest to convict before men. Before Allah the routing is the aggravation: the town that studied its harbor's calendar and built its workaround had not failed to understand the Sabbath. It had understood it well enough to defeat it, and the sentence that fell on understanding of that kind was not a fine but a species-change.
