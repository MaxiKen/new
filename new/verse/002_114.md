## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:114

> Who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them? Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear. For them there is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question of Comparison**

The verse opens with a rhetorical question of the kind the sūrah uses when it wants to fix the limits of wrongdoing: who does more wrong than the person described here? The Book measures offences against one another, and it places the obstruction of worship at the extreme end of the scale, above the slanders and distortions that the preceding verses have been describing.

The charge has two halves. The first concerns hindering the mention of God's name in the places set aside for it, and the second concerns active effort to bring those places down. The Book treats the two together as a single offence against the right of human beings to worship, and it attaches the verdict to the conduct rather than to the community carrying it out.

**Which Places of Worship Are Meant**

The Arabic word rendered as places of worship is the plural of the term from which the act of prostration is derived, and the commentators applied it to different buildings. Some pointed to the Temple in Jerusalem and the Kaʿbah in Makkah, since the Book names both as mosques, and they connected the verse with differing accounts of the Temple's destruction. Others connected it with the incident at Ḥudaybiyah, where the Makkans prevented the Prophet ﷺ and his companions from reaching the Kaʿbah for worship in that year.

Al-Ṭabarī reasoned that the reference must be to the Temple, since the idolaters of Makkah never attempted to destroy the Kaʿbah and in fact maintained it. Other commentators read the sentence more widely, applying it to anyone who obstructs access to any house of worship. All the readings agree on the conduct the verse condemns, and the difference concerns which event supplied the occasion.

**Striving for Their Ruin**

The word for ruin in the original carries the sense of making something desolate, and the commentators extended its meaning beyond physical demolition. Filling the Kaʿbah with idols was described by Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī and others as a form of destruction, since the building no longer served the worship of the One God even while its walls stood.

That reading gives the verse a wide application. A place of worship can be ruined by what is put inside it, by whom it is closed to, and by the purposes it is made to serve, and the Book's language of desolation covers all of these. Some commentators connected the verse with a small mosque that Abū Bakr had in Makkah and which was destroyed by the idolaters, and the reports show how concrete the charge was in the earliest community.

**Entering With Fear**

The sentence then states that such people have no right to enter these places except with fear, and the commentators took the clause either as a description of their eventual condition or as an instruction about the standing they should have. The Book often allows an outcome to appear as a statement of what is fitting, and the effect here is that those who closed the doors of worship ended by standing outside them in fear themselves.

Some reports applied the clause to the indemnity paid by the People of the Book in later centuries, and others treated it as a general description of the humiliation that accompanies conduct of this kind. Ibn Kathīr preferred the broader reading and noted that the Prophet ﷺ used to ask God not to afflict him or his community with disgrace in this world, which shows that the outcome described here is a punishment rather than a policy.

**Disgrace in This World**

The verse assigns two consequences, one in this life and one in the next, and the first is described as disgrace. The Book distinguishes between hardship and humiliation, and it reserves the second term for people whose conduct involved suppressing the worship of God or the standing of His messengers. The ruin they sought for others returns to them in the form of dishonour.

Later history gave the sentence an unusual visibility, since communities that destroyed or closed places of worship repeatedly found their own standing diminished in the same regions. The Book presents the outcome as part of the pattern it describes rather than as a coincidence, and the pairing of the two punishments in a single sentence keeps the reader from treating the worldly consequence as the whole of the matter.

**A Tremendous Punishment**

The second consequence is reserved for the Hereafter, and the Book describes it with the same word it uses for the gravest offences. The sūrah has been listing charges against a community that received a scripture, and the sentence here stands apart from that sequence because its subject is conduct that any person or group can commit against any house of worship.

The verse therefore functions as a general statement placed inside a particular argument. It fixes a boundary that applies wherever and whenever it is crossed, and it explains why the Book treats the freedom to worship as something more than a matter of convenience.

**A Reported Interior Reading**

Some readers in the tradition of Islamic spirituality applied the verse inwardly and described the heart as a house of remembrance whose desolation is caused by unchecked desires. The reading is a homily rather than an interpretation of the occasion, and it does not displace the plain reference to buildings. Its value lies in the reminder that the same conduct the verse condemns can be carried out at the level of a person's own relation with God, when the place set aside for Him inside a life is filled with other occupants.
