## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:114

> Who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them? Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear. For them there is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Superlative Question**

The verse opens with the Qur'an's formula for maximum injustice: *who does more wrong than those who…* — the rhetorical superlative that admits no answer because none exists. The crime it ranks at the top of the scale is double, and both halves concern the same object. The first: preventing Allah's Name from being mentioned in His places of worship — the silencing of dhikr where dhikr belongs. The second: striving for their ruin — *wa-saʿā fī kharābihā*, working, with the verb of exertion, toward the mosques' destruction. The pairing covers the two ways a house of worship dies: from within, by the banishment of its purpose, and from without, by the assault on its stones. The verse's ranking declares that no wrong exceeds these — not murder, not theft, not the whole catalog this sūrah has legislated against — because the crime aims at the relation between heaven and earth itself: the places where the Name is said are the infrastructure of that relation, and whoever attacks them declares war not on a people but on worship. The recital's audience in Madina heard the ranking with recent history in memory, and the verse's occasions — the exegetes supply several — will show that the ranking was not abstract.

**Masājid: The Places of Prostration**

The word under judgment is precise. *Masjid* derives from the verb to prostrate — *sajada* — and names not a building type but a function: the place where prostration happens, where the body performs the Name's mention at its lowest and most submitted point. The function-based etymology governs the verse's whole argument, because a masjid is a masjid by what occurs in it, and its ruin is measured accordingly. Which masājid the verse names, the exegetes debate with characteristic thoroughness. Some point to the two houses the Qur'an itself calls masjid: the Temple of Jerusalem — the Farthest Mosque of Sūrah al-Isrāʾ's opening (17:1) — and the Kaʿbah, with differing accounts of the Temple's destruction, some involving Christian agency. Others read the verse against al-Ḥudaybiyah, in the sixth year of the Hijrah, when the Makkans barred the Prophet ﷺ and his pilgrims from entering the city to pray at the Kaʿbah, and the party turned back to Madina under truce with the return deferred a year. Al-Ṭabarī reasons from history: since even the idolaters never attempted the Kaʿbah's destruction, the ruin-striving must refer to the Temple. Al-Qushayrī generalizes past every occasion: the verse applies to anyone who prevents people from entering any house of worship. The general reading carries the strongest claim on the verse's grammar — the plural, unqualified *masājid Allah* — while the occasions supply its case law: Jerusalem's destroyed Temple, Makkah's barred House, and, in a report al-Rāzī preserves, a small mosque of Abū Bakr's in Makkah that the Quraysh demolished before the migration.

**Ruin by Desecration**

The broader reading of *kharāb* — ruin — belongs to Ibn Kathīr, al-Qushayrī, and al-Rāzī, and it deepens the verse's first crime into its second. The idolaters who filled the Kaʿbah with statues were, on this reading, striving for its ruin even while maintaining its structure: a house of the One God converted into a pantheon is destroyed in the only dimension that makes it a house of God. The stones stand; the masjid is gone, because the masjid is the Name's mention and the Name has been evicted. The reading unifies the verse's two clauses into one crime with two instruments — the barrier against dhikr and the idol in its place — and it extends the indictment past demolition to desecration in every form: worship-houses maintained as museums, as markets, as monuments to anything other than the prostration their name denotes. Sūrah al-Tawbah legislates the same principle from the maintenance side: *it is not for the polytheists to maintain the mosques of Allah while they openly profess disbelief* (9:17) — physical upkeep performed by disbelief does not constitute maintenance, because the maintenance a masjid requires is the function it was built for. The verse thereby defines its key term against intuition: ruin is not collapse, and standing is not preservation; a masjid is preserved by its prayer and ruined by whatever replaces it.

**The Entry of Fear**

The sentence between crime and sentence — *such people have no right to enter these places except with fear* — has been read along two axes, and both concern the barer's standing. On the legal axis, the clause bars entry as of right: those who prevent the Name's mention hold no entitlement to the houses they prevent in, and if they enter, they enter without right. On the historical axis, some exegetes connected the fear and the verse's coming disgrace to the concrete humiliations of defeat — including, in al-Ṭabarī's report, the jizyah's stipulated payment (9:29) as the worldly form of the clause's *disgrace in this world*. Others demur: disgrace is broad, and the tradition preserves the Prophet ﷺ regularly praying that disgrace not be visited on him or his community in this life (Ibn Kathīr's note) — a prayer that treats the verse's worldly disgrace as a real possibility for every community, not a fixed fate of one. The weighing favors the broad reading, and the broad reading sharpens the verse's symmetry: the party that barred worshippers from the house in confidence is stripped of confident entry itself; the fear they were entitled to inflict returns as the condition of their own approach. The houses keep their rule — entered in humility, by those whose business inside is the Name.

**Disgrace Here, Tremendous Punishment There**

The verse's sanctions run on the two-world ledger the recital has used throughout: *disgrace in this world, and a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter*. The pairing is the same structure that sentenced the Scripture-traders of verse 85 — exposure here, severity there — and its logic is proportional: a crime committed in public against the public houses of worship receives its first answer in public. The worldly disgrace is not merely added punishment but the crime's natural fruit: whoever strives against worship becomes known for it, and the knowledge is the beginning of the sentence. The otherworldly term carries the superlative's weight — *tremendous*, ʿaẓīm, matching the opening question's *more wrong than*: the greatest of wrongs draws the greatest of punishments, and the verse leaves no gap between its ranking and its sentence. The history the occasions supply reads as the clause's execution: the Temple's destroyers passed into proverb, the bar at al-Ḥudaybiyah reversed within two years when the same pilgrims entered Makkah in aUmrah completed under the truce's own terms, and the bar's authors entered the city the year after that beneath the conquest's amnesty — every party of the verse receiving its portion, in the world, on the record. The tremendous punishment remains where the verse files it, in the Hereafter's jurisdiction, against a crime the two worlds together were required to answer.

**The House the Heart**

The esoteric commentators add the verse's inward register, and the addition is not decorative but structural: the heart is the house of the love and knowledge of Allah — the Kaʿbah is called the House of Allah, and the heart bears the same name in the tradition's inward grammar — and whoever destroys it through false desires commits, in the interior, the wrong this verse ranks above all others (the readings of al-Jurayrī's circle and al-Qushayrī). The inward register explains why the outward crime deserves its superlative: the masjid of stone exists for the sake of the masjid of the heart, the Name's mention in the house for the Name's dwelling in the organ, and the assault on the first is an assault on the second's appointed means. The two registers also share their law of ruin — the heart, like the house, is destroyed not by demolition but by displacement, occupied with whatever evicts its proper function, maintained in form while abandoned in purpose. The verse leaves its readers with both jurisdictions open: the houses on the earth, whose sanctity no political convenience suspends, and the house in the chest, whose sanctity no private excuse suspends — both built for one function, both ruined by one substitution, and both answered, the verse promises, by the two-world sentence of the One whose Name they exist to carry.
