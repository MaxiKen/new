## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:66

> Make no excuses! You have lost faith after your belief. If We pardon a group of you, We will punish others for their wickedness.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Excuse Has Reached Its Limit**

The verse refuses the defense offered by those who mocked sacred things. “Make no excuses” does not mean that repentance is impossible. It means that explanations designed to erase the act will not be accepted. The speakers want to turn deliberate contempt into harmless entertainment, but the command names the seriousness of what they chose.

There is a difference between an excuse and an explanation. An explanation can help establish facts and correct an unfair accusation. An excuse, in this setting, is a shield used to avoid responsibility after the facts are clear. The believer should be willing to hear evidence, yet he should not keep reopening a settled matter merely because the guilty person has found a new wording for the same denial.

**Mockery Can Undo a Public Claim of Faith**

The verse describes the act as losing faith after belief. Scholars have treated this as a warning that deliberate mockery of Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger is not a small lapse. The point is not to authorize ordinary people to issue careless verdicts about a person's final state. The point is to prevent sacred contempt from being minimized by the language of comedy.

Faith is not only a verbal badge. It includes reverence for the One believed in and respect for the message through which belief is known. When a person knowingly attacks those foundations, his outward claim becomes contradictory. The contradiction requires repentance and renewed submission, not a debate about whether the audience should have laughed.

**Pardon and Punishment Can Be Distributed Justly**

The verse says that a group may be pardoned while others are punished for wickedness. This shows that people who share a gathering or a general label are not judged as one undifferentiated mass. Allah knows who initiated the wrong, who encouraged it, who laughed knowingly, who objected, and who later repented sincerely.

That distinction should make believers careful in public judgment. They should condemn the act clearly without claiming knowledge of every heart. A person who repents may be restored to obedience, while one who persists may remain accountable. Collective responsibility can exist when people support a wrong, but individual differences still matter before Allah.

**Repentance Requires More Than a Better Story**

The passage leaves a door open through the possibility of pardon. Pardon is not gained by finding a more acceptable description of the offense. It requires regret, abandonment of the wrong, and a return to reverence. If the mockery harmed people, repair may also require an apology or a public correction.

A servant should not delay repentance while waiting to feel dignified. The feeling of humiliation is often the ego losing control of the story. Better to bear that feeling than to meet Allah with a defense built from lies. A truthful confession does not make the offense good; it makes change possible.

**Wickedness Is a Chosen Pattern**

The verse refers to wickedness, not to an accidental stumble. A pattern of contempt, evasion, and hostility reveals a person choosing the wrong direction. One careless word can be serious, but it can also be followed by immediate sorrow. Repeated mockery followed by repeated excuses shows that the heart is protecting the sin.

This principle applies to every moral defect. A servant should distinguish an involuntary weakness from a habit he keeps defending. If the pattern is visible, he should act before it becomes a settled identity. Allah's warning is severe because the path can be reversed while life remains, but the path cannot be reversed by calling it harmless.

**Truthful Speech Is a Form of Faithfulness**

The verse finally teaches that faith and truthfulness cannot be separated. The hypocrites' excuse fails because it denies what they knowingly did. The believer may be corrected, but he does not call black white to preserve his standing. He accepts the cost of truth because Allah's judgment is more important than the audience's relief.

A community should reward this honesty. It should allow a person who repents to return, while still protecting people from repeated harm. Pardon is not the same as pretending that no wrong occurred. Justice records the wrong, repentance changes the person, and Allah decides the final account with perfect knowledge.

This is why the command against excuses is followed by the possibility of pardon. Allah does not ask the servant to produce a perfect past before returning. He asks him to stop arguing with the truth. The person who repents may need to accept that some listeners will remember the offense, but he does not use their memory as a reason to remain false. He repairs what he can, learns reverence, and lets consistent conduct speak. The community protects the faith by refusing mockery and protects repentance by allowing the sincere return. Both firmness and mercy are required because the same act can be treated as a joke by the proud and as a wound by the one who reveres Allah.

The verse also warns leaders not to confuse punishment with repair. A person who has crossed a serious boundary may need limits, public clarification, or protection of the community. Those measures should serve justice, not the leader's anger. The possibility that one group is pardoned means that the door of sincere return must remain visible. The possibility that another is punished means that pardon cannot be granted merely because an apology is convenient. Allah knows the difference between a changed heart and a new performance. Human beings look for evidence of change while leaving the final judgment to Him.
