## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:20

> It is as if the lightning were about to snatch away their sight. Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light, but when darkness covers them, they stand still. Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sight Nearly Snatched by the Thing That Lights the Road**

The verse keeps its subjects under the same sky as the previous one and changes the problem. What threatens them now is the flash itself, bright enough to be felt as an assault on the eyes. The Arabic phrasing describes a near-event: the lightning almost takes the sight it is being used to see by, so that the very illumination these men depend on is also the danger they cannot escape.

That double-edged quality is the point of the image. Guidance in this Book lights the road, and it also exposes what is on it, which means the same revelation that shows a man where to walk also shows him what he would rather not have seen. The men described here want the benefit of the light without the discomfort of what it reveals, and the flash that helps them is nearly the thing that blinds them.

**Walking Only While the Flash Lasts**

The second clause reports a pattern of movement tied entirely to conditions: whenever the light is on, they walk. They are not described as running from the storm, sheltering, or turning back; they advance while the way is visible and only for as long as visibility lasts. The Book uses the same picture for people whose commitment follows their convenience rather than preceding it.

The Qur'an's portrait of this group elsewhere records the same timing. It reports that when they stand for prayer they perform it *“half-heartedly only to be seen by people—hardly remembering Allah at all”* (4:142). The obedience is real and it is intermittent, and its boundaries are set by what the situation rewards. In the parable the intermittency is written into the weather itself: the men do not choose the rhythm they live by, and they follow it exactly.

**Standing Still When the Light Goes**

The third movement is a halt. When the darkness covers them, they stand; the Arabic verb describes a person planted in place, making no progress in either direction. The failure is not rebellion but paralysis, and the verse gives it the same amount of attention as the walking, because in this Book a man who never moves is as lost as a man who moves on the wrong road.

The imagery here connects back to the earlier verdict that the people under discussion will not return to the right path. Standing still is the physical equivalent of that sentence: a person who has left the road, cannot see it, and will not go looking. Nothing in the verse suggests that these men are waiting for guidance. They have arranged their lives so that faith is a resource drawn upon when it is visible and dispensed with when it is not.

**The Unfulfilled "If God Had Willed"**

The sentence that follows ends the storm with a statement about what did not happen: if God had willed, their hearing and sight would have gone. The Book uses this construction repeatedly, and its function is to place what a person keeps in the same category as what he has already lost, both being grants held at the discretion of the one who gave them.

A verse in Sūrah al-Anʿām states the same conditional as a question put to the deniers: *“Imagine if Allah were to take away your hearing or sight, or seal your hearts—who else other than Allah could restore it?”* (6:46). The reminder in both places is about ownership rather than punishment. The faculties named in this parable, hearing and sight, are the same ones that were reported lost in the previous verse, so that the conditional in this verse touches something the reader has just watched happen to somebody else.

**The Closing Name: Capable of Everything**

The verse ends with *qadīr*, rendered here as Most Capable. The word is built on the root of measuring and determining, and it names a power that operates by design rather than by impulse. Spoken at the end of a passage about light granted and withdrawn, the name explains the pattern rather than softening it.

The Book puts the same name where it wants the reader to consider the scale of what can be granted or withheld: *“Blessed is the One in Whose Hands rests all authority. And He is Most Capable of everything”* (67:1), and again at the end of a passage about the creation of the human being out of clay and the breathing of a spirit into him (32:9-30). In this verse the name covers both halves of what the storm has already shown: the power that sustains the sight of a man walking by a flash, and the power that could end it between two steps.

**Two Parables That Leave the Same Impression**

The first likeness ended with a man in complete darkness, and the second ends with men who cannot keep their footing when the light goes. Neither closes with a punishment described in detail; both close with the position of the people concerned, which is why each is remembered as a picture rather than an argument. The Book has been unusually patient with its portrait of these men, giving them two parables in addition to the report of what they say in private, and the images make the same point twice so that it cannot be missed.

What the images have in common is a dependence on light that the men themselves cannot secure. Their walking, their standing and their blindness are all decided by a flash that arrives when it will. The rest of the sūrah moves away from these men to address everyone in the city, and the address begins with a call that puts their situation in a single sentence.
