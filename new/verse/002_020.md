## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:20

> It is as if the lightning were about to snatch away their sight. Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light, but when darkness covers them, they stand still. Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Almost**

The parable opens on the word *almost*. The lightning *were about to snatch away their sight* (2:20) — it does not do so. The threat stays at the edge of happening, and the suspense is the point. A single destructive flash would have finished the scene and let the reader off; a light that nearly blinds, over and over, describes a state that continues.

What is nearly taken is the sight itself, not the object of sight. The distinction runs through the whole passage. The thunder could have killed them; the lightning could have blinded them; and the merciful outcome in both cases is that they are left with the faculty and without the benefit of it. They keep their eyes and see nothing they can use.

**Walk, Stop**

The rhythm of the next clause is the parable's centre. *Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light, but when darkness covers them, they stand still* (2:20). The movement is entirely reactive: motion when there is light, paralysis when there is none, and no carrying of what was seen from one moment into the next.

A journey made this way covers almost no ground. Each flash moves a man a few steps; each interval returns him to where he was; and the sum of a night's travelling is a man standing in the same place, exhausted, having been shown the road repeatedly. That is the sūrah's account of what a life of intermittent conviction produces.

**Knowledge That Cannot Be Banked**

The parable is precise about what these people lack, and it is not information. They see the road. They see it as often as the lightning comes, and the lightning comes constantly. What they cannot do is retain it — to hold the picture in the dark and keep moving by it.

That is the difference between seeing and believing, and the Quran builds a great deal on it. Faith in this vocabulary is not a fresh piece of data; it is the capacity to keep walking on data one already has. A person who sees the road in every flash and stops in every interval has all the evidence he is going to get. What he lacks is the thing that makes evidence into a direction.

**If God Had Willed**

The closing clause is the most important sentence in the parable, and it is the one a reader skips. *Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight* (2:20). The statement is conditional and the condition is not met: He has not taken them. That is the verse's argument, and it is aimed directly at anyone who would read this passage as a description of divine compulsion.

The logic runs like this. If their deafness and blindness had been produced by an act of removal, the appropriate response would be despair — there is nothing to be done about a faculty that has been confiscated. The verse forecloses that reading. The faculties are intact. The incapacity is theirs, and the sūrah has spent fifteen verses describing how it was acquired: by lying, mocking, trading, and refusing to answer.

**Capability Stated at the Moment of Restraint**

The sūrah closes the parable with an attribute: *Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything* (2:20). The pairing is deliberate. Power over all things is asserted in the same breath as a statement that a specific exercise of power has not occurred.

The Quran does this repeatedly, and the shape is always the same: the capability is named so that the restraint is visible. A God who could not have taken their hearing could not be said to have spared it, and a reader who misses the capability misses the mercy. The sūrah is not describing a God who lacks the means to force these people. It is describing a God who has the means and is not using them, which is the sūrah's answer, in advance, to every deterministic reading of this passage.

The clause also removes the complaint that the parable is unfair. These people did not ask for a storm, a reader might object, and it is hard to walk in lightning. The verse's answer is that the conditions are not the issue: the faculties are intact, the flashes keep coming, and the darkness between them is not total. What is being measured here is not whether the road was easy but whether a person moves when he can see, and the sūrah has already told us the answer. They walk in the light and stop in the dark, and after a night of that they are standing where they started. The parable's cruelty is that it shows them the road each time and takes it away each time, and the showing is never the problem.

**What the Two Parables Established**

Between them, the fire and the storm have said everything the sūrah needs to say about the condition it opened with at 2:8. The hypocrite had light and lost it; he has light and cannot use it; he is terrified and cannot escape; his faculties are intact and his position is unchanged. Both parables end with the person still in the dark and still responsible for being there.

The passage closes, therefore, not with a threat but with a fact: God could have blinded them and did not. Whatever happens next, the sūrah has made clear, will not be because the capacity to see was withdrawn. The next address opens with the widest possible summons.
