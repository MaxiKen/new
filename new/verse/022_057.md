## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:57

> But those who disbelieve and deny Our revelations, it is they who will suffer a humiliating punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Judgment**

Verse 56 gave the authority, the judgment and the reward. Verse 57 gives the other outcome, and the two verses are one sentence in argument.

The symmetry is exact and it is the point. The believers are *in Gardens of bliss*; the disbelievers *have a humiliating punishment*. Neither outcome is described at length here, because the sūrah has already described both at length — the Gardens in verse 23 and the Fire in verses 19 to 22. What these two verses add is the authority under which the distribution is made.

**Two Conditions Joined**

*Wa-alladhīna kafarū wa-kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — and those who disbelieved and denied Our signs.

Two verbs describe one group. The first, *kafarū*, is the state: they covered over, they refused. The second, *kadhdhabū*, is the act: they declared the signs false.

The combination is more specific than either alone. A person can fail to believe through neglect; a person who actively declares the signs false has taken a position on them, and the sūrah has been describing that position since verse 3. The offence here is the same one verse 51 named as striving against the signs — an expenditure of effort rather than a mere absence.

**"A Humiliating Punishment"**

*Fa-ulāʾika lahum ʿadhābun muhīn* — those, for them is a humiliating punishment.

The adjective is *muhīn*, from the root meaning to make small, to abase, to render contemptible. It is the same word the sūrah used for the disgrace promised in verse 9, and the repetition ties the two passages together: what was announced as coming in this world and the next is here described as delivered.

The choice of adjective is precise. The punishment is not described primarily as painful in this verse — the sūrah has used *alīm*, painful, elsewhere and will use it again. Here the emphasis is on the abasement, and the abasement fits the offence. The people described spent their effort trying to make the signs of God ineffective, and what they receive is the experience of being made small.

**Humiliation Is the Natural Answer to Arrogance**

The sūrah has diagnosed its opponents' condition as one of pride more than once. Verse 9 described a man turning his shoulder away in contempt. Verse 15 described a man in a rage at God's help going to someone else. Verse 51 described people striving to frustrate the signs.

What all three share is a refusal to be small. The punishment named here is the imposition of smallness, and the sūrah's logic is not vindictive but reparative: the humiliation is what happens when a claim to greatness is measured against the actual size of the One it was asserted against.

Sūrah al-Kahf makes the same point about the destination rather than the punishment: those who strive against the signs find the worst of places to be, and what makes it worst is not the temperature.

**"Those" — the Emphatic Pronoun**

*Fa-ulāʾika lahum* — so *those*, for them. The Arabic uses the demonstrative pronoun for distance as the subject of the sentence before the predicate arrives.

The effect is to point. The verse is not describing a class in the abstract; it is identifying the specific people who did the denying, and the pointing pronoun is the grammar's way of singling them out for what follows. The sūrah used the same construction in verse 51 for the same group, and it will use it again at verse 72.

There is a secondary effect as well. The distance implied by *ulāʾika* — those, over there — quietly separates the group from the speaker and from the reader. They are named as a party that has been identified, and the pronoun is part of the verdict rather than incidental to it.

**Why the Punishment Is "Humiliating" Rather Than Merely Painful Elsewhere**

The sūrah has a vocabulary for punishment and it uses it selectively. Verse 9 promised *the torment of burning*; verse 19 described garments of fire and scalding water; verse 25 promised a *painful* punishment. Here the adjective is *muhīn*, humiliating.

The variation is not random. Verse 25's punishment was announced for people who had prevented others from the Sacred Mosque — a public crime against a public institution — and it was described in the language of pain. This verse's group is described by what they denied, and the punishment is described by what it does to their standing.

The match is exact. The offence was the attempt to make the signs of God ineffective, which is an attempt to make God's word small. What is imposed in return is smallness.

**The Verse's Pairing With 56**

Read the two verses together and the sūrah's eschatology is stated in its barest form. Sovereignty belongs to God; He judges; one group is in ease and the other in abasement.

The pairing also answers the objection the sūrah raised at verse 17 and deferred. Every religious community claimed that it had the truth and that the others were wrong, and this sūrah refused to adjudicate between them in this world. Verses 56 and 57 are the adjudication, and the criteria stated here are not communal membership: belief and sound deeds on one side, disbelief and active denial of the signs on the other.

**Where the Sūrah Goes From Here**

The next two verses will address the specific case of the emigrants, and the passage will then turn to a series of short statements about God's power and truth. Verse 57 is the last full statement of judgment in the sūrah; everything after it is either particular application or the evidence on which the judgment rests.

It is also, in a sense, the last statement of the sūrah's main argument. The chapter opened with the Hour, argued for the Resurrection, legislated the pilgrimage, authorised defence, and traced the pattern of destroyed communities. Verses 56 and 57 are where all of that arrives: a judgment, executed by the sole authority, dividing people by what they did with what they were given.

What remains after this is not new argument but application. The emigrants, the retaliators, the signs in the natural world, the parable of the fly, and the closing command to strive are all addressed to a reader who already knows how the matter ends.
