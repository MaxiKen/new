## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:122

> Can those who had been dead, to whom We gave life and a light with which they can walk among people, be compared to those in complete darkness from which they can never emerge? That is how the misdeeds of the disbelievers have been made appealing to them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Parable of the Dead and the Living**

Can those who had been dead, to whom We gave life and a light with which they can walk among people, be compared to those in complete darkness from which they can never emerge? The verse states a comparison and leaves the answer to the reader, and the images it uses are the sūrah's own vocabulary from its earlier verses. The person who was dead and received life is the one who heard the message and responded; the light he walks by is the guidance; and the person in the darkness is the one who has been left in the condition described in the passage about the turning of the hearts.

The Book uses the same language about life and light in several places. The sūrah of Fāṭir says that the dead and the living are not equal and that God makes whom He wills hear (35:22); the sūrah of the Cow describes God as the guardian of the believers, bringing them out of darkness into light, while the disbelievers have their guardians in false gods who lead them from light into darkness (2:257); and the sūrah of the Cattle itself uses the phrase about walking among people in the present verse.

**The Light and the Darkness**

The two images are the Book's standard figures for guidance and its absence. To walk among people by a light is to move through the world with a course set and a destination known, and the Book contrasts that condition with the man who gropes in a darkness from which he cannot emerge. The sūrah of the Cattle earlier described the person whom the satans have seduced in the open country, calling to others who call him back to guidance he does not follow (6:71), which is the same condition stated by a different image.

The Book's use of light for revelation appears throughout its descriptions of the earlier scriptures as well. The sūrah of the Table describes the Torah as containing guidance and light (5:44), and the sūrah of the Cattle will later describe the Book given to Moses as a light and guidance for people (6:91). The verse in front of us treats the effect of that guidance on a person's conduct, which is the light by which he walks.

**The Deeds Made Appealing**

That is how the misdeeds of the disbelievers have been made appealing to them. The sentence explains the condition of the second figure in the comparison, and it uses the same vocabulary as the earlier passages about the enemies of the prophets and the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter (6:112–113). The Book's account of the made-appealing of evil conduct appears at several points in this chapter and outside it, and it is always attached to the people who have chosen their course.

The commentators described the appeal of the deeds as something produced by God's granting of respite and prosperity, which leads the people to suppose that their way is approved. The Book describes the same arrangement in the sūrah of the Family of Imran, where the disbelievers are warned against thinking that the time granted to them is good, since they are given time only to increase in sin. The verse in front of us states the arrangement as a fact of the disbelievers' condition rather than as a future threat.

**What the Two Conditions Mean for the Audience**

The comparison in this verse is addressed to people who were making the judgement implied by the question. The members of the audience who had denied the message judged the believers to be the losers, and the verse asks them to state whether the two conditions are alike. The Book uses this form of argument at several points, and it usually states the answer inside the question.

The verse's force lies in the change of perspective it requires. The person who walks by a light is the one whose way leads somewhere, and the person in the darkness is the one whose movement has no direction. The audience's assumption was that the established position of the majority constituted the direction, and the verse states the opposite in terms of sight and walking.

**The Book's Use of This Comparison**

The comparison between the living and the dead and between light and darkness runs through the Book's descriptions of the two responses to revelation. The sūrah of Fāṭir states that the dead and the living are not equal, and it places the statement in the context of God's making whom He wills hear, since the Prophet cannot make those in the graves hear (35:22). The sūrah of the Cow makes the same distinction at the end of its account of the covenant.

The verse in front of us therefore expresses a contrast the Book draws everywhere. What the sūrah has been describing since its opening verses, through the argument about the signs and the accounts of the earlier messengers, is how the two conditions are produced: the guidance comes from God, and the people who are left in their darkness have refused what was offered.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The verse that follows states that God has placed in every society the most wicked of its people to conspire in it, and that they plot only against themselves without perceiving it (6:123). The two verses belong together, since the darkness described here is the condition of the people who conspire in the next verse: they are active opponents of the message, and the sūrah describes them as plotting against their own interest.

That transition continues the sūrah's account of the opposition to the messengers. Having described the individual conditions of the guided and the lost, the chapter turns to the corporate activity of the people who work against the message, and it treats their activity with the same knowledge of the outcome that the earlier passages supplied.
