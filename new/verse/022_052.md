## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:52

> Whenever We sent a messenger or a prophet before you ˹O Prophet˺ and he recited ˹Our revelations˺, Satan would influence ˹people’s understanding of˺ his recitation. But ˹eventually˺ Allah would eliminate Satan’s influence. Then Allah would ˹firmly˺ establish His revelations. And Allah is AllKnowing, All-Wise.
**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Verse Actually Says**

*Wa-mā arsalnā min qablika min rasūlin wa-lā nabiyyin illā idhā tamannā alqā al-shayṭānu fī umniyyatihi* — We sent no messenger or prophet before you but that, when he *tamannā*, Satan cast something into his *umniyyah*.

The two words govern every reading of the verse. *Tamannā* means to long for, to wish, to desire. *Umiyyah*, from the same root, means a longing, a desire, or, in several Qur'anic occurrences, a false hope — as in the hearsay of 2:78, the wishes of 2:111, the desires of 4:119, and the false hopes of 57:14. A secondary sense of "recitation" exists and is the one many commentators adopted, but it is rare and it cuts against the Qur'an's own usage of the term.

The translation used here takes *umniyyah* as the recitation the messenger delivered, and reads the casting as interference with how that recitation lands rather than with what was revealed. That reading has strong support, and the rest of this commentary is built on it.

**"Messenger or Prophet"**

The verse names both categories: no *rasūl* and no *nabī* was sent before you without this happening. The pairing is not redundant for its own sake; it is the Arabic way of closing off every exception.

The two words have been distinguished in various ways in the tradition — a messenger as one sent with a new scripture, a prophet as one who calls to an existing one — but the point of naming both here is totality. Whoever is sent, in whatever capacity, meets interference. The verse is not describing an occasional hazard of one office; it is describing a standing feature of the job.

Sūrah al-Anʿām states the standing feature as a rule about every prophet: *“And so We have made for every prophet enemies — devilish humans and jinn — whispering to one another with elegant words of deception”* (6:112). Every prophet, enemies appointed — human devils and jinn devils both, working in concert with polished words. Verse 52's casting is that enmity in operation: the whisperers do what they were made for, the interference arrives on schedule, and the universality is the consolation. No messenger was exempted, so none was failed by it.

**Interference With Reception, Not With Revelation**

On this reading the verse makes a claim that the rest of the Qur'an makes repeatedly: a messenger delivers, and the delivery is met with interference. Satan casts into the reception — into what people hear, into the interpretation that circulates, into the rumour that follows the message around.

This is not a small claim, and it is not consolation. It says that the corruption of a message after it leaves the messenger's mouth is a predictable feature of the world rather than a sign that the message failed. Every messenger experienced it.

Al-Rāzī recorded a related reading with approval: that the verse establishes the humanity of the prophets. They are not angels; they feel, long for and suffer rejection, and they are not impervious to prompting. What protects them is not insensibility but God. The Qur'an describes the Prophet as grief-stricken at his people's refusal — *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial”* (18:6) — which is the condition this verse speaks to.

**The Story of the Cranes**

No verse in this sūrah comes trailing a longer and more contested appendix. A number of early commentators connected this verse — along with Sūrah 53's verses on the goddesses and the passage at 17:73–75 — to an account known as the "story of the cranes," and in Western literature as the "Satanic Verses."

The account, in its various versions, runs as follows. The Prophet was reciting Sūrah al-Najm near the Kaʿbah. After the verses naming three of the goddesses, Satan cast words onto his tongue during a lapse, and he was heard to utter a sentence praising them as exalted intercessors whose mediation could be hoped for. The Quraysh, pleased that their goddesses had been honoured, joined the prostration at the sūrah's close. Gabriel afterwards corrected him, and this verse was revealed to console him.

The passage at Sūrah al-Isrāʾ that the account leans on says the opposite of what the account needs: *“They definitely ˹thought they˺ were about to lure you away from what We have revealed to you … hoping that you would attribute something else to Us falsely”* (17:73). *About* to lure — *la-kādū*, they nearly did — and the nearly is the whole point. The verse records an attempt that did not succeed, which is why it cannot support a story about an interpolation that did. What the Qur'an says its enemies almost achieved, the appendix claims they achieved; the two cannot both be read as history.

**Why the Account Fails**

The grounds for rejecting it are cumulative, and the classical commentators assembled them methodically.

*Transmission.* The versions contradict one another on who spoke the words, whether they were spoken at all, and what happened next. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both noted that early exegetes and historians applied inconsistent filters to what they recorded, and that the story is poorly sourced.

*Psychology.* A man known for trustworthiness, who had endured years of persecution for condemning those very idols and had refused offers of wealth and power to abandon his mission, would have destroyed his own credibility by making the concession. Al-Rāzī observed that the struggle against idols was what defined the mission. Al-Māturīdī put the sharper question: if the Prophet could not tell one caller from the other the first time, on what basis would he have recognised the second one as the correction?

*Chronology.* Most versions date the incident to year five of the calling. Sūrah 17 is placed five or six years after that, and this sūrah later still. A verse sent down to console the Prophet for an episode eight years in the past would be anomalous, and nothing accounts for the alleged verses circulating in the interval.

*Theology.* The prophets are held to be protected from error of this kind. Al-Qurṭubī rejects the premise outright, citing Satan's own testimony: *“I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me”* (14:22). If Satan could seize a person's speech, no one could withstand it.

*Literary fit.* The verse's two halves are not joined in the manner the account requires. God first wipes out what Satan cast, and afterwards establishes His signs — so the establishing follows the erasure rather than being produced by it, and it attaches most naturally to verse 51: they labour to frustrate the signs, and God makes them firm. The alleged interpolation would also contradict the sūrah it supposedly entered, which argues that intercession belongs to God alone (53:26).

**What Remains**

Set the story aside and the verse is clearer, not thinner. What it says is that every messenger's delivery was interfered with, that God removes the interference, and that God then establishes His signs — in that order, and as three separate acts.

The closing names are the verse's own emphasis: *All-Knowing*, so the interference was not hidden from Him, and *All-Wise*, so the interval in which it operated was not a lapse. What looks like a message being corrupted is, on the sūrah's account, a message being delivered, interfered with, and then established more firmly than before.

Sūrah al-Raʿd gives the erasure-and-establishment its parable, in foam and metal: *“The ˹worthless˺ residue is then cast away, but what benefits people remains on the earth”* (13:17). Foam carried off by the current, slag skimmed from the melt — the worthless removed first, the beneficial remaining after. Verse 52's three acts follow the same order: the casting arrives like foam on the water, God wipes it away, and then His signs stand established. The interference was never part of the message; it was the scum on it, and scum does not survive the settling.
