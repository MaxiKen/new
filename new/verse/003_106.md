## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:106

> On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy. To the gloomy-faced it will be said, “Did you disbelieve after having believed? So taste the punishment for your disbelief.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Day That Shows on the Face**

The verse describes the Day of Judgment as the day when some faces become bright and others dark, and it reports what is said to the second group. The image is drawn from everyday experience, where the face carries news of the inner state before any word is spoken.

Arabic uses the darkening of the face for shame, distress, and suppressed anger, and the Book employs the idiom in this world before it applies it to the next. One passage describes a man whose face grows gloomy when he is given news of the daughter he attributes to God, and who suppresses his rage as he does so (43:17). The verse before the reader transfers that familiar reaction to the day when nothing is concealed.

**The Faces That Brighten**

The bright face is the Book's image for joy, relief, and approval, and the spiritual sense the commentators added is that the light on those faces is the light of faith made visible. The Book describes one group of people as receiving the finest reward and even more, and explains that neither gloom nor disgrace will cover their faces (10:26).

The quiet detail in that passage is the two negations. The reward is positive, and the description of what the faces will not carry is what makes the joy concrete, since it names the two things a person dreads most in public: sadness and humiliation. The verse before the reader divides humanity into these two conditions and gives no third category, which is the Book's usual way of describing the final state.

**The Faces That Darken**

The dark face belongs to the people the Book elsewhere describes as liars against God. One passage states that on the Day of Judgment those who lied about God will be seen with their faces gloomy, and then asks whether the Fire is not a fitting home for the arrogant (39:60).

The connection between the face and the accusation is exact. What the dark faces display is the truth that those who lied had denied for a lifetime, and the Book's language of darkening describes the moment when the denial is no longer available. The verse before the reader does not describe the mechanism; it reports the condition, and the sentence that follows supplies the words addressed to the people in it.

**The Implied Words in the Sentence**

The clause about what is said to the dark-faced has no introductory verb in the text, and the commentators supplied it. On their reading, the sentence means that it will be said to them, and the implied words stand before the question that follows.

The grammatical point is small and has an effect on how the verse is heard. What the people receive is not a punishment imposed without explanation but a question, and the question itself is the first part of what they face. The Book's final scenes repeatedly include this element of address: the condemned are spoken to, their conduct is named before them, and the sentence follows. The verse's structure matches that pattern, and it explains why the question comes before the command to taste the punishment.

**"Did You Disbelieve After Having Believed?"**

The question is put to people who had once accepted the message, and the commentators identified them in several ways. The most natural identification, on the sūrah's own argument, is the people the previous verses described as having been warned about being drawn away from their belief, since the warning and the outcome belong to the same passage.

Others read the verse as addressed to those who broke the primordial acknowledgement of God's lordship, the moment the Book describes when humanity testified that God is its Lord (7:172). A further reading applies it to hypocrites, whose attestation of faith was contradicted by their actions. The three readings converge on the phrase the verse uses, which names faith as something the people had and then lost.

**The Punishment Named After the Offence**

The words spoken to the dark-faced tell them to taste the punishment for their disbelief, and the Book's use of the language of tasting describes direct, personal experience of a thing rather than knowledge of it.

The pairing of punishment with the offence is the Book's consistent practice, and the placement of the phrase here gives the passage its logic. The sūrah has described a community that believed and then turned, warned against following those who work to draw others away, and commanded the believers to hold together. The verse shows the end of one of those courses in a single sentence, and the taste it names belongs to the disbelief rather than to an arbitrary decree.

**Where the Verse Sits in the Passage**

The verse follows the warning against imitating communities that divided after receiving proofs, and it supplies the scene in which the consequence of that conduct is displayed. The commands and the warnings have been given; what remains is the outcome.

The passage's movement is characteristic of the sūrah. It describes a duty, gives the history that illustrates it, names the punishment of those who did the opposite, and then names the reward of those who held to the message. The reader reaches this verse with the previous instructions still in view, and the faces he is shown are the picture of what the instructions were protecting him from.

**The Day and the Book's Habit of Speaking of Faces**

The Book describes human beings in the Hereafter through the condition of their faces more often than through any other part of the body, and the choice is not arbitrary. The face is the one part a person cannot hide and the first thing another person reads.

Passages describe faces that are humbled, faces that are radiant, and faces that are turned down, and each of them conveys a state faster than any list of deeds could. In this verse the device carries the whole contrast between the two groups, so that the reader is shown what the two lives produced rather than being told about it. The verse's next sentence then supplies the one piece of speech in the scene, and the words are addressed to the people whose faces have already answered the question.

**The Sects to Which the Phrase Was Applied**

Al-Qurṭūbī and al-Rāzī recorded that the words about disbelieving after belief were applied by some commentators to the groups that formed within the community's later history, among them the Khawārij, who emerged within a generation of the Prophet's death. The identification extends the sentence beyond the first audience without changing its sense, since the pattern it describes is a departure from a position once held. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭūbī also report that the Prophet was shown something of the Hereafter in which people he knew were driven away from him, and that he was told he did not know what they would do after him. The accounts place the sentence within the warnings the community carried about itself, and the sūrah's own context, which speaks of disbelievers and of a community warned about turning back, supports the same reading.
