## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:135

> The Jews and Christians each say, “Follow our faith to be ˹rightly˺ guided.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Invitations, Neither a Choice**

The verse reports a dueling pair of claims: the Jews say follow our faith, the Christians say follow ours, and each promises that its road is the road to guidance. These were not two offers on one table from which a neutral shopper might choose. Each community was speaking for its own religion alone, and each treated the other's invitation as void — the claim is exclusive on both lips. The Qur'an's answer refuses the terms of the contest entirely. It does not adjudicate between Jerusalem's synagogue and the church, because the argument as framed assumes that guidance arrived with one of these institutions. The reply reaches behind both, to a faith older than either name: the *millah* of Abraham. By declining to choose between the two sects, the verse dissolves the question they were fighting over.

**The Millah of Abraham**

*Millah* denotes a creed, a way of religious life held by a community — and here it is identified with Islam itself. This is not an isolated gesture. The command to follow Abraham's faith recurs at the close of the Meccan sūrahs: *"Then We revealed to you ˹O Prophet, saying˺: 'Follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists'"* (16:123); *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Surely my Lord has guided me to the Straight Path, a perfect way, the faith of Abraham, the upright'"* (6:161); and Abraham's way is called the best faith in the company of doing good and full submission (4:125). The pattern is consistent: whenever the Qur'an needs a name for the religion it preaches that predates every sect, it names Abraham's. The prophet whom all three traditions claim belongs, in the Qur'an's telling, to none of them and authorizes the one that follows him in substance.

**Ḥanīf: Inclining Away from Every Party**

The word attached to Abraham here is *ḥanīf*, and its etymology is famously strange. The root can mean to be lame or crooked, and it can also mean to incline toward; Semitic cognates carry unflattering senses — hypocrite, vile, pagan, heathen in the sense of neither Jew nor Christian. Christian Arabic writers used it of Muslims, sometimes with those negative echoes intact. Yet in the Qur'an the word is uniformly a title of honor. The likely path of meaning is a reversal: one who inclines away from a group is reproached by that group, and praised when the group is judged to be in error. A ḥanīf is therefore someone not bound to a party's limitations, distortions, and tribal loyalties — not hidebound, not sectarian to the detriment of universal truth. Grammatically the word appears in the Qur'an almost always in the indefinite accusative, describing the state one is in when believing or worshipping: upright, as a ḥanīf, in the condition of pure undivided devotion.

**The Uprightness Written Into Human Nature**

That condition corresponds to what the Qur'an calls *fiṭrah* — the unspoiled primordial nature God gives every human being. The sūrah of the Romans ties the two together in one command: *"So be steadfast in faith in all uprightness ˹O Prophet˺—the natural Way of Allah which He has instilled in ˹all˺ people. Let there be no change in this creation of Allah"* (30:30). To be ḥanīf is not to join a new sect but to return to the orientation the soul shipped with. The same word governs worship in the sūrah of the Clear Proof — serving God *"with sincere devotion to Him in all uprightness"* (98:5) — and standing upright for God without association in the sūrah of the Pilgrimage (22:31). Judaism and Christianity, on this reading, are particularizations of a universal religion, and each particularization accumulated what the ḥanīf inclines away from.

**Not the Pilgrim, Not the Circumcised**

Early exegetes tested narrower definitions of ḥanīf and rejected them for good reasons. Some proposed "pilgrim," but pilgrimage to the House was performed by the pagan Arabs too, so the label would have included idolaters. Some proposed "follower" in the sense of one who kept Abraham's practices such as circumcision, but circumcision was kept by the Jews, so the label would have collapsed into one of the very parties the verse is declining. Al-Ṭabarī settled on the sense of straight, upright following of Abraham's way. He added a clarification that matters: the verse does not imply that the prophets before Abraham lacked uprightness; Abraham is named here because God made him an imam of pure monotheism with a standing role for all monotheists after him — the same imamate granted in 2:124. The name anchors a lineage of faith that runs through conduct, not through the boundary markers the sects were trading.

**The Ḥunafāʾ Already in Arabia**

The word was not a Qur'anic invention landing on deaf ears. Even among the non-Muslim Arabs of the Prophet's time, the quality of being ḥanīf was associated with Abraham, with renouncing idols, and with leading a moral life, and the biographical literature remembers individual Arab seekers who abandoned idolatry for a monotheism traced to Abraham before the revelation ever reached them. It was never a label for Jews or Christians, and it never denoted an organized group with meetings and membership — it named a posture. That is precisely why the Qur'an's answer works in this argument. When the two sects say follow us, the reply names the one thing both had already conceded was older and purer than their dispute: the upright devotion of Abraham, which no party owns and which every party is measured against.
