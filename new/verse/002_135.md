## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:135

> The Jews and Christians each say, “Follow our faith to be ˹rightly˺ guided.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Invitation Reported**

The verse records an invitation made to the Muslim community by the two communities that possessed scriptures, and the invitation is conditioned on guidance. What is being asked is not that the believers study the Torah or the Gospel but that they adopt the religious identity of the group issuing the invitation.

Commentators explained that the sentence does not describe a joint proposal offering a choice between the two paths. Each community was making the claim for itself, and the verse reports both claims together so that the reader sees the pattern they share. Each believed that guidance passed through its own door, and the answer the sūrah supplies applies to both without distinguishing between them.

**The Answer That Names Abraham**

The response commands the Prophet ﷺ to identify the community with a figure older than both, and the phrase used for faith carries the sense of a path or a way of life. The sūrah has just finished telling the story of that man, and the answer is therefore not a claim but a reference to material the reader has already received.

The Book's method here is to move the discussion to common ground. Both communities honoured Abraham, and both claimed a connection to him, so the sūrah answers the invitation by pointing to him rather than by defending a separate tradition. What the reader is asked to accept is that the religion of Abraham is the one that submission to the final revelation restores.

**The Word Translated as Upright**

The Arabic term rendered here as upright is one of the Book's technical descriptions of Abraham, and it appears in several passages where his faith is contrasted with the paths of the later communities. The word describes a person who turns away from error toward a pure monotheism, and it is used in the Book only of those who hold that position without the distortions the sūrah has been describing.

Commentators noted that the term is not the name of a sect or a group. It describes a state, and the Book uses it in forms that indicate a person is or does something in that condition. The quality belongs to the unspoiled nature God gave to human beings, and the sūrah's later verse about the natural way of God connects the two ideas directly.

**The Range of Meanings Discussed**

The commentators explored the term at length. Al-Ṭabarī understood it as the straight following of Abraham's way, and he rejected narrower readings, such as the identification with pilgrims, which would have included idolaters, or with the circumcised, which would have included the Jews. Others read it as one who is devoted entirely to God, or as a follower of Abraham in the practices he established.

The discussions of the word's origin are less important than its force in the verse. Whatever its etymology, the meaning was clear to the community being addressed, and the sūrah uses it to describe a position defined by what it is not: Abraham was not a polytheist, and the religion being claimed in his name must therefore be free of the associations the sentence excludes.

**Not a Community but a Position**

The Book's use of the term in the plural, describing people who are in that state rather than members of a body, supports the reading that it names a condition rather than an institution. The sūrah's answer to the two communities therefore does not consist of setting up a third community alongside them; it consists of identifying a position that existed before the divisions and that the final revelation restores.

That has consequences for the way the passage should be read. The verse is not an argument that one group's pedigree is better than another's. It is a statement that the religion of Abraham is defined by its content, and the content has been set out in the preceding verses: submission to God, the worship of the House, and the transmission of the same charge to the children.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah**

The sentence follows the reminder that the earlier communities have passed and that each party is answerable for its own deeds, and it precedes the confession of faith that the believers are commanded to make. The three items fit together: individual accountability, the identification of the community with the faith of Abraham, and the statement of what that faith contains.

The sūrah returns to this theme later when it commands the followers of the Prophet ﷺ to follow the way of Abraham and describes him as one who submitted in uprightness and was not a polytheist. The repetition indicates that the identification is central to the passage's argument rather than incidental to it.

**What the Believers Inherit**

The verse gives the community a lineage that is not tribal and a history that is not its own invention. What the believers are told to claim is the position of a man who lived before the scriptures they were invited to adopt, and whose religion the Book describes as pure monotheism. The claim is therefore a responsibility rather than an honour: the community is asserting that it maintains what Abraham established, and the following verses state what that maintenance involves.

**The Tone of the Refusal**

The answer begins with a negation that clears the ground, and then states the alternative in a single clause. The Book does not rebut the two communities at length in this verse, because the material for the answer has already been supplied in the narrative. The reader who has followed the account of the House and the patriarchs knows why the community belongs to Abraham rather than to those who claimed him afterwards, and the sentence trusts that knowledge.
