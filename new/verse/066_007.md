## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:7

> ˹The deniers will then be told,˺ “O disbelievers! Make no excuses this Day! You are only rewarded for what you used to do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Scene Rather Than a Statement**

The verse does not describe the disbelievers; it quotes what is said to them. The reader is placed at the moment of arrival, and the words are addressed to people who are already there. The commentators locate the scene precisely: this is spoken to the deniers as they enter the Fire, and *“this Day”* is the Day of Resurrection. The previous verse described the keepers of that Fire, harsh and strong and incapable of disobedience, so the voice here carries no possibility of appeal.

Presenting judgement as reported speech rather than as narration does something a description could not. The reader overhears a sentence that will one day be addressed to somebody, and the identity of that somebody is not fixed by the text in front of him. The chapter has been building toward this: it has just told every believer to guard his family from a Fire, and now it lets him listen to the reception of those who were not guarded.

**The Excuse Forbidden Before It Is Offered**

*“Make no excuses this Day!”* (66:7) is a prohibition on an act that has not yet been attempted. The prohibition implies the impulse: people arriving at that moment will want to explain themselves, as people do in every other setting where they are held to account. Excuse-making is the most reliable human response to exposure, and it works, in this world, surprisingly often. Circumstances were difficult, the information was unclear, others were worse, the times were against us.

What removes the excuse is not a refusal to hear it but the conditions under which it would be offered. When the reality of a life's deeds is laid out in full, an excuse requires a gap between what happened and what is known to have happened, and there is no gap. The Qur'an describes people at that moment recognising the truth of their own record, and the recognition is what silences them. This is also why the verse does not bother to refute any particular excuse. It simply forbids the category, the way one forbids something that has become pointless rather than something that is dangerous.

**Requital Exactly Proportioned to Conduct**

*“You are only rewarded for what you used to do”* (66:7) contains the justification for everything in the verse. The restrictive particle is doing the work: only for what you did, nothing added, nothing invented. The identical sentence is addressed to the deniers elsewhere, in the middle of a passage about the Fire: *“You are only rewarded for what you used to do”* (52:16).

The principle behind it is stated as a purpose of creation itself: *“For Allah created the heavens and the earth for a purpose, so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed. And none will be wronged”* (45:22). Two things follow. First, the punishment is not an excess of divine anger but a return of what was sent; the recipient is receiving his own consignment. Second, the phrase *mā kuntum taʿmalūn* uses a construction denoting habitual action — what you used to do, repeatedly, as a settled practice. The judgement is on a pattern of life rather than on an isolated failure, which is consistent with a Book that assigns absolution for lesser faults to those who avoid the major ones.

**Who the Verse Is Actually Addressed To**

A sentence spoken to disbelievers in the Fire cannot be heard by disbelievers in the Fire, since the matter is settled for them. Its audience is the one reading it now, with the term still running. This is the pattern for Qur'anic scenes of judgement generally: they are addressed in the second person to people who no longer have choices, in order to be read by people who do.

The chapter's arrangement makes this unusually clear. Verse six commanded believers to guard themselves and their families; verse seven shows the outcome for those who were not guarded; verse eight will call the believers to sincere repentance and describe their light spreading before them on the same Day. Three consecutive verses, one Day, two receptions. Placing the deniers' arrival between the command and the promise is not an interruption of the argument but its evidence. What makes the call to repentance urgent is that the alternative has just been quoted aloud, and the excuse that everyone privately intends to make has been ruled out in advance.

**The Word "Disbelievers" as a Description of Conduct**

The address names its audience by what they refused rather than by tribe, nation or era. *Kafara* in Arabic carries the sense of covering something over, and the Qur'an uses it for people who had the truth put before them and buried it. That is a description of behaviour, and it explains why the second half of the verse speaks about deeds rather than beliefs: the requital is for *“what you used to do”* (66:7), because what they did is what their refusal amounted to in practice.

The chapter's arrangement supports reading the term this way. Two verses earlier, believers were told to guard their families, and the guarding described was a matter of conduct — teaching, correcting, keeping commands in a house where children watch. Nothing in this sūrah treats faith as a position held privately and separately from behaviour. The wives corrected in the opening verses were believers whose hearts had tilted; the disbelievers addressed here are people whose covering-over ran all the way through their lives. Between those two cases lies the whole audience of the chapter, which is why the call to repentance follows immediately.

**Silence as the Final Condition**

What the verse ultimately describes is a state in which speech has lost its function. Excuses are forbidden, the record is complete, and the only thing left to say has already been said by the deeds themselves. For creatures who spend their lives explaining, negotiating and presenting themselves, this is a strange and specific deprivation.

It also clarifies what the Qur'an means by a Day of accounting. The accounting is not an argument in which two sides present cases; it is the reading out of a ledger that both parties can see. Every ordinary human tribunal leaves room for advocacy precisely because the facts are never fully known, and advocacy is how the gaps get filled. Where nothing is unknown, advocacy has no material to work with. The verse thus closes the door that the rest of the chapter keeps holding open: while the term runs, a person may turn, repent, correct a tilted heart and guard his household. At the point described here, all of that has passed into the record, and the record speaks instead of him.
