## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:37

> to bring you every skilled magician.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pharaoh's Court Mobilizes Expertise Against Truth**

The chiefs want every skilled magician brought to Pharaoh. The word every gives the plan an air of completeness. If the kingdom can assemble its best practitioners, it can make Moses' sign appear ordinary or defeat it in public. The court is not content with one rival performer. It wants a national search for the greatest available expertise, as though quantity and reputation can settle the source of a miracle.

This response reveals how power treats knowledge when knowledge threatens power. Expertise is not invited to investigate the event freely. It is recruited to defend a conclusion already chosen. The magicians are valuable because they can support Pharaoh's interpretation, not because the court is willing to follow whatever the evidence shows.

**Skill Is Real but Not the Same as Prophetic Authority**

The verse does not deny that the magicians possess skill. Their craft has recognizable techniques and an audience that knows its effects. The coming encounter will distinguish between what skilled humans can produce and what God gives as a sign. That distinction requires taking human ability seriously without making it ultimate.

A person can be highly trained and still work within the limits of created means. Skill can manipulate appearances, influence attention, and produce fear. It cannot make the practitioner independent of God or turn a human claim into divine authority. Pharaoh's court confuses technical mastery with the right to define reality. The verse prepares the reader to see that expertise must itself be judged by its source and purpose.

**Every City Is Searched for a Defender of the Throne**

The order reaches all the cities. Pharaoh's state is using its geography and administration to find people who can serve the royal case. The search has a political dimension: specialists from across the land will make the response look broad, national, and legitimate. The ruler wants the public to see not one frightened official but a united kingdom arrayed against Moses.

That display can influence people before the contest begins. A large number of experts creates an impression that the court must be right. The audience may assume that a government would not assemble so many practitioners unless the matter had been settled. Numbers and organization become substitutes for proof. The verse quietly exposes that substitution by describing the mobilization without saying that the court has answered Moses' claim.

**The Magicians Are Drawn Into a Political Contest**

The specialists may have begun as performers serving private or religious needs, but Pharaoh's order turns their craft into state policy. Their livelihood and status now connect them to the ruler's success. They are not merely demonstrating art; they are being asked to protect a political order from a messenger who has challenged its theology.

This creates a moral test for expertise. A craft is not neutral simply because it is skillful. The practitioner must ask what the skill is being used to accomplish. Is it serving truth, healing, and lawful benefit, or is it being used to deceive a population and preserve oppression? Pharaoh's request places every summoned magician inside that question even before he offers them reward.

**The Court Believes a Crowd Can Outvote a Sign**

By gathering every skilled magician, Pharaoh's advisers hope to create a contest whose winner will be accepted as the truth. That logic treats revelation as a competition between performances. If enough experts side with the ruler, the public may forget that the original issue was the release of Israel and the authority of God.

A crowd can make a falsehood feel secure, but it cannot change the nature of an event. If a hundred people repeat an accusation, the accusation still requires evidence. If a specialist imitates an appearance, the imitation does not erase the reality it imitates. The court's plan depends on the audience confusing social dominance with truth. Moses' sign will test that confusion.

**Skill Can Become a Veil Over Moral Responsibility**

The word skilled may draw admiration, but technical excellence does not excuse the end to which it is put. An expert who helps a tyrant deceive the people remains responsible for that help. The court may praise the magicians as clever servants, yet the verse's context places their craft under a moral judgment. The question is not only whether they can perform, but whether they will use their ability for falsehood.

This applies to every profession that can serve power. Writers can polish propaganda, lawyers can disguise injustice, and officials can make oppression look orderly. Skill increases responsibility because the skilled person understands the effect of his work. Pharaoh's search for every magician therefore becomes a search for people willing to lend expertise to a threatened throne.

**The Order Cannot Guarantee the Result**

Pharaoh can command a search, but he cannot command the sign to lose its meaning. He can bring people together, yet he cannot decide what they will recognize once they witness the contest. The state controls transportation and public staging, not the truth of what happens before the public.

The verse ends with the arrival still ahead. That delay lets the court display its confidence and organize its defense, but it also places the summoned experts on the road toward a witness they did not choose. The search for every skilled magician will not enlarge Pharaoh's lordship. It will create the audience before whom the limits of human skill become clear.

The court's language of expertise cannot alter the spiritual question. Even the most celebrated practitioner remains a dependent creature whose craft operates within created limits. The order to find every magician therefore gathers witnesses not to an equal contest between rival lords, but to a scene in which human skill will be measured against a sign from the Creator.
