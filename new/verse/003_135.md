## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:135

> ˹They are˺ those who, upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness and do not knowingly persist in sin—and who forgives sins except Allah?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Description of How Such People Handle Failure**

The verse completes the portrait of the people described in the two verses before it, and it deals with the one thing the earlier description left out: what happens when they do wrong. The answer is given in three steps, and the third is the one that defines the state.

The sequence is an act, a remembering, and a refusal to continue. The Book presents repentance in this practical shape rather than as an emotional state, and the sentence is one of its most complete statements of what the return to God involves.

**The Two Ways of Doing Wrong**

The verse names two things that can be committed, and the commentators distinguished them. The first term covers a shameful and serious deed; the second is broader and covers any wrong that a person does, including the ones that seem small, described as an injury done to oneself.

The distinction matters because it removes the excuse available to a person who regards his offence as minor. Whether the wrong is grave or slight, the verse describes what the people for whom the garden is prepared do about it, and the response is the same in both cases.

**Remembering God as the Turning Point**

The step between the act and the request for forgiveness is the remembrance of God, and its position in the sentence is the key to how the Book understands the whole process. What produces repentance is not a calculation of consequences but an awareness of the one against whom the wrong was done.

The Book connects remembrance with reform in other passages, and the sūrah itself has used the same root in its commands to the community about mindfulness. A person who carries the awareness of God into his daily conduct will find that a wrong act produces the response described here, and the verse presents that response as the natural fruit of the awareness rather than as a separate discipline.

**The Question About Forgiveness**

The sentence interrupts its own description with a question: who forgives sins except God? The question is rhetorical, and its function is to remove the possibility that the person might seek the remedy elsewhere or despair of finding it.

The Book's statements about forgiveness support the question on both sides. One passage states that God does not forgive the association of others with Him but forgives anything else for whoever He wills (4:48). Another addresses those who have exceeded all limits against themselves and tells them not to lose hope in the mercy of God, who forgives all sins (39:53). The question in this verse stands between the two statements and confirms both, since it locates the remedy with the one who has set the terms and rules out any other source of pardon.

**Refusing to Persist**

The final clause describes people who do not continue in what they have done knowingly, and the qualifier carries the weight of the definition. The refusal to persist is what separates repentance from a cycle of offence and regret.

The Book states the same standard elsewhere in its warnings to those who take forgiveness for granted, and its descriptions of the people it praises consistently include the element of change. The verse's wording is careful: the people described are not those who never wrong themselves, since the sentence opens by acknowledging that they do. They are the people who, having done so, do not settle into it.

**What the Book Says About the Outcome of Such Repentance**

The Book's other statements about repentance describe its effects on the person who turns back. One passage promises that those who repent, believe, and do good will have their evil deeds changed into good deeds, and describes God as forgiving and merciful (25:70). Another states that whoever commits evil or wrongs himself and then seeks God's forgiveness will find Him forgiving and merciful (4:110).

These sentences describe the same movement the verse before the reader outlines, and they use the same vocabulary of seeking forgiveness. What they add is the assurance that the return is met rather than merely required, and that the person who makes it is not left where he was.


With this verse, the description of the people for whom the garden is prepared is complete. They give in both conditions, govern their anger, pardon those in their power, and when they fail they turn back without delay.

The sequence is worth noting as a unit because it defines the community's ideal without any reference to status, lineage, or achievement. The Book has described a group of people by what they do with their property, their tempers, their authority, and their mistakes, and the next verse states what awaits them.

**Why the Book Describes Repentance in Steps**

The verse sets out the sequence of a return to God, and the sequence is instructive because it separates the components of an act that people often treat as a single feeling. The wrongdoing comes first, then the remembrance, then the request, and finally the refusal to continue.

Naming the steps serves a practical purpose. A person who has done wrong can test himself against each of them, and the fourth is the one that cannot be faked: continuing in a fault while intending to ask forgiveness is precisely what the verse excludes. The Book's descriptions of repentance elsewhere use the same components, and the sūrah's placement of this description among the marks of the people prepared for the garden shows where it belongs in the community's understanding of itself.

**Ways of Remembering and the Two Sayings**

The commentators described the remembrance named in this verse in three ways: by recalling God's anger and the punishment that follows sin, by living with the awareness that God is watching, and by pronouncing the divine name. The three belong together in the practice the verse recommends, and al-Rāzī drew from them a principle that the community's teachers repeated: no sin is great once forgiveness is sought for it, and no sin is small if it is committed deliberately and persistently. The sentence therefore attaches the outcome to the direction the person is facing rather than to the size of what he has done, and the phrase about persistence is what makes that clear.

**What Persistence Means**

Al-Ṭabarī explained the phrase rendered as persisting in sin as describing those who continue in an act knowingly, whether the knowledge is of the act's prohibition or of its character as disobedience. The qualification is significant because it excludes the person who falls into a fault and regrets it. Some commentaries connect the verse with a report about a man who bought dates from a woman, embraced and kissed her, and then came to the Prophet regretting what he had done. The report illustrates the situation the sentence describes, and the sūrah's answer to it is the description of the people who remember God, ask forgiveness, and do not continue. The words for workers of righteousness in the following verse were read by al-Rāzī as evidence that the reward of the garden involves what a person does as well as the grace that makes his work possible, and the two passages are read together for that reason.
