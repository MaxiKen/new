## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:5

> But once the Sacred Months have passed, kill the polytheists ˹who violated their treaties˺ wherever you find them, capture them, besiege them, and lie in wait for them on every way. But if they repent, perform prayers, and pay alms-tax, then set them free. Indeed, Allah is AllForgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Has a Defined Setting**

This is the passage's hardest sentence, and it must be read with every condition the verse itself supplies. The command concerns the polytheists who violated their treaties, and it follows a public notice and a period of security. It is not a free-standing order directed at every non-Muslim in every place and time. Verse 4 has just protected treaty keepers, and verse 6 will require asylum and safe escort for one who asks to hear the message. Those neighboring verses are not optional commentary added later; they are part of the same legal unit.

The opening time condition is also significant. Fighting is not presented as the first response to the treaty crisis. A known period must pass. Classical exegetes differed over whether the sacred months here are the four traditionally designated months or the four-month safe period announced earlier. Both readings preserve a limit. The verse does not describe an unbounded season in which force may begin whenever a commander chooses. Its chronology belongs to the law of notice, breach, and response.

**The Hostile Case Is Defined by Breach**

The translation itself supplies an important clarification by identifying the polytheists as those who violated their treaties. That reading fits the chapter's internal argument. Verse 13 names three features of the hostile group: they broke their oaths, sought to expel the Messenger, and attacked first. Verse 4 protects those who remained faithful. Verse 7 repeats the rule that loyalty is answered with loyalty. Taken together, these statements make it difficult to treat idolatry alone as the operative cause of the fighting described here.

Some jurists did read the verse more broadly, and their reasoning must be acknowledged rather than hidden. They focused on the force of the command and on the political transformation of Arabia after years of conflict. Other jurists emphasized the explicit exceptions and the prophetic practice of honoring treaties and granting protection. The stronger contextual reading sees a war order directed at a hostile political configuration, not a permission to kill any person whose creed differs. Verse 5 itself gives the terms of release: when the hostile alignment is abandoned through repentance, prayer, and alms, the person is to be set free.

**The Exit Clause Is Inside the Sentence**

The verse moves through the harsh verbs of war and then immediately states what happens when the accused leave the hostile category. They are not merely spared from that moment onward; the command says to set them free. Repentance changes their legal and spiritual position. The same sentence that authorizes capture refuses to make captivity the final purpose. Its ending invokes forgiveness and mercy rather than triumphal boasting.

This structure matters for any ethical reading of force. A war order that has no stated conditions for stopping can become an appetite. Here the command includes its own exit. The people who repent, pray, and pay the alms-tax are released. Verse 11 will describe them as brothers in faith. Even if one debates how the conditions operated in the historical setting, the text's direction is unmistakable: the purpose is not destruction for its own sake. The hostile arrangement is to end; the person who abandons it is not to be treated as though he remains inside it.

**Why the Verse Cannot Be Separated From Verse 6**

The next verse addresses an idolater who asks for protection so that he may hear God's Word. The Prophet is commanded to grant protection and then escort the person to safety, whether or not he accepts the message. This case shows that the war command is not triggered by the bare fact that a person is an idolater. A person within the same broad religious category can receive safe conduct because his actual action is to seek information rather than to continue the hostile campaign.

The passage therefore contains a legal scale. Faithful treaty keeping receives protection. A seeker of asylum receives protection. A treaty-breaking leadership that attacks first is confronted. The categories are determined by conduct and circumstance. Removing verse 5 from that scale creates a rule the chapter itself does not state. Applying it without the notice period, treaty exception, and asylum command is not strictness; it is textual distortion.

**Mercy Does Not Deny the Cost of Aggression**

The verse should not be softened into a statement that says nothing about war. The historical community faced armed alliances that had repeatedly used treaties to prepare further attacks. A political order has a duty to protect its people. The Qur'an recognizes that patience can become a way of abandoning those who are exposed to aggression. Force may be required to end an organized threat, but the legitimacy of that force depends on the conditions that produced it and the limits governing it.

The verse's final names, All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, do not erase the preceding command. They explain its moral horizon. Forgiveness remains available to the individual who turns back, and mercy governs how the former enemy is treated after the change. A just response can be severe toward an active threat while remaining open to repentance. That combination is harder than either sentimental permissiveness or permanent vengeance.

**The Verse Must Not Be Turned Into a Modern Slogan**

For readers today, the main obligation is interpretive honesty. Do not use this verse to justify violence against civilians, neighbors, or people who have not entered the historical hostile category. Do not use it to deny the Qur'an's recognition that organized aggression sometimes must be stopped. And do not remove the conditions that make the verse legally and morally intelligible: a broken treaty, an announced interval, a hostile campaign, a path of repentance, and release for those who take it.

A verse this forceful requires more context, not less. Its lesson is that justice protects the faithful, confronts treachery, and preserves a way back. The sword is not the chapter's only instrument. Notice, distinction, asylum, repentance, and mercy surround it. Read within that architecture, verse 5 is a command about a defined war. Read as a detached slogan, it becomes the opposite of what the surrounding verses teach.
