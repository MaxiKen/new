## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:30

> And whoever does this sinfully and unjustly, We will burn them in the Fire. That is easy for Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Whoever Does This**

The pronoun *hādhā* — this — refers to the two prohibitions just named: devouring one another’s wealth illegally and killing one another or yourselves (4:29). The verse does not repeat those acts in detail; it gathers them as a single *this* and adds the condition that determines its recompense. The sūrah has spent its opening chapters defining what counts as *bāṭil* — mixing orphan wealth with one’s own (4:2), substituting bad for good, hastily consuming before the orphan grows up (4:6), reclaiming dowries by slander (4:20), exceeding inheritance limits (4:14) — and what counts as lawful trade by mutual consent (4:29). Now it says that doing *this* — the devouring or the killing — with two additional qualities leads to a named end. The brevity matters. After a long sequence of family and market laws, the warning is condensed to a single sentence so that it can be remembered in the market and at the estate table. A person who handles a sale or a legacy can recall *whoever does this* and know immediately which acts are meant, because the sūrah has already shown their many forms.

**Sinfully and Unjustly — The Inner State and the Outer Wrong**

The condition *ʿudwānan wa ẓulman* — sinfully and unjustly — was explained as covering both the motive and the measure. *ʿUdwān* — aggression, overstepping, hostility — describes the inner drive to transgress a limit knowingly, to push beyond what is allowed because desire or anger urges. *Ẓulm* — injustice, misplacement — describes the outer act of putting a thing where it does not belong, taking a share that was not assigned, spending another’s property as one’s own. Early authorities noted that the pair excludes the person who errs out of ignorance and then returns soon (4:17), or who acts under mistake, coercion, or genuine doubt. The sūrah has repeatedly distinguished ignorance with soon repentance, which is pardoned, from persistence until death is present, which is not (4:17–18). Here the same distinction operates at the level of commerce and homicide: the one who devours wealth or sheds blood while knowing it is forbidden and intending to overstep is the one the threat addresses. That qualification keeps the law from becoming a trap for the weak — whom the sūrah has already called created weak (4:28) — while keeping it firm against the strong who calculate overstep as policy.

**We Will Burn Them in the Fire**

The recompense *fa sawwfa nuṣlīhi nāran* — We will burn them in the Fire — uses the emphatic future *sawfa* to stress certainty. *Nuṣlīhi* — to roast, to cause to burn — conveys direct contact with fire, as the same verb described those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth eating fire in their bellies and then being burned in a blazing Hell (4:10), and those who exceed limits abiding in Fire with humiliation (4:14). The sūrah keeps the image of burning for wealth wrongs for a reason. Wealth taken as food becomes fire as food, and wealth taken as gain becomes fire as dwelling. The Fire is not an arbitrary penalty attached to a civil infraction; it is the true form of the act when its cover is removed. The Qur’an elsewhere says that wrongfully taken wealth will be hung around necks on the Day of Resurrection, showing that the property itself becomes the punishment. Here the same thought is kept simple: devouring what is not yours and killing where killing is not permitted lead to being devoured by Fire. The subject is *whoever* — any person, regardless of status — which matches the sūrah’s consistent equalization from a single soul (4:1) to equal witnessing for the orphan (4:6) to obligatory shares for men and women (4:7).

**That Is Easy for Allah**

The closing *wa kāna dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīrā* — and that is easy for Allah — was read as both warning and reassurance. As warning, it says the punishment is not difficult for God to execute; the barrier is not capability but forbearance, which is why the sūrah elsewhere calls Him All-Forgiving, Most Merciful (4:25–26) and Most Forbearing (4:12) yet still names the burning as sure for the deliberate overstep. As reassurance, it tells the wronged that justice does not depend on their own power to enforce it. The orphan whose wealth was eaten, the heir whose share was withheld, the victim of a killing — none needs to produce the Fire; the One who is Reckoner (4:6) and Witness over all things (4:33) and ever Merciful to believers (4:29) will see that what is easy for Him is done. The phrase also deters the calculation that power protects from consequence. A person who devours a small amount may think the amount is too small to matter, or that his status will shield him; the verse answers that the act’s ease for God makes status irrelevant. What is easy for Him does not require a large apparatus; it requires only His will to recompense, which the sūrah has already said He wills to turn toward you in mercy when you keep limits (4:27) and wills to punish when you persist.

**A Bridge Between Property and Life**

Placed between the double prohibition and the promise that avoiding major sins leads to absolution of lesser ones (4:31), this verse functions as a bridge. It gathers the property and life violations together under one recompense, showing that both strike at the same trust: the community as *yourselves* — *anfusakum* — a single self-hood (4:29). The sūrah began with that selfhood as creation from one soul (4:1) and has returned to it repeatedly — *you are from one another* (4:25), *do not kill yourselves* (4:29). To devour another’s wealth unjustly is to wound that single self; to kill is to sever it. Both are described as *ʿudwān wa ẓulm* because both put appetite ahead of another’s right to wealth and life. The ease with which God will burn the deliberate doer therefore guards the ease with which the weak might otherwise be devoured and killed. The sūrah does not leave that guard as threat alone; the very next verse offers a lighter path — avoid the major sins forbidden, and lesser faults will be absolved and you will enter a place of honour (4:31). Between the easy burning for persistence and the promised honour for avoidance, the reader is given a clear choice where the weak may still hope.

**When Ease Should Deter**

The practical force of *that is easy for Allah* is deterrence through proportion. A person weighing whether to falsify a measure or to pressure a widow to surrender a dowry may weigh the immediate gain against the likelihood of being caught by people. The verse shifts the scale to being caught by God, for whom it is easy. That shift was already prepared by *“Allah is ever Watchful over you”* (4:1) and *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6) and *“Allah is a Witness over all things”* (4:33). Each of those Names makes detection certain; this clause makes recompense certain as well. At the same time, the qualifier *ʿudwānan wa ẓulman* keeps the deterrence from becoming despair. A person who slipped, who devoured out of ignorance and then turned soon (4:17), is not the one the burning addresses; the burning addresses deliberation and injustice persisted in. That distinction lets the weak who err take the next verse’s promise as their own, while those who calculate overstep read this verse as addressed directly to them. The Fire that is easy for Allah to fuel is therefore also easy for the deliberate wrongdoer to avoid: do not do this sinfully and unjustly, and the path to honour remains open.
