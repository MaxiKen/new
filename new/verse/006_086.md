## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:86

> ˹We also guided˺ Ishmael, Elisha, Jonah, and Lot, favouring each over other people ˹of their time˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Group of Names**

We also guided Ishmael, Elisha, Jonah, and Lot, favouring each over other people of their time. The catalogue that began with Isaac and Jacob and continued through the later Israelite prophets now closes with four names, and the four come from different settings and different centuries. Ishmael is Abraham's first son and the ancestor the Arabian audience claimed; Lot is his nephew and the prophet of the cities that were destroyed; Jonah is the man of the whale; Elisha appears in the Book only in lists like this one and with the companions of Elijah.

Bringing these four together in a single sentence is the sūrah's statement about the breadth of guidance. The men named here did not belong to one family or one land, and their stories in the Book take place among different peoples and in different conditions. What they share is the favour the sentence names at its end.

**The Favour Named at the End**

The phrase translated as favouring each over other people of their time is the Book's language of election, and it is used for the prophetic calling in several places. The sūrah of the Family of Imran says that God chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ʿImrān above all people of their time (3:33); the sūrah of the Bee describes Abraham as a model of excellence whom God chose and guided to a straight path (16:121); and the sūrah of the Ranged records Lot being described as one of the messengers (37:133).

The wording also makes clear what the favour was not. Being preferred over the people of one's time is a statement about a mission and its reception, not a claim about the inherent superiority of one lineage over another. The sūrah has already said that God elevates in rank whoever He pleases (6:83), which removes the possibility of reading the catalogue as a genealogy of merit.

**Ishmael and the Arabian Line**

Ishmael's place in the list would have struck the sūrah's first audience with particular force, since the northern and central Arabian tribes traced their descent to him. The Book records him building the foundations of the House with Abraham and praying for acceptance (2:127), and it describes him as true to his promise and as a messenger and a prophet. The sūrah of Maryam gives him a place among those mentioned with honour, and the sūrah of the Prophets describes him with Enoch and Dhū al-Kifl as steadfast.

His inclusion in this verse means that the Arabian line is not absent from the prophetic catalogue. The audience could not claim that God had confined guidance to the descendants of Isaac, and they could not claim that their own ancestor had been passed over. The sūrah places Ishmael in the same list as the Israelite prophets and gives the whole group the same description.

**Lot and the Cities**

Lot's story is the Book's account of a community destroyed for conduct that the Book describes in detail, and his name in this list places him among the messengers who warned their own people. The sūrah of Hūd records the angels' arrival at his house and their instruction that he should travel with his family in the dark of night and not look back, with the exception of his wife, and it records their announcement that the appointed time of the cities was the morning (11:81).

The choice of Lot for this catalogue is significant because his community's destruction is one of the Book's starkest examples of a people who refused a messenger. His presence in a list of the guided means the sūrah is not presenting the prophets as men whose missions succeeded by human measures, and it prepares the reader for the statement in the following verses about those who were guided and those who turned away.

**Jonah and the Prayer From the Dark**

Jonah is the prophet whose story the Book tells in the sūrah of the Prophets, where he leaves his city in anger, is swallowed, and calls out from the veils of darkness that there is no god but God and that he has certainly done wrong (21:87). The Book records that his prayer was answered and that he was rescued from anguish, and it adds the sentence that this is how the believers are saved (21:88).

His name in this verse brings the theme of mercy into the catalogue. The Book's description of the prophets includes men who were rescued from their own errors as well as men who led their peoples to safety, and the sūrah's audience, which had been told that the punishment would reach the wrongdoers, is shown that the door remains open to the one who calls from the dark.

**The Whole List and Its Purpose**

The three verses of names have now covered the fathers of the tradition from Noah to Jesus, and the sūrah has inserted no ranking among them. The purpose of assembling them becomes explicit in the verses that follow, where the sūrah states that this guidance is God's and is given to whoever He wills of His servants, and where it warns that association with God would have voided the deeds of any of them.

The catalogue therefore serves two arguments at once. It answers the audience's claim to a special line by showing how many lines there were, and it answers their confidence in ancestry by stating that the deeds even of these men would have been wasted had they associated others with God. The next verse widens the circle once more by including the forefathers, descendants, and brothers of the men named, and the one after that states the principle behind all of it.
