## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:154

> You are only a human being like us, so bring forth a sign if what you say is true.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Humanity Becomes Their Objection**

Thamûd expands its accusation by saying that Ṣāliḥ is a human being like them. The phrase sounds reasonable until its implication is examined. They are not merely observing that the messenger shares human needs. They are using shared humanity to deny that God can choose a human envoy.

The Qur'an presents the objection as a recurring one. Quran 25:7 records critics objecting to a messenger's ordinary eating and movement among people, while Quran 18:110 affirms human likeness together with revelation and divine oneness. The pattern shows that humanity is not the contradiction Thamûd imagines. The contradiction lies in expecting guidance while refusing the form God gives it.


If Ṣāliḥ were an angel, Thamûd could claim that obedience was impossible for ordinary people. A human messenger removes that excuse. He lives within the same limits of bodily life, yet he demonstrates what it means to submit to God. His humanity makes the path concrete rather than remote.

The people do not need to imitate an otherworldly creature. They can hear a person who knows hunger, work, family, fear, and social pressure. The messenger's example tells them that taqwa is not reserved for a different kind of being. It is a real obligation for people like themselves.

**The Demand for a Sign Is Conditional**

They ask for a sign if Ṣāliḥ is truthful. A request for evidence can be sincere, but this wording is placed after their accusation and rejection. It sounds less like an open investigation than a challenge designed to control the terms of belief. They will decide what counts as proof before considering the message.

A sign cannot replace willingness to obey. Someone determined to resist may demand another sign after receiving one, not because evidence is absent but because submission is unwelcome. Ṣāliḥ's earlier call already has moral clarity; the requested sign will test whether the people recognize God's authority or treat a miracle as a spectacle they command.


A prophet does not manufacture signs to prove personal strength. They are acts of God that support the truth of the messenger's call. This distinction prevents the messenger from becoming a magician who controls extraordinary events for applause. Ṣāliḥ's role is to convey and explain, while God decides what sign will appear.

Quran 7:73 presents the she-camel as a clear proof from the Lord and calls it a sign for Thamûd. That parallel answers the demand in the present verse. The sign is not a performance by Ṣāliḥ; it is a divine test placed before the people.

**The Demand Reveals a Narrow View of Truth**

Thamûd treats truth as something that must be forced into the form its pride can recognize. It has already heard the call to worship God and has seen Ṣāliḥ's character, but it wants a display on its own terms. The people may be less interested in knowing the truth than in making the messenger pass an exam they designed.

This attitude appears whenever a person accepts only evidence that leaves his habits untouched. He may ask for certainty while refusing the consequences of certainty. Ṣāliḥ's opponents show that intellectual language can conceal a moral demand: prove yourself in a way that does not require us to change.


Thamûd separates Ṣāliḥ's humanity from God's power, as if the two cannot meet. Revelation joins them correctly. A human messenger conveys the guidance, and a divine sign confirms that the message is not his invention. The sign does not make Ṣāliḥ divine; it makes his received mission visible.

That arrangement protects both sides of faith. The community hears a language it understands, and it sees that the authority behind the message exceeds the messenger's personal capacity. Humility should lead the people to receive the sign as a mercy rather than demand it as a trophy.

**Proof Does Not Remove Responsibility**

Even after a sign appears, Thamûd will remain responsible for what it does. Evidence is not coercion that eliminates moral choice. It clarifies the path and exposes the motives of those who refuse it. The people cannot later say that no public indication was given.

This is why a miracle can increase accountability. It is a gift, but it also removes an excuse. The one who sees a sign must ask what obedience it requires, not only whether it is impressive. Ṣāliḥ's opponents ask for a sign; the real question is whether they will submit when God answers.


Ṣāliḥ is like them in bodily need, but he is not like them in the trust he carries. A ruler and a subject may share humanity while holding different responsibilities. The people's mistake is to treat shared nature as proof that no one can receive revelation.

Human equality before God does not mean that every person has the same role. It means that every person remains dependent and accountable. Ṣāliḥ's message honors the people by calling them to the same Lord, while his mission gives him the duty to warn them.


A fair request for evidence is willing to follow where evidence leads. Thamûd's challenge will be judged by what it does after the sign arrives. If the people continue to treat revelation as an insult to their status, the request will be exposed as a delaying tactic.

For the reader, the verse asks whether demands for proof are joined to readiness to obey. God does not owe a person a spectacle tailored to preserve his pride. When He provides a clear sign through a human messenger, the honest response is to recognize the Lord's power and accept the responsibility that recognition brings.
