## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:200

> When you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺, or even more passionately. There are some who say, “Our Lord! Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world,” but they will have no share in the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**When the Rites Are Done**

The verse addresses the pilgrim at the pilgrimage's completion: when you have carried out your rites — your manāsik — remember God. The word *manāsik* was explained by the exegetes at two scopes: the sacrifices of the ʿīd al-aḍḥā, the Feast of Sacrifice that marks the hajj's end, or the rituals of the hajj collectively — the circuit, the standing, the surge, the stoning, the shave — whose essential purpose, al-Qurṭubī notes, is the remembrance of God itself. The timing of the command is its first lesson. The danger-moment of a completed rite is the vacuum after it: the weeks of consecrated routine end, the iḥrām is exited, the crowd disperses into ordinary life, and the heart that was full stands suddenly empty. The verse stations remembrance exactly there — not as one more rite added to the list but as the rite that survives the rites, the dhikr that continues when the stations are behind. The whole hajj, in this legislation, is a machine for producing remembrance that outlasts the machine: months consecrated, conduct fenced, stations capped by praise — all so that when the manāsik are finished, the remembering is just beginning.

**As You Remembered Your Fathers**

The verse's measure for the new remembrance is the old Arabian boast. Al-Qurṭubī records the custom it targets: at the conclusion of the hajj the Arabs engaged in the magnification and celebration of their ancestors' past glories — the genealogical contests after the rites, when the tribes gathered and each recited the deeds of its fathers, often in a spirit of open rivalry, poet against poet, lineage against lineage. The verse does not condemn that ardor; it conscripts it. Remember God *as you remembered your fathers* — with the same heat, the same fluency, the same pride of enumeration — *or with a remembrance yet more ardent*. The comparative is the verse's genius: the passion of jāhilī tribal glory, one of the strongest social forces in pre-Islamic Arabia, is not suppressed but transferred, and the transfer is required to lose nothing of its intensity. The fathers' glories were remembered loudly; God is to be remembered louder. The rite that the Arabs had capped with ancestry is capped instead with divinity, and the pilgrim returns home carrying the boast redirected — the same tongue's power, the same gathering's eloquence, pointed at the One who made the tongues and gathered the tribes.

**The Child's Reading**

The exegetes preserved a second reading of the comparison, inward rather than historical: remember God as a small child remembers and calls upon its father and mother — in helplessness, in utter dependence, in total trust. The reading transforms the verse's psychology. The first sense transfers the adult's tribal pride into divine praise; this one recovers the child's primal recourse — the call that assumes answer, the dependence that does not argue, the trust that has no alternative to its object. Both readings live in the verse's wording, and they compose rather than conflict: the remembrance commanded is at once ardent as the boast and helpless as the infant's cry, eloquent and dependent, the pilgrim's full-grown passion spent in the posture of a child at its parent's knee. The second reading also deepens the verse's placement after the rites: the manāsik completed, the pilgrim is not a graduate but a small person again — the hajj strips its performer of rank, lineage, property, and clothing alike, until what remains at the completion is exactly the child's equipment: need, trust, and a voice. Remember God, the verse says, with what the rites left you with.

**The Prayer for This World Alone**

The verse's second half turns from remembrance to petition, and begins the duʿāʾ-diptych that the next two verses complete: among people are those who say, *Our Lord, give to us in this world* — and who have, in the Hereafter, no share. The prayer is quoted in its poverty: one clause, one horizon, one world. The askers are not described as praying falsely or for forbidden things; their petition is simply truncated — the whole of their wanting ends at the world's edge, and the verse reports the truncation as the diagnosis. What a person asks for reveals what a person is: the duʿāʾ as the register of the heart's actual eschatology. And the verdict follows the diagnosis with arithmetic precision: whoever asks only this world receives its terms — no share in the Hereafter, since no share was ever requested there. The sūrah of Consultation states the same economy from the side of desire: whoever desires the harvest of this world is given some of it, but has no share in the Hereafter (42:20). The verse thus completes the hajj's re-education of the tongue: the boast of the fathers transferred to God, and now the petition of the heart audited — the pilgrim who has just spent weeks performing rites for the Hereafter is caught, at the rites' end, praying like a man with no Hereafter.

**The Two Horizons of Asking**

The truncated prayer is set against the whole prayer the next verse will quote, and the contrast between them is the passage's moral anatomy. One group says: give to us in this world. The other says: give us good in this world *and* good in the Hereafter, and guard us from the Fire. The difference is not between worldliness and asceticism — the second prayer asks for this world too, and asks without embarrassment — but between a single-horizon soul and a double-horizon one. The verse's verdict on the first group is not that they asked wrongly but that they asked *only*: the world requested alone is the world as finality, and the finality forfeits the share. Islam's balance is legislated in the two duʿāʾs together — neither the rejection of this world nor its absorption, but the asking that holds both in one sentence with the Fire's guard between them. The hajj-context sharpens the lesson: the pilgrim is the person who has just physically enacted the double horizon, leaving home and wealth for a rite whose entire yield is otherworldly, and the verse asks him to make his tongue consistent with his feet. The prayer for this world alone, spoken at the end of that journey, is the contradiction the verse exists to catch.

**Remembrance and Petition at the Rites' End**

Read as a whole, the verse legislates the pilgrim's speech at the completion: first the remembrance measured against the fathers' boast, then the petition measured against the single-world prayer. Both measurements are comparisons to Arabian habit — the genealogical vaunt and the this-worldly want — and both are corrected in the same direction: toward God and toward both worlds. The custom after the rites had been ancestry and appetite; the legislation makes it dhikr and duʿāʾ, and audits the duʿāʾ's horizon. The names the diptych will end on — swift in reckoning (2:202) — already loom over this verse's verdict: the share in the Hereafter is not a metaphor but an accounting, and the prayer that omits the Hereafter is filed accordingly. The pilgrim leaves Minā with two sentences installed in his tongue: the remembrance more ardent than the fathers', and the petition wider than the world — and the verses that follow will give him the second sentence's exact wording, its prophetic provenance, and the portion earned by those who pray it.
