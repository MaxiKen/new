## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:132

> Fear the One Who has provided you with ˹the good˺ things you know:

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning Turns Toward Provision**

Hūd now gives ʿĀd a reason to fear Allah: He is the One who has provided them with the good things they know. The people have been thinking about what they build and control, but Hūd directs them behind every visible possession to the Provider. Their prosperity did not originate in their own independence.

The phrase about the things they know points to familiar blessings rather than distant abstractions. They experience provision in their bodies, homes, animals, families, land, and social life. The evidence of God's care is already around them. They do not need to invent a sign before acknowledging the One who sustains them.


To know a benefit is not yet to be grateful for it. A person may recognize that food, water, health, or security is valuable while behaving as if he produced them without help. Hūd asks ʿĀd to move from awareness to reverence. The Provider deserves more than a passing acknowledgment; He deserves obedient gratitude.

Gratitude changes the use of a blessing. The grateful person asks whether his wealth can relieve harm, whether his strength can protect rather than oppress, and whether his knowledge can serve rather than deceive. The ungrateful person sees every gift as evidence that he may do whatever he wishes with it.

**The Provider Stands Behind the Means**

ʿĀd may have used agriculture, labor, animals, skill, and planning to obtain its prosperity. Hūd does not deny those means. He reminds the people that means themselves are provided. The capacity to work, the material to work with, and the conditions that make success possible are not self-created.

This perspective avoids two errors. It does not excuse idleness by telling people to wait for provision without effort, and it does not turn effort into an idol. Work is a responsibility, while provision remains a gift whose arrival and limits are governed by God. The servant acts diligently and remains humble about the result.


The word good does not mean that every desire is morally good simply because it is enjoyed. A provision becomes a test through the way it is acquired, distributed, and used. ʿĀd's wealth can feed families and strengthen society, but it can also support vanity and aggression. The gift reveals the character of its recipient.

A person may thank God for abundance while using it to humiliate others. That contradiction exposes a shallow gratitude. True thanks includes lawful conduct, restraint, generosity, and readiness to be corrected. Hūd connects fear with provision so that the people will not mistake abundance for exemption from judgment.

**Their Blessings Were Common Knowledge**

The next verses name cattle, children, gardens, and springs. The present verse prepares the people to look at those blessings as a coherent provision from one Lord. Hūd is not flattering ʿĀd with vague praise; he is identifying concrete realities they cannot reasonably deny.

The list also prevents the chiefs from claiming that only their elite experience God's favor. Animals, family, cultivation, and water support the whole community in different ways. The Provider's gifts are not proof that one class is inherently superior. They are trusts distributed through a society that must answer for how it shares them. Quran 16:5–6 likewise describes cattle through the benefits and beauty people receive from them, joining usefulness to a reminder of the Giver.


When blessings continue for a long time, they can appear normal and deserved. The recipient forgets that life could have been otherwise. Hūd's command interrupts that forgetfulness. The more familiar a benefit becomes, the more deliberately it may need to be remembered as mercy.

Entitlement is dangerous because it turns a gift into a claim against both God and people. The entitled person expects provision to continue while refusing the duties attached to it. Fear of Allah restores proportion: enjoy what is lawful, acknowledge its source, and do not use it as an excuse to disregard the Giver or harm His creation.

**Provision Does Not Confirm Every Choice**

ʿĀd could argue that its prosperity proves that God approves its way of life. Hūd's warning rejects that conclusion. A blessing can be granted as an opportunity, a test, or a temporary respite. Continued wealth is not a certificate that a society's worship and violence are right.

This point protects the reader from judging truth by immediate material success. Some people prosper while acting unjustly, and some righteous people endure hardship. The Provider gives in wisdom, while accountability concerns the response. The proper question is not merely, “What do we possess?” but, “What are we doing with what we possess?”


Hūd's reasoning is relational. Fear Allah because He has provided what you know. The command is not an arbitrary demand from a distant ruler. It is a call to respond rightly to the One whose care has sustained the people from the beginning.

If ʿĀd acknowledges God as Provider, it must abandon the fantasy of self-sufficiency. It must recognize that its buildings, attacks, and social order are answerable to the source of its life. Provision becomes a doorway to worship when the people let gratitude govern their power.

**A Blessing Remembered Becomes a Sign**

The good things around ʿĀd are signs not because they force belief mechanically, but because they invite reflection. They reveal dependence, order, and generosity. The person who looks with humility sees a reason to fear God; the person who looks with vanity sees only material for further self-display.

Hūd asks the people to choose the first way of seeing. Their cattle, children, gardens, and springs will soon be named, but the central lesson is already given: what they know and enjoy comes from the Lord. To remember the Provider is to let every blessing lead back to obedience.

The reminder also teaches the difference between possession and ownership. A person may hold a thing in trust and be responsible for it, but he cannot make himself its ultimate source. This humility leaves room for gratitude, generosity, and repentance when a blessing has been misused.
