## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:23

> looking at their Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward Named in Two Words**

The sūrah has described the blessed faces, and the reward it names is neither a garden nor a drink nor companionship. It is a gaze: *ilā rabbihā nāẓira*, looking toward their Lord. The construction is explicit — the verb of looking followed by the preposition *ilā*, toward — and it is this preposition that has carried the weight of fourteen centuries of argument. The placement is what should strike a reader first. The Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise elsewhere are full of sensory detail, and the sūrah of al-Insān, revealed around the same period, gives silk, couches, silver vessels and springs in abundance. Here all of that is passed over in order to say that the faces are looking at their Lord, which makes the vision, on any reading, the summit of what is being promised.

**The Majority Reading and Its Evidence**

The great majority of commentators understood this as the literal vision of God in the Hereafter, and they did so on the strength of reports that are among the most securely transmitted in the corpus. Jābir's narration is explicit: the Prophet came out to his companions on the night of the full moon and said, *“You will see your Lord on the Day of Resurrection as you see this ˹full moon˺ and you will have no difficulty in seeing Him.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7436). A longer version in the same collection adds the comparison to the sun and moon when no cloud obscures them and the instruction to guard the prayer before sunrise and before sunset, which ties the vision to the practice of worship (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 554; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 633). The wording of the verse and the wording of the reports converge, and the preposition is doing ordinary work in both.

**The Grammatical Dispute**

The minority reading turns on that preposition. *Naẓara* with *ilā* can mean looking at, but the verb also means to wait, to expect, and to consider, and a reader who takes it that way renders the clause as *waiting for their Lord*, understood as awaiting His reward. The strength of this reading is lexical: the verb does carry that sense elsewhere in Arabic. Its weakness is contextual. The clause stands in apposition to *nāḍira*, radiant, which describes a face in a state of flourishing, and a face waiting for something does not explain its radiance, whereas a face seeing its Lord does. A second weakness is that the waiting reading has to supply an object — waiting *for* the reward — that the verse does not mention.

**The Argument From 6:103**

The stronger objection is theological rather than lexical. *“No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision.”* (6:103) denies that sight comprehends God, and a reader who treats that verse as definitive must treat this one as figurative. Al-Zamakhsharī argued along these lines, and the discussion belongs to the larger question of how verses about God's attributes are to be handled. The majority answer distinguishes the predicates: the verb in 6:103 is *idrāk*, comprehending or overtaking, which is a claim about grasp, whereas the claim here is about sight. The hadith's own simile makes the same distinction, since the companions were not told they would understand the moon — only that seeing it involves no crowding and no doubt. Vision without encompassment is exactly what the reports describe. That reading is stronger, because it honours both texts without weakening either, and because a third passage settles where the vision is *not* available. When Moses asked to see God, the answer was *“You cannot see Me! But look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, only then will you see Me.”* (7:143). The mountain was levelled and Moses fell unconscious. A text that denies the vision in this life and promises it in the next is making a distinction the two parties both need: the limitation belongs to the creature in his present state, not to the object seen.

**Why the Vision Is the Highest Reward**

The spiritual commentators drew out why this should be the summit. The reward for obedience is Paradise; the reward for the realisation of God's oneness is the sight of Him, as one early authority put it, and the same writers connect the sight to love: whoever has been slain by love of God is repaid with the vision of Him. These statements are reports preserved in the tafsīr literature rather than hadith, and they are cited as such. What they capture is genuine to the Qur'an's own logic. Everything else promised to the blessed is a thing given; the vision is a presence, and the difference between the two is the difference between a gift and the Giver. A text that locates the highest reward in nearness rather than in possessions has told the reader what the whole religion is for.

**Correspondence Between the Life and the Sight**

The reward also fits the life that prepared for it, and the fit is the Qur'an's usual way of describing outcomes. Those who acted for the sake of God's countenance rather than for the thanks or repayment of people (76:9) are given the countenance itself. The sūrah opened by swearing by the self-reproaching soul, the faculty that keeps a person's account in front of him; it closes this passage with the people who kept that account honestly being brought into the presence of the One who was keeping it all along. The faces are radiant because they are looking at what they had been looking for, and the looking is described as continuing rather than as a single glance. Two conditions are named elsewhere and both fit. The first is awe: *“And whoever is in awe of standing before their Lord will have two Gardens.”* (55:46). The second is soundness of the organ itself: *“Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺.”* (26:89). A heart that has been trained to look toward its Lord in absence is the heart that will look at Him in presence.
