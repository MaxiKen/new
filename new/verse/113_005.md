## Sūrah al-Falaq 113:5

> and from the evil of an envier when they envy.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Evil Named Is Inside a Human Heart**

The chapter's list ends where it began to point all along: not in the dark, not at a distance, but in a person who knows you. The envier is usually a neighbour, a relative, a colleague, a friend. Of the four protections requested in this sūrah, this is the only one whose source may be sitting in the same room.

The order of the list is instructive. It moves outward to inward — from all created things, to the night that hides them, to a craft worked in secret, to a feeling no one can see. Each step narrows the distance between the reciter and the harm. The last item is the closest, and it is the one he is least able to guard against, because the guard would have to be inside the other person.

That is why the chapter closes here rather than on sorcery. A person can avoid a dark road and can refuse the services of a knot-blower. He cannot decline the envy of someone who is pleased to see him and unhappy at his good fortune. The final refuge is asked for the harm that has no practical defense.

**"When They Envy" Fixes the Moment of Danger**

The phrase *idhā ḥasada* — when he envies — attaches a time to the evil. The refuge is not from the envier as a person but from him in the act of envying, and the commentators took that restriction seriously.

Two readings follow from it. On the first, the qualifier distinguishes disposition from operation: many people feel the sting of another's advantage without doing anything about it, and the verse asks protection from the moment when the feeling becomes active, when it looks, speaks, or plots. On the second, the qualifier identifies the condition under which the evil works — envy is harmful when it is being exercised, in the same way that darkness is harmful when it has closed in and a knot is harmful while it is tied.

The first reading is the stronger, because it explains why the verse needed the clause at all. Without it, the prayer would be against a class of people, defined by a permanent trait, which would make every uncomfortable person an enemy. With it, the prayer is against an event: the moment at which someone's resentment of your good becomes an operation against you.

**Envy Is Not Admiration, and It Is Not Ambition**

Arabic separates three states that English often blurs. *Ḥasad* is the wish that another's blessing be removed — the pain is at his having it, and the desire is that he stop. *Ghibṭa* is the wish for the like of what he has, without his losing it; the tradition treats this as legitimate and even praiseworthy. Between them lies ordinary ambition, which is directed at a thing rather than at a person.

The Qur'an legislates the difference directly: *“And do not crave what Allah has given some of you over others. Men will be rewarded according to their deeds and women ˹equally˺ according to theirs. Rather, ask Allah for His bounties.”* (4:32). The instruction is structural: the craving is redirected from the other person's possession to a request addressed to God. Wanting what another has becomes wanting from the One who gives.

The same word appears in the description of those who oppose the revelation: *“Or do they envy the people for Allah's bounties? Indeed, We have given the descendants of Abraham the Book and wisdom, along with great authority.”* (4:54). Envy there is not a private feeling but a public position — a refusal to accept that a blessing has been given where it was given.

**The First Two Sins in the Qur'an Were Envy**

The tradition reads the chapter's last verse as an appeal against the oldest evil on record. The first sin in the heavens was Iblīs's refusal, and the Qur'an gives his reason in his own words: *“I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”* (7:12), after he alone refused to prostrate (7:11). The comparison that produced the refusal is the structure of envy — another's advantage experienced as one's own diminishment.

The first sin on earth was the killing of Abel. *“So Cain threatened, ‘I will kill you!’ His brother replied, ‘Allah only accepts ˹the offering˺ of the sincerely devout.’”* (5:27), and the accepted offering was Abel's. The verse records the outcome: *“Yet Cain convinced himself to kill his brother, so he killed him—becoming a loser.”* (5:30). The motive the commentators give is the same one: his brother's acceptance.

Placing envy last in a list of harms is therefore not a descent but an ascent. Darkness, sorcery, and envy are ordered by antiquity, and the final item is the one that has been operating since before human beings existed. Those who envy a favor given to another are, on this account, opposing the giving itself.

**The Envier Is Wronged Before He Wrongs Anyone**

ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, the Umayyad caliph who ruled from 717 to 720, is reported to have said that he could think of no wrongdoer more likely to be wronged than one who envies another. The remark is a summary of the mechanism rather than a moral platitude.

The envier suffers the blessing twice. Once in the possession of the person he envies, where it is a fact he cannot change; and once in himself, as a loss he cannot stop feeling, since its cause is not his own condition but someone else's. Nothing he does improves his position: gaining the same thing does not remove the other's, and the other's having it is precisely what hurts.

That is why the verse asks refuge from the envier rather than advice about him. There is no strategy available against a harm whose source is another person's pain at your good. The only response recorded in the Qur'an is patience and pardon — *“Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision.”* (2:109) — addressed to people being envied, with the decision left to God.

**The Evil Eye Is Treated as Real**

The tradition has read this verse against the evil eye from the earliest period, and the reading rests on a Prophetic statement rather than on inference. Abū Hurayrah reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: "The effect of an evil eye is a fact" — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5740, in the Book of Medicine, in a chapter headed by that claim.

The report explains the continuous use of these two chapters. From the first centuries to the present, *al-Muʿawwidhatān* have been recited for protection against the evil eye and against spells, over children, over the sick, at night, and on journeys. The practice is not a folk addition to the religion but the application of a verse whose last item is a look accompanied by resentment.

The connection also clarifies the previous verse. Sorcery worked through a cord and an incantation; the eye works through a glance. Both are invisible operations against a person, both are named in the same chapter, and both are answered by the same request. What the sūrah treats as one problem is the harm that arrives without a weapon.

**Envy Inside the Community, Not Only Outside It**

The verse is often read as protection against outsiders, and the Qur'an itself supplies the correction. The envy described in al-Baqarah belongs to people who had received a scripture: *“Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them.”* (2:109).

The same sūrah locates the origin of religious division inside a religious community: *“Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them.”* (3:19). Division followed knowledge, and its cause was not ignorance but resentment of where the blessing had landed.

So the envier in this verse may hold the same creed as the person who recites it. That possibility is what makes the petition general rather than polemical: it asks for protection from a state of heart that no community is free of, and it does so in a chapter recited nightly by people whose neighbours are mostly not their enemies.
