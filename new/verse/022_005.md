## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:5

> O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed—in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you. ˹Then˺ We settle whatever ˹embryo˺ We will in the womb for an appointed term, then bring you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime. Some of you ˹may˺ die ˹young˺, while others are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much. And you see the earth lifeless, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell, producing every type of pleasant plant.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Doubt Is Answered, Not Rebuked**

The verse opens by granting the doubt a name: *in kuntum fī raybin mina al-baʿthi* — if you are in doubt about the Resurrection. It does not call the doubter a liar or a rebel. It offers him an argument and asks him to follow it.

This is the Qur'an's standard procedure with the doctrine that its audience found hardest. Elsewhere the objection is quoted and answered with a comparison; here it is answered with something the objector already accepts — his own origin. The strategy is worth naming, because it is not the strategy of demanding assent. A person who doubts the second creation is asked to inspect the first one, which he has no grounds to doubt since it happened to him.

**Four Stages, and the Sequence Is the Argument**

The stages are given in order: dust, then a sperm-drop, then a clinging clot, then a lump of flesh. The parallel passage in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn lays out the same sequence with the bones made explicit: *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation. So Blessed is Allah, the Best of Creators”* (23:14).

Why the sequence matters more than the stages: each step is a transformation of the material preceding it, and none of them is explicable by the material alone. Dust does not arrange itself into a drop; a drop does not become a clot by chemistry the drop possesses. The Qur'an's point is not that the stages are unusual — every human being passes through them — but that they are ordinary and yet unaccountable. A God who brings a person through four such transitions has no difficulty with a fifth.

That the first stage is dust rather than nothing is also part of the argument. The Qur'an frequently pairs the two: *“He created him from dust, then said to him, ‘Be!’ And he was!”* (3:59). Creation from a substance is presented as the harder and stranger case, since something is being shaped; the Resurrection is described in this sūrah as the revival of what has already been shaped once.

**"Formed or Unformed" — the Lump That Decides**

*Mukhallaqatin wa-ghayri mukhallaqatin* — formed and unformed. The phrase describes the lump of flesh, and it has been read in two ways that are really one. Grammatically it qualifies the lump: the flesh at that stage may go on to be fully shaped, or it may not, and the difference is not visible at the time.

The theological weight sits in the clause that follows, about what God settles in the wombs for a stated term. Miscarriage is here placed inside the same decree as birth. The verse does not treat the unformed pregnancy as a failure of the creative process but as one of the two appointed outcomes of it, both decided by the One who decides. Commentators noted that this is why the phrase is attached to the lump specifically — the stage at which the outcome is still hidden from everyone but its Author.

**Two Endings for One Life: Death, or the Feeble Stage**

From birth the verse moves to maturity and then splits the road. Some die; others are returned to "the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much."

The second ending is the one the Qur'an dwells on, and it is a harder thing to contemplate than death. Sūrah al-Naḥl states it with the same words: *“And some of you are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much. Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, Most Capable”* (16:70). Sūrah Yā Sīn names the mechanism: *“And whoever We grant a long life, We reverse them in development. Will they not then understand?”* (36:68).

The choice of this as the second proof of the Resurrection is deliberate. A person who has watched a mind undo itself has watched a Creator take back what He gave, in the same body, over the same lifetime. If knowledge can be withdrawn from a living brain, it can be restored to a decayed one. The arc of weakness to strength and back again is drawn explicitly elsewhere: *“It is Allah Who created you in a state of weakness, then developed ˹your˺ weakness into strength, then developed ˹your˺ strength into weakness and old age”* (30:54).

**From the Womb to the Field**

The argument then leaves the body and takes up the ground: you see the earth lifeless, and when We send down water it stirs, swells, and puts out every kind of pleasant growth.

This is the Qur'an's single most repeated analogy for the Resurrection, and the repetition is not decorative. Sūrah al-Rūm states the conclusion in the same breath as the observation: *“See then the impact of Allah’s mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead. For He is Most Capable of everything”* (30:50). Sūrah al-Aʿrāf draws the line explicitly: *“Similarly, We will bring the dead to life, so perhaps you will be mindful”* (7:57).

The analogy works because both halves are the same kind of event: something dry, dead, and dispersed receives what it cannot produce for itself and becomes a living, ordered thing. The earth does not rain on itself. Neither does a grave.

**"For an Appointed Term": the Report Behind the Phrase**

On the appointed term in the womb, a ḥadīth of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd is central to the commentary tradition. The Prophet ﷺ said that each person is gathered in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop, becomes a clot for a like period, then a lump of flesh for a like period, after which an angel is sent to write four things — his provision, his lifespan, his deeds, and whether he will be wretched or blessed — and then the soul is breathed into him. It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (3208) and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2643).

The report and the verse illuminate each other. Where the verse says only that God settles what He wills for a stated term, the ḥadīth fills in the term's texture — a fixed span, known and written, inside which a life is shaped. Nothing in the report suggests the span is arbitrary; it is measured, and the One who measures it is the One who will end it.
