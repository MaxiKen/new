## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:76

> When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But in private they say ˹to each other˺, “Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you, so that they may use it against you before your Lord? Do you not understand?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Rooms Reappear**

The sūrah used this scene once before, when it described men who professed faith in public and withdrew it among their own people (2:14). Here the same structure is applied to a group from a community with a scripture, and the Book reports both halves of their day in one sentence, so that the reader hears the confession and the retraction in the space of a line.

What has changed between the two passages is the content of the private conversation. The earlier group mocked the faith it had just professed; this group worries about information and its consequences. The subject of the discussion is knowledge that has come to them, and the fear being expressed by the speakers is that it will be used as evidence against them.

**The Knowledge Being Withheld**

Commentators understood the phrase about what God has revealed in several ways. One reading takes it as instructions and judgments that had been given to this community and that the believers could now hold them to. Another takes it as knowledge they had obtained about the truth of the new revelation, and a third as the prophecies they possessed concerning the coming of a prophet.

The last reading matches the verses that follow, where the sūrah describes a people who had announced the coming of a prophet and then rejected him. Under all three readings the thing being guarded is the same: an advantage they believed the believers would use against them. The Book records their own words, and their words show that they knew the matter was real.

**"Before Your Lord"**

The phrase the speakers use locates the confrontation they fear. They are not worried about losing an argument in the market; they are worried about a dispute that will be settled before God, and the Arabic allows both the sense of a reckoning at the resurrection and the sense of an appeal to what is recorded in the Book.

Either way, the sentence exposes a position that the sūrah has been building toward. A person who fears that the truth will be used against him has already conceded that it is true, and the Book's portrait of this group rests on exactly that concession. The verse states the concession in their own voice and then asks a question that turns it back on the speakers.

**The Rebuke Inside Their Own Question**

The speakers address their question to one another: do you not understand? They are warning their colleagues about the danger of candour, and in doing so they identify themselves as people who have grasped the situation. The Book repeats the question at the end of the verse and leaves it in their mouths, so that the reader receives an admission rather than an accusation.

The sūrah has used the same closing question for its earlier portraits of the hypocrites, where three consecutive verses ended with a statement that the people described could not see what they were doing (2:9, 2:12, 2:13). Here the rebuke suits the case exactly. The speakers are protecting a position they know to be indefensible, and their own question shows that they have understood the calculation and chosen the wrong side of it.

**Concealment Treated as Strategy**

The conversation in this verse is a discussion of concealment as policy, and the Book elsewhere names the covenant that governs such knowledge: the people who had been given the Scripture were bound to make it known and not to hide it (3:187). The group in this verse is not described as ignorant of that obligation, and the passage that follows describes others with the same community writing a book and attributing it to God.

The Book's treatment of concealment is consistent throughout: it treats what has been revealed as a trust held for other people rather than as a possession. A community that manages its knowledge to preserve its standing has converted a message into an asset, and the verse reports the conversion in the words of the people doing it. Their strategy fails by its own estimate, since the next verse states what they cannot hide from.

**A Portrait Placed Between Two Warnings**

The verse sits between the question about the believers' expectations in the previous sentence and a direct statement about God's knowledge in the next. The arrangement is deliberate. The community is told not to expect good faith from everyone who holds a book, shown what the bad faith looks like from the inside, and then reminded that the calculation being made in private is being made in the presence of someone who hears both sides of it.

What the reader carries from the passage is not a verdict on a people but a description of a method. Concealment designed to keep an advantage over believers is described as an activity that its practitioners believe is invisible, and the sūrah's immediate correction is the sentence that follows.

**The Cost of Managing Knowledge**

The private advice in this verse has a long life outside the community it first described. A person who possesses information that raises his standing faces a permanent temptation to release only the parts that serve him. The Book's response is not to demand indiscriminate disclosure but to name the habit for what it is: a calculation in which the possession of truth has become a form of property. The speakers in the verse weigh what their knowledge can buy them and decide that concealment is the better investment. What they cannot measure is the damage to themselves, since a person who manages the truth to preserve an advantage eventually loses the ability to state it plainly. That loss is what the next verse addresses when it declares that God is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal.
