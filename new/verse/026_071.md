## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:71

> They replied, “We worship idols, to which we are fully devoted.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Community Names Its Objects Without Defending Them**

Abraham's people answer plainly that they worship idols. Their first response does not offer a proof that the idols created the world or control human destiny. It identifies the practice and then describes their devotion to it. The answer is important because it makes the issue visible. They are not merely respecting symbols or preserving art. They are directing worship toward created forms.

The word devoted suggests remaining fixed around an object and giving it sustained attention. Their idolatry is not an occasional act that can be separated from daily life. It organizes a community's habits, ceremonies, loyalties, and imagination. The idols have become points around which people gather, even though the people have not yet shown that the idols can hear or act.

**Devotion Can Be Deep Without Being True**

The people's commitment is real. They are not pretending to care about the idols. That sincerity, however, does not make the objects worthy of worship. A person can love a false object with great intensity and still be deceived about what the object is. The depth of feeling must be tested by the truth of the thing receiving it.

This distinction matters in religious reform. Abraham does not need to deny that his people are devoted. He needs to ask whether devotion has been placed in the right direction. Emotional strength can make a practice harder to question, but it cannot supply the object with powers it does not possess. The idols remain dependent creations even when a community gathers around them with passion.

**An Idol Is a Social Center Before It Is a Stone**

The word idols points to visible forms, but the practice includes the social order built around them. Shrines, rituals, caretakers, festivals, and family expectations can all support the object's authority. People may fear that abandoning the idol will mean abandoning their ancestors, neighbors, and place in society. Devotion is therefore reinforced by belonging.

Abraham's question reaches that whole structure. He does not ask only whether a statue is attractive. He asks what the community is doing when it worships. An object can become powerful in social life because people agree to treat it as powerful. The agreement does not create divine ability. It creates pressure to conform.

**The Claim Reveals Habit Rather Than Evidence**

Their response tells Abraham what they do, but it does not tell him why the idols deserve worship. The absence of a reason is already revealing. When a practice is inherited and surrounded by ceremony, people may assume that the practice explains itself. Abraham's method refuses that assumption. A long-standing ritual still needs a true object and a sound basis.

This is not a demand for a philosophical treatise before every act of worship. It is a demand that worship not be aimed at something plainly unable to receive it. The people have named their devotion; they must now face questions about the idols' capacities. Abraham will move from their statement to tests that ordinary experience can understand.

**Devotion to Idols Competes With Devotion to God**

The phrase fully devoted describes the community's attachment, but the chapter's wider argument places that attachment beside the Lord of all worlds. Worship is not an empty space that can be divided without consequence. When created things receive the acts and hopes due to God, they become rivals in the heart even if the worshipper continues to speak about a supreme deity.

This is why Abraham will not accept a compromise in which idols are honored as secondary helpers. Their role must be examined, because calling them intermediaries can preserve the same dependence. A person who bows before a created object, fears its displeasure, or seeks its favor has already assigned it a place that belongs to the Creator alone.

**The Community's Answer Contains Its Own Opening**

By saying that they worship idols, the people have given Abraham a clear subject. They have not yet claimed that the idols hear, heal, create, or judge. Their answer leaves a gap between devotion and ability. A wise questioner can enter that gap without first attacking every custom surrounding the objects.

The opening is narrow because devotion can become defensive once its foundations are challenged. Abraham must ask about capacities that the people themselves can assess. If the idols cannot answer, help, or harm, then the community will have to explain why it remains devoted. The next questions will press that issue while keeping the discussion tied to observable reality.

**A Related Example Shows How Devotion Can Persist**

The Qur'an later describes the Israelites saying that they will remain devoted to a calf until Moses returns (20:91). That parallel shows how quickly a community can attach intense loyalty to a created form, even after receiving signs of God's care. Devotion can persist through waiting, uncertainty, and social pressure because it gives the heart a visible object around which to gather.

Abraham's people stand at the same danger. Their devotion is not proof that the idols deserve it. It is the very reason the practice must be examined. The question is whether the heart's steadfastness is anchored in truth or merely in the strength of habit.

Steadfastness is a virtue only when the object is worthy. Persistence can preserve truth, but it can also preserve error when no one is willing to ask what the devotion rests upon. The community's strong attachment therefore makes Abraham's questions more necessary, not less. A loved practice should be tested honestly rather than protected from examination. Truth does not become less true because a family has cherished an error. The heart must value guidance above the comfort of belonging to a familiar crowd.
