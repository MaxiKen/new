## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:136

> The pagans set aside for Allah a share of the crops and cattle He created, saying, “This ˹portion˺ is for Allah,” so they claim, “and this ˹one˺ for our associate-gods.” Yet the portion of their associate-gods is not shared with Allah while Allah’s portion is shared with their associate-gods. What unfair judgment!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shares Assigned to God and the Idols**

The pagans set aside for Allah a share of the crops and cattle He created, saying, This portion is for Allah, so they claim, and this one for our associate-gods. The verse opens the sūrah's detailed treatment of the audience's ritual economy, and it describes a practice in which the produce of the land and the herds was divided between the Creator and the beings associated with Him. The Book records the same custom elsewhere, saying that the idolaters assign to the idols, who know nothing, a share of what God has provided for them (16:56).

The phrase so they claim marks the practice as an invention, and the Book's commentators noted that the arrangement had no basis in any revealed command. What the audience had done was to take a custom inherited from their ancestors and to place it beside the worship of God as though the two belonged to the same system.

**The Asymmetry in the Practice**

Yet the portion of their associate-gods is not shared with Allah while Allah's portion is shared with their associate-gods. The sentence describes how the arrangement worked when the portions were disturbed, and the commentators explained the two halves: if part of what had been set aside for the idols fell in with the portion belonging to God, the people returned it to the idols' share; but if part of God's portion fell in with the idols', they left it where it landed, saying that God was self-sufficient and had no need of it.

The reasoning they offered revealed the actual ordering of their loyalties. A practice that treated one party's property as recoverable and the other's as forfeit was not a division of equals, and the Book states the arrangement in order to show which of the two the audience actually served.

**The Judgement Passed on the Practice**

What unfair judgment! The sentence closes the verse with the Book's assessment, and the term describes a ruling that is wrong in itself rather than merely inaccurate. The Book's word for judgement has been used throughout the sūrah in the discussion of the arbiter and the perfection of God's word, and the verse in front of us applies it to the audience's arrangement of their own offerings.

The Book's method in these verses is to let the practice testify against the people who defended it. The shares were assigned in God's name, and the way they were administered showed what the assigners believed about the two parties. The sentence the Book pronounces is a conclusion drawn from the arrangements the audience had made themselves.

**The Offence in the Arrangement**

The offence that the sūrah is describing is not only the association of other beings with God but the redirection of provision that God had given. The Book states that the crops and cattle were created by God, which establishes the claim of the Creator to what His creation produces, and the sūrah of the Bee records the charge that the idolaters assign shares to beings that know nothing of the gift.

The Book's treatment of offerings elsewhere makes the same point about the direction in which gratitude should run. The sūrah of the Cattle will later say that the audience forbade what God had provided, attributing the prohibition to Him without warrant (6:140), and the verse in front of us shows the same system operating in the opposite direction, with gifts directed to the idols instead of to their maker.

**The Place of the Verse in the Section**

The verse begins a passage of several verses that examines the audience's rules about crops, cattle, and the offspring of the herds (6:136–139). Each verse takes up a particular practice, states it in the audience's own words, and passes judgement on it. The arrangement is the sūrah's method of showing the reader what the religion of the Makkans looked like when it was examined piece by piece.

The passage also continues the argument the sūrah has been conducting about authority. The verses about guidance and error established that the path belongs to God and that its signs are clear; the verses about the dietary rules established that permission and prohibition belong to God; and the passage that opens with this verse shows the audience transferring that authority to beings of their own invention.

**What the Verse Says About Custom**

The verse shows a community carrying out a practice under the name of God while its structure contradicted what they claimed for it. The Book's term for their own description of the arrangement, "so they claim", separates the custom from the revelation, and the reader is left with a practice that had no sanction other than inherited usage.

That separation is the sūrah's constant concern in its dealings with the audience's religion. The chapter has argued against their theology, their demand for signs, their dietary rules, and their associations with the jinn, and it now shows how the same habits of invention shaped the offerings they brought to their gods.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse describes the effect of the evil associates on the audience, saying that they made the killing of their own children appealing to them and that this led to their destruction and to confusion in their faith, before instructing the Prophet to leave them and their falsehood (6:137). The two verses belong together, since both describe the influence of the associated beings on the community's conduct.

The sūrah thus moves from the division of harvests to the destruction of children, and the passage as a whole shows the consequences of the religion the audience had inherited. What the verses state is the practical result of the associations the sūrah has been examining since its account of the jinn (6:100).
