## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:20

> So if they argue with you ˹O Prophet˺, say, “I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers.” And ask those who were given the Scripture and the illiterate ˹people˺, “Have you submitted yourselves ˹to Allah˺?” If they submit, they will be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants.

**Expanded Commentary**

**When the Argument Ends, a Position Is Stated**

The previous verse described a dispute that had nothing to do with missing information. This verse answers the situation: the Prophet is told what to say when the arguing begins, and what he is told to say is not an argument at all. He states his own position and asks his questioners to state theirs.

The move is more powerful than another round of proofs. Debates in which each side holds a partial claim can continue indefinitely; a plain declaration of where a person stands ends the exchange by transferring the matter to the audience. The Prophet is instructed to say what he has done with his own life and to ask the others what they have done with theirs, which converts a contest of interpretations into a question every listener must answer privately.

**"I Have Submitted Myself" — a Verb, Not a Label**

The Arabic is *aslamtu*, from the same root as *islām* in the verse before it: I have handed myself over. The statement is an act, described in the first person singular, and it is followed immediately by a claim about a community: *and so have my followers*.

That second clause is a remarkable thing to say at the beginning of a mission. The Prophet speaks for a body of people who have entered the same submission, and his own claim is put on the same footing as theirs. The verse also quietly locates the standard of *islām* where the sūrah has already placed it: not in genealogy, not in the possession of books, but in the handing over of the self. A messenger who declares that he and his followers have submitted is inviting his questioners to compare their own standing with that, not with an ancestry.

**The Two Groups Who Are Asked**

The question goes to two audiences: *those who were given the Scripture* and *the illiterate*. The second term, *al-ummīyūn*, describes people without a revealed book — those who had no scripture to argue from, and among whom the Prophet himself appeared, which is why the Book calls him an unlettered prophet (7:158).

Pairing the two groups is the sūrah's way of removing a distinction that had organised the religious world. People with ancient scriptures and people without them are asked exactly the same question, and the same consequence is attached to the same answer. Neither possessing a book nor lacking one substitutes for the submission the question demands, and no community can claim that the question was addressed to someone else.

**"If They Submit, They Are Guided"**

The answer given is not a reward for joining a movement. The verse says that submission *is* guidance, and the equation is deliberate: the thing that saves a person is the thing the verse names, not the community he joins. A reader who takes the sentence seriously is being told that the boundary runs through individuals rather than around nations.

The mercy in this construction deserves notice too. The Book does not say that whoever submits becomes a member of a favoured party; it says his path has been set right. Guidance in this vocabulary is a direction rather than a status, and a person who has taken the direction holds it as a direction — something he must continue to travel, not a certificate he once acquired.

**"Your Duty Is Only to Deliver the Message"**

The verse closes the invitation by removing a burden the Prophet might have carried: the outcome is not his responsibility. If the questioners turn away, his task is finished rather than failed, and the Book says as much elsewhere with unusual directness: *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:21–22).

The same limit is stated about guidance generally: *“You are not responsible for people's guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills.”* (2:272). The instruction shapes how the message is delivered in practice. A messenger who believes he must produce the result will be tempted to coerce, to exaggerate, or to trade away parts of the message; a messenger who knows his duty ends at delivery is free to be honest. The Book's model of preaching rests on that division of labour.

**The Attribute That Sees Servants**

The verse ends with the same closing attribute that ended verse 15: *Allah is All-Seeing of His servants*. Its placement is a final reminder about where the delivery is judged, and by whom. The messenger is not asked to grade his audience, and the audience is not asked to grade the messenger.

Both are servants of the one who sees them, and the sūrah has already told its readers how accurately He sees. The people who refuse and the people who submit are not merely counted; they are seen, with their reasons and their private arguments included. Nothing in the closing clause invites a community to assess its own membership. It invites each person to consider what is visible to the one who will assess him.

There is also a practical lesson about how a believer handles disagreement. The Prophet is given a formula that avoids both silence and escalation: state the position, ask the question, leave the outcome. Applied to the arguments in the following verses, this means the invitation stays open even while the record of what the other side has done is being described without flinching. The messenger's duty ends at delivery, and the Book repeats the point because the temptation to go past it is constant — in a person, in a preacher, and in a community with power.
