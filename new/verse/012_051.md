## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:51

> The King asked ˹the women˺, “What did you get when you tried to seduce Joseph?” They replied, “Allah forbid! We know nothing indecent about him.” Then the Chief Minister’s wife admitted, “Now the truth has come to light. It was I who tried to seduce him, and he is surely truthful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The King Asks the Question Joseph Specified**

The enquiry happens exactly as the prisoner requested. The king summons the women and puts the matter to them, using a form of words that asks for an account: what was your affair, what did you arrive at, when you sought to seduce Joseph. The verse does not describe the setting, the number of women present or the procedure, but it records that the state conducted the investigation rather than leaving it to the household. That transfer is the substance of Joseph's demand. The case had been settled inside a private residence by a husband with an interest in silence (12:29). It is now reopened before a ruler with no such interest, and the sūrah treats the change of forum as the change that matters.

**The Question Itself Presumes the Attempt**

The king's wording is worth examining. He asks what they got *when they tried to seduce Joseph*, using the same verb the narrator used in verse 23 and Joseph used in verse 26. The attempt is taken for granted in the question; what is asked is its outcome. That framing is either the result of information reaching the king from the cupbearer's errand, or a deliberate device that leaves the women no room to deny the premise. Either way it disposes of the version of events the household had maintained for years. Nobody in the room can now claim that nothing happened, and the only available answers concern who did what — which is the question the torn shirt already answered in private and that the banquet answered in public.

**The Women's Answer Clears Him in One Sentence**

*Ḥāsha lillāh, God forbid; we know nothing indecent about him.* The exclamation is the same one they used at the banquet (12:31), and its function is the same: to register that the suggestion is unthinkable. What follows is a statement about knowledge rather than opinion — we know no evil of him — and it is spoken collectively by the women of the city's establishment. Their testimony is not that Joseph was attractive or that they understood the ʿAzīz's wife. It is that they possess no information damaging to him. Since the accusation against him originated in this same social circle and circulated among these same households, their joint denial removes its entire social basis. The verse therefore does more than acquit him; it withdraws the report.

**"Now the Truth Has Come to Light"**

The wife of the Chief Minister speaks after the others, and her opening words are an idiom of arrival: *al-āna ḥaṣḥaṣa al-ḥaqq*, now the truth has become fixed, settled, established. The verb carries the sense of something that has come to rest after movement, and its use here marks the end of a long displacement. The truth about this affair had been available since verse 28, when a husband examined a shirt and announced the result to two people in a room. What verse 51 supplies is the same truth in public, before the king, in the mouth of the person who displaced it. Her confession is complete: she names herself as the one who sought the seduction, and she names Joseph as truthful. Nothing is qualified, nothing is shared with him, and no mitigating circumstance is offered.

**A Love That Has Changed Its Nature**

The commentators track a progression through her three appearances in the chapter. In verse 25 she protects herself by prosecuting him, which is desire preferring its own survival. In verse 32 she admits the attempt in front of forty women, which is desire seeking exoneration from scorn. Here she admits it before the king, with no advantage to be gained and every disadvantage to be borne, which the tradition reads as the point where her feeling ceases to be self-interested. Al-Maybudī's account is that her love, having begun as appetite, has by now become a recognition of his inward quality — the truthfulness she names in the same breath. Whether or not that reading is pressed, the structural fact stands: her last recorded statement about Joseph costs her everything and gains her nothing.

**The Testimony Is Given in His Absence**

Joseph is not in the room. He is still in the prison, waiting for the result of the enquiry he demanded. The absence is what gives the testimony its weight, and the following verse turns on it explicitly. A person praised in his presence may be flattered; a person cleared behind his back is cleared. The chapter has already shown the opposite case — the accusation made against him in verse 25 was also delivered to his face, in a room where he was allowed no reply until addressed. The symmetry is exact: a false account given publicly, a true one given publicly, both about a man who was not consulted about either. The sūrah is describing how reputations are made and unmade in the absence of the person they concern.

**What Verse 50 Asked For Has Been Delivered**

Joseph's demand was that the king ask about the case of the women who cut their hands. The king asked, and the answer is recorded here in full. No punishment follows for anyone; the verse contains no sentence, no reproof from the king and no consequence for the ʿAzīz's wife beyond the confession itself. What has changed is the record. A man who was imprisoned after his innocence had been demonstrated now has that innocence stated before the highest authority in Egypt by the person who caused his imprisonment and by the social circle that repeated the charge. The chapter treats that change as sufficient, and the next verses treat it as the precondition of everything that follows: he is summoned, interviewed, trusted and given the granaries.
