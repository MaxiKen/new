## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:79

> It is not appropriate for someone who Allah has blessed with the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to say to people, “Worship me instead of Allah.” Rather, he would say, “Be devoted to the worship of your Lord ˹alone˺”—in accordance with what these prophets read in the Scripture and what they taught.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Limit Set on the Gifts of God**

The verse is a statement about what is not appropriate for a human being who has been given scripture, wisdom, and prophethood. The list ascends, and the point of the sentence is that the highest accumulation of divine gifts still does not license the invitation to be worshipped.

The Book's method here is to argue from the nature of the office rather than from the character of any individual. A prophet is constituted by the gifts named: he has been given a book, understanding of it, and a mission. Every one of those is a relationship of dependence, and the verse's claim is that the man who has received them cannot turn them into a claim on the worship of others without denying what they are. The grammar of the sentence is impersonal — it is not appropriate for any human being — which makes it a rule rather than an accusation.

**The Three Gifts Named**

The Book mentions scripture, wisdom, and prophethood here in a single list, and it uses the same combination in other places when it speaks of what God bestows on His servants. Scripture is the revealed text; wisdom is the capacity to use it rightly; prophethood is the commission to deliver it.

Each of the three is a trust, and the verse's argument is that a trust cannot be converted into a title. The community that received this verse had a live example in front of it, since the dispute about Jesus concerned precisely the question of what follows from a prophet's extraordinary gifts, and the sūrah had already answered that question with the comparison to Adam. What the sentence adds is the general form of the argument, stated in the abstract so that it applies wherever the gifts appear.

**The Command the Prophet Would Give Instead**

The second half of the verse supplies the alternative. A person in that position would say: be devoted to the worship of your Lord alone, in accordance with what these prophets read in the scripture and taught.

The phrase about what the prophets read and taught is worth attention. The command being issued is not presented as a new religious instruction; it is what the recipients of the earlier books already had in front of them. The verse's picture of a prophet is therefore of a teacher who directs people back to what has been given, which is the pattern the sūrah has drawn throughout its narrative of the messengers. Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus all come with a message that is recognisable in its content, and the instruction to worship God alone appears in each of their traditions.

**Devotion Instead of Deification**

The Arabic word rendered "be devoted" belongs to the vocabulary of religious instruction and describes a person who gives himself to the service of his Lord. It is the term the Book also uses for those who devote themselves to the study and teaching of religion, and its appearance here in the mouth of a prophet is exact.

The contrast the verse draws is between two invitations: worship me, and serve your Lord. One treats the prophet as the destination and the other treats him as the pointer, and the sūrah's argument is that a man who knows what he has been given would never confuse the two. The alternative in the sentence is not modesty as a personal quality; it is the accurate description of the relation between a messenger and the Lord who sent him, and the verse presents it as the only thing a prophet in that position could say.

**Why the Verse Is in This Passage**

The passage has been describing a community that attributed statements to God which He had not made, and the earlier verses reported their views about obligations and about the credibility of outsiders. This verse turns to the question that lies underneath those manoeuvres, which is the question of who is entitled to be worshipped.

The connection between the two subjects is closer than it looks. A community that has appointed itself the guardian of who may be recognised also tends toward a position in which its teachers occupy the place its religion reserves for God, and the Book names that tendency in other passages when it describes people who took their rabbis and monks as lords apart from God. The verse's refutation is not about procedures; it restores the relation between a servant and his Lord, and it does so by quoting what a prophet would say if he were standing in front of his people.

**The Historical Setting and Its Comfort**

The commentaries connect the verse to questions put to the Prophet about whether he wished to be treated as an object of veneration, and to the wider controversy about how Jesus was regarded among the Christians. In both readings the verse is a categorical denial of any claim on worship by a human being, however exalted.

The sūrah's readers were in a position to profit from the statement in a different way. A community formed around a prophet will always face the question of how far veneration may go, and the answer given here is built into the message itself. The Prophet is presented as a teacher who directs his people to the exclusive worship of God, in accordance with what the previous prophets read and taught, and the verse makes that direction part of the definition of his office.
