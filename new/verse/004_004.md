## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:4

> Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Gift That Belongs to the Woman Herself**

The command *ātū al-nisāʾ ṣaduqātihinna niḥlah* — give women their dowries graciously — establishes a property right that cuts against the customs around it. The word *ṣaduqah* is not a price for the woman; its root *ṣ-d-q* signals truthfulness and sincerity, as though the payment proves the seriousness of the commitment. *Niḥlah* deepens that sense: a free gift, given warmly and without grudging, not a bargain extracted under pressure. Earlier practice allowed a guardian or family to intercept the bridal gift, or to trade women in ways that diverted wealth away from the bride. This verse closes those paths by making the woman herself the owner. The Qur’an elsewhere confirms that wealth a woman receives through dowry, inheritance, or earnings remains hers to manage, as reflected in the very next laws where women are named as heirs alongside men (4:7) and where their property is not to be handed to those unable to manage (4:5) without proper terms. Al-Ṭabarī reads *niḥlah* as a gift with no return expected, which distinguishes it from a loan or a conditional transfer. Once given, it belongs to her in the full legal sense.

**Why the Language of Joy Matters**

The second half of the verse permits a husband to benefit from part of the dowry only if the wife offers it — *fa in ṭibna lakum ʿan shayʾ minhu nafsan* — if they waive some of it willingly, with a contented soul. The condition *ṭibna ... nafsan* is deliberate. It is not enough that she says yes while under pressure; her soul must be at ease. Classical commentators stressed that the gift must be without coercion, shame, or manipulation, because a yes extracted by persistence is not a good will. The Qur’an then adds *fa kulūhu hanīʾan marīʾan* — then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience. The pair *hanīʾ* and *marīʾ* was understood as food that is pleasant going down and wholesome afterward, with no aftertaste of guilt. That food image was chosen for a reason. Wealth taken wrongly is elsewhere described as fire in the belly (4:10); wealth given gladly is described as wholesome enjoyment. The same act — a husband receiving from his wife’s property — changes its moral nature entirely depending on whether her heart consented. The wording trains the reader to ask not only did she give, but how did she feel while giving.

**From Guardian Control to Personal Ownership**

Read after verse 3, this verse completes a movement from stewardship to ownership. Verse 2 ordered guardians to return orphan property; verse 3 told them not to marry orphan girls to discount their dowries; this verse names the dowry as the woman’s personal entitlement and makes its transfer to the husband dependent on her uncoerced will. That sequence dismantles a system where a guardian could control both wealth and marriage. The Qur’an also addresses practices where widows were inherited like property or where a father kept his daughter’s dowry. By naming women directly — *al-nisāʾ* — as recipients, the verse bypasses intermediaries. It does not say give it to their families or to those who arrange their marriages; it says give it to the women. The sūrah will later reinforce that a husband who has given a great amount and then wishes to replace one wife with another may not take back any of it, *“even if you have given the former a stack of gold”* (4:20), and it asks *“how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately and she has taken from you a firm commitment?”* (4:21). Those later verses show that the dowry is not a deposit to be reclaimed when the relationship ends; it is a settled gift that the woman retains.

**What Coercion Looks Like in Practice**

The condition of willingness invites careful attention to real life. Consent is not only a signature; it is a state of heart at the moment of giving. Pressure can be open — threats, anger, withdrawal — or quiet — repeated hints, appeals to loyalty, or making the wife feel that refusal equals disloyalty. The verse’s wording excludes all such pressure, because *ṭīb al-nafs* cannot coexist with fear or embarrassment. Jurists drew from this a practical rule: if it becomes clear that the wife gave while feeling straitened, the husband should return it, even if she voiced consent. The principle extends beyond dowry to any gift between spouses. The Qur’an elsewhere describes marital wealth in terms of mutual consent generally, as in *“not in fornication”* but through lawful marriage sought *“with your wealth”* (4:24), and the same consent standard governs trade in *“do not devour one another’s wealth illegally, but trade by mutual consent”* (4:29). The dowry rule is thus one instance of a larger ethic where lawful enjoyment depends on the other party’s genuine willingness, not merely on a formal yes.

**A Free Gift, Not a Fee**

The root of *ṣaduqah* deserves attention because it corrects a common misunderstanding. The dowry is not a purchase price, and the size of the gift does not measure the worth of the woman. The Qur’an elsewhere calls the marital bond *“a firm commitment”* (4:21), *mīthāq ghalīẓ*, language used for covenants of gravity. A gift that seals such a covenant is naturally significant, but its meaning comes from sincerity, not from amount. The Prophet ﷺ accepted dowries that were substantial and ones that were modest, including teaching verses of the Qur’an where wealth was lacking, which shows that what matters is the genuine transfer of something valued. Verse 4 does not fix an amount; it fixes an owner. That shift from amount to owner explains why the verse immediately allows the wife to give back a portion if she wishes. Once ownership is secure, generosity can flow either way without confusion about entitlement. What the law prevents is confusion in the other direction — treating her entitlement as his asset to collect.

**The Wholesome Enjoyment That Follows**

The permission to enjoy what is freely given teaches that property in marriage is not a zone of constant suspicion. The sūrah will later set careful limits for those unable to manage wealth (4:5) and testing for orphans (4:6), but here it speaks about the ordinary case where a wife, content and unpressured, chooses to share. In that case the Qur’an does not require witnesses or paperwork; it says *eat it as pleasant and wholesome*, language that signals moral ease. Two ideas meet in that ease: her autonomy is preserved, and his use is then blessed. The pairing matters for the sūrah’s overall vision of family wealth, where men and women each have shares (4:7, 4:11–12), each has obligations, and kindness is commanded alongside calculation. A household where the dowry was honored freely becomes a place where later sharing is also more likely to be free. The verse therefore does more than settle a single transfer; it shapes a habit. It trains the husband to receive only what is given with an untroubled heart on the other side, and it trains the community to see a woman’s wealth as hers to keep, to keep whole, or to give — as she herself decides.
