## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:4

> Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience.

### Expanded Commentary

**The bridewealth belongs to the woman**

The command to give women their dowries establishes the bridewealth as the wife’s own entitlement. It is not a payment to her father, clan, guardian, or husband’s relatives, and it is not a fee that purchases a woman. The Arabic imperative presents it as a gift offered in good faith, while the legal effect is an enforceable financial right arising from the marriage contract. In a society where women’s property could be appropriated by male relatives, that clarification was foundational.

The word commonly translated as dowry, *ṣaduqāt*, is related to truthfulness. A marriage gift thus signifies the seriousness of the covenant and the sincerity expected of the husband; it cannot be replaced by a symbolic gesture while the real wealth is secretly retained by someone else. The amount may be modest or substantial, but the right is not modest merely because the amount is.

**Consent distinguishes gift from extraction**

The second part permits the husband to accept some of the dowry only when the wife gives it willingly. The condition is more demanding than a spoken “yes” obtained under pressure. A wife must possess the legal capacity to dispose of her property, and the surrounding circumstances must not make refusal dangerous or humiliating. A demand followed by emotional threats, family pressure, or fear of divorce is not the gracious remission described by the verse.

This principle also protects against a common reversal: treating the wife’s generosity as if it were the husband’s entitlement. Once the property has been delivered, she may keep it, invest it, donate it, or return part of it. Her husband may benefit only through a genuine act of her choice.

**A material right with spiritual meaning**

The verse does not reduce marriage to economics. Rather, it shows that affection and law reinforce one another. A contract is more likely to be humane when the vulnerable party enters it with recognized property and an acknowledged voice. The dowry gives practical shape to respect; it also exposes the false piety of a man who speaks tenderly while withholding what the law has assigned to his wife.

**Guidance for contemporary contracts**

The rule applies to delayed and immediate dowries alike. A delayed amount remains a debt, not a ceremonial promise that disappears when the marriage becomes difficult. Families may help negotiate an amount, but they cannot take ownership of it. Communities that record the obligation clearly, distinguish it from wedding expenses, and ensure that the bride understands her rights are acting in the spirit of the verse. The final phrase—permitting the husband to enjoy what she freely remits—preserves generosity while placing consent, not custom or coercion, at its centre.

**The wife’s financial independence**

Families may celebrate a marriage by contributing to its expenses, but custom cannot silently redirect the bride’s dowry. The woman should know what is due, whether it is immediate or deferred, and how she may use it. A husband who accepts a voluntary gift should be able to show that it was truly voluntary. This is how gratitude remains generosity instead of becoming a second claim upon her property.
