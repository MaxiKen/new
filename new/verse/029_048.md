## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48

> You ˹O Prophet˺ could not read any writing ˹even˺ before this ˹revelation˺, nor could you write at all. Otherwise, the people of falsehood would have been suspicious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Argument Built From a Life Story**

The verse asks the reader to consider a biography rather than a doctrine. Before the revelation, the man who brought it had never read a book to anyone and had written nothing himself, and the community he lived in had watched him for decades. In a city where literacy was a mark of standing and where the Jewish and Christian communities possessed written scriptures, an illiterate man among them produced a recitation of unmatched quality, and the Book's argument is that this is the fact requiring explanation. The claim is not that the Prophet was a miracle apart from the revelation; it is that his known circumstances make the alternative explanations fail. The sūrah here follows the method it has used throughout: state the evidence, and let the audience draw the conclusion it can see.

**The Unlettered Prophet**

The Book elsewhere calls him the unlettered prophet, describing him as the figure foretold in the Torah and the Gospel, who commands what is right, forbids what is wrong, permits the good and prohibits the foul, and lifts the burdens his followers had carried (7:157). The term is significant in the argument of this verse because it is a public fact rather than a private claim. His tribe knew his occupation and his habits; his companions had grown up around him; his opponents were the first to catalogue his history when they tried to find a more comfortable explanation for the recitation. A claim about literacy is exactly the kind of claim that an audience in one city is qualified to check, and the Book makes it in front of the audience best placed to contradict it.

The description also closes an avenue the audience would otherwise have used. Had the Prophet been a reader of earlier books, the accusation of copying could have been pressed with some plausibility, so the verse builds its argument on forty years spent among them without producing or reading a text.

**The Suspicion That Never Formed**

The verse states what the accusation would have been if the facts had been different. Had the Prophet been a reader of earlier books or a writer of documents, the charge would have been that he had assembled his material from the scriptures of the Jews and the Christians, and the Book records that charge being made anyway. Sūrah al-Furqān reports the allegation that these are ancient fables which he has had written down and which are rehearsed to him morning and evening (25:5). The answer supplied by this verse is the state of the man's own hands and eyes. The accusation presupposes a technical capacity he did not have, and in a market town where gossip travels faster than caravans, a detail of that kind could not have been concealed from people who had every motive to expose it.

**Why Literacy Matters in This Particular Case**

The argument from illiteracy is not a claim that revelation must come through unlettered men, and it is not an objection to study. It addresses a specific dispute in a specific setting. The people pressing the Prophet for a sign in the following verses were the same people who had access to written traditions and who were unwilling to accept a recitation delivered by word of mouth by a man of their own city. The Book answers them on their own ground by removing the possibility that the Prophet had assembled the material from documents, and the removal is accomplished not by an assertion of his integrity but by a fact about his education. His character is attested elsewhere; here the argument is structural.

The argument from his illiteracy also keeps the credit where the Book places it. A community that watched a man for decades could measure the change in him, and the verse asks them to draw their conclusion from the distance between the neighbour they knew and the recitation they were hearing.

**The Book the Prophet Could Not Have Written**

The closing phrase of the verse prepares the reader for what follows, where the Book is described as clear revelations preserved in the hearts of those who have been given knowledge (29:49). The contrast runs through both verses: the revelation is not carried in a document the Prophet composed, but in the memory of a community, and its transmission does not depend on the literacy of one man. The Book states elsewhere that it has been made easy to remember (54:17) and that it is a reminder and a clear recitation rather than poetry (36:69). What the Prophet could not write, a whole community learned to recite, and the two facts are connected in the sūrah's argument about where the Book came from.

**What the Verse Cannot Be Turned Into**

A reader should be careful not to inflate the claim. The verse does not say the Prophet was incapable of learning or that ignorance is a virtue, and the tradition that he later dictated letters and treaties to scribes does not conflict with it, since the verse is concerned with the period before the revelation and with the act of writing itself. What it establishes is narrow and sufficient: the Prophet did not obtain the Book from the written word, and his audience knew it. That is the whole of the argument, and it is made in a sentence that any Meccan merchant could have verified by walking to the man's house. The next verses take up the accusations that his opponents made in spite of the fact.

The sentence also sets a limit on what may be inferred from it. Nothing here suggests that learning is suspect or that a scholar stands at a disadvantage; the Book praises knowledge elsewhere and commands its readers to think, and this verse states a fact about one man's history rather than a rule about books.

**The Teacher They Invented Instead**

The Book records the accusation that replaced the older one. When the charge of authorship was removed by the Prophet's circumstances, his opponents produced a different explanation: the Book says that they claimed nobody was teaching him except a human being, and the answer given is that the man they point to speaks a foreign tongue while the recitation is in eloquent Arabic (16:103). The reply does not rest on the Prophet's integrity or on his companions' denials; it rests on a difference between two languages that anyone in the audience could hear. This is the same method the verse under discussion uses. In both cases the Book takes the accusation seriously enough to answer it with a fact the accusers could verify, and in both cases the fact concerns something the community had already observed for decades.

The invention also explains why the Book answers it with an observation rather than a denial. Anyone in the town could check whether the Prophet read, and the reply rests on what his neighbours had already seen, which keeps the argument at the level of public knowledge rather than private assurance.

**Witnesses Who Could Have Refuted Him**

The strength of this argument is that it was made to the one group on earth best equipped to disprove it. The Prophet's opponents in Makkah knew his childhood, his trade, his travels, and his habits; they had every reason to produce a single witness who had ever seen him read a sheet of writing or copy a line from a scroll. The Book makes its claim in front of them, and the historical record preserves no rebuttal, only the accusation that the material came from old stories recited to him morning and evening. That shift from the specific charge to the vague one is itself informative, and the verse's quiet statement of fact is designed to leave the reader with exactly that comparison.
