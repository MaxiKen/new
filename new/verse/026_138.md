## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:138

> And we will never be punished.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Denial Reaches Its Extreme**

ʿĀd's appeal to the past ends with a confident denial of punishment. They do not merely say that they have no evidence of consequence. They say that punishment will never come. The word never reveals a certainty that exceeds their knowledge and turns a hope of escape into a creed.

Their earlier indifference now has a foundation. If no punishment exists, warning can be ignored, inherited custom can govern without review, and power can act without fear. Denial of accountability is not a small theoretical error; it changes the moral atmosphere of a whole society.


The claim can coexist with acknowledgment of a creator while denying that people will be held to account after death. Whether expressed through that distinction or through complete rejection, the practical result is similar: God's claim over conduct is emptied of consequence. Quran 46:26 makes the parallel point that hearing, sight, and understanding do not benefit a people who persist in denying God's signs.

A person may admit that life has a source and still resist the idea that life has a destination and judgment. Hūd's warning joins the two. The Provider who gives cattle, children, gardens, and springs is also the Lord before whom the recipients will answer for their use of those gifts.

**Denial Protects the Powerful From Moral Cost**

If there is no punishment, the strongest person can interpret success as permission. A blow against another becomes merely a victory, a seized resource becomes deserved, and a monument becomes proof that the builder is right. The denial is convenient because it removes the cost of choices that benefit the powerful.

This does not mean every person who doubts accountability is consciously defending oppression. But a society without a serious expectation of judgment is vulnerable to impunity. Its laws may restrain some harm, yet those who control the laws can begin to regard themselves as beyond the standard imposed on others.


Some wrongs are punished in this life, but many are hidden, delayed, or left apparently successful. If the present world were the entire measure, victims could conclude that injustice has no answer and oppressors could mistake delay for approval. The promise of a tremendous day completes the moral picture.

Hūd does not ask ʿĀd to believe in punishment because every consequence is immediately visible. He asks them to trust the Lord's truth about what lies beyond their limited observation. The absence of instant judgment is not proof that no judgment exists.


Cattle, children, gardens, and springs have filled the people's horizon with evidence of favor. They interpret those gifts as security from punishment. Hūd's message reverses that interpretation. Blessings are opportunities to obey, and their misuse can increase responsibility.

A prosperous society may assume that continued success means God has endorsed it. The verse exposes that assumption as dangerous. Time and abundance can be mercy before a person changes, not a guarantee that his conduct will never be judged. The people are warned precisely because their confidence has become unjustified.

**“Never” Converts a Claim Into a Barrier**

To say that punishment will never come closes the door before evidence can be considered. The people are not simply asking for clarification; they are announcing that no future event could correct them. Such language makes repentance appear unnecessary and turns the heart against every warning.

Humility leaves room for correction. It can say, “I do not yet understand,” or, “I need to examine this.” Arrogance says, “This cannot be true,” even when the speaker lacks authority to make that judgment. Hūd's people choose the second posture and make their certainty part of their guilt.


Belief in accountability should protect the rights of people whom worldly power can ignore. If ʿĀd is convinced that no punishment awaits, the suffering of those it attacks can be dismissed as an unfortunate part of dominance. Hūd's warning gives the victim a future before God even when earthly society refuses justice.

That future is not a license for private revenge. It is a reason for the oppressed to trust God's judgment and for the powerful to restrain themselves. The certainty of accountability should make every person more careful with authority, property, speech, and force.


Hūd has identified their Lord, recalled their provision, and expressed fear for their future. The people answer with denial. Their problem is not that no argument was offered. They refuse the framework in which an argument could matter: the framework of revelation, gratitude, and judgment.

A warning can be rejected in many forms, but this is the most complete. They do not merely continue an old practice; they deny the consequence that would make repentance urgent. Their confidence is now directed against the Lord's promise rather than only against Hūd's counsel.

**The Reader Should Not Borrow Their Certainty**

The verse asks the reader to notice the difference between honest doubt and defiant assurance. Not knowing every detail of the unseen is not the same as declaring that accountability is impossible. Faith requires humility before what God reveals, while arrogance turns limited human experience into a boundary for reality.

ʿĀd's statement therefore remains a warning against building a life on the assumption that nothing follows death. The proper response is not panic but preparation: use provision gratefully, correct inherited wrongs, and live as someone who will meet the Lord who knows every hidden deed.

Accountability also gives meaning to private choices that no public court observes. The promise of judgment assures the wronged that silence does not erase their claim and reminds the wrongdoer that concealment is not escape. A society shaped by this knowledge will seek justice even when enforcement is difficult.

ʿĀd's certainty says that punishment will never come; faith answers that no deed is lost. That answer should make the heart cautious, not self-righteous. The unseen belongs to God, and the servant prepares by acting well within what he can see.
