## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:30

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “A Day has ˹already˺ been appointed for you, which you can neither delay nor advance by a ˹single˺ moment.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Answer That Refuses the Question's Terms**

They asked when. The reply does not say when, and it does not evade either. *Lakum mīʿādu yawm* — you have an appointment of a Day. The information supplied is not the date but the status of the date: it exists, it is fixed, and it is yours. This is a precise response to a question that was not really asking for a calendar entry. The taunt assumed that an unspecified threat is an empty one; the answer separates specification from certainty and hands back the half that matters. A man told that his court hearing is scheduled, and that the clerk will not tell him the day, has not been reassured. The verse works because it concedes the one thing the questioners wanted — no date — while removing the conclusion they meant to draw from it.

**mīʿād: The Vocabulary of an Appointment**

The noun is *mīʿād*, from the same root as *waʿd*, the promise the opponents mentioned in their question. A *mīʿād* is an appointed time or place of meeting — the word used for a rendezvous, a due date, a tryst. Arabic builds it on the pattern of nouns of place and instrument, which gives it a concrete feel: not merely a promise but a fixed point at which two parties come together. The choice answers their wording directly. They said *this promise*, using a vague noun; the reply upgrades it to an appointment already entered in the register. And the possessive is pointed: *lakum*, for **you**, the same personal address the sūrah used when they claimed the Hour would not come *to us* (34:3). What they tried to push away as a general doctrine is returned to them as a personal engagement.

**Neither Delayed Nor Advanced**

The double negative covers both directions, and the second one is the surprise. That a fixed Day cannot be postponed is the obvious half; anyone expecting a threat expects that. But it also cannot be brought forward, which means the arrangement is not at the disposal of the impatient any more than of the fearful. The Qur'an uses this pair often, usually of the term granted to a community: *"When their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it"* (7:34), and again at (10:49), (15:5), (16:61) and (23:43). Nūḥ states it of the divine term generally: *"But when Allah's appointed time comes, it cannot be delayed, if only you knew!"* (71:4). Applying the formula here extends it from the fate of nations to the Day itself. The same rule that governs how long a civilisation stands governs when the whole account closes.

**Not by a Single Hour**

The unit named is *sāʿah*, translated here as *a single moment*. This is the same word the sūrah has been using throughout for the Hour itself — *al-sāʿah*, the Day of Judgment, which the opponents said would never come to them (34:3). Using it now as a measure of time produces a deliberate resonance: they cannot shift the Hour by an hour. The choice of unit is also rhetorically shrewd. A verse saying the Day cannot be moved by a century would leave room for the thought that smaller adjustments are available, and human beings negotiate in exactly that space. Naming the smallest common unit of the working day closes it. The sūrah has done this before, measuring divine knowledge by the smallest weight on a merchant's scale (34:3) and now measuring divine scheduling by the smallest division of a merchant's day.

**Why Fixity Is the Point Rather Than Secrecy**

The verse could have replied by stressing that the timing is God's secret, and the Qur'an does say that elsewhere. Instead it stresses that the timing is fixed and immovable, which is a different emphasis with a different practical consequence. Secrecy alone invites speculation and the hope that the thing may be indefinitely deferred. Fixity forecloses both. An appointment that is already set is not affected by the ignorance of the party who must attend; his not knowing the hour is a fact about him, not about the schedule. This is why the verse does not treat the withheld date as a limitation on the claim. The Day is described with three properties — appointed, unmovable, theirs — and none of the three requires the date to be disclosed in order to hold.

**The Door Left Open by the Silence**

There is a mercy in the arrangement that the verse does not state but that follows from it. A man who knew the date would organise his life around it, and the organisation would be arithmetic rather than character: postpone the correction until the last feasible moment, then repent on schedule. A man who knows only that the appointment exists has no way to game it, which means every choice he makes has to be made on its own merits. The unknown hour is therefore not a gap in the information but a condition of the test the sūrah described a few verses earlier, where the arrangement exists so that belief and doubt become visible (34:21).

**The Reply Is Handed Over as Speech, Not Doctrine**

Like the verses around it, this one opens with *qul*, and the instruction matters for how the sentence should be read. It is a line given to the Prophet for use against a taunt delivered in public, and its usefulness in that setting depends on its brevity. A long theological reply to a heckler loses the room; a single sentence that concedes nothing and returns the challenge does not. This is the fourth of the chapter's consecutive *say* commands, and the sequence has a discernible shape: the accounts are separated (34:25), the judge is named (34:26), the evidence is demanded (34:27), the mission is defined (34:28), and now the schedule is asserted. Each reply is short, each answers a specific move, and none of them describes the punishment the opponents were daring the Prophet to describe. Having answered the taunt, the chapter turns from what the deniers say now to what they will say to each other later.
