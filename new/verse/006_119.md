## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:119

> Why should you not eat of what is slaughtered in Allah’s Name when He has already explained to you what He has forbidden to you—except when compelled by necessity? Many ˹deviants˺ certainly mislead others by their whims out of ignorance. Surely your Lord knows the transgressors best.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put in the Verse**

Why should you not eat of what is slaughtered in Allah's Name when He has already explained to you what He has forbidden to you, except when compelled by necessity? The sentence answers an objection to the previous command, and the objection was about the food the community might eat. The verse's answer is that the rule has been stated by God, that the permission is clear, and that the exception has been made for the person who has no alternative.

The structure of the answer is worth noticing. The Book does not argue the case from the audience's customs or from the judgment of the community; it refers the question to what God has explained, which is the standard the sūrah has been applying to every subject it has treated in this chapter.

**The Explanation Already Given**

When He has already explained to you what He has forbidden. The sūrah refers to a body of rulings that the audience had received, and the commentators discussed whether the reference is to the detailed dietary laws given later in the sūrah of the Table or to the principle stated in this very passage. The Book's own statement in the sūrah of the Cattle is that God has explained what He has forbidden (6:119), and the sūrah goes on in later verses to list the prohibitions concerning dead animals, blood, and swine.

What matters for the argument is that the explanation exists and that the community has access to it. The Book's position throughout this section is that the rules come from God rather than from the customs of a region, and the verse states that the rulings have already been set out.

**The Exception for Necessity**

Except when compelled by necessity. The exception appears in the Book's dietary passages generally, and it is one of the features that distinguishes the rules from the arbitrary taboos of the audience's tradition. The sūrah of the Cow permits the person in danger of death to eat what is otherwise forbidden without incurring sin, and the sūrah of the Table and the sūrah of the Bee state the same exception in the same terms.

The Book's treatment of necessity shows the purpose the rules serve. They are not a burden designed to test endurance to the point of death, and the Book states elsewhere that God does not impose on a soul more than it can bear. The exception in this verse is stated as a fact of the law rather than as a concession wrung from it, and the audience's arguments about food are answered on that footing.

**The People Who Mislead by Their Desires**

Many deviants certainly mislead others by their whims out of ignorance. The sentence identifies the people who were pressing the objection, and it describes their teaching as the pursuit of desire rather than the weighing of evidence. The commentators understood the reference to include those who argued that carrion, which had died without human slaughter, was preferable to meat that a person had killed himself, and the argument was based on inherited assumptions rather than on the revelation.

The Book's description of the misleaders repeats the terms it has used throughout this section. The sūrah has described the audience as following assumption and lying (6:116), and the verse before us states the same condition in the vocabulary of desire. What changes from passage to passage in this sūrah is the subject under discussion, and the characterization of the people who argue against the revelation remains constant.

**The Closing Statement About Knowledge**

Surely your Lord knows the transgressors best. The verse closes with the same kind of statement that ended the previous passage about the majority, where the sūrah said that the Lord knows best who has strayed and who is guided (6:117). Here the object of the knowledge is the transgressors, and the sentence leaves their identification to God rather than to the community.

The repetition of the formula in consecutive verses serves the sūrah's argument by keeping the judgment where it belongs. The community is given its rules and told to keep them; the people who oppose them are described; and the final assessment of who has overstepped the limits is stated as a matter of God's knowledge.

**What the Passage Establishes About Law and Custom**

The two verses together state the Book's position on the relationship between revelation and inherited practice. Food that has been consecrated to God is permitted, what has been forbidden is forbidden, necessity is recognised, and the people who teach otherwise are following their desires. The sūrah has applied this reasoning to the idols, to the signs, and to the requirement of a judge, and it applies it here to what the community puts in its mouth.

That application matters for the way the chapter's later passages about livestock and harvests are to be read. The audience had built an elaborate system of shares and prohibitions, and the sūrah will describe it in detail, but the criterion for judging it has already been stated in these two verses: what God has explained and what people have invented.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse commands the community to shun open and secret sin and states that those who commit sin will be punished for what they earn (6:120). The command follows naturally from the dietary instruction, since the sūrah has just been describing conduct that rests on the desires of those who mislead, and the new sentence broadens the subject from food to the whole of a person's conduct.

The section that continues from here moves through the prohibition of what has not been consecrated to God, the whisperings of the devils and their associates, and the errors of the audience's dietary system, and it ends with directions about the path of God and the testimony of the Book. Each of these passages rests on the same foundation, which is the explanation God has given and the responsibility of the people who receive it.
