## Sūrah al-Naḥl 16:51

> **And Allah has said, “Do not take two gods. There is only One God. So be in awe of Me ˹alone˺.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Prohibition Of Taking Two Gods**

Wa-qāla Allāhu lā tattakhidhū ilāhayni ithnayn — and Allah has said, do not take two gods, two deities. Qāla Allāhu, Allah has said, direct speech from Allah, showing importance. Lā tattakhidhū, do not take, do not adopt, from a-kh-dh, to take. Ilāhayni ithnayn, two gods, dual, emphasizing prohibition of duality. Innama huwa ilāhun wāḥid — indeed He is only One God. Innama for exclusivity, only, He is One God. Fa-iyyāya fa-irhabūn — so be in awe of Me alone, fear Me alone, from r-h-b, to fear, be in awe. Al-Ṭabarī explains that this verse explicitly forbids taking two gods, meaning forbids shirk, forbids worshipping Allah and idols together. The dual ilāhayni ithnayn is emphatic: do not take two, meaning do not take more than One, whether two or many. The command lā tattakhidhū is general, for all people, all times. The reason innama huwa ilāhun wāḥid, He is only One God, gives basis: because He is One, you should not take two.

**Why Two Is Mentioned**

Why say two gods specifically, not many? Al-Zamakhsharī explains that two is minimal plural that constitutes shirk; if two is forbidden, then many is even more forbidden. Also, Arabs sometimes said Allah and idols as two kinds, so prohibition of two directly addresses their practice: Allah plus idols as two. Al-Rāzī notes that ithnayn, two, after ilāhayni is for emphasis and clarification, to remove any ambiguity that ilāhayn could mean two types of one god; it means two distinct gods. The verse therefore closes door to any duality: do not take two gods, whether you think second is equal or lesser, intercessor or partner. All duality is forbidden.

**So Be In Awe Of Me Alone**

Fa-iyyāya fa-irhabūn — so Me alone be in awe of, fear. Iyyāya is for emphasis: Me alone, only Me. Irhabūn from r-h-b means to fear with awe, to be in dread, to revere. Al-Ṭabarī says irhabūn means fear Me, be in awe of Me, not of idols, not of people. The command to fear Allah alone follows prohibition of two gods because fear is part of worship. If you take two gods, you will fear both, hope from both, which divides heart. If you take only One God, you fear only One, which unifies heart. The sūrah's earlier description of angels fearing Lord above them and doing what commanded models this: they fear One above, not two. The command fa-iyyāya fa-irhabūn is therefore call to exclusive awe.

**Connection To Universal Prostration**

Verses 48-50 described shadows prostrating, living creatures and angels bowing, fearing Lord above, doing what commanded. Verse 51 says Allah said do not take two gods, there is only One, so be in awe of Me alone. The connection is that universal prostration to One God shows that taking two gods contradicts what all creation does. All creation bows to One; why do you take two? The command to be in awe of Me alone is supported by universal submission: if everything bows to One, you should too. The verse also prepares for next: to Him belongs whatever in heavens and earth and to Him alone is everlasting devotion, will you fear other than Allah? If you fear Me alone, you will give everlasting devotion to Me alone.

**A Verse That Summarizes Tawḥīd**

This verse summarizes tawḥīd in three parts: prohibition, affirmation, consequence. Prohibition: do not take two gods. Affirmation: indeed He is One God. Consequence: so be in awe of Me alone. This mirrors lā ilāha illā Allāh: no god except Allah, so fear Him alone. Al-Qurṭubī says this verse is one of clearest statements of tawḥīd in Qur'an, comparable to 112:1-4. It is direct speech from Allah, not reported, to emphasize authority: Allah Himself says do not take two gods. The direct speech makes it more powerful than if messenger said it. The listener hears Allah speaking, commanding oneness and awe.

**Linguistic Note On Ilāhayn And Rahbah**

Ilāhayn dual of ilāh, god, from a-l-h, to worship. Ithnayn, two, from th-n-y, two. Innama huwa ilāhun wāḥid, He is only One God, with innama for exclusivity. Fa-iyyāya, so Me, with iyyā for exclusive object. Fa-irhabūn, so be in awe, from r-h-b, to fear, awe, with fa- for consequence. Rahbah is fear with reverence, stronger than khawf, includes awe. The verse's structure — Allah said do not take two gods, indeed He is One, so be in awe of Me — moves from divine speech to prohibition to affirmation to command, creating clear, memorable statement of tawḥīd that is both theological and practical: believe One, fear One.

**How This Verse Fits Sūrah's Argument**

Sūrah al-Naḥl argues from blessings to Creator to oneness to accountability. This verse is pivot from argument to command: after showing that all creation submits to One, that idols create nothing and are dead, that Allah knows what you conceal and reveal, now Allah commands directly: do not take two gods. The command is not new information but conclusion of previous argument. If you accept that Allah alone creates, sustains, knows, and that everything prostrates to Him, then you should not take two gods. The command to be in awe of Me alone also prepares for next verses about blessings being from Allah and crying to Him alone in hardship, showing that exclusive awe leads to exclusive reliance.

**A Lesson On Exclusive Devotion**

Exclusive awe of Allah alone means heart fears displeasing Him more than displeasing others, hopes for His mercy more than others' approval, and relies on Him alone. Al-Rāzī notes that irhabūn, be in awe, includes both fear of punishment and awe of greatness, which together produce obedience. When heart is in awe of One alone, it is freed from fear of many, which is true freedom. The verse therefore offers liberation: do not fear idols, tyrants, or people; fear Me alone, and you will be free from many fears. This is practical tawḥīd that transforms daily life: decisions made to please One, not many.
