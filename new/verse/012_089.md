## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:89

> He asked, “Do you remember what you did to Joseph and his brother in your ignorance?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Instead of an Answer**

The brothers have asked for grain and for charity. Joseph answers neither. The verse records a question about an event thirty or forty years old, in Canaan, involving a boy and a family that no Egyptian official has any reason to know about. The non-answer is the disclosure. Nothing else in the chapter could have made these men understand who was speaking to them, and the chapter chooses the least expected route: not a name, not a sign, not a removal of the barrier between them, but a sentence that only one person in the world could have constructed. The plea of verse 88 is left hanging, and the reader is given the reason in the following verse.

**"What You Did to Joseph and His Brother"**

The commentators specify the content of the question. It concerns the separation of Joseph from Benjamin when they were young, and the harm done to Joseph together with the injustices done to Benjamin — debasing him and reviling him after his brother was taken. The chapter's own narrative supports the first half: the brothers planned the disposal of one son (12:8–10), executed it (12:15), and reported it with a bloodied shirt (12:16–18). The treatment of the remaining child is not narrated anywhere in the sūrah, and the question supplies it. Joseph and Benjamin are named together as joint objects of the same conduct, which is a claim the brothers have never had to answer before. The pairing also revises the family's account of itself. For twenty years the brothers have lived with one crime and one victim; this sentence places a second victim beside the first and describes what followed as continuous rather than concluded. Benjamin's separation, their father's blindness and their own guilt are gathered into a single act with a single set of authors.

**"When You Were Ignorant"**

The qualifying phrase is the verse's most discussed feature. The commentators read it as Joseph making an excuse for his brothers: they did not know the vileness of what they were doing, or they were in the general state of ignorance that belongs to youth. The phrase is not an accusation softened by circumstance; it is an accusation that includes its own mitigation, offered by the person injured. Nothing in the chapter obliges Joseph to supply it. He could have asked the question bare, and the men in front of him would have had no answer to a bare version. Instead the verse contains a judgement and a concession in a single sentence, and the concession comes from the injured party rather than from the offenders.

**The Question Names What an Egyptian Could Not Know**

Ten brothers stand before the minister of Egypt's granaries. They have told him about a father, a brother and a household in Canaan. They have not told him about a well, a caravan, a sale or a shirt. The question refers to all of it, and refers to it as something the hearers did. The disclosure is therefore epistemic rather than physical: no curtain is drawn back, no identity is announced, and the recognition has to be produced by the content of a sentence. The chapter has prepared for this moment since verse 58, where one side knew and the other did not, and it resolves the asymmetry with words rather than with a reveal. The resolution makes the scene a recognition rather than a reveal. Joseph produces no evidence, summons no witness and recounts no history; he asks a question that only a brother could ask, and the answer identifies the men before him as surely as any face would. What has been concealed for thirteen verses is disclosed by a sentence that could come from nowhere else.

**How They Recognized Him**

The commentators record three accounts. One holds that the brothers realised who was speaking to them when he said these words. Another reports that recognition came when he smiled. A third states that he removed his crown and they saw him. The Qur'an gives none of these details; verse 90 reports only their question back. The variety of the traditions is worth noting because it shows the exegetes aware that the text does not describe the moment of recognition, and that a moment described only through a question needs some account of how the hearers understood it. The chapter's own answer is in the following verse, where their reply is a question rather than a statement. The question is the recognition. By asking whether he is indeed Joseph the brothers concede that the possibility has occurred to them, that the evidence before them points that way, and that they need confirmation before acting on it. The chapter treats that suspension between suspicion and certainty as the true moment of recognition, and it lasts exactly one verse.

**The Injury Is Named Before the Confession**

The order matters for everything that follows. Joseph states what was done before the brothers admit anything. Verse 91 has them acknowledge preference and sin; verse 97 has them ask their father to pray for their forgiveness. Neither admission comes first. The chapter is describing a reconciliation initiated entirely by the injured party: he names the injury, he supplies the mitigation, he discloses himself, and he forgives (12:92), all before any confession is offered. The structure excludes the possibility that the forgiveness is a response to repentance. It is prior to it, and it is what makes the repentance possible. The order makes the confession specific rather than general. A man who says we were wrong has said almost nothing; a man who has just heard the injury enumerated — you did this, in ignorance, to these two — can only respond by accepting the enumeration. Joseph's description is the form his brothers' admission will take, and the chapter supplies the form first.

**"His Brother" Speaks of Himself in the Third Person**

Joseph does not say *me*. He says Joseph and his brother, and the phrasing keeps him inside the sentence as an object rather than as the speaker. The effect is twofold. It preserves the concealment for the length of the question, since a first-person account would disclose him immediately. And it describes the crime accurately: what was done was done to two children, one of whom was taken and one of whom was left behind with the people who took him. The third person is not a device of coyness. It is the form in which the injury can be stated completely, and the chapter uses it once, at the exact moment when the statement is needed. Its use here is precise. The phrase completes the pairing established in the question, so injury and forgiveness are both addressed to two brothers rather than to one, and it does so without breaking the disclosure. Joseph names himself and names Benjamin in the same construction, and the construction tells his brothers that the man before them has been counting them both.

**The Verse Contains No Anger**

Nothing in the wording marks the speaker's feeling. There is no reproach, no reminder of the well, no reference to the shirt, the sale, the twenty dirhams or the years in prison. Joseph asks whether they know what they did, adds that they were ignorant when they did it, and stops. The chapter has shown him suppressing a far sharper response in verse 77, and it shows the suppression ending here — but what emerges is a question, not an accusation. A reader expecting the confrontation to carry the weight of thirty years will find the verse almost administrative in tone, and that is the point. The sūrah has been building toward this sentence for eighty-eight verses and delivers it without emphasis, because the emphasis has already been supplied by everything that came before it.
