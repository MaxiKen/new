## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:106

> If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection Being Answered**

The verse responds to a question that the community had raised about the revelation: if the Book is from God, why does one instruction give way to another? The sūrah does not avoid the question. It states the principle behind the practice and attaches to it a claim about God's capability, so that the reader understands the change as an exercise of authority rather than a correction of an error.

The Book's method here is to describe what is happening in its own terms before its opponents can frame it. The revelation announces that rulings may be replaced, and it supplies the guarantee that accompanies the replacement, so that the practice is a declared part of the message rather than a problem discovered in it.

**What the Word Behind Abrogation Means**

The Arabic root behind the term carries the sense of removing or copying out, and the classical explanations compare its action to the sun erasing a shadow, the wind smoothing over dust, or old age effacing the strength of youth. The word is used sparingly in the Book, and the four places where it appears are instructive: this verse, the inscription of the tablets given to Moses, the passage where God eliminates what Satan casts into a prophet's recitation, and the record of human deeds that speaks on the Day of Judgment.

Those uses share a single idea, which is the removal of one thing by another that takes its place. Applied to rulings, the term describes a legal instruction being superseded by a later one, with the original text remaining in the Book as recitation while the practice it commanded gives way.

**The Scope of the Concept**

The scholars of the community developed the concept carefully, and their discussions set limits on it. Abrogation in the classical understanding concerns commands and prohibitions, which is to say legal rulings, and it does not apply to statements about God, the Hereafter, history, or the moral law. A verse such as the one declaring that God has power over all things is not a ruling, and it cannot be abrogated.

The mainstream position also excludes the universal commands, such as the prohibition of murder and theft and the obligation of kindness to parents, from the operation of abrogation, because they were never subject to change. What the concept covers is the area of practice where the Book legislates for a community at a particular stage of its development, and the sūrah's later verses about fasting and prayer illustrate the change in the same chapter.

**Better or Similar**

The verse states that a replacement is either better than what it replaces or comparable to it, and the commentators discussed what superiority could mean where a divine instruction is concerned. One line of explanation takes it as relief in matters of this world and greater reward in the next, and the examples usually given are the easing of the night prayer and the establishment of the Ramadan fast.

Al-Ṭabarī noted that the comparison applies to the ruling rather than to the wording, since the wording of the Book does not admit degrees. He drew a parallel with a verse in this sūrah where an implied phrase has to be understood for the sentence to make sense, showing that the Book's argument sometimes requires the reader to supply a word. Under his reading the verse says that no ruling is replaced without a ruling better or comparable in its place.

**The Range of Opinion Among the Scholars**

The community's scholars differed widely about how many instances of abrogation the Book actually contains. Shāh Walī Allāh of Delhi counted five, al-Suyūṭī listed twenty-one, and Ibn al-Jawzī named a number in the hundreds, while a minority position, associated with Abū Muslim al-Iṣfahānī, argued that the apparently conflicting rulings can be reconciled without abrogation at all.

The disagreements indicate how carefully the concept was handled. The mainstream scholars also distinguished abrogation from specification, where one verse narrows the application of another without cancelling it, and from the disappearance of the circumstances that gave a ruling its occasion. The practical question was whether two texts could be reconciled, and only when they could not did the term apply.

**Causing a Verse to Be Forgotten**

The verse also mentions the removal of a verse from memory, and the commentators explained the phrase in two ways. One reading takes it as God causing a text to be forgotten, and a report preserved by Ibn Kathīr describes two men who had learned a sūrah from the Prophet ﷺ and later could not manage a letter of it, and were told that it was one of those that had been abrogated and forgotten. The other reading takes the verb to mean abandoning or forsaking, as the Book uses the same root when it says of the hypocrites that they neglected God and He neglected them (9:67).

Under either reading the sentence asserts something stronger than the replacement of a command. It asserts that the revelation is under God's care in its transmission as well as in its content, and that nothing remains binding except what He establishes.

**The Closing Question**

The verse ends by asking whether the reader knows that God is capable of everything, and the question is the answer to the objection. If the authority that sent the revelation governs the affairs of creation, then the revision of an instruction is not a difficulty to be explained away; it is a demonstration of the same authority.

The next verse continues with a related question about the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and about the absence of any protector besides God, and the two sentences belong to a single argument. What the sūrah establishes in this passage is that the revelation comes from a source that is free to legislate for its creatures as it wills, and that the community's confidence should rest on that source rather than on the stability of any particular ruling.
