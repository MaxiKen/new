## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:106

> If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one. Do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naskh: The Word and Its Range**

This verse is the Qur'an's own charter for a doctrine that became one of the Islamic sciences' most consequential tools, and the doctrine begins in the word. *Naskh* — abrogation — carries in Arabic a cluster of related senses the exegetes set out carefully. It can mean conveyance or transcription from an original: a copy is a *nuskha* of its source, and in this extended sense the entire Qur'an stands in naskh-relation to its origin in the Preserved Tablet (85:22). It can mean, more relevantly here, erasure and obliteration — the kind of effacing the sun performs on a shadow, the wind on footprints in dust, or age on youth. The root appears four times in the Qur'an, each time on one of these axes: here, on the replacement of verses; at 7:154, on the inscription of the tablets given to Moses; at 22:52, where Allah effaces what Satan casts into a prophet's recitation; and at 45:29, on the record of a community's deeds kept for judgment. The word's range already suggests the doctrine's shape: naskh is not destruction but transfer — something written, then written over; something standing, then superseded by what replaces it — and the verse's own promise is in that direction, since every erasure it announces comes with a replacement attached.

**The Doctrine the Sciences Built**

In its mainstream technical sense, developed across jurisprudence, theology, and commentary, naskh denotes the replacement of one legal ruling by another revealed later in time — the earlier text remaining in the Qur'an while its force as binding law ceases. The doctrine's boundaries were drawn as precisely as its core. It operates only on commands and prohibitions — rulings, the determinations of legal status — never on passages of doctrine: nothing abrogates the descriptions of Allah, the accounts of the Hereafter, the narratives of the prophets, the moral universals. The commentators' test cases make the boundary concrete: a verse declaring Allah's power over all things is not the kind of statement that can be superseded, nor the promise that whoever believes and does right will have his reward without fear or grief, nor any of the great commandments — the prohibitions of murder, theft, adultery, the duty of kindness to parents — which stand as universal and irrevocable. Within these limits naskh became indispensable, because the revelation descended across twenty-three years into a community being built in stages, and its later rulings sometimes replaced its earlier ones: the direction of prayer was turned, the rules of the fast were revised, the treatment of alcohol moved in stages from acknowledgment to prohibition. Without the doctrine, the corpus would contain standing contradictions; with it, the contradictions resolve into a sequence — legislation as pedagogy, Allah's rulings forming a people by degrees.

**Disputed Varieties and the Scale of Claims**

The tradition's internal debates map the doctrine's edges, and the mapping belongs to any honest account of this verse. Beyond the mainstream case — ruling replaced, text retained — two further types were posited. The first is removal of text and ruling together: a report from ʿĀʾishah describes Qur'anic rulings on suckling that were, in the tradition's account, neither acted upon nor retained in the written text. The second is the text's removal with the ruling's survival: the most cited case is the punishment of stoning, which a report from ʿUmar describes as resting on a Qur'anic verse no longer part of the received text while the ruling itself remained in force for most jurists. The scale of claimed instances varied enormously — Shāh Walī Allāh of Delhi counted five abrogated verses, al-Suyūṭī listed twenty-one, Ibn al-Jawzī no fewer than two hundred forty-seven — and the spread itself is evidence of how much of the material is contested interpretation rather than settled text. A minority position, associated with Abū Muslim al-Iṣfahānī, rejected the technical doctrine outright: apparent conflicts between rulings can be reconciled without supposing erasure, and *āyah* in this verse may mean miracle or sign rather than recited text. The mainstream answered with the tradition's own practice and the reports of withdrawal, but the reconcilers' challenge left a permanent mark: mainstream scholarship came to insist that naskh be claimed only where reconciliation genuinely fails, and to distinguish abrogation proper from three neighbors — specification, where a later verse details a general ruling without contradict it; simple accumulation of law; and the lapse of a ruling when the circumstance that occasioned it disappears. The verse itself, notably, promises replacement with what is *better or similar* — a formulation wide enough to carry the mainstream doctrine without requiring the maximal lists.

**“Or Cause It to Be Forgotten”**

The verse's second verb opens a reading the exegetes pursued with care. *Nunsīhā* — cause it to be forgotten — can be understood as withdrawal from memory: a revelation recited, then allowed to pass from the community's retention. The word can also carry the sense of abandonment, as in 9:67's *they forgot Allah, so He forgot them*, where forgetting from Allah's side means forsaking — a sense the commentators note cannot imply actual failure of divine memory. And with a different voweling, *nansaʾahā*, the verb would mean deferment, making the verse's pair a classification: verses abrogated and verses postponed. The forgotten-reading found support in the exegetical literature's reports of revelation withdrawn from memory during the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime — accounts of recitations that ceased to be reproducible by those who had learned them, explained as among the abrogated and forgotten. Whatever the exact mechanism, the verse's pairing of abrogation with forgetting establishes a principle the tradition drew explicitly: the Qur'an's preservation includes the preservation of its boundaries — what remains, remains by divine retention, and what withdrew, withdrew by the same governance. The community's memory of revelation is itself under the verse's doctrine: not a human archive that loses items by accident, but a custodianship whose contents are fixed by the Custodian.

**“Better or Similar”: The Question the Verse Invites**

How can one word of Allah be *better* than another? The commentators faced the question directly, and their answers define the verse's theology. The dominant solution restricts the comparison to effect rather than essence: the replacement is better in what it does for its recipients — alleviation in the matters of this world, intensification of reward in the matters of the next. The night prayer's obligation relaxed into recommendation eased the community's worldly burden; the fast of Ramaḍān imposed in place of earlier practice opened a greater reward. On this reading the verse speaks elliptically: no *ruling from a* verse do We abrogate — the text as revelation is never more or less than divine, and it is the ruling's operation that admits of better. Al-Ṭabarī anchors the ellipsis in the sūrah's own precedent: verse 93's *they were made to drink the calf* means, obviously, the love of the calf, the missing words understood from sense — and *better than it* is understood the same way, from the doctrine's context. Others take the word *khayr* in its non-comparative force: a *good* from Allah rather than something better than Allah — the replacement carries goodness, and the comparison language belongs to the recipients' economy, not the revelation's. Both answers guard the same line: the abrogating and the abrogated are equally Allah's word, and the gradation runs through the servants' benefit, never through the speech's quality.

**The Closing Question: Power Over Everything**

The verse ends where its argument rests: *do you not know that Allah is Most Capable of everything?* The question-form is the recital's standing device for knowledge the hearer already holds but has not applied — the same *do you not know* that opens the next verse's declaration of the kingdom. The capability named is the warrant for the whole doctrine. Abrogation is not inconsistency; inconsistency is a defect of agents who change their minds under new information, and the verse locates every replacement inside an omniscience that possessed all the information before the first ruling descended. What the sequence reveals is not divine revision but divine method: a single will, legislating for a community in formation, choosing for each stage the ruling that stage requires — easier or stricter, similar or better — with the whole arc known from its beginning. The polemic context the Qur'an itself preserves makes the question's edge visible: when a verse is replaced, the objectors say *you are just a fabricator* (16:101), reading sequence as invention and revision as proof against revelation. The verse answers at the level of the objection's premise. Fabricators change their output because they learn; Allah replaces rulings because He wills — and a will that owns all capability cannot be surprised by its own legislation. The Muslims hearing the sūrah received in this verse both their jurisprudential tool and its theological floor: the sequence in their Book is a mercy administered in stages by a Power that never discovers, and the doctrine of naskh is that administration's name.
