## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:93

> Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies against Allah or claims, “I have received revelations!”—although nothing was revealed to them—or the one who says, “I can reveal the like of Allah’s revelations!”? If you ˹O Prophet˺ could only see the wrongdoers in the throes of death while the angels are stretching out their hands ˹saying˺, “Give up your souls! Today you will be rewarded with the torment of disgrace for telling lies about Allah and for being arrogant towards His revelations!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Greatest Wrong Restated**

Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies against Allah? The formula appears at several points in the Book, and each time it is attached to a particular offence. The sūrah used it near its beginning for the man who invents a lie about God or denies His signs (6:21), it appears in the sūrah of the Heights and the sūrah of the Spider in similar forms, and here it introduces a passage about people who claimed to receive revelation.

The sūrah has been dealing with the Makkans' claims about the Qur'an, and this verse turns the accusation around. The men who said that God had revealed nothing to any human being (6:91) were themselves accused of manufacturing revelation, and the verse's three examples show what that manufacture looked like in practice.

**Three Ways of Fabricating Revelation**

The verse names three offenders in one sentence. The first invents a lie against God, which the Book treats as the broad category covering false doctrine and false attribution. The second claims to have received revelations when nothing was revealed to him, and the third announces that he can produce something like what God has sent down. Each of the three is a claim to a share in the authority that belongs to the revelation, and the sūrah has been answering the audiences' own claims to authority for many verses.

Commentators connected the second and third categories with figures from the Prophet's lifetime, including men who claimed prophethood among the Arabian tribes and a scribe who had recorded parts of the revelation and then came to believe that he could produce its like. The verse is stated in general terms, and the sūrah's argument applies it to everyone who asserts a comparable claim.

**The Scene at Death**

If you could only see the wrongdoers in the throes of death while the angels are stretching out their hands. The sūrah has already described the taking of the soul by the angels who never neglect their duty (6:61), and it now supplies the scene that the earlier verse left implicit. The angels extend their hands and order the dying to give up their souls, and the order is accompanied by the announcement of what awaits them.

The Book describes the same scene elsewhere with harsher detail, recording the angels striking the faces and backs of those who disbelieve (47:27) and the angels seizing the souls of those who wronged themselves and asking them what they had been doing (4:97). The picture the sūrah paints here is of a moment when the claims made during a life of argument are answered by the arrival of the angels, and the answer takes the form of an order that cannot be refused.

**The Charge Attached to the Death**

Today you will be rewarded with the torment of disgrace for telling lies about Allah and for being arrogant towards His revelations. The sentence names two offences, and they correspond to the two sides of the audience's conduct throughout this sūrah. They spoke about God what they did not know, which is the charge the sūrah made in the passage about the idols and their false intercession, and they turned away from the signs that had been made clear to them.

The Book treats arrogance toward the signs as the more serious of the two because it determines what a person does with evidence. The sūrah's earlier verses described people who denied the signs and turned away from them while they were still able to see them (6:46), and this verse states what that conduct receives at the end.

**The Condition the Verse Describes**

The phrase that introduces the scene, if you could only see, is the Book's way of presenting a reality that the audience cannot observe and of asking them to consider it as though they could. The address is to the Prophet, and the effect of the construction is to place the reader in the position of a witness to something that has not yet happened.

The Book uses the same form in other passages about the day of judgment, and it consistently uses it to bring the outcome of a dispute into the present. The argument the sūrah has been conducting with the Makkans concerned matters they could not see, and this verse describes what the dispute will look like at the moment when the claims are checked.

**What the Verse Establishes in the Chapter**

The verse completes a section that began with the audience's claim that God had revealed nothing to anyone (6:91) and continues with the treatment of those who claimed to produce revelation themselves. The sūrah has moved from the gift of revelation to its denial and now to its counterfeiting, and it has placed all three in the same frame of accountability.

The next verse records the address given to the same people when they arrive before God with nothing in their hands, and the two passages belong together. What the angels announce at the deathbed is stated in full at the judgment, and the sūrah supplies both so that the audience can see the whole sequence at once.
