## Sūrah an-Naṣr 110:1

> When Allah’s ˹ultimate˺ help comes and the victory ˹over Mecca is achieved˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sentence Stretched Across Three Verses**

Nothing in this chapter stands by itself. The Arabic opens with *idhā*, the particle that sets a condition for what will come, and the whole sūrah runs as a single period: when help arrives and the crowds are seen, then praise and ask forgiveness. This verse supplies only the opening condition. It carries no command and no conclusion; it is the first half of a sentence whose meaning is completed two verses later.

That shape decides how it should be read. *Idhā* is not "if." Where the other conditional particle, *in*, leaves the outcome genuinely open, *idhā* introduces what is expected to arrive — the particle of the assured future. The help is not offered as a contingency but announced as an event on its way, and the only open question is what the one who receives it will do with it. Taken in isolation the verse looks like a promise of victory. Set inside the sentence it belongs to, it is the trigger of an obligation: the triumph is the premise, the worship is the conclusion. A chapter of three verses is short enough that every one of them has to carry structure, and this one carries the structure of the whole.

**Help and Opening Are Two Different Gifts**

Two nouns hold the verse up, and they are not synonyms. *Naṣr* is help given inside a struggle — support arriving when one side cannot win alone, which is why the Qur'an makes it God's to give rather than a property of armies: *“If Allah helps you, none can defeat you. But if He denies you help, then who else can help you?”* (3:160). *Fatḥ* is an opening. Its root *f-t-ḥ* means to open what was shut, and from that base it names both the opening of a city's gates and the opening of a case — a verdict delivered between parties who have argued too long. A city that resisted is opened; a dispute is settled.

The verse puts help first and opening second, which is the order in which such things happen. Aid arrives while the outcome is still unclear; the opening follows it. Attaching both to God removes the achievement from human accounting. What comes is not a military success that a leader afterwards dedicates to heaven. It is divine help, delivered through a leader who receives it, and the grammar of the phrase keeps that direction of travel visible.

**Mecca Fell in Ramadan of the Eighth Year**

The bracketed words in the translation name the event most commentators mean: the conquest of Mecca. It took place in Ramadan 8 AH, which falls in January 630 CE. The Prophet ﷺ marched with roughly ten thousand men, the largest force ever to leave Medina, and the city came over with almost no fighting; the general amnesty granted afterwards is what made the second verse of this sūrah possible. Quraysh had held out for eight years after the Hijrah, and their resistance had been the last public argument against the message.

Two readings compete with this one. Some take the *fatḥ* to be the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah of 6 AH (628 CE), because the Qur'an itself calls that treaty *“a clear triumph ˹O Prophet˺”* (48:1) and because the truce that followed produced conversions on a scale Mecca had never seen. Others read the verse as pointing to the conquests after the Prophet's death, when Islam crossed the borders of Arabia. The first has the stronger claim. This sūrah joins the *fatḥ* to crowds entering the religion, and the historical record puts those crowds in the ninth year, when tribal embassies came to Medina in numbers after the fall of the city they had been waiting on.

**Past-Tense Verbs for Events Still to Come**

Both verbs of the verse stand in the perfect: the help *came*, the victory *was achieved*. At the moment of revelation neither had happened. Arabic regularly puts a future event in the perfect when its realization is so certain that the language treats it as finished, and the Qur'an uses that device wherever certainty is the point: *“The command of Allah is at hand, so do not hasten it.”* (16:1). The Hour has not arrived; the sentence speaks of it as though it has.

The grammar here does the same work. A promise can fail; a report cannot. By placing the coming victory in the tense of accomplished fact, the verse tells its first audience — the Companions in Medina, and the Quraysh who heard it quoted back to them — that whether Islam would survive in Arabia was already a settled matter. A few early readers took the perfect literally and understood the verse as describing what had in fact occurred by the time the report was passed down, which turned the tense into a small chronological puzzle. That reading costs the verse its force. Its certainty is a rhetorical achievement rather than a note about dates, and the puzzle disappears once the idiom is recognized.

**The Oldest Reading of This Verse Was That It Announced a Death**

The earliest interpretation on record does not treat the verse as news of a conquest at all. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb used to seat the young Ibn ʿAbbās with the veterans of Badr, and some of them resented it. One day ʿUmar asked the gathering what they made of *“When comes the Help of Allah and the Conquest.”* Some answered that believers are commanded to praise God and seek His forgiveness when help and conquest reach them; others said nothing at all. Turning to Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿUmar was told that this is the appointed term of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, made known to him: the help and the conquest are the sign of it, and the praise and the seeking of forgiveness are what he is to do with the time remaining. ʿUmar's answer is recorded in the same report — he knew nothing about the verse beyond what this young man had said. It stands in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4970, from Ibn ʿAbbās through Saʿīd ibn Jubayr.

So the first generation read the condition as a notice of departure. The logic is plain: when a message has been delivered and accepted in crowds, the one who carried it has finished. The Qur'an states the principle elsewhere without softening it: *“We have not granted immortality to any human before you ˹O Prophet˺: so if you die, will they live forever?”* (21:34).

**The Last Complete Chapter, With a Few Verses After It**

Most exegetes put this sūrah at the end of the revelation, as the last chapter given whole. Individual verses came later — among them 2:281, 4:176 and 9:128, and, on the strongest account, 5:3 at the Farewell Pilgrimage. The disagreement concerns the setting rather than the rank. Some place the revelation at Minā on the tenth of Dhū l-Ḥijjah during that pilgrimage, a day after 5:3; others place it immediately before 5:3; others hold that it came in Medina after the Prophet ﷺ had returned home. A minority dates it to right after Ḥudaybiyyah in 6 AH, and al-Wāḥidī sets it at the departure for Ḥunayn in 8 AH, roughly two years before the end.

Weighed against each other, the late Madinan placement fits the internal evidence best. Nothing in the vocabulary belongs to the Meccan style of oath and image, and the whole chapter reads as an address to a man whose work is complete. Ibn Masʿūd called it the Sūrah of Farewell, *sūrat al-tawdīʿ*, and the name stuck.

**Nothing Human Is Named in It**

No enemy appears, no battle, no date. The verse reporting the greatest political event of the Prophet's career does not mention Quraysh, does not mention Mecca, and does not mention a single person; the city enters the English text only through a translator's bracket. The absence is the argument. An event recorded as divine action is described with the two words that belong to God — help and opening — while the human beings who fought for it appear later only as a crowd, without names or genealogies.

Contrast that with the chapter two places on in the codex, where the Qur'an names a living opponent in its very first line (111:1). Where a person is the subject of the message, the naming happens; where God is the subject, the persons drop out. That economy is why this sūrah can be recited in any century and read as describing the present. The next verse supplies the human half of the scene.
