## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50

> They responded, “˹That would be˺ no harm! Surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Magicians Refuse to Let Threat Define Harm**

The magicians answer Pharaoh's threat with a brief judgment: it will cause no harm in the final measure. They do not deny that mutilation and crucifixion are painful or that Pharaoh can inflict them. They deny that the ruler's violence can make their return to God a loss. By changing the measure of harm, they break the spell of Pharaoh's power.

This is not a romantic claim that bodily suffering is pleasant. It is a claim about proportion. Physical safety is valuable, but it is not the highest good. A person who preserves the body by surrendering truth may suffer a deeper loss than the person whose body is harmed for refusing falsehood. The magicians now judge Pharaoh's threat from the horizon of return to God rather than from the horizon of royal favor.

**Return to the Lord Makes Courage Rational**

Their reason is that they will return to their Lord. This belief gives death a destination and history a final judge. Pharaoh can decide what happens to them in the court of Egypt, but he cannot prevent their return to the One who created them. The threat remains serious in the world, yet it is no longer ultimate.

The statement also corrects Pharaoh's claim to ownership. The ruler speaks as though the magicians' bodies and beliefs belong to him. They answer that their final belonging is to God. Return means that life is not a possession held permanently by the palace. It is a trust that comes from the Lord and goes back to Him, whether the ruler approves or not.

**No Harm Does Not Mean No Responsibility**

The phrase no harm can be misunderstood if separated from its context. It does not teach that oppression is harmless or that people may abuse one another because the Hereafter exists. Pharaoh's punishment remains evil, and the ruler remains accountable for it. The magicians are speaking about what the threat can ultimately do to their relationship with God, not excusing what Pharaoh intends to do.

This distinction allows courage without glorifying violence. A believer may seek safety, resist injustice, and protect life while refusing to worship fear. If a person is forced to choose between denying truth and suffering for truth, the magicians' answer shows which loss is greater. Their words condemn the tyrant even as they refuse to let him dictate their spiritual condition.

**The Word All Makes the Confession Communal**

They say that all will return to the Lord. The plural joins the magicians together and gives their answer a shared horizon. Pharaoh's threat is addressed to a group, and their courage is also collective. They do not leave one person alone to face the punishment or negotiate a private escape through betrayal of the others.

The plural may also carry a broader truth about human beings. Every person, ruler and subject alike, returns to God. Pharaoh's court tries to divide people into those who command and those who suffer, but the Hereafter places all before one Lord. The magicians' words restore that equality. They may be threatened as subjects, but they know that Pharaoh will also stand before the Judge.

**The Threat Cannot Erase the Meaning of the Sign**

Pharaoh has attempted to reverse the public result by threatening the witnesses. The magicians respond as though the threat has no power to change what they saw. The sign remains a sign, their prostration remains a confession, and the ruler's anger becomes evidence of his refusal rather than evidence against Moses.

This is the strength of testimony under pressure. A witness need not be able to prevent punishment in order to speak truthfully. The ability to harm a witness may make the testimony costly, but it does not make it false. Indeed, a threat offered after recognition can show that the ruler fears the truth's effect on the public.

**Courage Does Not Depend on Royal Permission**

The magicians were previously promised closeness to Pharaoh. They now speak as people who have lost interest in that reward. Their return to God has loosened the bond created by status. The ruler can withdraw promotion or threaten execution, but neither offer nor threat determines the value of their new faith.

This is a turning point in the narrative of power. Before the sign, the magicians measured the encounter by reward and victory. After the sign, they measure it by return and truth. Their courage is not produced by self-confidence. It comes from recognizing that Pharaoh's world is temporary and that the Lord to whom they return is permanent.

**The Answer Places Pharaoh's Violence Under Judgment**

By naming the Lord as the one to whom they return, the magicians put Pharaoh's action inside a larger court. The king may announce a sentence, but he does not deliver the final verdict. Every injury he orders will be known, and every act of courage will be weighed. His power can make the road difficult, not decide where it ends.

The answer therefore refuses both panic and defiance for its own sake. The magicians do not insult Pharaoh or pretend that his soldiers cannot hurt them. They simply deny his ability to make them abandon God. Their short reply is a complete reordering of fear: the ruler is no longer the final horizon, because the Lord is.

Their words do not seek a bargain with Pharaoh. They do not ask for a lighter sentence or promise to keep silent. They answer the threat by naming the truth that makes the threat bearable. A person may still plead for safety in another setting, but no bargain can require him to call Pharaoh's power ultimate. The magicians have discovered a freedom that the prison cannot remove.
