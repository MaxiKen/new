## Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt 29:21

> He punishes whoever He wills, and shows mercy to whoever He wills. And you will ˹all˺ be returned to Him.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Actions Held in One Hand**

The sentence places punishment and mercy side by side as acts of the same agent, and it does so without embarrassment. Both are attributed to God's will, which is the Book's way of saying that neither outcome lies outside His authority or at the disposal of any created power. The placement matters after the previous verses, which used creation and travel as evidence that the resurrection will happen. Judgment follows the demonstration. The Book's method is to establish that the event is certain before it says anything about the verdict, and this verse supplies the second half: the God who can call the creation back is the God who decides what happens to the people standing in it. Nothing in the sentence softens the threat and nothing inflates it; the two possibilities are named with equal brevity.

**What the Attribution to Will Does Not Mean**

A reader may hear the phrase "whoever He wills" as a description of arbitrary power, and the rest of the Book prevents that reading. The sayings collected outside the Qur'an report the divine statement that mercy prevails over wrath, and the Book itself declares that God has prescribed mercy for Himself and that His mercy encompasses all things (7:156). Punishment in the Qur'an is consistently attached to deeds, and the passages that attribute it to God's will never present it as random. Commentators explain the difference in the two clauses by noting that mercy is named in the Book as that which God has taken upon Himself as an obligation, while punishment is described as the consequence that follows when people insist on the course that produces it. The will in the sentence is therefore not the will of a capricious ruler but the will of a sovereign who has published His policy.

**The Return That Gives the Sentence Its Weight**

The verse closes with the same destination named in the previous two, and the repetition serves the argument. Punishment and mercy are not abstractions distributed from a distance; they are received at a meeting. The Book's standard formula is that people return to God and are then informed of what they did, and here the return is stated without the explanation attached, as though the audience already knows the procedure. What the short clause accomplishes is a shift from speculation to expectation. A person may argue about the attributes of God in the abstract, but the sentence places him on a road that ends in his own arrival, and the argument becomes a matter of preparation rather than definition.

The return also makes the sentence a statement about direction rather than distance. A person may travel far from the community he was born into, and the verse's claim is that the road ends in the same place for everyone, which is what makes the warning relevant to hearers who assumed that leaving Makkah settled the matter.

**Why the Sentence Runs From Threat to Promise**

The order of the two clauses is the opposite of the order a cautious reader might choose. Punishment is named first, mercy second, so that the sentence ends in mercy rather than in severity. The Book's habit of closing passages on the note of mercy appears repeatedly, and it fits the purpose of a text that wants repentance rather than despair. A person who hears only a threat hardens, and a person who hears only a promise becomes careless; the Book usually sets the two in a sequence that leaves the door visible at the end. The final clause then adds the return, which is the fact that makes both clauses relevant to every listener. The sentence is short enough to be memorized and complete enough to govern a life.

Putting the warning first also marks the audience as people who still have time to respond. Had the order been reversed, the passage would read as a description of a finished state; arranged as it stands, the sentence presents a possibility that is closed only by the outcome it names in advance.

**The Same Pairing in the Book's Warnings**

The pairing used here appears whenever the Book describes the end of a community or a life. Mercy and punishment are named together in the passages about the destruction of earlier peoples, in the descriptions of reward and loss in the Hereafter, and in the closing lines of sūrah after sūrah. What the repetition establishes is that the Book has no interest in a single-sided portrait of the divine. A God who is only severe cannot be trusted with the weak, and a God who is only indulgent cannot be trusted with justice. The attributes are held together in this sentence the way the Book holds them everywhere: the same authority that raised the creation governs its outcome, and the two outcomes are distributed by a will that has already explained itself. A reader who wants a single rule for the arrangement will not find one, and the absence is deliberate, since a rule would let a person calculate with God instead of turning to Him. What the Book supplies instead is a description of the One who decides, and it anchors the claim about mercy in the declaration that His mercy encompasses everything (7:156), a statement broad enough to keep hope alive in the worst case and specific enough to be attached to deeds in the clause that follows.

**Mercy Described as an Obligation Taken On**

The Book avoids leaving mercy as an inference from God's character, and it states that He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful before it says anything about the accounting that follows (6:12). The same sureness runs through the two announcements placed side by side in another chapter, where the Prophet is told to inform God's servants that He is the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful, and that His torment is the most painful (15:49–50). The pairing is the Book's standard way of presenting the two outcomes: neither is an accident of temperament, and both are announced in advance so that a person can order his life accordingly. The verse under discussion uses the same structure, and it leaves both clauses standing without softening either one.

The two clauses also hold each other in check. A reader who leans on mercy alone loses the urgency the verse has just established, and a reader who fixes on punishment alone loses the invitation that follows, so the sentence leaves neither error available to him.

**What This Verse Asks of the Reader Who Believes in Return**

If the return is certain and the outcome is decided by God's will, the believer's practical task is to place himself where mercy is found rather than where punishment is earned. The Book describes that placement in terms of repentance, good action, and the refusal to persist in wrongdoing. Nothing in this verse names those means, because the surrounding passage is dealing with proof rather than instruction, and the next verses will move to a scene that shows how the argument of Abraham was answered. What the sentence does supply is the horizon. Every discussion in the chapter about testing, patience, and pressure takes place in front of that horizon, and the two words about punishment and mercy are what make the testing a matter of consequence rather than an exercise.
