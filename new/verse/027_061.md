## Sūrah an-Naml 27:61

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who made the earth a place of settlement, caused rivers to flow through it, placed firm mountains upon it, and set a barrier between ˹fresh and salt˺ bodies of water? Was it another god besides Allah?” Absolutely not! But most of them do not know. 2) Who Is the Most Gracious?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Features, All of Them Underfoot**

The previous question looked up — heavens, sky, rain. This one looks down, and lists four things about the ground people stand on: it is habitable, it has rivers, it has mountains that hold it, and its waters are kept apart.

The shift is deliberate. Having established the argument at cosmic scale, the chapter now runs it at human scale, naming the conditions that make settled life possible. Each item on the list is something a person in seventh-century Arabia depended on daily and had no part in producing. The word for the earth's condition is *qarāran*, a place of settling — from a root meaning to come to rest. The claim is not merely that the earth exists but that it is stable enough to build on, which is a different and more specific assertion.

**Rivers in a Chapter Addressed to a Desert Audience**

Naming rivers to Meccans is pointed. Mecca had no river; its water came from a well, and the surrounding region was defined by the absence of permanent surface water. The audience knew rivers from travel and from the agricultural regions to the north and south.

That makes the item an argument rather than an observation. A community that lives on a well knows exactly what a river is worth, and the knowledge is the kind that comes from lacking something. The Qur'an's questions in this section consistently name provisions that are unevenly distributed, which prevents the audience from treating them as automatic features of the world. Rain that sometimes does not fall, rivers that exist elsewhere, gardens that fail — the argument works because these things are visibly contingent, and contingency is what points to a giver.

**Mountains Described as Holding the Earth Steady**

The word is *rawāsī*, from a root meaning to be firmly anchored; a ship at anchor uses the same root. The Qur'an repeatedly describes mountains this way, as stabilisers placed on the earth.

What the image asserts is a functional relationship: mountains are not merely large objects but are doing something for the ground they stand on. The verse pairs them with the earth's habitability, which is the order the argument requires — first the ground is a place of settlement, then the reason it holds. Commentators have long connected this to the practical experience of solid terrain versus shifting sand, and the modern reader may connect it to what is known about crustal structure. Neither reading is required by the verse. What the text asserts is that the arrangement is an arrangement, made rather than happened.

**The Barrier Between Two Waters**

The fourth item is the strangest: a barrier set between two bodies of water. The Qur'an returns to this image in several places, sometimes calling the divider *ḥājiz* and sometimes *barzakh*, and the pairing is usually understood as fresh water and salt.

The phenomenon is observable. Where a large river meets the sea, the two waters remain visibly distinct for a considerable distance, differing in colour, density and salinity, and they mix slowly rather than at once. Estuaries maintain a measurable boundary layer between outflowing fresh water and intruding salt. What the verse names is therefore something a traveller could see at any major river mouth on the coasts the Arabs traded along. Al-Rāzī also records an inward reading, in which the two seas are the soul's faith and its rebellion, with a barrier held between them by God's grace for as long as the soul does not turn insolent. That is allegory rather than exegesis, and it is worth reporting as his and not as the verse's.

**"Most of Them Do Not Know"**

The refrain is answered here with a statement about knowledge: *bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn* — rather, most of them do not know. The previous verse ended by calling them people who set up equals. This one is gentler and more diagnostic.

Two things follow from the wording. The first is *most*, not all, which keeps the judgment honest — the Qur'an's polemic against Meccan paganism regularly leaves room for exceptions, and this chapter has just spent thirty verses on a queen who was wrong and correctable. The second is that ignorance is named rather than malice. Pharaoh's court in verse 14 knew and refused, which is the worst case; these are people who have not put the question to themselves. That diagnosis explains the form of this whole section. You do not answer ignorance with a threat. You answer it with a question, repeated in enough different domains that the answer becomes hard to avoid, which is exactly what the chapter is doing.

**The Heading in the Translation Line**

The quoted translation ends with an editorial heading that belongs to the printed edition rather than to the revelation — a label announcing the next of the questions in this sequence. It is reproduced because the blockquote follows the translation exactly.

Such headings are added by translators and publishers to help a reader find their place, and they differ from edition to edition. They carry no authority and are not recited. What this one does register accurately is a real feature of the passage: the questions in this section are distinct units, each self-contained, each ending on the same refrain, and an editor working through the chapter felt the need to number them. The verse itself ends at *most of them do not know*, and everything after that belongs to the apparatus of the book rather than to the text.
