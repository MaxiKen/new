## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:29

> They insisted, “There is nothing beyond this worldly life and we will never be resurrected.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim That Explains All the Others**

The verse names the position underneath every other objection in the sūrah. Requests for signs, arguments about the ancestors, mockery of the warnings, and the obstruction described a few verses earlier all rest on this one conclusion: there is nothing beyond this life, and no return to a judgment. The Book reports the same position in the mouths of other audiences, and it repeats it because it is the foundation on which a whole approach to life can be built.

Elsewhere the denial appears with the same edge: they say that once they are dead and reduced to dust and bones, they will not be resurrected (23:82); they ask whether, when they die and become dust and bones, they will really be judged (37:53); and the disbelievers claim the Hour will never come to them (34:3), a claim answered with an oath by the Lord of the unseen.

**A Sentence About the Boundaries of Reality**

The wording is not "we doubt the resurrection." It is a statement about what exists: nothing beyond this worldly life. That is a complete metaphysics delivered in nine words, and its confidence is worth noticing, because the people asserting it had no means of inspecting the boundary they were describing. A claim of that shape can be made only by a person who has decided what the limits of reality are and then allowed no evidence from outside them.

The Book treats the sentence as a boast rather than a finding. The verse says they insisted, which suggests repetition and reinforcement, the way a position is held by being restated among people who agree with one another.

**Why the Position Was So Useful**

Denial of the resurrection was not an unlucky guess that the Makkans had happened to make. It was the assumption that made their arrangements comfortable. Property, privilege, shrines, slave-trading, and inherited standing all lose their force if a person is going to be held to account for how he acquired and used them. The Book makes the connection explicit elsewhere, where the evil consequences of deeds unfold before people who had taken their religion as play and were deluded by worldly life (7:51).

The sūrah's next verses describe those who deny the meeting with God as the ones who will be caught by surprise, and the connection is the same in each place. A person who has removed the accounting from reality has removed the one limit on how he will treat the world.

**The Book's Name for This Denial**

Note the vocabulary the Book prefers. Rather than arguing about the mechanics of resurrection, it describes the position as denying the meeting with God (6:31); elsewhere as those who do not expect to meet their Lord (25:21). The choice of words places the issue where scripture wants it: not an argument about biology and dust, but a refusal that the account will ever be settled face to face.

That reframing explains why the Book answers this denial with evidence the audience already accepted. It points to the earth being revived after its death and says that people will be brought out in the same way (30:19); it points to the God who causes seeds to sprout and brings the living out of the dead (6:95). The audience had no quarrel with any of those observations. The quarrel was about where they led.

**Proof From the Ordinary World**

The Book's argument from nature is not a metaphor. It is the same Maker doing the same kind of work in front of witnesses every season, and the audience consisted of people who watched it for a living. Rain revives the ground, grain is produced from a seed, and a creature comes out of the dead matter of the earth, all of it under the same authority that the Makkans already conceded when asked who created the heavens and the earth.

The denial the verse reports therefore sits on top of a conceded premise. Acknowledging a Creator who can produce a harvest out of a dry field and then denying that He can produce human beings out of dust is a position that cannot be maintained on grounds available in the audience's own experience.

**The Claim Tested Against What the Audience Already Knew**

The Book does not treat the denial as an unanswerable position, and it does not ask the Makkans to accept a new principle in order to reject it. It asks them about the things they already granted. They accepted that God created the heavens and the earth; they accepted the rain, the harvest, and the span of a human life as coming from a power beyond the shrines. Against that backdrop, the sentence in this verse is not a cautious conclusion drawn from the evidence, and the Book says as much where it reports those who deny the meeting with God asking who will give life to decayed bones while forgetting that they were created in the first place (36:78).

A man who grants a first creation and denies a second has not followed his own premises to their conclusion. He has stopped where the conclusion became inconvenient, which is why the Book repeatedly reaches for the same argument and never bothers to look for a different one.

**First Creation as the Standing Proof**

The sūrah has already made the argument in its opening verses. The One who created human beings from clay appointed a term, and another term rests with Him, and the audience is asked why they still doubt (6:2). The reasoning runs from the creation the Makkans could see in their own bodies to the appointment they could not see, and it uses the visible fact as the guarantee of the invisible one.

That order is deliberate, and it matches the way the Book argues about the earth being revived for its harvest (30:19). The audience is not being asked to believe something without any analogue in experience. It is being asked to accept that the power which produced the analogue can repeat the operation on a larger scale, which is the smallest possible extension of what the Makkans already conceded.

**What the Denial Leaves in Its Wake**

Once the account is removed, the only remaining measure of a life is what can be enjoyed and shown to other people, and the verse that follows describes exactly that. It is worth reading the two sentences as a pair, since the first is the belief and the second is what happens when the belief runs its course. The sūrah will go on to describe how the world, arranged according to this belief, is play and amusement, and how the Home of the Hereafter is better for the mindful, and the argument continues from there.
