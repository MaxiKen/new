## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:4

> So He may reward those who believe and do good. It is they who will have forgiveness and an honourable provision.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Clause That Explains the Whole Reckoning**

The verse opens with *li-yajziya* — the Arabic prefix *li-* here is the lām of purpose, "so that He may." Grammatically this clause hangs on what came before it, which means the exhaustive record described in the previous verse is not presented as an end in itself. Surveillance is not the point; payment is. This matters for how the whole passage is read. A universe in which nothing escapes notice could be a machine of unrelieved threat, and plenty of religious imaginations have understood it that way. The Qur'an instead states the purpose in a single clause and makes the first beneficiaries of total knowledge the people who did good quietly. Al-Rūm attaches the same purpose to the same theme in almost the same shape, ending an argument about the Hour with *"so that He may ˹generously˺ reward those who believe and do good, out of His grace"* (30:45), and Yūnus ties it to the resurrection itself: *"Indeed, He originates the creation then resurrects it so that He may justly reward those who believe and do good"* (10:4). Judgment exists because merit would otherwise go unpaid.

**Faith and Action Named as One Qualification, Not Two**

The phrase *alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — those who believe and do good — is one of the most frequent formulas in the Qur'an, and its grammar keeps the two elements welded. Belief is a verb here, not a noun: not "the people of faith" but "those who believed," an act performed. The good works are then joined by a simple conjunction with no conditional between them, so the formula never allows the reading that either half might stand alone. The word *ṣāliḥāt* is worth unfolding, because it is not primarily an ethical word in Arabic. Its root, *ṣ-l-ḥ*, means to be sound, intact, fit for use, and it is used of repairing a wall or mending a relationship; its opposite, *fasād*, means rot. So the deeds in question are not decorative acts of piety but works that leave something in better repair than they found it. Naming this pairing in a sūrah about deniers of the Hour also draws a line. The dispute over resurrection was never merely theoretical, since a man's answer to it decides whether unwitnessed repair work is worth doing.

**Forgiveness Is Listed First, Before Any Reward**

The order of the two gifts repays attention: *maghfirah*, then *rizq karīm* — forgiveness, then an honourable provision. If reward were strictly a matter of accounting, forgiveness would be out of place in the list, since it is not something earned by good works but something granted despite bad ones. Its position at the head of the pair concedes something the passage never states outright: the believers being rewarded here also have a record, and the record has debts in it. The reckoning that catches an atom's weight (34:3) catches theirs too. So the first thing they receive is the cancellation of what the record holds against them, and only then the payment for what stands to their credit. This sequence is not accidental to this verse alone. Al-Ḥajj uses the identical pairing in the identical order when describing the same people: *"So those who believe and do good will have forgiveness and an honourable provision"* (22:50). Al-Anfāl adds a third element and keeps the order: *"They will have elevated ranks, forgiveness, and an honourable provision from their Lord"* (8:4).

**"Honourable Provision" Describes the Receiving, Not the Goods**

*Rizq karīm* is usually translated "an honourable provision" or "a generous provision," and the adjective is doing something specific. *Karīm* in Arabic is applied to a person of noble character, to a gift given without being asked for, and to a thing of high quality; its root sense combines generosity with dignity. Attached to *rizq*, sustenance, it says as much about the manner of the giving as about the substance given. Provision in this world often arrives with humiliation attached — earned under a harsh master, begged for, or handed down with visible reluctance. The Qur'an's picture of the next world removes that residue. The word choice also carries a quiet rebuke to the men whose voices fill this sūrah, who measured divine favour by the size of a man's estate and will shortly boast about it in their own words (34:35). Provision of the kind promised here is not indexed to wealth at all. It is indexed to belief and repair, and it arrives from a Giver whose generosity is part of the gift.

**Reward That Is Owed, and Reward That Is Grace**

A tension runs under this verse which the Qur'an handles openly rather than smoothing away. On one side, the language is that of payment: *jazāʾ*, recompense, the settling of accounts, and the Book insists elsewhere that the exchange is exactly proportionate for wrongdoing — *"Whoever does an evil deed will only be paid back with its equivalent"* (40:40). On the other side, the reward for good is described as multiplied far beyond proportion: *"Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold"* (6:160), and in the parable of the grain, seven hundredfold and more (2:261). Justice sets the floor for punishment; grace sets no ceiling for reward. Al-Rūm makes the asymmetry explicit by adding *out of His grace* to the very same purpose clause (30:45). The two need not be reconciled by weakening either. What a servant is owed, he is paid in full and not a mote short; what he is given beyond that is not owed at all, which is why forgiveness can head the list without making the accounting incoherent.

**Why the Sūrah Answers a Denial With a Promise**

Structurally this is the gentler half of a two-part reply to men who said the Hour would never reach them. Having established that the reckoning is possible because everything is known and written, the sūrah first states what the reckoning is for, and the answer is the vindication of people whose faith and repair work went unrewarded in a world that measured worth in caravans and sons. Only afterwards does the passage turn to the other party. The order is deliberate and it recurs across the Book: the promise is stated before the threat, and the threat is stated as the alternative to a promise already on the table. For an audience listening in Mecca, many of them poor and some of them enslaved, the sequence carried practical weight. The Day they were told to fear was, for them, the Day their unpaid ledger would finally be honoured. The following verse turns to those on the opposite side of that ledger.
