## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:278

> O believers! Fear Allah, and give up outstanding interest if you are ˹true˺ believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command's Two Halves**

The ribā-block's legislation proper opens with a sentence whose two halves the tradition reads as one command: *Fear Allah, and give up what remains of the interest*. The taqwā-clause is not decorative framing — the commentators treat it as the command's operative ground, the fear-of-God being the interior state whose absence produced the devourers' slogan and whose presence makes the relinquishment possible. The verb of the second half is dharaū — leave, abandon, let lie — and its object is mā baqiya, what remains: the remainder, the residue, the still-standing excess. The object's precision is the verse's first legislation: the command does not ask the creditors to disgorge their history (the amnesty of 2:275 covered what has passed — the previous gains kept, the case left to God) but to stop the machine where it stands — every accrued increase not yet collected, every deferment-fee on the books, every compounding clause in every ledger of the community, abandoned as of the command's arrival. The tradition's historical frame is the Farewell declaration the previous verse carried — all the ribā of the Age of Ignorance remitted, the principal alone retained — and this verse is that declaration's legislative form: the remission applied, creditor by creditor, to the outstanding balance.

**What Remains**

The exegetes work the phrase mā baqiya with juristic care, because the distinction it draws governs the whole transition. What remains is the increase that exists as claim rather than as possession — the amounts owed to the creditor under the ribā-terms and not yet paid. Those claims are voided: the debtor owes the principal and nothing beyond it, and the creditor's ledger is to be rewritten to match. What does not remain — what has already been taken — is treated by the amnesty-clause's rule: not clawed back, its case left to Allah (2:275), with al-Qurṭubī recording the consensus that past ribā was not open to reclamation. The distinction produces the transition's social shape: no retroactive confiscation, no debt-jubilee of principals, but an immediate and total stop to the increase-mechanism. The jurists read the same distinction into the repentance-clause of the next verse's formula — the principal retained, neither wronging nor being wronged — as the settlement this command's obedience produces: the creditor keeps his capital and loses his surplus; the debtor keeps his property and loses his compounding. What remains, on both sides, is the loan as the tradition understands it — an act whose return is with God rather than in the fee.

**If You Are Believers**

The verse's final clause is its sharpest instrument: *if you are true believers* — in tuʾminūn, the condition attached to the abandonment. The commentators read the conditional in the register the Qur'an reserves for such attachments: not a doubt cast on the addressees' faith but a definition of it. The faith named is being tested against its own content — the community that has just been described as believing, doing good, establishing prayer, and paying alms (2:277) is told that the outstanding ribā in its ledgers is incompatible with the description, and the conditional makes the incompatibility active: belief, if it is belief, gives up the remainder. The structure echoes the sūrah's own faith-verses — the handhold grasped by the renouncing and believing (2:256), the fear-of-God that the annulment-prohibitions assumed — and it does the same work here on the economy: faith is not a state that coexists with the increase-claims but a state that releases them. The tradition's ascetic commentators draw the clause's full edge: the verse makes the abandoned ribā the criterion, so that a believer's ledger is itself a testimony, and what remains in it after the command is the measure of what the in tuʾminūn found inside.

**The Taqwā-Frame**

The fear-of-God that heads the command connects the economy-block to the sūrah's whole legislation, and the commentators mark the connection deliberately: this is the same taqwā the code invoked at its thresholds — nearer to reverence in the waiver-ranking (2:237), the duty on those mindful in the provision-ruling (2:241), beware of Him in the heart-knowledge verse (2:235). The placement means that the ribā-relinquishment is legislated as an act of taqwā rather than as a commercial adjustment: what makes the abandonment possible is not economic argument but the feared-and-hoped relationship the sūrah has been installing since its opening portrait of the God-fearers (2:2–5). Al-Rāzī's cascade in the two-voices verse (2:268) is the psychology behind the frame: the soul that obeys the poverty-threat keeps and compounds, and the soul that fears God releases — the taqwā-clause is the command's statement of which fear it expects to find operative. The verse thus does not argue with the creditors' self-interest; it addresses the fear that outranks self-interest, and makes the argument's conclusion — leave the remainder — that fear's immediate act.

**The Community Addressed as Creditors**

One feature of the verse's address has drawn the commentators' notice: it speaks to believers as people who hold ribā-claims — the community's own ledgers, not the enemy's. The address assumes what Medina's economy actually contained: Muslim creditors holding pre-Islamic deferment-claims against Muslim and non-Muslim debtors alike, the mechanism of the Age of Ignorance still running inside the believing community's own books. The verse's legislation is therefore internal reform at its most concrete — a community ordered to rewrite its own contracts, cancel its own accrued income, and absorb the loss as the cost of the faith it claimed. The commentators connect this internality to the sequence's structure: the portrait-verse (2:277) described the community's four terms, and this verse tests the fourth — the alms-payters against the interest-takers are supposed to be the same people, and where they are not, the command makes them so by force of the conditional. No external enemy is named in the ribā-block; the war-declaration that follows is addressed to whoever among the believers does not comply — which is why the taqwā-frame comes first: the battlefield of this legislation is the believer's own ledger, and the fear of God is the only authority that can order a man to burn his own outstanding income.
