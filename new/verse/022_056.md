## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:56

> All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them. So those who believe and do good will be in the Gardens of Bliss.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sovereignty on That Day**

*Al-mulku yawmaʾidhin lillāh* — the dominion on that Day belongs to God. The sentence is a noun sentence with a fronted predicate, which in Arabic gives it the force of exclusivity: the authority is God's, and on that Day it is visibly, undeniably His.

The *that Day* is the barren day of the previous verse — the day after which nothing follows. The connection is the point. Sovereignty is not being asserted for a day that will be followed by another arrangement; it is being asserted for the day that ends the series.

There is an implied contrast with every other day. Authority in this life is distributed, contested and mostly invisible; kings, judges and assemblies hold pieces of it and the pieces are frequently in the wrong hands. The Qur'an's claim is not that God is absent from that arrangement but that on this day the distribution stops.

Sūrah al-Furqān states the formula with the attribute that decides its tone: *“True authority on that Day will belong ˹only˺ to the Most Compassionate. And it will be a hard day for the disbelievers”* (25:26). The Most Compassionate — *al-Raḥmān* — as the holder of undivided authority, and the hardness of the day falling only on one party. Verse 56's sovereignty is that sentence without the second clause: dominion God's, judgment His act, and the Gardens receiving those the compassion covers.

**The Same Claim Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The statement is one of the Qur'an's fixed formulae for the Resurrection, and the wording is nearly identical wherever it appears: *“the Day all will appear ˹before Allah˺. Nothing about them will be hidden from Him. ˹He will ask,˺ ‘Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!’”* (40:16).

Sūrah al-Infiṭār states it with an added consequence: *“˹It is˺ the Day no soul will be of ˹any˺ benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺”* (82:19). The two halves belong together. Human authority in this life includes the ability to intercede, to protect, to pull strings. On that day there is none of it, and the reason given is that the authority is not divided.

Sūrah al-Anʿām pairs the same claim with the act of creation itself: *“All authority is His ˹alone˺ on the Day the Trumpet will be blown. He is the Knower of all—seen or unseen”* (6:73).

**"He Will Judge Between Them"**

*Yaḥkumu baynahum* — He judges between them. The preposition *bayna* is the same one the sūrah used in verse 17, when it said that God would judge between the believers, the Jews, the Sabians, the Christians, the Magians and the associators.

The repetition is deliberate: the deferred adjudication of verse 17 is the adjudication being executed here. Everything the sūrah said about religious disputes being settled by God rather than by human verdicts comes due in this clause.

That clause is also the answer to a natural objection. If authority on that day is God's alone, what does He do with it? The verse says: He judges. Sovereignty is not described as raw power exercised for its own sake but as the capacity to settle cases justly, which is what the sūrah argued in verse 10 when it insisted that God is never unjust to His servants.

Sūrah al-Zumar stages the judgment verse 56 announces, with every element in place: *“The earth will shine with the light of its Lord, the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, the prophets and the witnesses will be brought forward — and judgment will be passed on all with fairness. None will be wronged”* (39:69). Light, record, witnesses, fairness — the full apparatus of the *yaḥkumu baynahum*. Verse 56 states the act in two words; the staging shows what the two words contain, and the closing *none will be wronged* is verse 10's verdict executed.

**The Gardens of Bliss**

*Fa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fī jannāti al-naʿīm* — so those who believe and do sound deeds are in Gardens of bliss.

The word *naʿīm* is not the ordinary word for pleasure. It denotes ease, softness of life, the condition of a person for whom nothing is sharp or scarce. A garden of *naʿīm* is therefore the opposite of the Fire in a specific register: not heat against cool, but hardship against ease.

The reward is stated in the present tense — they *are* in it — even though the day being described is still future. The Qur'an does this regularly in its judgment scenes, and the effect is to make the outcome as good as settled.

Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn gives the state its shortest description, as the standing of the righteous: *“Surely the virtuous will be in bliss”* (83:22). *Naʿīm* alone — no garden named, no river, no furnishing. The word by itself carries the whole condition: ease without edge, life without sharpness or scarcity. Verse 56's *jannāti al-naʿīm* adds the place to the state, but the state is what the word promises, and the sūrah is content to let it stand nearly bare.

**"On That Day" Implies Other Days**

The phrase *yawmaʾidhin* — on that day — is a demonstrative of time, and the Qur'an uses it almost exclusively for the Resurrection and its scenes.

The implication is that the distribution of authority on other days is different, or at least appears so. In this life, authority is exercised by people who frequently have no right to it and who are rarely held to account for it. The Qur'an's language does not deny that they hold it; it denies that it is ultimately theirs, and it says that the day is coming when the appearance and the reality coincide.

That is why the claim is consoling rather than merely threatening. A person wronged by a judge, a ruler or an occupying power is being told that the arrangement is temporary and that the day on which it ends is a real date in the calendar.

Sūrah al-Rūm diagnoses the misreading the verse corrects: *“They ˹only˺ know the worldly affairs of this life, but are ˹totally˺ oblivious to the Hereafter”* (30:7). The outward known, the Hereafter missed — the distribution of authority in this life mistaken for the reality because it is all that is seen. Verse 56's *yawmaʾidhin* answers exactly that myopia: the apparent holders hold for other days, and the day coming ends the appearance by ending the division.

**What "Gardens" Implies About the Body**

The reward is a garden, and the sūrah has been consistent about what that means: a place, with water, shade and food, entered by people who have bodies.

This matters in a sūrah that opened by arguing for bodily resurrection from embryos and dry fields (22:5–7). If the resurrection were of souls only, a garden would be a strange promise, and the sūrah's whole first argument would have been beside the point. The reward and the argument stand together.

Sūrah Yā-Sīn pictures the bodily reward in a single line of activity: *“Indeed, on that Day the residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves”* (36:55). Busy — *shughl*, occupation, engagement — enjoying with bodies that eat, recline and converse. The promise presumes everything verses 5 to 7 argued: the raised dead are the same creatures that fell, and the garden is fitted to them rather than to abstractions. A resurrection of souls would need no garden; the sūrah promises one because it raised bodies.

**The Verse's Structure**

Three clauses, in order: whose the authority is, what is done with it, and where one group ends up. The sūrah has been building toward this formulation since verse 17, and it will restate it in the next verse for the other group.

What is notable is how little description the reward receives. The gardens of verse 23 had bracelets and silk; this one has only *naʿīm*. The emphasis in this verse falls entirely on the sovereignty clause, and the reward is named as a consequence rather than described as a place.

That economy is deliberate. The sūrah has already given the full description twice, at verse 14 and again at verse 23. By this point in the chapter, the reward does not need to be pictured; it needs to be located. Where it is located is inside a sovereignty that has no partners, and the verse's work is to say who holds the authority rather than what the gardens contain.

There is also a rhetorical effect worth noticing. The verse moves from the largest possible subject — who rules the universe on the final day — to a single clause about a group of people standing in a garden. The contrast is the point: the same sovereignty that settles the fate of every community is the one that attends to these particular believers.
