## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:275

> Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to madness by Satan's touch. That is because they say, "Trade is no different than interest." But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest. Whoever refrains—after having received warning from their Lord —may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to Allah. As for those who persist, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Devourers' Rising**

The sūrah's economy-legislation turns on its darkest case, and the verse opens with its eschatological image: the interest-eaters *will stand like those driven to madness by Satan's touch*. Al-Ṭabarī reads the standing as the rising from the graves — the resurrection of the ribā-devourer pictured as the epileptic's collapsing-and-rising, the body knocked down by an unseen hand and jerking upright, again and again. The ḥadīth literature the exegetes gather describes the Day's version of the devourer in harsher registers: raised choking, in a maddened state, or with a belly swollen to the size of a house, snakes visible inside it. The image's logic is retribution by resemblance: the trade that convulsed the debtor's household — debt compounding until possessions were swallowed — convulses its operator at the standing, and the touch attributed to Satan names the transaction's real counterparty. The verse's verb is worth holding: they *devour* — akala — the Qur'an's word for consumption that annihilates what it consumes, the same verb the orphan-wealth prohibition used (2:274's section's earlier fire, 2:210's devouring, 4:10's eating of the orphans' property): ribā is classified with the sūrah's consuming-sins, not its contracting-sins.

**Ribā: The Word and Its Two Kinds**

The verse's subject needs its definition, and the commentators supply it with unusual care because the usual translation misleads. Ribā means, in the root, increase, gain, growth — and "usury," understood as exorbitant interest or even any interest, is an imperfect rendering: not all interest is ribā, and not all ribā is interest. The Qur'an and the ḥadīth legislate two distinct transaction-types under the one name. The first is increase through deferment — ribā al-nasīʾah: a charge added to a debt at its maturity in exchange for time. The second is increase through surplus — ribā al-faḍl: an excess taken in the exchange of like commodities, gold for gold, or one grade of a staple for a better grade in unequal measure. The two types look nothing alike on the surface — one is credit's price, the other is a barter's imbalance — and the jurists spent centuries working out the single moral geometry they share; the verse's ruling covers both, which is why the tradition calls this passage one of Islamic law's most complex and multifaceted terrains.

**The Practice of the Age of Ignorance**

The deferment-type had a concrete pre-Islamic form the sources describe precisely. Imām Mālik records it in the Muwaṭṭaʾ: a man is owed a debt for a set term; when the term falls due he asks the debtor — will you settle, or will you increase? — pay now, or add to the debt and extend the time. If the debtor paid, the creditor took; if not, the debt grew and the term lengthened. The mechanism's engine was the doubling: the deferment-fee commonly doubled the principal across a year, and doubled again at the next deferment — the compounding the sūrah of ʿImrān legislates against directly: *Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over* (3:130). The end-state of the mechanism was total: a debtor could lose every possession he had to the creditor through doubling upon doubling. Read against this practice, the verse's devouring-verb is not metaphor but description — the loan that began as help ended as the systematic absorption of the borrower's estate, and the resurrection-image gives the absorption its final form.

**The Slogan Refuted**

The verse names the devourers' argument and answers it in one movement: *That is because they say, "Trade is no different than interest." But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest.* The slogan's form is an equivalence-claim — bayʿ, ordinary sale, is mithl ribā, the same thing as the increase — and the traditional glosses preserve its historical voice: the Meccan creditor-class's defense of the practice, sale is only money-for-goods and ribā is money-for-money, both are trade. The refutation declines the terrain entirely. It does not argue the equivalence false on economic grounds; it states the legislative fact: God has allowed the one and forbidden the other, and the permission-and-prohibition are themselves the difference. The Qur'an's wider treatment supplies the economics the slogan hides — the sūrah of the Byzantines states it flatly: *Whatever loans you give, seeking interest at the expense of people's wealth will not increase with Allah. But whatever charity you give, seeking the pleasure of Allah—it is they whose reward will be multiplied* (30:39). The increase taken from people's wealth does not increase where increases are actually recorded; the two transactions are not the same because they run in opposite directions — one toward the debtor's loss, one toward the needy's gain.

**The Six Commodities and Bilāl's Dates**

The surplus-type's legislation is the ḥadīth's famous commodity-rule: gold for gold, silver for silver, wheat for wheat, barley for barley, dates for dates, salt for salt — like for like, equal for equal, hand to hand; and whatever exceeds that is ribā. The rule's operative story is Bilāl's dates: the Companion brought the Prophet choice dates, and asked their origin, learned he had traded two measures of common dates for one measure of fine — the Prophet's verdict was immediate: that is precisely the ribā; return to the seller, sell the common dates for money, and with the money buy the fine ones. The two-step sale, grounding the exchange in priced market transactions, is the rule's remedy for the invisibility of the surplus: unequal like-for-like hides its excess, and the market's money-prices expose it. The jurists then debated the commodities' shared legal attribute — measurability, fungibility, storability, edibility, or some combination — because the attribute determines how far the six-item list extends across other goods; the debate is the fiqh of ribā al-faḍl's first chapter, and it exists because the verse legislates a type whose instances no list can exhaust.

**Risk, Uncertainty, and the Permitted Exceptions**

The ribā-rules live inside a wider transaction-ethics the commentators describe here: the law of gharar, the unlawful uncertainty whose root is semantically tied to deception. The governing principle — one may not sell what one does not possess, what whose attributes are unknown, or what does not yet exist — carries its ḥadīth-list of instances: the unborn calf in its mother's womb, the unmeasured milk still in the udder, spoils sold before distribution, the diver's future catch. But risk, the jurists note, is present in every honest transaction, and the law therefore admits exceptions where social benefit outweighs the uncertainty — above all the forward sale of crops, payment now for delivery in one, two, or three years, which Medina's agricultural economy required and which the verse-section ahead will legislate in its documentation-rules (2:282). The Prophet permitted the forward sale under strict conditions — known quantity, known quality, known term — precisely so that necessity's exception would not become ribā's disguise. The ribā-prohibition is thus not a blanket risk-aversion but a calibrated economy of permitted and forbidden uncertainty, and the two ribā-types are its two forbidden poles: time priced against the debtor, and surplus hidden inside the like-for-like.

**Whoever Refrains After the Warning**

The verse's amnesty-clause legislates the prohibition's arrival: *Whoever refrains — after having received warning from their Lord — may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to Allah.* The commentators' rulings give the clause its exact reach: what is cancelled is the outstanding — the increases accrued and not yet collected at the warning's arrival; what has already been consumed is not reclaimed. The Prophetic statement attached to the clause fixes the formula: all the ribā of the Age of Ignorance is remitted; you have your principal; you neither wrong nor are wronged (the declaration made at the Farewell Pilgrimage's sermon, weeks before the Prophet's death). *Their case is left to Allah* covers the amnesty's interior: the desisting's sincerity, the past's weight, the heart's history with the practice — the file closed to human jurisdiction and left open with God. The clause is the verse's mercy-statement, and its timing is the tradition's observation: this passage is said to be among the very last revelations — some count it the last of all, with 4:176 and 5:3 the rival candidates — which the exegetes cite to explain why the ḥadīth record on ribā's fine mechanics is comparatively thin: the law arrived late, and its elaboration fell to the jurists of the following generations.

**Those Who Persist**

The final sentence closes the alternative: *As for those who persist, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.* The going-back — the return to the practice after the warning — is what the residency answers, and the verse's structure makes the persistence, not the past, the operative fact: the pre-warning gains were remitted, and only the post-warning return carries the Fire. The residency-language is the sūrah's permanence-formula, and its assignment here places ribā's operators in the company of the verse-sequence's other final residents — the apostates of 2:217, the darkness-led of 2:257 — while its occasion, an economic practice, is the sequence's sharpest statement that the ledger's deepest entries are commercial. The devourer's portrait is now complete in three strokes: the convulsed rising, the slogan refuted by permission-and-prohibition, and the residency earned by persistence after amnesty. Between the amnesty and the residency stands the verse's only open question — *their case is left to Allah* — and the case it leaves is every reader's own: what he will do with the increase in his ledger, now that trading has been permitted and this forbidden.
