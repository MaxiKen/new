## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:4

> ˹It will be better˺ if you ˹wives˺ both turn to Allah in repentance, for your hearts have certainly faltered. But if you ˹continue to˺ collaborate against him, then ˹know that˺ Allah Himself is his Guardian. And Gabriel, the righteous believers, and the angels are ˹all˺ his supporters as well.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Left Deliberately Unfinished**

The Arabic opens with a conditional whose answer is missing: if you two turn to God in repentance — and then nothing. Grammarians call this an ellipsis, and the translation supplies the sense with *“˹It will be better˺”*, while the commentators complete it as "it will be accepted of you both." The omission is not a defect in the text. A conditional left hanging invites the hearer to finish it, and what a person supplies reveals what they expect.

The rhetorical effect is gentleness rather than vagueness. The alternative branch, in contrast, is spelled out in full and at length: if you continue to back one another against him, here is the list of who stands with him. One road is left open with its ending unstated; the other is described in detail and is plainly not an invitation. The asymmetry is the argument, and it is made without a single word of abuse toward either woman.

**"Your Hearts Have Faltered"**

The diagnosis is placed in the middle of the verse and given as a fact: *“for your hearts have certainly faltered”* (66:4). The Arabic verb *ṣaghat* means to lean, tilt or swerve from true, and the commentators read the inclination in two connected ways. One takes it as the hearts having leaned toward approving the prohibition of what God had made lawful — that is, toward the error the sūrah opened by correcting. The other reads it more generally as a deviation from what is right.

Both readings identify the same underlying problem, and it is subtler than disobedience. Nothing is alleged here about an action. What is named is a tilt, a heart that has come slightly off true and now finds a wrong position comfortable. That is how most serious failures begin, and it is also why the remedy prescribed is repentance rather than restitution — there is nothing to give back. The Qur'an's willingness to address the inner posture of two named women in the Prophet's own household, and preserve it for recitation, is also a statement about who is subject to this kind of scrutiny. Proximity to the Messenger conferred no exemption from having one's heart examined.

**Collaboration Described Without Euphemism**

*“But if you ˹continue to˺ collaborate against him”* (66:4) uses a verb of mutual assistance, *taẓāharā*, from the root for backing someone up, putting one's back against theirs. It describes two people forming a bloc. This is the element that raises a private disagreement into something the Qur'an treats seriously: not that one woman was upset, but that two coordinated.

The distinction is worth drawing out, since it has application far beyond this house. A single person's complaint remains a complaint and can be answered. An alliance changes the situation structurally, because each party now has an interest in maintaining the other's position and neither can retreat without losing face before the other. Grievances that would have dissolved on their own are preserved by the partnership that formed around them. The verse names the alliance rather than the grievance, and offers repentance as the way out — which, for two people who have bound themselves together in a position, is the hardest possible exit and the only clean one.

**The Support Listed Against the Alliance**

Then comes the answer to the bloc, and its scale is extraordinary for a domestic dispute: *“then ˹know that˺ Allah Himself is his Guardian. And Gabriel, the righteous believers, and the angels are ˹all˺ his supporters as well”* (66:4). Four names, ascending and then widening. God is named first as *mawlā*, the same title given to the believers in the previous sūrah of this pair, and the point of listing anyone after Him is not that He needs help. The point is the disparity being set out.

The rhetorical strategy resembles what happens elsewhere when a small human coalition is confronted with what actually stands on the other side. Two people had made an arrangement between themselves; the reply enumerates the Messenger's backing in a sentence that runs from the Lord of the worlds through the greatest of the angels and out to the whole body of the righteous. Nothing in the verse threatens either woman with a penalty. It simply describes the field, and leaves the arithmetic to them.

**Who the Righteous Believers Are**

The phrase *ṣāliḥ al-muʾminīn* — rendered *“the righteous believers”* (66:4) — is grammatically singular in form and has attracted a long history of identification. Some commentators take it as a collective, meaning the best of the believers generally. Others name Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, which carries a certain edge, since they were the fathers of the two women being addressed. In Shia commentary the phrase is referred to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, supported by a report that the Prophet ﷺ took ʿAlī by the hand and identified him with the words of this verse.

The collective reading is the most defensible on the wording alone, since the singular form is regularly used for a class in Arabic and the verse is already listing categories rather than individuals. The identification with the two fathers is exegetically attractive for a different reason: it would mean that the two women's own families stood on the Prophet's side of the dispute, and the reports about ʿUmar's reaction when he learned what his daughter had done support exactly that. Whichever reading is preferred, the function of the phrase in the sentence is unaffected. Between Gabriel and the angels, the verse inserts human beings — ordinary believers, whose loyalty is counted alongside the support of heaven.
