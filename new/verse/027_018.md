## Sūrah an-Naml 27:18

> And when they came across a valley of ants, an ant warned, “O ants! Go quickly into your homes so Solomon and his armies do not crush you, unknowingly.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter's Title Comes From an Insect**

This is the verse that names the sūrah. Ninety-three verses covering Mūsā, Sulaymān, the queen of Sheba, Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ and the Day of Resurrection are called *an-Naml*, the Ants, after a single episode lasting two verses. The naming is not arbitrary in a chapter whose subject is who notices what.

Everything else in the surrounding passage is large. An army of three species, a king given a share of all things, a throne carried across a desert. Set against that, an ant issuing a warning is nothing, and the chapter takes its name from it. Al-Qurṭubī observed that Sulaymān's greatness shows in his attention to the smallest member of his realm, and the title makes the same point structurally. A reader who remembers only the marvels of this chapter has remembered the wrong things; the Qur'an has labelled it after the insect.

**A Warning That Is Competently Made**

Read the ant's speech as a piece of communication and it is impressively constructed. She addresses her audience, states the action required, specifies the destination, names the threat by its source, and supplies the reason the threat is not malicious. Five elements in one sentence, delivered under time pressure.

The command is *udkhulū masākinakum* — enter your dwellings. Not scatter, not flee: go in, because a colony's safety is underground and dispersal in open ground would be worse than useless. The threat is named as *Sulaymān wa junūduhu*, the king and his forces, which is accurate identification rather than panic. What makes the speech remarkable is its final clause, and it is the clause a frightened creature would omit. She tells them the army will not know. That is an exculpation offered to the very force about to crush them, issued by the intended victim, in the middle of an evacuation order. Nothing in the situation required it.

**"Unknowingly" — the Ant Defends the King**

Al-Rāzī's reading of that last word is the standard one and is hard to improve. The ant says *wa hum lā yashʿurūn* — while they are unaware — because she assumes that if a prophet like Sulaymān knew his column was about to destroy a colony, he would not do it. Her warning contains a judgment about his character, and it is a favourable one.

That judgment is the reason the episode functions as it does. Had she said *so Sulaymān does not crush you*, full stop, the sentence would be a warning about a tyrant. The added clause converts it into a warning about an accident, and accidents are the characteristic danger of large, well-run organisations. The army was described in the previous verse as perfectly organised, and organisation is exactly what produces this hazard: a disciplined column moves steadily and does not stop to look down. The ant has diagnosed the situation with precision. The threat to her people is not malice from the top but momentum in the middle, and she says so.

**Were They Ants, and Did She Have a Name?**

Two side-questions occupied the classical commentators, and they are worth reporting with their grounds. Some, cited by al-Qurṭubī and al-Thaʿlabī, proposed that these ants were winged, which would classify them as *ṭayr* — flying things — and so bring them under the language of birds Sulaymān had been taught. The suggestion is a harmonisation, designed to explain how he understood a creature outside the one category the previous verse specified.

It is unnecessary. Sulaymān's gift is described in verse 16 as the speech of birds, but the chapter never says that was the limit of what he could hear, and a king given a share of all things is not obviously confined to one taxon. The second question is stranger: several commentators transmit a personal name for this ant. Al-Qurṭubī records the reports and also records the objection, that there is no conceivable way anyone could know such a detail. The objection is correct and should be stated as the conclusion. **An Ant Colony Is a Community With Standing**

Behind this episode lies a principle the Qur'an states elsewhere and the Prophet ﷺ applied directly to ants. Every animal group is described in the Qur'an as a community like human communities, and that description carries weight.

Ants in particular were given protection in Islamic law on this basis. The jurists list them among the creatures that may not be killed without cause, and the prohibition is derived from the incident below rather than from any general principle of kindness.

The clearest illustration is a report Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both cite on this passage. Abū Hurayrah related that the Prophet ﷺ said an ant bit one of the prophets, and he ordered the ants' colony burned; God then revealed to him: *because one ant bit you, you have burned a nation from among the nations that glorify Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3019; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2241). Another sound narration of the same incident gives the rebuke as: could you not have burned one ant? The report is unambiguous about the principle. A colony is an *ummah* that praises God, and destroying it in retaliation for an insect's bite was a wrong serious enough to warrant direct revelation. Read against that, this verse's ant is not a charming detail. She is a member of a community with standing before God, warning her people about a danger she does not blame anyone for. What Sulaymān did when he heard her is reported next.
