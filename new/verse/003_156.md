## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:156

> O believers! Do not be like the unfaithful who say about their brothers who travel throughout the land or engage in battle, “If they had stayed with us, they would not have died or been killed.” Allah makes such thinking a cause of agony in their hearts. It is Allah who gives life and causes death. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prohibition and the Model Rejected**

The verse addresses the believers and forbids them to resemble those who deny in what they say about their dead. The comparison is the point of the sentence: the community is shown a way of speaking about loss and told that it does not belong to people of faith.

The Book often teaches by means of such contrasts, and the instruction here is about speech rather than about belief. What the community is told not to imitate is a habit of recording outcomes as though human decisions had controlled them, and the habit is presented as characteristic of the people who have not accepted the message.


The commentators identified the speakers of the sentence in several ways. Some held that the words came from the disbelievers in general, some that they came from the hypocrites, and one view assigns them to a particular leader among the hypocrites who is mentioned earlier in the sūrah's narrative.

The verse does not settle the question, and its wording covers the conduct rather than the names. The community is taught to recognise the sentiment wherever it appears, which is why the commentators could differ about the occasion without disagreeing about the lesson.

**The Claim About Staying Behind**

The claim the verse reports is that had the dead stayed at home they would not have died or been killed. The sentence presents death as the consequence of a decision to travel or to go out on campaign, and it treats the decision as the thing that could have been made differently.

The Book's answer is that the arrangement belongs elsewhere. Al-Rāzī put the point by saying that the verse restates what the earlier passage already established: human beings should never forget that God, not human agency, determines life and death. The claim in the verse is not merely mistaken; it takes away the one fact that gives a person security when he risks his life in a good cause.

**What the Words Were Spoken About**

Al-Zamakhsharī noted that the phrase about the brothers of the speakers carries the sense of speaking on account of them, so that the words were said because of what had happened to those men. The grief was real, and the remark was the shape it took.

That reading makes the verse a lesson about how grief expresses itself. The Book does not reproach the sorrow; it reproaches the analysis that turns the dead into victims of their own decision. The community is being taught to hold its losses without adopting a framework in which caution would have preserved anyone.

**Regret Made in the Hearts**

The verse states that God made this a source of regret in their hearts, and the commentators offered several explanations. One holds that the regret belongs to those who spoke, and that it is a fitting recompense for words that were meant to discourage others. Al-Rāzī noted that the regret may also come on the Day of Judgment for having said such things at all.

Another reading holds that the purpose was to create doubt in the hearts of weaker believers, so that the community's faith would be tested by the remarks it heard. The verse leaves room for both, and the difference between them concerns who is being corrected rather than what the correction is about.

**God Gives Life and Causes Death**

The sentence that answers the claim is a statement of divine authority over life and death, and it is the same statement that the passage has made twice already. The community was told that no soul dies except by God's permission at an appointed term, and it was told that those destined for death would have gone out to the places where they were to die.

The repetition is deliberate. The community had just heard the fatalistic argument, and the Book answers it by returning to the fact it had already established. What changes between the two statements is the context: what was first a reminder about a fixed term becomes here a response to a claim about the dead.

**The Spiritual Reading of the Sentence**

Al-Rāzī records a further explanation of the statement, according to which God gives life to the hearts of those close to Him and causes the hearts of His enemies to die. On this reading the sentence is about spiritual life and death as well as bodily.

The reading widens the verse's application without displacing its plain sense. The community is told that the one who controls the physical outcome also controls the inner outcome, and that the two are governed by a single authority. The claim made by the speakers in the verse was a claim about that authority, and the answer given restores it to its owner.

**The Closing Statement About Seeing**

The verse ends by stating that God sees what the community does. The sentence returns to the theme that appeared at the end of the passage about the retreat, and it places the community's conduct under the same observation.

The Book's statements about its knowledge are frequently placed at the end of an instruction, and the placement gives them a practical force. The community's speech about its dead, its conduct on the battlefield, and its treatment of the people who failed are all seen by the same God, and the sentence reminds the readers of the verse that no part of their behaviour is outside the account.
