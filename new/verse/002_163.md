## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:163

> Your God is ˹only˺ One God. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Declaration of Oneness**

The verse states that God is one, and it does so with the general noun for divinity rather than with the personal name that the sūrah uses elsewhere. The sentence then states that there is no god but Him and closes with two names, the compassionate and the merciful.

The community's scholars noted that the verse expresses the central claim of the religion in the fewest words the Book uses anywhere, and they connected it with the passage that follows, which calls on people to worship the One who has provided for them. The declaration is placed between the account of the punishment of those who disbelieve and the description of the signs in creation, and it gives the reader the subject of both.

**The General Noun and the Divine Name**

The word rendered as god is the general Arabic noun for a deity, and the scholars of the language explained that its use here establishes the claim at the level of the concept rather than only at that of a name. What is being asserted is that the category of divinity has one member, and the sentence then states that no being other than He belongs to it.

The personal name of God appears in the sūrah's opening verses and throughout the Book, and the community's teachers explained that the two words are related in the way that a name is related to what it names. What the reader receives from the sentence is a statement about God as the one who is worshipped, and the names at the end of the verse describe the manner in which He deals with His creation.

**The Meaning of One**

The commentators explained the term for one as one who is unique, who is not composed of parts, and who has no equal or likeness, and they contrasted it with the kind of oneness that can be described of created things. A number can be one and still be divided or compared, and the Book's statement excludes those possibilities.

The word's grammar in the sentence reinforces the point, since the predicate precedes its subject in the Arabic and gives the statement the force of an exclusive claim. What the community received from the verse is a formula that leaves no room for an associate, and the sūrah's earlier refutations of those who took rivals beside God supply the context in which the formula is understood.

**Compassion and Mercy**

The two names that close the sentence are among those with which the sūrah opens, and the community's teachers explained the first as describing the mercy that is general and the second as describing the mercy that is directed at the believers. The pairing appears in the Book's opening chapter and in the verses that describe God as the merciful and the compassionate.

The placement of the names after the statement of oneness shows that the one God is not described to the reader only as a power that judges. What the Book presents is a single deity whose relation to the creation is characterised by compassion and mercy, and the sentence gives the community both the claim about His nature and the description of how He acts.

**The Verse's Place in the Passage**

The sentence follows the account of those who died in disbelief and precedes the passage that sets out the signs of God in the creation. The Book has been describing the consequences of concealing the truth, and it now states who the truth concerns.

The arrangement gives the reader a transition from a subject of warning to a subject of contemplation, and the passage that follows lists phenomena that any person can observe. What the Book does is to supply the conclusion before it supplies the evidence, and the reader who continues into the following verses receives the account of the creation as the demonstration of the sentence he has just read.

The sentence stands after the account of those who died in disbelief and before the list of signs that runs through the following verse, and the arrangement gives the reader the conclusion before the evidence. The Book has been dealing with the concealment of knowledge and with the fate of those who conceal it, and it now states what the concealed truth concerns. What follows is a description of the heavens, the earth, the alternation of day and night, and the rest of the phenomena the community can observe, so that the reader meets the demonstration after the claim.

**The Reports About the Verse**

The verse is among the most frequently recited in the Book, and the community's teachers noted its use in the daily prayers and in the occasions of remembrance. The reports describe the Prophet ﷺ reciting the declaration of oneness and the verse that follows it together in the night prayer, and the community has preserved the pairing in its practice.

The reports also describe the verse being used in teaching and in the instruction of new believers, since it summarises what the community believes about God in a form that can be memorised and repeated. What the passage offers the reader is therefore a statement that has been carried in the community's worship and instruction, and its brevity is part of what made that possible.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes that there is one God, that no other being shares that status, and that the one God is described by compassion and mercy. The reader is given the claim that the whole sūrah will support, and the passage that follows sets out the signs by which the claim is established.

The community’s teachers treated the sentence as the statement of what the Book as a whole is about, since every command and prohibition in it is addressed to people who have accepted it. The verse names the one who is worshipped, denies that the status belongs to any other, and describes Him with the two names the sūrah has already used. The reader is given a complete summary in a single sentence, and the passage that follows supplies the evidence for the claim in the form of the creation itself.
