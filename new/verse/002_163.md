## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:163

> Your God is ˹only˺ One God. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Creed Between the Curse and the Signs**

After four verses of curse, exception, and sentence, the sūrah stops and states the ground of everything it has just legislated and threatened: *your God is one God*. The placement is architectural. The concealment that drew the curse, the repentance that opened the door, the abiding that closed the case — all of it presupposes an answer to the question of who God is and how many there are, and the passage now supplies the answer in the shortest complete creed the Qur'an contains. The verse reads like the still point of the section: no narrative, no occasion, no addressee beyond the possessive *your* — just the statement itself, delivered after the fires have been described so that the reader understands what the statement is worth. A creed recited in comfort is a formula; a creed recited between the curse and the signs is a position taken with full knowledge of both consequences.

**Ilāh, Not Allāh: The Grammar of the Claim**

The verse's wording was noted closely by the exegetes for a grammatical reason with theological consequences: in all three of its mentions of deity, the word used is *ilāh* — the common noun for a god, a divinity, anything worshipped — rather than *Allāh*, the supreme personal Name. *Your ilāh is one ilāh; there is no ilāh except Him.* The choice enacts the argument. To say "Allah is one" could be heard as a claim about one deity among others, a tribal statement about the God of the Arabs or of Abraham's line. To say "no *ilāh* except Him" is to sweep the entire category of the divine — every object any people has ever worshipped or could worship — and then to empty it, leaving one. The negation of the generic noun is what makes the confession exhaustive: it does not rank Allah above other gods; it denies that the other gods exist as gods at all. The Name that remains after the category is emptied is referred to by pronoun — *except Him* — the personal standing where the categorical ended.

**Wāḥid: One in a Sense That Excludes Parts**

Al-Ṭabarī unpacked the word for one — *wāḥid* — as carrying more than arithmetic: the unique, the one not made up of parts, the one without equal or like. Each of the three senses does separate work. Unique: there is no second of His kind, so no comparison between Him and anything is comparison between peers. Without parts: He is not composite, not divisible, not assembled — which excludes every genealogy of the gods, every pantheon's family tree, every theology that makes divinity a substance shared out among persons or emanations. Without equal or like: nothing in existence answers to Him as a counterpart, which is why the sūrah's later Throne verse will ask, rhetorically, whether any names or beings can be produced as His partners. The oneness confessed here is therefore dense — numerical, structural, and comparative at once — and the Qur'an's shortest sūrah will spend four verses restating exactly these three: One and Indivisible, the Sustainer needed by all, never begetting nor begotten, and with none comparable to Him (112:1–4). This verse is that sūrah in miniature, embedded in the middle of the longest one.

**The Two Mercies at the End**

The creed closes with two names: *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* — the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful. Their position after the severity of the preceding verses is the verse's pastoral genius. The confession could have ended with power — the One is also the Omnipotent, and the curse-verses had just displayed His authority. Instead the defining attributes appended to the oneness are the two intensives of mercy: *Raḥmān*, mercy as a total disposition, vast and encompassing; *Raḥīm*, mercy as an act, particular and repeated. The effect on the creed is decisive: the God whose oneness is confessed is not a bare unity but a unity whose innermost published character is compassion. The sinner who passed through 2:160's first-person acceptance arrives here to find that acceptance built into the Name; the reader who flinched at 2:162's unlightened punishment learns that the sentence flowed from a mercy that was refused to its limit, not from a deity indifferent to its subjects. Monotheism in this verse is not only the claim that God is one; it is the claim about what the One is like — and the like is mercy wearing two intensities.

**Among the Most Recited of Verses**

The tradition recognized the verse's density by its use: it stands among the most often recited verses of the Qur'an, quoted in prayer, in dhikr, and on the lips of the dying. Its confession has a history of confrontation attached to it. The sūrah of the Ranks records the other reception of the same sentence: *"For whenever it was said to them ˹in the world˺, 'There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah,' they acted arrogantly"* (37:35) — the creed that this verse states in calm is the creed whose first hearing provoked the Meccan elite's scorn, because its exclusivity dethroned every ancestral god at once. The two verses frame the confession's worldly fate: accepted by the believers as the summary of everything, refused by the arrogant as an intolerable narrowing. And within this sūrah the creed looks forward as well — to the Throne verse, which will open with the same formula of divinity and build the whole architecture of God's knowledge and power upon it, and to the seven signs of the verse that follows immediately, which will exhibit the one God's handwriting across creation, from the alternation of night and day to the clouds held between the sky and the earth.

**The Axis of the Sūrah**

This verse is the pivot on which the sūrah's argument turns. Everything before it has been preparing a people — the creed confessed (2:136), the dyeing of God (2:138), the disengagement from disputes about Him (2:139), the truth from the Lord (2:147) — and everything after it will be demonstrating the confession: the signs in creation (2:164), the analysis of love and its misplacements (2:165), the food laws that protect the confession from inherited falsehood (2:168–173), the legislative arc running to the Throne verse and beyond. The curse-verses, for their part, now read as the confession's negative space: what happens to those who hide it, die against it, and are sentenced under it. A single sentence — your God is one God, no god but Him, the Compassionate, the Merciful — is thus the thesis the sūrah spends its length defending, illustrating, and legislating around. The reader who holds this verse holds the key to every section that follows, because every section that follows is either a proof of the oneness, a consequence of it, or a law for those who confess it.
