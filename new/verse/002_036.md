## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:36

> But Satan deceived them—leading to their fall from the ˹blissful˺ state they were in, and We said, “Descend from the heavens ˹to the earth˺ as enemies to each other. You will find in the earth a residence and provision for your appointed stay.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Fall Traced to a Single Act of Deception**

The sentence begins with the cause. Satan caused them to slip, and the Arabic verb describes a foot losing its hold rather than a wall being pushed down. The Book had just recorded a command with a limit; here it records the movement across that limit, and it attributes the movement to an outside agent without removing the two who moved.

The Qur'an tells this part of the story in more detail in two other places. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf supplies the whisperer's words and his oath: *“Your Lord has forbidden this tree to you only to prevent you from becoming angels or immortals.”* (7:20), followed by *“And he swore to them, ‘I am truly your sincere advisor.’”* (7:21). Sūrah Ṭā Hā adds the promise of a kingdom: he offered them *“the Tree of Everlastingness and a kingdom that never decays”* (20:120). The sūrah of al-Baqarah compresses the whole episode into the verb.

**The Method of the Liar: Oaths and Promises**

The Book records what the tempter actually said, and the content is instructive. He did not tell them that obedience was wrong or that God was absent; he told them that the prohibition concealed something worth having, and he swore an oath about his own sincerity. Every element of the pitch is a distortion of something true — angels do exist, immortality is real — and the whole is delivered under the guarantee of a false oath.

That combination is what the Book means by deception, and it warns human beings about it from the beginning of their history. The sūrah's later portrait of the hypocrites keeps the same mechanics in view, where men use oaths as a shield and elegant words to cover their intentions. What changes across the Book is the scale of the audience, not the method of the argument.

**"Descend, Enemies to One Another"**

The command to descend is addressed in the plural, and the commentators took the addressees to include Adam, his wife, and the one who had deceived them, with all their descendants implied. The plural matters because it announces a condition rather than an eviction: from this point, the relationship between human beings and the deceiver is one of declared enmity, and the Qur'an keeps that word for Iblīs consistently.

The same command appears in three places with nearly identical wording, and it is paired in each with the promise of guidance to come. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records it after the exposure of their nakedness: *“Descend as enemies to each other. You will find in the earth a residence and provision for your appointed stay.”* (7:24), and Sūrah Ṭā Hā pairs the descent with a promise that whoever follows God's guidance will not go astray or fall into misery (20:123). The Book's account of the expulsion is therefore also an account of a commission.

**What the Fall Is Not**

This verse is not the story of a human nature corrupted in its essence by an inherited fault. The Qur'an does not carry the doctrine of original sin, and the verses that follow show Adam receiving words from his Lord and being forgiven, with the offence ending there for him. What propagates from this scene is the conditions of earthly life and the enmity of the deceiver, not guilt for an act somebody else committed.

The Book makes the same point about responsibility throughout: nothing is carried by one person for another, and every soul answers for what it has done. The fall is therefore a beginning in the geographical sense — the descent to a place where testing occurs — and a warning in the moral sense, since the method that worked in the Garden is still in circulation.

**"A Residence and Provision for an Appointed Stay"**

The terms of the descent are stated in two nouns. The earth will be a place of settlement, and the arrangement includes provision, which echoes the earlier statement that the earth was furnished for human beings before their arrival. The Book does not describe the earth as a prison; it describes it as a dwelling with supplies, and it adds a term limit.

The Arabic phrase rendered *appointed stay* names a fixed period, whether that period is a person's life or the whole span of human history on the earth. The Book uses the same vocabulary when it describes everything in the world as measured, and the sūrah's earlier passage on the appointment of a deputy said the same about the earth's provision. The human being is placed in a working environment with a deadline attached, and the deadline is stated to him from the start.

**Earth as the Place Where the Commission Runs**

The scene in heaven is finished, and the scene on earth begins. Everything the sūrah has described up to this point — the appointment of a representative, the demonstration of knowledge, the single prohibition, the fall — now takes place in the world that the reader actually inhabits, and the Book's account of that world will keep returning to the enmity announced here.

The verses that follow complete the sequence with forgiveness and guidance, so that the descent is not the last word of the passage. What the reader carries from this verse is the arrangement: a dwelling, a provision, a term, and an enemy — and a Book that will describe how to live inside all four. The dwelling, the provision, and the term are named together in the sentence, and the sūrah's later verses return to each of them as the account of the human life unfolds.
