## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:84

> And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant that you would neither shed each other’s blood nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge and bore witness.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Covenant of Blood and Homes**

The recital moves from the universal charter to a specific bond, and the specificity sharpens the charge. After the five terms of verse 83 — worship, kindness, speech, prayer, alms — this verse quotes a covenant in two prohibitions: *that you would neither shed each other's blood nor expel each other from their homes*. The two protected goods are the most elementary a community possesses: life and dwelling, the person and the place the person belongs to. And the covenant's grammar makes both goods reflexive — *each other's* blood, *each other's* homes — so that the crimes it forbids are not crimes against strangers but against the self extended: the brother's blood is your blood, the neighbor's house is continuous with your own. The exegetes read the verse as continuing the account of the earlier Israelites, the covenant of the community's founding laid beside the charter of the previous verse — and the pairing is deliberate in its architecture. Verse 83 bound the community upward and outward, to Allah and to people; verse 84 binds it inward, to its own members, in the two things no member may take from another. A people whose charter is perfect but whose internal bond is broken has religion without community, and the recital is about to document exactly that condition — which is why this verse first fixes the bond's terms and, in its second half, its ratification.

**You Gave Your Pledge and Bore Witness**

The verse ends where a contract ends: *you gave your pledge and bore witness*. The double formula is legal language, and its doubling is the point — the covenant was not merely received but confirmed, and confirmed twice over. The pledge is the community's own act of binding itself; the bearing of witness, in al-Rāzī's and al-Ṭabarī's readings, is either direct attestation — we heard, we accept, we testify to this bond — or presence itself, the standing of the whole group at the event as its witnesses. Either way the effect is evidentiary: a covenant witnessed cannot later be disowned, because the disowning party is on the witness list. The recital's method of address presses the point home. *Your* covenant — the second person runs through the sentence, so that the pledge belongs to the community as a present fact rather than an ancestral event, and every generation that says *we are the people of that covenant* re-signs the witness roll in the act of saying it. This is the verse's quiet machinery for everything that follows: the coming indictment will not be arguing that the bond was unclear or unwitnessed. The terms were elementary — do not kill each other, do not expel each other — the pledge was given, the witnesses were the pledgers. What remains to be examined is conduct, and conduct against a witnessed covenant admits no defense of ignorance.

**The Group and the Individual: Two Ways to Inherit a Crime**

The verse's communal address raises the question the tradition faced directly: how can a later generation be bound, or indicted, by a covenant its ancestors ratified? The exegetical discussion distinguishes two inheritances. The first is passive implication — the way children suffer from parents' crimes, the way a household pays for its head's debts: a burden carried without choice. The second is active participation — and here the principle turns moral: where individuals glory in a group identity, holding its honors as extensions of the self, they expose themselves to the crimes attached to that identity as well. The boast and the burden are one package; a people that says *we are the chosen* has claimed the choosing and the chosen's conduct together. This is the mechanism by which the "sins of the fathers" pass justly to later generations — not by blood but by identification: the descendant who accepts the ancestral glory without repudiating the ancestral crime ratifies the crime, and his ratification is real because his acceptance of the glory was real. The verse's second-person grammar operates inside this logic. *We took your covenant* — the address treats the community across its generations as a single moral person, and the treatment is fair on the community's own premises: a people that remembers its covenant as *ours* has already merged its account with the covenant's.

**The Inheritance of Character**

Beneath the legal mechanism runs a social one, and the exegetes trace it plainly: people take the shape of the society around them. A generation that is malicious tends to raise children who copy its malice, because the child's moral vocabulary is the environment's — imitation transmits the vices of fathers as reliably as their surnames, and the Qur'an's standing rebuke of those who argue from ancestral practice (*we found our fathers doing this*) is aimed at exactly this transmission taken as justification. The converse holds with equal force: to be raised among truth-tellers, inside an environment that enshrines compassion and hands it forward, is a better inheritance than any estate — environments are the vessels in which a community's character crosses generations. The verse's covenant of blood and homes is therefore not only a prohibition but a piece of social engineering: a community that keeps it raises children for whom fratricide and expulsion are unthinkable, and a community that breaks it raises children fluent in both. The tragedy the recital documents is the second case running at scale — a people whose charter forbade shedding each other's blood, whose witness roll was signed, and whose later generations inherited not the charter's restraint but its violations, performing them with the confidence of custom. The individual's escape from that inheritance is named in the tradition and is narrow but real: repentance for one's own sins and a life of active virtue — the deliberate re-weaving of character against the received pattern.

**The Fire Next Door**

The discussion of communal responsibility keeps one sober limit in view, and the limit is the verse's darkest realism. Even the person who repudiates his community's crimes — who deplores them while still belonging, who keeps the covenant alone among covenant-breakers — may not escape the effects of the communal sin, any more than a man escapes the blaze that has taken his neighbor's house. The image is exact: communal corruption is a fire, and fires do not check title deeds at the property line. The righteous remnant of verse 83 — the *few* excepted from the turning away — lives inside this hazard: their exception saves their standing before Allah, but not necessarily their houses, their city, or their century. The tradition balances the bleakness with its own counterweight: a culture sunk in moral darkness can be rescued by an infusion of virtue — saints and sages in ordinary ages, prophets in the ages of revelation — though the average community retains the qualities of its founders rather than discarding them, and rescue is the exception history records rather than its rule. The two facts together give the verse its full pastoral register. Belonging to a good community is a protection no individual virtue fully replaces; belonging to a corrupt one is a hazard no individual virtue fully escapes; and the covenant of blood and homes is addressed to the community precisely because the community is the unit that either keeps its members safe or burns their houses down around them, whatever any single member intended.

**The Present-Tense You**

The verse's grammar carries its permanent application, and the exegetes mark the feature deliberately: the Qur'an addresses past and future generations in the present tense — *We took your covenant* — folding every generation of the community into the moment of ratification. The you of verse 84 is thus a standing address. Every community that claims a revealed charter has taken the covenant of blood and homes; every community whose members kill each other and expel each other has broken it with its pledge fresh and its witness list intact. The first hearers in Madina recognized the address in its historical form — the tribes and alliances of their own city about to be held against the covenant's terms — and every later hearer inherits the recognition in his own form: the factionalism that calls itself fidelity, the purges that call themselves discipline, the exile of the inconvenient that calls itself protection of the community. The two prohibitions are elementary because they are permanent — life and home are what any group's members may never take from each other, whatever the group's grievances — and the ratification clause is what makes the indictment inescapable: you gave your pledge, and you bore witness. The recital files the bond before filing its violation because the order matters to justice: no one will be shown the crime before being shown the signature. The signature is on the page, in the second person, witnessed — and the audit that follows reads conduct against it, verse by verse.
