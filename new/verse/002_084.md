## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:84

> And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant that you would neither shed each other’s blood nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge and bore witness.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Clause of the Same Agreement**

The sūrah returns to the covenant with a different set of terms. The previous verse listed what the community was to do; this one lists what it was forbidden to do to itself. Both are introduced with the command to remember, and both end with evidence that the agreement had been accepted.

The Book's method in this passage is to build the case in layers. It first established the positive obligations, then the prohibition on internal violence, and it will then report the violation in detail. Each step narrows the gap between what the community claimed about itself and what the record shows. The two lists cover different ground: what the community owed to God and to its weakest members, then what it owed to one another simply by belonging to the same people. The second list requires no shared belief to be understood, which is how the Book can hold a community to it on the strength of its own agreement.

**Bloodshed Within a Community**

The prohibition is not about war with an outside enemy. The verse speaks of shedding one another's blood, and the wording identifies the victims and the perpetrators as members of the same people. What is being forbidden is civil conflict, and the covenant places the life of a member of the community under the protection of the agreement rather than of the tribe.

The Book treats this category of killing as a distinct offence. Elsewhere it states that whoever kills a believer deliberately has a punishment awaiting him from God, and it treats the killing of a person without lawful cause as a crime against the whole of humanity (5:32). The list in this verse shows what a community's own commitments look like when written out in full. Elsewhere the Book treats the killing of a single person as an offence against the whole of humanity, and the wording is sterner still when the victim belongs to the same covenant. The chapter of the women records the community's own pledge on this point, so the prohibition in this verse was not a new demand invented for the occasion.

**Expulsion Treated as a Form of Killing**

The second prohibition extends the first: not to expel one another from their homes. The Book pairs the two offences consistently, here and in the verse that reports the violation, and the pairing shows how it weighs dispossession. A person driven from his land with his family loses what a killed man loses, with the difference that he lives to watch it.

The pairing appears again in the sūrah's account of the punishment of a people who drove others from their homes, and the Book applies its language about oppression to the act of dispossession itself. In this covenant the two clauses stand side by side as a single undertaking, and the community's failure is recorded against both. The association of the two offences outlived the occasion. The Book treats the protection of life and the security of residence as parts of a single guarantee, which is why the following sentence can describe a people shedding blood and driving out families in one breath and treat both as the same breach.

**They Gave Their Pledge and Bore Witness**

The verse closes by recording that the community consented to the terms and confirmed them. The two verbs are legal in flavour: a pledge freely given, and testimony offered by the ones who had agreed. The record is therefore not an outsider's account but the community's own acknowledgment.

The Book returns to this point repeatedly in the passage because it removes the defence of ignorance. A community that gave its own word and then acted against it is not being accused of a mistake; it is being held to an undertaking whose terms it fully understood. The same standard is applied to the listeners of the revelation when the sūrah turns to the Muslims later in the chapter. Legally the two verbs do different work: the first says the community accepted the terms, and the second says it can be called to account for them. The charges in these verses are always of that kind, resting on what a community acknowledged rather than on what an outside party alleges about it.

**The Parallel With the Book's Other Covenants**

The Qur'an records covenants with other communities in similar terms, and the comparison is instructive. The chapter of the table describes an agreement taken from the children of Israel with promises of God's support attached, and the chapter of the women summarizes the same history in a single verse about their pledge and their subsequent breaking of it.

The Book's use of these accounts is not archival. When it reports an earlier community's covenant and its failure, the report is directed at the community reading it, and the sequence of commands and prohibitions in this passage is followed by warnings addressed directly to the believers. The sūrah's later verses will tell the Muslims to keep their own agreements and not to make their oaths a means of deceiving one another.

**Why the Two Clauses Are Stated Before the Breach**

The order of the verses is the sūrah's argument. If the breach had come first, the reader would judge the community on the strength of an accusation. Coming after the terms, the breach is measured against the community's own undertaking, and the reader is placed in a position to see how a people can hold a written covenant and violate it in practice.

That position is what the next verse exploits. It addresses the same community in the present tense, describing the conduct that contradicts what they have just been reminded they promised, and it asks them how they reconcile the two. The sūrah builds the question carefully so that its force does not depend on an orator's skill. Something similar happens later when the sūrah turns to the Muslim community, where the commands come before the warnings and the warnings before the reminder of what is at stake. The pattern is a method of teaching, and in this passage it also serves an argument: the community is shown what it promised before it is shown what it did.

**A Covenant That Concerns the Community Itself**

The terms in this verse are unusual among the Book's commands in that they protect the community against itself. No foreign threat is mentioned and no external enemy is named. The undertaking is to refrain from violence inside the household of the covenant, and the Book treats the violation of that internal limit as the clearest evidence of what a people's religion has become in practice.

**An Undertaking Containing Its Own Enforcement**

Covenants in the Book are usually described as obligations owed to God, and this one adds a feature that makes it unusual. Its terms protect the community from itself, and their enforcement depends on the members of that community. No outside power is named in the verse to punish a man who kills his own cousin, and no foreign enemy can be blamed for the expulsion of a family from its home. The offences are described as things done to one another, which means the damage and the responsibility stay inside the household of the agreement. That is why the following verse addresses the community in the present tense and simply describes what they are doing, naming the acts by which the covenant was broken rather than accusing them of breaking it in the abstract.

**Why the Terms Sound Like a Legal Document**

The vocabulary of the verse is transactional. A covenant is taken, a pledge is given, and witnesses are named, so the community's commitment is recorded in the same forms that governed agreements between tribes. The Book adopts those forms deliberately, because it is describing an undertaking that was publicly known and publicly accepted rather than a private understanding. What the phrasing rules out is the possibility that a later generation could treat the covenant as a matter of interpretation. Its terms were stated, its acceptance was witnessed, and the community that accepted it did so in the plainest available language. The Book's case against the community therefore does not rest on an argument about intention. It rest on what was agreed and what was done, in that order.
