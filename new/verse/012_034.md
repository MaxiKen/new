## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:34

> So his Lord responded to him, turning their cunning away from him. Surely He is the All-Hearing, AllKnowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Uses the Words of the Request**

The verse opens with *fa-stajāba lahu rabbuh*, so his Lord responded to him. The verb is the tenth form of the root for answering — a response given to a request, not a general act of favour — and it is followed by the very phrase Joseph used: turning away their cunning from him. The correspondence is deliberate and complete. He asked for one specific thing in one specific form and the narrative reports the granting of that thing in the same terms. Nothing is added, nothing is substituted, and no alternative blessing is offered in place of the one requested. The Qur'an rarely documents a supplication and its answer in adjacent verses with this precision, and when it does the pairing is meant to be read as evidence of the general promise stated at 40:60, that the Lord responds to those who call on Him.

**The Cunning Was Turned Away by Changing Him**

How the answer operated is the verse's real subject. The demand was not withdrawn; the threat of prison was carried out in the very next verse; the woman did not repent at this point and the guests did not disperse. Everything external remained exactly as it was. What changed, on the commentators' reading, was Joseph's capacity: God strengthened his resistance to the point where he accepted the hardship of imprisonment rather than continuing to negotiate with it. Al-Qushayrī's formulation is that the cunning was turned away by making the prison dearer to him than the alternative. That is an unusual definition of deliverance. The threat was not removed; it was neutralised by removing its power to frighten him, which is a form of rescue that leaves every visible circumstance unchanged.

**Two Names for the Two Halves of a Prayer**

*Innahu huwa al-samīʿu al-ʿalīm* — He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. The pairing is not a decorative flourish. Hearing answers the words Joseph spoke; knowledge answers the situation he was in. A person praying from a locked room in a hostile household needs both: someone who heard the sentence, and someone who knows what the sentence was about, including the parts nobody present could see. The second name also covers what Joseph did not say — the inclination he had experienced in verse 24, the fear he admitted in verse 33, the pressure of a room agreeing against one man. The names together assert that the prayer was received in full, with its unspoken content included, which is why the answer could be so precisely fitted to the need.

**The Shortest Verse in the Sequence Carries the Argument**

Fourteen words in English close an arc that began in verse 23. Between them sit a locked door, a chase, a torn shirt, a family witness, a husband's verdict, an instruction to keep quiet, a city's gossip, a banquet, forty knives, a public confession and a collective threat. Verse 34 disposes of all of it in a single clause. The compression is the point: the chapter has spent eleven verses building the machinery of a household's pressure, and it takes one verse to state that the machinery did not work. Nothing in the sequence is dismissed as unreal — the woman's campaign was persistent, the husband's settlement failed, the city's opinion was formed, the threat was credible — and yet the whole structure is answered by a sentence about God responding to a prayer.

**An Answer That Grants the Worse of Two Options**

Joseph asked that the cunning be turned away, and what he received was prison. The verse does not treat that as a partial answer or a delayed one. It reports the response as complete, and the following verse reports the imprisonment as the outcome. The theology here is precise and uncomfortable: God's answer to a request for protection can consist of placing the petitioner inside the harm he feared, in a condition where the harm no longer does what it was meant to do. The prison does not seduce him, does not disgrace him in his own estimation, and does not separate him from God. It is still a prison, and the chapter will keep him in it for years (12:42). Deliverance in this verse means the plot's failure, not the prisoner's release.

**What the Verse Establishes for the Rest of the Chapter**

Everything after this point depends on the fact recorded here. Joseph enters the prison as a man whose resistance has been divinely secured, which is why the sūrah can put him in a cell for years without worrying about what the cell will do to him. It also fixes the interpretation of the earlier temptation scene: he did not escape it by strength of character, and the text has now said so twice, once in verse 24 and once here. The chapter's portrait of prophetic virtue is therefore not a portrait of capacity. It is a portrait of dependence exercised consistently, at the moment of maximum pressure, in the direction of the only party able to help. That is the lesson the sūrah wants from the Egyptian section, and verse 34 is where it is stated.

The answer's cost is recorded in the next two verses and should not be softened. Joseph goes to prison and stays there. Verse 42 states the length: *so he remained in prison for several years*, the consequence of one man's forgetfulness rather than of any new decision about him. The prayer of verse 33 was granted in full and the grant did not shorten his sentence by a day. Readers expecting divine intervention to look like release will find this verse difficult, and the difficulty is intentional. What was promised was protection from the cunning, and the cunning was defeated; what was not promised was comfort. Joseph's own summary of the whole episode, spoken from a throne at the end of the chapter, mentions the prison as an act of kindness: *“He was truly kind to me when He freed me from prison”* (12:100). The kindness he identifies is the release, not the years.
