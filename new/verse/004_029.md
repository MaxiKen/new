## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:29

> O believers! Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally, but rather trade by mutual consent. And do not kill ˹each other or˺ yourselves. Surely Allah is ever Merciful to you.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Do Not Devour One Another’s Wealth Illegally**

The address *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* — O believers — reappears to mark a new, general principle after the detailed family laws. *Lā taʾkulū amwālakum baynakum bi al-bāṭil* — do not devour one another’s wealth illegally — uses the same verb *akala* — to eat — that earlier described consuming orphans’ wealth as eating fire (4:10) and that warned against mixing orphan wealth with one’s own to consume it (4:2) and against hasty consumption before the orphan grows up (4:6). Here the scope widens from guardianship to all transactions among believers. *Bi al-bāṭil* — illegally, by falsehood — covers every means where consent or right is absent: theft, fraud, usury where increase is taken without trade, gambling where property changes hands without productive exchange, bribery, and taking by coercion. The sūrah has spent its first thirty verses guarding wealth at its most sensitive points — orphan property, dowries, inheritance shares, and the estate around which relatives, orphans, and the needy gather (4:8). This verse lifts that guard to the level of the market and the household economy as a whole. What was forbidden with the orphan because he cannot defend himself is forbidden with everyone because no one’s wealth is lawful to another without right.

**But Trade by Mutual Consent**

The exception *illā an takūna tijāratan ʿan tarāḍin minkum* — but rather trade by mutual consent — defines the lawful path that replaces *bāṭil*. *Tijārah* — trade, commerce — was read as exchange where both parties knowingly consent to the object, the price, and the terms, without deception or compulsion. *Tarāḍ* — mutual pleasure, contentment — matches the standard the sūrah already set for dowries, where only what is waived with a contented soul is wholesome (4:4), and for adjustments to the set dowry where only what is mutually agreed is permitted (4:24). The same standard appears here for every sale. Consent must be real, not assumed; knowledge must be shared, not hidden. The Qur’an elsewhere requires witnessing for debts and discourages concealing defects, both of which protect *tarāḍ* from being a formality. The sūrah’s ethic is consistent: wealth moves lawfully when the other party’s willingness is genuine, as with witnesses at the orphan’s handover (4:6) and with provision offered to bystanders with kind speech (4:8). Trade that hides a flaw, invents a price under pressure, or exploits information asymmetry fails that test, even if the paperwork is correct, because the consent was not true.

**Do Not Kill Each Other or Yourselves**

The second prohibition *wa lā taqtulū anfusakum* — and do not kill each other or yourselves — was read in two linked senses, both supported by the wording *anfusakum* — yourselves, your own selves, one another. One sense is literal: do not kill one another, which the Qur’an elsewhere forbids except by right of justice, and do not kill yourselves, which Prophetic teaching counts among grave sins. The other sense is social: in a community described as being from a single soul (4:1) and as *you are from one another* (4:25), killing another is killing the collective self, and devouring another’s wealth illegally is a slow killing of the community’s life as well. The placement here is deliberate. After forbidding fraudulent enrichment, the verse forbids the violent taking of life, showing that both are forms of taking what is not yours — property and blood. The sūrah has already warned that exceeding God’s limits in inheritance leads to abiding in Fire with humiliation (4:14) and that failing to repent until death is present leads to a prepared painful punishment (4:18). This verse adds the direct threat that follows illicit devouring and killing in the next verse: *“whoever does this sinfully and unjustly, We will burn them in the Fire”* (4:30). The sequence keeps property and life under the same guard.

**Surely Allah Is Ever Merciful to You**

The closing *inna Allāha kāna bikum raḥīmā* — surely Allah is ever Merciful to you — gives the reason that both prohibitions are mercy themselves. The mercy is not only in forgiving those who have done wrong and then turn soon (4:17); it is also in forbidding the wrong that would destroy them and others. A market where *bāṭil* is allowed quickly becomes a place where the weak — the very orphans, women, and needy the sūrah has been protecting — are devoured; a community where killing is tolerated cannot keep any of the family limits the sūrah has drawn. By prohibiting both, God spares the community the fire that illicit gain already is (4:10) and the Fire that deliberate injustice leads to (4:30). The Name *Raḥīm* here matches its use after the concession for bondwomen where patience is better (4:25) and after the promise to make things clear and lighten burdens (4:26–28). In each place mercy appears as the character of the lawgiver who both draws the line and eases the path inside it. The sūrah has said He wills to turn toward you (4:27) and to lighten because you are weak (4:28); this verse shows that turning and lightening include clear prohibitions that keep the weak from being eaten.

**Trade as the Lawful Alternative to Devouring**

The juxtaposition of *bāṭil* and *tijārah ʿan tarāḍ* teaches that economic life has only two modes in this sūrah’s vision: devouring or trading. Devouring is taking without right — substitution of bad for good (4:2), seizure of dowries (4:20–21), excess bequests that harm heirs (4:12). Trading is giving and receiving with knowledge and willingness. The sūrah’s repeated insistence on witnesses, documentation, and measured shares is the infrastructure for trade: it makes consent verifiable. Even the small provision for relatives, orphans, and the needy present at distribution, offered with kind speech (4:8), is a form of lawful transfer where consent is not between buyer and seller but between heir and bystander, yet it still requires the heir’s genuine willingness, not compulsion. The verse therefore does more than condemn fraud; it commends a market ethic where the other’s contentment is a condition of lawfulness. A profit taken with someone’s hidden ignorance or pressured silence may look like trade, but the sūrah calls it devouring, because the soul inside the transaction did not truly consent. That redefinition trains the reader to audit not only contracts but conversations.

**A Guard That Holds Property and Life Together**

Placed after the verses on weakening and lightening burdens, this double prohibition holds the community’s two most basic trusts together. The sūrah began with creation from a single soul and watchfulness over womb-ties (4:1), moved through guardianship of orphans with witnessing and reckoning (4:6), fixed inheritance after debts and bequests (4:11–12), and governed marriage with dowries and chastity (4:24–25). Now it says, in the market and in the street, keep the same guard: do not take wealth by falsehood, and do not take life at all without right. Both takings are framed as eating and killing that rebound on the taker — here as burning in the next verse, earlier as eating fire (4:10), and always under a Reckoner who suffices (4:6). The closing mercy — ever Merciful to you — assures the believer that the command is not a burden added to weakness but a protection given to weakness, so that trade can be pursued with confidence and life can be lived without fear of the neighbour’s hand. Where that protection is kept, wealth circulates by consent and lives are spared; where it is crossed, the sūrah’s next sentence names the end.
