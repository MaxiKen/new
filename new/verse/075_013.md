## Sūrah al-Qiyāmah 75:13

> All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Informed, Not Interrogated**

The verb is *yunabbaʾu* — he is told, he is given news — and it belongs to the family of *nabaʾ*, the weighty report. The passive matters: the human being on that Day is not asked to supply information, because the information is already complete and comes from the One who kept it. A trial in this world exists to establish facts that are in dispute; a notification presupposes that nothing is in dispute. That is why the Qur'an's scenes of the Judgment so often skip the investigation and go straight to the disclosure. The purpose of the occasion is not to find out what happened but to have the person face it. He arrives suspecting, hoping, and half-believing his own account of himself, and the first thing that happens to him is that he is told.

**What Was Sent Ahead**

The first half of the account is what a person sent on before him. The image is of a traveller dispatching his goods ahead to the place he is going, so that they arrive before he does. Every deed in the Qur'an is described as travelling: *“It is certainly We Who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and what they leave behind. Everything is listed by Us in a perfect Record.”* (36:12); *“˹then˺ each soul will know what it has sent forth or left behind.”* (82:5). The point of the metaphor is that a deed does not vanish when the moment of doing it passes. It goes somewhere and waits. A life is therefore not a series of events that are over, but a convoy arriving at a destination ahead of its owner, and the owner walks into a room already furnished with what he did.

**What Was Left Behind: Two Readings**

The second half of the pair is read in two ways, and both have strong support. On the first reading, *what he left behind* is the good he did not do — the omissions, the prayers not prayed, the money not given, the reconciliation not attempted. On the second, it is the trace that outlasts him: the practice he began that others continued, the knowledge he taught, the child he raised, the harm he set in motion that kept moving after he stopped. The first reading has the parallelism of the verse on its side, since sent-forth and left-behind then divide neatly into acts and omissions. The second has the Arabic on its side, since the verb can carry the sense of what a person leaves after him. The second is also the heavier claim, and the sunnah supports it: the Prophet said that when a person dies his deeds end except for three — ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, and a righteous child who prays for him (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1631). A man's account therefore does not close when he stops breathing, which is exactly the sort of thing a person arranging his affairs should want to know in advance.

**Nothing Too Small to Record**

Both halves of the account are kept with a precision the Qur'an describes in some detail. Two angels record, one on the right and one on the left, and *“not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺.”* (50:18). Secret conversations are included: *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺.”* (43:80). The same precision is stated of creation at large — *“Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge”* (6:59) — which means the record is not a special surveillance installed for human beings but a consequence of the kind of world this is. Nothing is too small to be counted, and the smallest item on the list is what the Qur'an singles out elsewhere: *“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it.”* (99:7).

**The Record Is Not Separable From the Person**

What is being disclosed is not a file produced from outside. The record is what the person has made of himself, and the Qur'an's language consistently binds the two. *“And We have everything recorded precisely.”* (78:29) is a statement about the keeping, but the content kept is the man's own history, held in a form he cannot now edit. That is why the disclosure is described as a recognition rather than a revelation: the person reads and knows. *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, ‘Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?’”* (18:49). The cry is not about unfairness. It is the shock of completeness.

**Why the Telling Is the Judgment**

Once the disclosure has happened, surprisingly little remains to be done. There is no cross-examination in these scenes, no dispute about mitigating circumstances, no argument about whether the record is accurate. The person's own knowledge of what he did is the evidence, and the Qur'an states the principle directly: *“Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself.”* (17:14). The judgment therefore does not need to be imposed from outside. It is the encounter between what a person is and what he always knew he was, conducted in the open, in front of the One whose knowledge was never in question. The disclosure is also strictly individual. *“˹Consider˺ the Day ˹when˺ every soul will come pleading for itself, and each will be paid in full for what it did, and none will be wronged.”* (16:111), and no soul bears another's load (17:15). The company a person kept, the crowd whose approval he shared, and the community whose habits he absorbed will all be present, but they will not dilute his account. The convoy he sent ahead contained his deeds and no one else's.
