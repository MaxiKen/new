## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:126

> Allah ordained this ˹reinforcement˺ only as good news for you and reassurance for your hearts. And victory comes only from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Behind the Promise**

The verse states that the reinforcement was intended as good news and as a means of reassurance for the believers' hearts, and it ends by attributing victory to God alone. The sentence explains why the promise of angels was given rather than describing what the angels did.

The same explanation appears in the other account of the battle, where the aid is called a sign of victory and a reassurance, followed by the statement that help comes only from God (8:10). The Book's consistent position is that the language of aid is addressed to the inner state of the people who receive it, and the verse before the reader states that purpose in its opening words.

**Good News as a Category in the Book**

The Book uses the vocabulary of good tidings for the messages that messengers bring and for the promises attached to faith. In this passage the term applies to the announcement of support, and the announcement itself is described as something done for the believers' sake.

That framing keeps the promise from being a piece of information about the unseen. What the community receives is not a report to be verified but an encouragement to be acted upon, and the Book's use of the same word for prophecy and for encouragement shows how it thinks about the effect of its own statements. A community told that help is coming is expected to be steadied by the telling, which is the purpose named in the verse.

**Reassurance for the Hearts**

The phrase the verse uses for the purpose of the promise is about the settling of the heart, and the concern is characteristic of this sūrah. Its early verses described hearts that swerve and hearts that are kept, and its prayer for the community is that their hearts not be made to deviate after being guided.

The promised reinforcement is therefore part of the same concern. What the community needed on the day was not merely a favourable balance of forces but the capacity to stand, and the Book locates that capacity in the heart. The sentence treats the promise as a means of producing an inner condition, and it treats the inner condition as the thing that matters most.

**Victory Attributed to God Alone**

The clause that victory belongs to God alone is the sentence's centre. The Book states it elsewhere in its most direct form, telling the believers that if God helps them none can defeat them, and that if He withholds help nobody else can supply it (3:160).

The Book's accounts of battles are written so that this attribution is never in doubt. The community marched and fought, and the outcome is credited to God, and the two facts are stated side by side without embarrassment. What the verse before the reader denies is the inference that the means produce the result, and the denial is stated immediately after the means are named.

**The Two Names at the End**

The verse closes with the names that describe God as mighty and wise. The pairing is frequent in the Book, and in contexts of difficulty it does specific work: the first name establishes that the power to act is present, and the second establishes that the timing and manner of the action are governed by knowledge rather than by the pressure of events.

A community facing a stronger enemy needs both facts. Power alone would leave it wondering whether God will use it on its behalf; wisdom alone would leave it wondering whether anything can be done. The verse supplies the two together, and the sentence that follows states the outcome the wisdom intends.

**Whether the Angels Fought or Strengthened**

The commentaries discuss what the angels did at the two battles. Most hold that they fought at Badr and that their participation is described in the sūrah of the Spoils; on the accepted view they did not fight at Uḥud, and their mention in this passage is understood as moral support. Ibn Kathīr and others record this position, and al-Ṭabarī notes that God does not need angels to help the believers.

The debate is a reminder of what the verse itself says about the purpose of the promise. If the promise is given for the sake of the hearts, then the question of how the angels participated is secondary to the question of what the announcement produced in the community that heard it.

**Means and Outcomes in the Book's Teaching**

The verse distinguishes between the means by which a community is strengthened and the power that determines the result. The distinction is practical. A community that confuses the two will either neglect the means, expecting help without effort, or rely on the means, expecting the result from its own preparation.

The Book's instructions to the believers keep both in view, and this verse states the relationship between them in a single sentence. What the community is given is a reason for confidence, and the reason is located neither in its own strength nor in the promised aid but in the one who gives both.

**How the Verse Closes the Promises**

The three verses before this one described the promised reinforcement, the condition attached to it, and then the purpose behind the whole arrangement. This verse gathers them and states the reason once more.

The passage's lesson is thus complete before the narrative continues: the aid was for the sake of the community's hearts, and the outcome of the fighting was never in the community's hands. The verses that follow describe what the divine purpose achieved among the enemy, and the reader arrives at them with the promise already understood for what it was.
