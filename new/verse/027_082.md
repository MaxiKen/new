## Sūrah an-Naml 27:82

> And when the decree ˹of the Hour˺ comes to pass against them, We will bring forth for them a beast from the earth, telling them that the people had no sure faith in Our revelations.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sign at the End of the Argument**

The chapter has spent a long section saying that some people cannot be reached by speech. This verse describes what comes when the time for speech has run out: a creature brought out of the earth to state what the situation is.

The placement is exact. Verses 80 and 81 established that the deaf and the blind cannot be made to hear; this verse says that at the end a sign will be produced which does not depend on being heard. It marks and declares rather than persuades. What the chapter has been arguing — that the interval is for deciding and the decision must be made inside it — reaches its terminus here, at the point where the evidence becomes unavoidable and therefore useless.

**"When the Word Comes Upon Them"**

The phrase is *idhā waqaʿa al-qawlu ʿalayhim*, and al-Ṭabarī connects it to similar language elsewhere about a word or decree falling due. The sense is of a sentence that has been pronounced and now takes effect.

Others among the commentators read it as the point at which the divine wrath becomes necessary against them. Both readings agree on the structure: something already determined arrives at its appointed moment. The Qur'an uses the same construction in several chapters, always for the transition from warning to execution. What the verse adds to that familiar formula is the creature, which appears in the Qur'an only here and which the tradition identifies as one of the portents preceding the end.

**What "Beast" Means**

*Dābbah* is a broad word. Its root sense covers anything that moves along the ground, and the Qur'an uses it for animals generally, for creatures in the heavens and the earth, and in one place for a worm. It can mean a riding animal, an insect, or a sea creature.

The vagueness is in the Arabic itself, not in the translation. What the verse specifies is only that it is brought out *from the earth* and that it addresses people about the state of their belief. Everything beyond that — size, shape, place of emergence — comes from reports rather than from the text, and the reports do not agree with each other. This is one of the few places where the Qur'an introduces a concrete future event and then declines to describe it, and the restraint is itself a datum.

**The Commentators' Descriptions and al-Rāzī's Caution**

The tafsīr literature carries a large and colourful body of material. Some give the creature a length of sixty or seventy cubits. Others say it emerges carrying the staff of Mūsā and the seal of Sulaymān, marking believers with the staff and stamping deniers with the seal so that each is recognisable on sight. One composite description assembles it from the parts of a dozen animals — a bull's head, an elephant's trunk, a stag's horns, a camel's legs. There is disagreement, too, about where it appears, with Mecca, Minā, Yemen and the desert all named.

Al-Rāzī's response to this abundance is the soundest position available. Since the Qur'an gives no indication of any of these details, a report should be accepted only so far as it can be authenticated from the Prophet, and otherwise disregarded. The methodological point is worth keeping: the density of a tradition is not evidence for it, and a text that says little about something was probably not intending to say more. [UNVERIFIED: the physical descriptions of the beast reported by al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, ath-Thaʿlabī and al-Qurṭubī are transmitted accounts that the Qur'anic text does not support or specify.]

**A Reading Difference in the Verb**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both note an alternative vocalisation. Read as *tukallimuhum* the creature speaks to them; read as *taklimuhum* it wounds them. The consonantal text supports either.

The variant matters more than it looks. On the first reading the event is an announcement — the creature declares that people had no certainty about the signs. On the second it is a physical marking, which fits the reports about the staff and the seal. Most commentators, including az-Zamakhsharī, take the declaration as the creature's own speech. The chapter's own concerns favour that reading too, since an-Naml is a sūrah in which animals speak and are understood: an ant warns her colony, a hoopoe delivers a report, and the last creature in the chapter announces a verdict.

**The Content of the Announcement**

What the creature says is short and specific: *anna al-nāsa kānū bi-āyātinā lā yūqinūn* — that people had no certainty about Our signs. Not that they disbelieved, and not that they disobeyed. That they were not certain.

The word is *yūqinūn*, from *yaqīn*, and it takes the chapter back to its third verse, where the believers were described as being certain about the Hereafter. Certainty is the quality the sūrah named first and returns to last. It also connects to Pharaoh's court in verse 14, whose souls were certain while their tongues denied — the one case in the chapter where certainty existed and made no difference. The final sign in the Qur'anic chapter about signs announces that the problem was never the availability of evidence.

**A Threshold After Which Belief Does Not Count**

A well-known report names three events after which no soul's faith will benefit it if it did not believe before: the sun rising from the west, the appearance of the Dajjāl, and the beast of the earth. The creature of this verse is one of the three.

That classification tells us what the sign is for. It does not open a further opportunity; it closes the one that was open. The logic matches what the chapter has been saying about certainty. Belief under conditions that make disbelief impossible is not the thing the Qur'an has been asking for, and verse 3 defined the believers as those who are certain about the Hereafter while it remains unseen. When the evidence becomes overwhelming the quality that was being tested no longer exists to be tested, which is why the arrival of this sign functions as a terminus rather than as a final appeal.

**A Sign for a Time Without Correction**

One report cited by al-Ṭabarī connects the creature's emergence to a condition of the community rather than to a date: it comes when no one any longer enjoins what is right and forbids what is wrong.

That is a diagnostic rather than a calendar, and it fits the verse's refusal to give a time. What the condition describes is a society in which the ordinary internal mechanism of correction has stopped operating — not a society full of wrongdoing, which every society is, but one in which nobody speaks against it. The chapter has shown the mechanism working elsewhere. An ant warned her colony, a hoopoe contradicted a king, a councillor advised a queen, and Ṣāliḥ addressed his people directly. The sign arrives when nothing of that kind is left, which is to say when speech has already failed.
