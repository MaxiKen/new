## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:21

> And awaiting them are maces of iron.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence With No Verb**

*Wa-lahum maqāmiʿu min ḥadīdin* — and for them are hooked rods of iron. The Arabic is a nominal sentence: a prepositional phrase, then a noun. There is no verb in it at all.

The absence is the point. Everything in verses 19 and 20 was described as happening — garments cut, water poured, insides melted. Here nothing happens. The rods simply *are*, and they are *for them*, in the same way a possession is. The punishment is being described as a standing provision rather than as an event, and the shift in grammar is what carries it. A verb would have placed the beating in time. The nominal sentence places it outside time.

Sūrah Fāṭir states what the timeless grammar implies: *“As for the disbelievers, they will have the Fire of Hell, where they will not be ˹allowed to be˺ finished by death, nor will its torment be lightened for them”* (35:36). No death to finish them, no lightening to relieve them — the two exits closed in a single sentence. The nominal *wa-lahum maqāmiʿ* works the same way: the rods are not an event approaching in time but equipment belonging to a place, and the belonging has no tense because it has no end.

**What the Word Means**

*Maqāmiʿ* is the plural of *miqmaʿa*, an iron implement with a hooked or angled end. The lexicographers describe it as the bar used to strike a stubborn animal and the hook used to drive or restrain one; the root *q-m-ʿ* carries the sense of subduing, of breaking a thing's resistance.

The image is therefore not simply a weapon. A *miqmaʿa* is a tool for dealing with something that will not go where it is put. That is precisely the condition of the people described in the preceding verses, whose defining action in verse 22 will be that they keep trying to leave.

Commentators took the rods as instruments of beating, and the plain sense supports them. What the word adds, beyond the beating, is the element of being driven — the humiliation of being managed like an animal that refuses to move.

Sūrah al-Qamar shows the driving in motion: *“On the Day they will be dragged into the Fire on their faces”* (54:48). Dragged, on the face — the posture of a thing that refused to walk and is now moved without its consent. The *miqmaʿa* is the instrument of that motion: what drags must have something to drag with, and the hooked iron is named here while the dragging is shown there. The implement and the action belong to one scene, split across two sūrahs.

**Why Iron**

The material is named, and in the Qur'an's vocabulary iron is never a casual detail. Sūrah al-Ḥadīd describes it as something sent down: *“And We sent down iron with its great might, benefits for humanity, and means for Allah to prove who ˹is willing to˺ stand up for Him and His messengers without seeing Him”* (57:25).

That verse gives the metal a double character, and both halves are present here. Iron is the material of human strength — tools, weapons, the things that build and the things that break — and it is named in the sūrah that bears its name as a means by which God tests who stands up for Him. Used as an instrument of punishment in this sūrah, it carries the same double edge: the strongest material of the world that people put their trust in becomes the material of their subduing.

There is a physical aptness too. Iron is heavy, hard, and cold, and it does not soften in a fire of the kind the Qur'an is describing. The implement outlasts what it strikes.

**The Instrument Fits the Sin**

The people described in this passage are the ones who turned their shoulders away in pride (22:9), who disputed without knowledge (22:8), and who were enraged at the thought that God would help someone other than them (22:15). Their defining posture was that they would not be managed.

The implement named here is the one used on what will not be managed. There is an exactness to that which the Qur'an achieves repeatedly and which is easy to miss: the punishment is not an arbitrary tariff but a mirror. The arrogant are ruled by the instrument of compulsion; the man who would not be led is driven.

Sūrah al-Tawbah gives the clearest instance of the mirror, in the branding of hoarders with their own hoard: *“their foreheads, sides, and backs branded with it. ˹It will be said to them,˺ ‘This is the treasure you hoarded for yourselves. Now taste what you hoarded!’”* (9:35). The treasure heated, the body marked where the hoard was kept from reaching — forehead, sides, back. Verse 21 works by the same logic of fittedness: the men who would not be led are met by the tool for leading the unwilling, and the fit is the verdict.

**What the Verse Refuses to Describe**

Notice how much is left out. The rods are named; the beating is not. No blow lands in this verse, nobody is struck in front of the reader, and no sound is reported.

The restraint is characteristic of the Qur'an's eschatological vocabulary and worth contrasting with what it *does* specify. The Qur'an is precise about the instruments — garments, water, rods, chains, depths — and almost entirely silent about the violence done with them. What is described is the equipment of the place and the state of the people in it, which is quite different from narrating their pain.

The effect is that the reader's imagination is given materials and then left alone with them, which is more forceful than any description could be. A text that spelled out the beating would have set a limit to it. A text that says *and for them are hooked rods of iron* sets no limit at all.

**The Allegorical Reading and Its Limits**

Some commentators, among them al-Aj, read the rods as a description of the soul's condition rather than of a place: the malicious soul, unable to see past the surface of this world, is beaten back from rising by the hooked irons of its own belief in its self-sufficiency, and returned continuously to suffering by its pride.

Read as application, that interpretation is sound and pastorally useful. Read as a replacement for the plain sense, it fails the sūrah's own argument, and it is worth saying why. Verses 5 to 7 of this sūrah were spent establishing that the dead are raised bodily, from dust, from wombs, from graves. A commentary that spiritualizes the punishment is in tension with a sūrah that has just expended that much argument on the resurrection of bodies.

The two readings can be held in the right order. The Fire is real, and the self-exaltation that puts a person there is also real, and the second is what makes the first intelligible rather than arbitrary.

**The Four Verses as One Picture**

Verses 19 to 22 belong together and are best read as a single movement with four beats. First the garment: something to wear, cut to fit. Then the water: poured from above, reaching the inside and the outside. Then the rods: the standing equipment of the place. Then the attempt to leave and the return.

The progression is from the personal to the total. A garment is on the skin; water gets under it; the rods are what is waiting regardless; and the last verse closes the door. None of the four contradicts the others, and together they answer the objection that the Qur'an's hell is vague. It is not vague. It is specific about everything except the thing readers most want specified, which is an ending.

Sūrah al-Furqān closes a similar movement the way verse 22 will close this one: *“And when they are tossed into a narrow place inside ˹Hell˺, chained together, then and there they will cry out for ˹instant˺ destruction”* (25:13). Tossed, chained, crying for an end — the narrowing of the place answering the narrowing of every hope. Verses 19 to 22 follow the same narrowing: garment, water, rods, and then the attempt to leave that produces only return. The specificity the passage accumulates is all about the confinement; the one thing never specified is an exit.
