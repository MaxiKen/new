## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:71

> O People of the Book! Why do you mix the truth with falsehood and hide the truth knowingly?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Question**

The verse keeps the same addressee and the same interrogative form, and it sharpens the charge. The first question concerned rejection; this one concerns the handling of truth and falsehood, and it names two acts: mixing the two, and concealing the truth while knowing it.

The pair is significant because the second act is hidden and the first is public. A person can hide a text, a reading, or a fact; he can also construct a presentation in which the true and the false are blended so that the distinction no longer shows. The Book's charge covers both operations, and its framing as a question means the addressees are being asked to explain a method rather than to deny a conclusion.

**The Image Behind "Mixing"**

The verb rendered "mix" describes the blending of one thing into another until they cannot be separated, and the Arabic term is the ordinary word for confusion and for murky water. What the verse describes is not the honest mistake of a reader who cannot tell two meanings apart; it is the deliberate production of a compound in which true material carries false conclusions.

The Book states the same charge in the sūrah of the Cow in almost identical words, warning the community not to clothe truth with falsehood nor to conceal the truth while knowing it (2:42). The two verses are usually read together, and the commentators treated them as describing one practice rather than two. The practice is the characteristic one of a community that wants to hold its position while retaining the authority of the revelation it holds. The truth is not denied; it is used as a vessel.

**Concealment and the Charge of Distortion**

The second act named is concealment, and the Book's treatment of it elsewhere is explicit about what is being hidden. In this sūrah's later verses, a group is described as distorting the book with their tongues so that what they say appears to be from it, and the verse ends by saying that they knowingly attribute lies to God (3:78).

The classical commentators developed the distinction between altering a text and altering its interpretation, and they disagreed about which the Book accuses the earlier communities of. The verse above does not need that dispute resolved. It accuses the addressees of hiding something they know, which presupposes that they still have access to it; a community that had genuinely lost the material could not be charged with concealing it. What the verse assumes, then, is the presence of the truth among these people and the decision not to present it.

**The Word "Knowingly" and Its Weight**

The sentence ends with the phrase that recurs in the Book's most serious accusations: they act knowingly. The word appears in the warning against mixing truth with falsehood in the sūrah of the Cow, in the account of people who attribute lies to God knowingly, and here.

The addition of the adverb changes the category of the offence from error to something else. The Book is consistently careful about the difference between a person acting in ignorance and a person acting against his own knowledge, and its severest language is reserved for the second. That is why the verse's question is put to the community rather than to individuals who might have been misinformed: the charge is corporate, and it concerns a policy rather than a mistake.

**What the Charge Implies About the Scriptures**

A reader might expect a passage such as this to deny that the other community possesses anything genuine, and the verse does not do that. It accuses the community of concealing and mixing what it has, which is an accusation that makes sense only if what it has includes the truth.

This is the sūrah's position on the earlier scriptures throughout. It treats them as genuine revelations, describes the Qur'an as confirming them, and places the failure of the communities in the handling rather than in the holding. The distinction matters for how the passage reads: the argument is not that the Torah and the Gospel are worthless but that their custodians have dealt with them in a way that obscures what they contain. That is a charge about conduct, and conduct can be changed.

**The Question as an Invitation**

The verse asks a question, and a question addressed to someone who is concealing something functions as a notice that the concealment is visible. The sūrah does not produce the evidence in this sentence; it states the charge in the form of an enquiry and leaves the question standing.

That form is the Book's usual way of addressing a party that still has a chance to respond. The verses that follow describe a stratagem the same community devised, so the sūrah is not naive about the likelihood of a change of heart; it continues to address them anyway. What a reader is being shown is a method of argument that states the accusation publicly, refuses to hide behind implication, and leaves the door open even after the conduct has been described.

**A Charge That Applies to Any Custodian of a Text**

Read without the immediate controversy, the verse states a rule for anyone who holds a revelation. The two acts it forbids — blending the true with the false, and withholding what one knows — are the ordinary operations by which a religious community defends a position it can no longer support from its own sources.

The sūrah's own community is not exempt from the warning, and the Book's habit of recording such charges against another party is not a licence for the reader. It is the earlier community's story told in advance, and the Muslim community is shown both what was done and what it looked like from outside.
