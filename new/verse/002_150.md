## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:150

> Wherever you are ˹O Prophet˺, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque. And wherever you ˹believers˺ are, face towards it, so that people will have no argument against you, except the wrongdoers among them. Do not fear them; fear Me, so that I may ˹continue to˺ perfect My favour upon you and so you may be ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Statement, and the Reason Finally Given**

The qiblah command now appears for the third time in seven verses — to the Prophet wherever he goes out, and to the believers wherever they are — and this repetition earns its place by adding what the first two statements withheld: a purpose clause. *So that people will have no argument against you.* The settled community was given the direction (2:144), the traveler was given the direction (2:149); now everyone is given the direction together with the reason the direction removes an objection. Legal repetition in the Qur'an typically functions this way — the ruling restated until each dimension of its application and its rationale has been given its own verse. The structure of this one is worth mapping because the verse carries five movements in a single sentence: the command, the purpose, an exception to the purpose, the transfer of fear, and the double goal of completed favour and guidance. Each movement answers a distinct problem raised by the qiblah controversy.

**Silencing the People of the Book**

The "people" whose argument is removed are, in the majority reading preserved by al-Ṭabarī, the People of the Book — specifically the Jewish community of Medina, and specifically the boast the verse's background had quoted: *he follows us in our religion and uses our qiblah*. That claim had real polemical force while the Jerusalem direction stood; it made Muslim prayer look like borrowed practice and the Prophet's community look like a satellite. The turn to the Kaʿbah nullified it at the root: the community now faced a direction no other living community faced, assigned by its own revelation, toward the House of its own faith's patriarch. The argument was not answered by counter-argument but dissolved by fact — which is the strongest form of refutation the verse's purpose-clause imagines. A believer praying toward the Sacred Mosque could no longer be described as following anyone's religion but his own, and the recognition the Scripture-people carried in their books (2:146) now faced a community whose practice matched what their books described rather than contradicted it.

**The Exception: Those Who Do Wrong Anyway**

The purpose clause carries a rider: *except the wrongdoers among them*. Al-Ṭabarī identifies them as the idolatrous Meccans, and the logic he gives is instructive. The Meccans would not be silenced by the change, because one of their taunts had been precisely that the Muslims' qiblah was *not* the Kaʿbah — the House they custodied. When the Muslims turned toward it, the idolaters gained a new argument rather than losing an old one: Muhammad inclines toward our sanctuary, perhaps he will drift back toward our religion — and they, as the House's custodians, could pose as the party he was returning to. The exception therefore names a category the purpose-clause cannot satisfy: the objector whose objection is not evidential but dispositional, who will find in every fact a fresh ground for the hostility he had already decided on. The verse does not try to answer these — it classifies them (*the wrongdoers*) and moves to the only treatment their case admits: do not fear them. An argument removed is a mercy to the honest; an argument manufactured is a test for the faithful, and the two are handled in one clause each.

**Fear Transferred From Them to Me**

*Do not fear them; fear Me* — the command transfers the direction of fear the way the earlier verses transferred the direction of prayer, and the parallel is exact: as the face turns from the creatures' approval to the Creator's House, so the heart turns from the creatures' menace to the Creator's majesty. The formula is a Quranic refrain at the moments of covenant: Satan's intimidation is answered with *do not fear them; fear Me if you are ˹true˺ believers* (3:175); the day the religion was perfected opens with the same words — *today the disbelievers have given up all hope of your faith, so do not fear them; fear Me* (5:3). In this verse's historical situation the command carried a literal edge: the Kaʿbah the community now faced was occupied by the very people they were told not to fear, and the qiblah could be maintained only by hearts that had relocated their dread. The promise implicit in the transfer — that the feared would eventually lose what they held — was fulfilled within the decade: the House the believers faced from a distance became the House they cleansed and entered, and the fear of its custodians had been, as commanded, outlived.

**The Favour Completed**

The transfer of fear opens onto the verse's positive goal: *so that I may complete My favour upon you*. The fixing of the qiblah is here counted among God's completed blessings — not a burden of orientation but a gift of it. The phrase connects this verse to the two other places where completing the favour frames a ritual law: the day at ʿArafah when the faith was perfected and the favour completed with Islam chosen as the way (5:3), and the law of ablution, given *to purify you and complete His favour upon you* (5:6). The pattern those verses share with this one is distinctive: God's blessings are completed through commanded practice. The favour is not an abstraction poured on passive recipients; it takes the shape of directions to face, water to wash with, laws to live under — ritual form as the vessel of grace. For the qiblah specifically, the completion is intelligible: a community without a fixed direction has an unfinished worship, and the worship finished is the community finished. The blessing named here will be itemized in the verse that follows, where the sending of the messenger is presented as the favour whose instrument the community has become.

**So That You May Be Guided**

The verse's final words name the ultimate purpose beneath the immediate ones: *and so you may be guided*. The sequence is now complete and each link depends on the last. The direction is fixed — so the honest have no argument; the wrongdoers argue anyway — so fear them not, fear Me; fear relocated — so the favour is completed without the heart's distraction; favour completed in practice — so guidance, which is the practice's meaning, becomes the community's standing state. Guidance here is not the initial guidance of conversion but its continuation: the guided direction of the body in prayer sustaining the guided direction of the heart in life. The verse thus closes the entire qiblah section on the same note the section opened — God guides whoever He wills to a straight path (2:142) — but now the guidance has been given its machinery: a direction, a rationale, a command about fear, a completed blessing. The community that asked what had turned it from its old facing has been answered with more than a reason. It has been given the full anatomy of its new orientation, ending where every divine command ends: in guidance itself.
