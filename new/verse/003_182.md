## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:182

> This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason Stated**

The verse explains the punishment announced in the sentence before it by pointing to what the people in question had themselves sent ahead, and by stating that God does not wrong His servants. The sentence is short and functions as the justification of what has just been described.

The Book often follows a description of punishment with a clause of this kind, and the clause prevents the reader from understanding the outcome as an act of arbitrary severity. What people receive, in the Book's account, is the consequence of what they have done, and the sentence states that consequence in the language of a balance rather than of a penalty imposed from outside. The clause about God's justice follows descriptions of punishment throughout the Book, and its function is to close the door on the thought that the outcome could have been otherwise.

**What the Hands Have Sent Ahead**

The phrase about what the hands have sent ahead appears in other passages of the Book, and its image is of deeds that precede the person who will meet them. One sūrah states that a person will be told that the treatment received is for what his hands have done, and another uses the same formula in a context of punishment.

The Book uses the image to make a person's responsibility concrete. What is done in this life is not left behind when a person moves on; it goes ahead of him, and the sentence in this verse treats the punishment of the group as something they had dispatched with their own conduct.

**The Justice of the Sentence**

The statement that God does not wrong His servants is one of the Book's recurring declarations, and it appears in several sūrahs in nearly the same words. One passage states that whoever does good does so for himself and whoever does evil does so against himself, and adds that God is not unjust to His creatures.

Another states that God's word cannot be changed and that He is not unjust to His creation. The verse before the reader belongs to that group, and its placement after the announcement of a punishment gives the sentence its force: what is about to happen is not a departure from justice but an instance of it.

**The Word for Wronging**

The word the Book uses here describes the placing of a burden where it does not belong, or the reduction of a person's due. The negation of the term in this verse means that nothing of what the group is owed will be withheld and nothing undeserved will be added.

The Book's teaching about human responsibility is built on this negation. The sūrah had already stated, in its passage about the defrauder, that every soul will be paid in full and will not be wronged, and the sentence before the reader repeats the principle for the case of those whose conduct has been described in the preceding verse.

**Deeds as a Store Set Aside**

The Book's description of deeds as what is sent ahead belongs to a family of images that includes the account of the miser collared by what he withheld, a few verses earlier in this sūrah. Both pictures treat a person's conduct as something that remains attached to him and is produced at the end.

The two images work from opposite directions. The hoarder's property becomes a weight around his neck, and the deeds of the group in this verse become the reason for what they are told to taste. The Book's imagery of the Day of Judgment makes a person's history into an object that can be presented, and the sentences in this passage use that imagery twice.

**Why the Statement Follows the Taunt**

The verse explains the punishment of the group by their conduct rather than by their words alone, and the sentence includes the deeds in the account that the previous verse began. The taunt about God's poverty was one item among many.

The Book's treatment of its opponents in this passage is consistently cumulative. The verse before the reader gathers their speech and their actions together and states what the sum produces, and the sentence about justice explains why the outcome was inevitable. The sūrah's readers are meant to see the connection between the conduct and the treatment, and the verse exists to make that connection explicit.

**What the Community Learned**

The community that recited these verses was being taught how to think about the treatment its opponents would receive, and the sentence about justice kept it from reading the punishment as evidence of divine anger alone. The Book's descriptions of the next life always attach the outcome to the deed.

The verses also gave the community a way to think about its own case. The sūrah had said the same thing about the community's own losses, and the sentence in this verse states the measure that applies to both: the account is exact, and no one is wronged in either direction.

**The Verse's Function in the Passage**

The verse stands between the announcement of the taunt's consequence and the account of another challenge that the same quarter presented to the Prophet. Its brevity gives it the quality of a conclusion, and its reference to justice prepares the reader for the exchange that follows.

The passage as a whole deals with the refusal of the message by people who had been given the most evidence, and the verses move from one form of that refusal to another. The sentence about what the hands send ahead supplies the principle by which all of them are judged, and the next verse takes up a demand that the group made of the Prophet himself.
