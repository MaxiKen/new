## Sūrah an-Naml 27:28

> Go with this letter of mine and deliver it to them, then stand aside and see how they will respond.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Test Is the Message**

Sulaymān said he would find out whether the bird was truthful. This verse is how. He does not send a spy after the hoopoe or wait for a caravan's report. He writes a letter, sends it by the same bird, and instructs him to watch what comes back.

The design is efficient. If Sheba does not exist, or has no queen, or has no court to receive a letter, the errand fails and the report collapses with it. If the letter is received and answered, the kingdom is confirmed and something further is learned — how its ruler thinks. A single act of correspondence tests the witness and probes the subject at the same time. And the messenger chosen is the one witness whose credibility is on the line, which means the hoopoe is required to produce the evidence that will convict or clear him. There is no more economical way to settle the question, and no way that places the burden more exactly where it belongs.

**A Letter, Not an Army**

The most consequential word in this verse is the one that is absent. Sulaymān commands a force of jinn, humans and birds, arranged in marshalled ranks. He has just been told that a foreign kingdom worships the sun. What he sends is a piece of writing.

The choice sets the terms of everything that follows. The entire Sheba narrative — the queen's consultation with her chiefs, the gift she sends, her journey, her recognition in the palace — proceeds as an exchange rather than a conquest, and it proceeds that way because the first move was a letter. Nothing in the text says an army was ruled out on principle, and Sulaymān will later speak firmly about what would follow refusal. But the opening move is an address to a mind. That is consistent with how the hoopoe framed the problem: a people misled by an adorned appearance, barred from a road they cannot see. People in that condition are not corrected by force, because force changes behaviour and leaves the appearance intact.

**"Then Stand Aside"**

The instruction *thumma tawalla ʿanhum* — then turn away from them — is a piece of practical tradecraft. The hoopoe is to deliver and withdraw, not to stay and argue, explain, or press for an answer.

Two things are accomplished by the withdrawal. The recipients are left to react without a foreign presence standing over them, which means the reaction observed is genuine rather than performed for an audience. And the messenger is protected; a lone bird in a strange court after delivering a demand from a rival king is in an exposed position. The Arabic of the next instruction, *fa-anẓur mādhā yarjiʿūn*, tells him to watch what they send back — so the withdrawal is not a departure. He is to get out of the room and keep observing. Anyone who has tried to learn what people really think of a proposal will recognise the method: deliver it, leave, and watch what happens when you are assumed to be gone.

**The Excuse Is Accepted in Practice**

Al-Qurṭubī draws from this exchange the principle that a leader should accept the legitimate excuses of those under him, and the text bears him out in action rather than in words. Sulaymān never announces that the hoopoe is forgiven. He simply gives him a mission.

That is the more convincing form of acceptance. A pardon can be announced and then quietly withdrawn in the assignment of duties; entrusting a diplomatic errand to a bird suspected of desertion is a statement that cannot be taken back. Al-Qurṭubī cites in this connection a report that none loves excuses more than God, and that this is why He revealed the Books and sent the messengers — the idea being that revelation itself functions as an acceptance of excuses in advance, since a warned people can plead ignorance no longer, and a people given the means to correct themselves are being offered the route out rather than the verdict. I record the report as he transmits it, without a chain I have been able to check. **Watching the Reply Is the Real Intelligence**

The final clause reveals what Sulaymān is actually after. He does not ask the bird to bring back an answer; he asks him to observe *how* they respond. The content of the reply matters less than the manner of it.

That distinction is sophisticated. A ruler who receives a demanding letter from a more powerful neighbour can react in several ways — defiance, submission, silence, delay, negotiation — and each reveals something the words of the reply may conceal. As it happens, the queen's reaction is to summon her council and ask for advice, which is precisely the kind of information a formal answer would not have carried. Sulaymān's instruction anticipates that a response is a behaviour before it is a text. What his letter said, and how she received it, are reported next.

**Writing as an Instrument of Government**

The verse assumes something easy to pass over: Sulaymān has a letter, already composed, and a means of sealing and sending it. His kingdom has a chancery. Alongside the jinn and the birds, the apparatus described here is the ordinary machinery of a literate state.

This matters for how the Qur'an presents prophetic kingship. Dāwūd is credited with metalwork, Sulaymān with a written diplomacy, Yūsuf with the administration of a granary during famine. None of these is magical, and the Book records them alongside the miracles without distinguishing in tone. A prophet's competence in the ordinary crafts of government is treated as part of what God gave him, which is consistent with verse 15's unqualified *knowledge* and verse 16's share of all things. The letter is the least spectacular thing in this passage and the most consequential: it is what actually changes Sheba. Two thousand kilometres away, a wind-commanding king with jinn at his disposal achieves his result with ink.
