## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:22

> Do not marry former wives of your fathers—except what was done previously. It was indeed a shameful, despicable, and evil practice.

### Expanded Commentary

**Ending a pre-Islamic marriage custom**

The verse prohibits marrying women whom one’s fathers married. In the society addressed by the Qur’an, a deceased man’s widow could be treated as available to his male heir or stepson, sometimes as part of the property of the household. The ban is absolute for the future, while “except what has passed” recognizes that people had acted before the ruling and are not condemned for an act whose prohibition had not yet reached them.

**Marriage is not inheritance**

The placement after the prohibition on inheriting women is significant. A widow’s connection to a deceased husband does not make her transferable to his sons or brothers. She has her own will and dignity. A family may feel that preserving property or alliances justifies the arrangement, but revelation separates lineage from ownership. The widow is not a bridge by which property remains in the male line.

The rule also protects the integrity of family roles. The woman who was a father’s wife cannot become a son’s wife; what one generation treated as marriage cannot be repackaged by another generation as an ordinary contract. The prohibition is about the structure of kinship as well as about consent.

**Three moral descriptions**

The act is called indecent, hateful, and an evil way. These terms address different dimensions: its violation of modesty, its moral repugnance, and its consequences as a path that deforms family life. The Qur’an does not treat every inherited custom as sacred merely because ancestors practiced it. A custom is tested against justice, revelation, and the protection of persons.

The verse illustrates a broader method of reform. It names the practice clearly, closes the door for the future, and does not turn pre-revelation history into a pretext for endless guilt. Communities today likewise need the courage to end customs that make widows, divorced women, or dependants into objects of family control, even when those customs carry the prestige of age.

**Reform begins by naming the object**

The widow who was once treated as part of a household inventory becomes visible as a person with a decision. Communities often need this same act of naming when harmful customs are defended through euphemism. Once a practice is described as coercion rather than tradition, its moral evaluation becomes harder to evade.
