## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:22

> Do not marry former wives of your fathers—except what was done previously. It was indeed a shameful, despicable, and evil practice.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Marriage the New Community Must Leave Behind**

The command *wa lā tankiḥū mā nakaḥa ābāʾukum min al-nisāʾ* — do not marry former wives of your fathers — addresses a practice that pre-Islamic Arabia allowed and that the new Medinan community had to abandon. In that custom, a son might claim his father’s widow as part of the inheritance, sometimes marrying her himself, sometimes marrying her off for a price, sometimes blocking her marriage until she paid to free herself. The verse severs that chain with a plain prohibition. The exception *illā mā qad salaf* — except what was done previously — lifts blame for marriages already contracted before the ruling, without validating them going forward. The Qur’an uses the same form elsewhere to close a prior allowance while opening a new standard, as with the prohibition of marrying two sisters together except what is past (4:23). By naming the act and then excepting the past, the sūrah shows a community in transition: what was done without knowledge while the old norm stood is left to God’s forbearance, but what is done after clarity is a chosen breach.

**Three Names for One Act**

The condemnation *innahu kāna fāḥishatan wa maqtan wa sāʾa sabīlā* — it was indeed a shameful, despicable, and evil practice — gathers three distinct terms. *Fāḥishah* marks it as openly foul, an act whose indecency is visible. *Maqt* — despicable, hated — marks how God and sound nature regard it; it is not merely improper but loathed. *Sāʾa sabīlā* — evil way, evil path — marks its social effect as a road that spreads harm if taken. The layering is deliberate. A marriage that confuses generations blurs lineage, injures the honour of the father, wounds the son by placing him in his father’s intimate place, and reduces the widow at her most vulnerable moment to an asset passed on. Early commentators noted the specific occasion where a man sought to marry his father’s widow after the father’s death and the verse came to forbid it, but the ruling is general. The severity of the three names matches the gravity of those harms. The act is not described as merely disliked or discouraged; it is described as hateful in a way that should stop imitation before it starts.

**Why the Father’s Wife Is Singled Out**

The fuller list of prohibited degrees will follow immediately (4:23), naming mothers, daughters, sisters, aunts, nieces, foster-mothers and sisters, mothers-in-law, stepdaughters under guardianship where the mother’s marriage was consummated, sons’ wives, and two sisters together. This verse stands before that list as a separate, emphatic prohibition because the father’s widow had been treated differently from other relatives in custom. She was not seen as a mother, yet she held the father’s place in the household. The Qur’an treats her as inviolable for marriage to the son not because she is biologically a mother but because the father’s intimate covenant deserves reverence after his death. The same principle appears in the protection of dowries as a firm commitment (4:21); both teach that intimacy creates limits that outlive the moment. To marry the father’s former wife erases the distinction between father and son and turns the widow’s waiting period into an auction. By forbidding it with the three names, the verse restores the father’s honour, secures the widow’s freedom to be treated as a person with her own share and choice, and clarifies lineage for inheritance that depends on clear degrees.

**Except What Was Done Previously**

The clause except what was done previously functions as clemency and as a limit. It tells those who had already contracted such a marriage before revelation that they are not to be punished retroactively, but they are to separate going forward. Jurists understood *mā qad salaf* here as annulling the continuity of such unions, requiring separation where the marriage still stood, while not treating the prior act as sin with worldly penalty where it was done in ignorance of the coming law. The same phrasing for two sisters together in the next verse (4:23) was read similarly, while the later clarification *“Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”* (4:23) confirms that repentance and correction are accepted. That pairing of prohibition and forbearance reflects the sūrah’s broader pattern of setting limits — *ḥudūd* — and then showing the path back for those who had crossed them before the limit was drawn. The sūrah has already linked limits to Gardens and Fire (4:13–14) and has defined soon repentance versus delayed words at death (4:17–18). Here it applies that pattern to family formation: the limit is now clear, the past is pardoned where it was done without that clarity, and persistence after clarity changes the moral status entirely.

**Honouring the Father Without Inheriting the Woman**

The verse’s prohibition complements the earlier verse that forbade inheriting women against their will (4:19). There, the practice was to inherit widows as part of the estate; here, the specific form is the son taking the father’s widow. Both practices share the same error: treating a woman as transferable property rather than as a rights-bearing person who has her own obligatory share (4:7, 4:12), her own dowry (4:4), and her own choice. The sūrah’s opening — *“be mindful of your Lord who created you from a single soul, and from it its mate”* (4:1) — already frames women as the mate from the single soul, not as an item to be passed. The prohibition therefore does more than regulate marriage; it rehumanizes the widow at a moment when custom had depersonalized her. She is not to be claimed by a son, not to be blocked from remarriage, and not to be priced. Her future is to be decided by her own consent within the lawful degrees that follow, where her choice is respected and her dowry is again secured.

**A Shameless Path That Leaves No Good Behind**

The final phrase *sāʾa sabīlā* — evil way — warns that allowing this path harms more than the two persons involved. A way — *sabīl* — is a beaten track; once opened, others follow. If sons could marry fathers’ widows, households would become sites of expectation and rivalry while the father lived, and of dispute and coercion after he died. The sūrah’s later command to appoint mediators from both families when a split is feared (4:35) assumes families can mediate because roles are clear; a practice that confuses father and son destroys that clarity. By naming the practice fāḥishah, maqt, and evil way, the verse makes its rejection a matter of taste as well as law. The believer is asked not only to obey a rule but to feel dislike for what God calls hateful. That dislike, once learned, steadies the longer list of prohibitions that follows. If the son’s claim to his father’s wife is hateful, then the many other forms of overreaching with women — seizing dowries, harassing wives to ransom themselves, and the list of forbidden marriages — will be recognized as part of the same wrong and left behind as well.
