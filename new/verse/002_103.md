## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:103

> If only they were faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, there would have been a better reward from Allah, if only they knew!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counter-Offer Filed Against the Purchase**

One sentence answers the ledger of verse 102, and the answer is structured as the offer the buyers never took: *if only they were faithful and mindful of Allah, there would have been a better reward from Allah*. The commentators state the comparison's terms plainly — Allah's recompense is better than what they purchased with their souls — and the verse's whole force is in the word *better*. Not merely other, not merely safe: better on the buyer's own axis of evaluation, better as return, better as acquisition, better in the currency the purchase was made in. The counter-offer thereby meets the magic-buyers inside their own economics rather than outside it: you sought gain, the verse says, and the gain you refused exceeds the gain you took — not in the Hereafter alone but at the moment of transaction, since the reward named is *from Allah*, held by the party whose possession nothing devalues. The conditional's mood carries the tragedy: *if only they were* — the offer is stated in the irrealis, the mood of the unactualized, which files what the counter-offer's status now is. It was available at the purchase; it remains the verse's content; and its unclaimed standing is the recital's quietest indictment — the better reward not withdrawn but bypassed, sitting in the offer while the buyers walked away holding harm.

**Faith and Mindfulness: The Two Conditions**

The offer's conditions are the pair the sūrah runs on: faith — *āmanū* — and mindfulness of Allah — *ittaqaw*, the taqwā that names the whole posture of guarded reverence, the life organized around the presence of the One to be guarded against offending. The pairing is not additive but structural, and verse 102's own scene demonstrates why both are needed. The magic-buyers' failure ran at both levels: they disbelieved in the share they knew they were forfeiting — faith's absence — and they crossed the warned boundary the angels' teaching had marked — taqwā's absence. The warning-clause built into that teaching, *we are only a test, so do not abandon your faith*, addressed precisely the two conditions together: the test required faith to be believed and taqwā to be passed. A knowledge of hidden forces without faith is a menu with no forbidden items; the same knowledge without taqwā is a boundary with no fear at its edge. The verse's conditions are therefore the exact complement of the purchase: faith answers the *no share in the Hereafter* the buyers knew and paid anyway, and mindfulness answers the *do not disbelieve* they heard and crossed. The pair also carries the sūrah's standing definition of the religion it offers — belief held as assent and enacted as vigilance — and its appearance here, at the end of the magic-episode, restates the definition against the episode's whole alternative: a life steered by unseen forces purchased from warned-against sources, set against a life steered by the Unseen Himself, received on faith and kept in fear.

**“If Only They Knew”: The Suspended Knowing**

The verse ends where verse 102 ended, on the same suspended phrase — *if only they knew* — and the repetition is the passage's hinge. Verse 102's clause landed against a stated fact: they *already knew* the buyer's cost, which made the closing *if only they knew* an irony — the knowing present and inoperative. Verse 103's clause lands against the counter-offer: the better reward is real, from Allah, available on two conditions — and the knowing of *that* is what remains missing. The two verses together complete the diagnosis of this community's epistemic tragedy: the knowledge they possessed did not save them from the purchase, and the knowledge they lacked concerned the only thing worth having instead. The recital has been building this portrait for thirty verses — distortion after understanding, wishes stopped by records, denials classified as rebellion, covenants cast by non-believers — and the doubled *if only they knew* names the pattern's essence: knowledge and faith are not the same possession, and the gap between them is the width of the will. The verse's exclamation is therefore not condescension but grief in grammatical form — the *if only* of a speaker watching someone choose the lesser good with full information about the greater, which is the one failure argument cannot repair, because argument supplies information and the information was already supplied.

**The Better Reward, Unnamed**

The reward the verse offers is not described, and the absence of description is the offer's rhetoric. Verse 102's merchandise came itemized: the rift between spouses, the harm without benefit, the share forfeited — the purchase's contents fully specified, and specified as loss. Verse 103's better reward carries no inventory: *a reward from Allah*, defined by its source alone. The asymmetry is deliberate and it teaches the two economies' natures. The devils' recitation sells named effects — precise, bounded, and hollow, every item a harm wearing a benefit's label. Allah's reward is named by its Giver because its contents exceed itemization: the recital has already filed samples of it — gardens with rivers beneath, whatever the heart's labor finds waiting with Allah — and its description-language consistently outruns list-making. The buyer between the two offers is therefore choosing between a specified small thing and an unspecified great one, and the choice requires exactly the two conditions the verse names: faith to trust an unseen reward's reality, taqwā to hold a warned boundary against a visible technique's allure. The unnamed reward is not vagueness but superabundance — and the verse's final service to its reader is making the comparison honestly: the purchase promises what it can specify and delivers harm; the offer specifies nothing and delivers from Allah. Between a named loss and an unnamed better, the recital asks its audience to locate the real ignorance — and it is not on the offer's side.

**The Offer Stands**

The conditional's tense — *there would have been* — reads as past, closing a case on people who chose; but the recital's whole method forbids taking its closures as final, because every verdict it files in the second person is filed to be re-heard. The offer's conditions are standing: faith and mindfulness remain available, the better reward remains from Allah, and the counter-offer is restated every time the verse is recited — which is to say, in every prayer that carries this sūrah's recitation forward. The magic-buyers of Madina are the verse's occasion, not its boundary; the purchase it condemns has successors in every technique of every age that promises steering of the unseen at a price the warned boundary forbids — and the counter-offer addresses each successor's buyers in the same irrealis, which becomes real the moment a buyer turns. This is the verse's structural mercy inside the passage's severity: verse 102's ledger closed with a soul sold and a share forfeited; verse 103 reopens the transaction from the credit side, stating the better that was refused in terms that remain purchasable. The sūrah's economics has now completed its first full circuit — the cheap price of verse 41, the fleeting gain of verse 79, the trade of verse 86, the miserable price of verse 90 and verse 102 — and at the circuit's end stands the only counter-entry the recital ever files: a better reward, from Allah, for faith and mindfulness. The knowing the verse wishes for its subjects is the knowing it offers its readers, and the offer's final clause is addressed to whoever is still choosing: if only they knew.
