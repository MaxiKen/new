## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:103

> If only they were faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, there would have been a better reward from Allah, if only they knew!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence of Counterfactual Regret**

The verse states what would have resulted from a different course of conduct, and it does so without describing the reward. The Book's habit in such sentences is to leave the content unspecified and to make the comparison the point: whatever God gives is better than what the community purchased, and the reader is left to weigh the two.

The sentence follows the long account of magic and its costs, and its brevity is part of its effect. After a description of practices that harm those who use them, the verse offers a single alternative and states its superiority, and the exclamation at the end conveys regret rather than argument. The comparison also assumes that two things are being weighed, which the language of purchase in the previous verse supports. One of them could be counted and spent; the other is described as better without a figure attached, and the Book leaves the magnitude of the difference to the reader's own sense of what lasts.

**Faith and Mindfulness Together**

The two conditions named are the ones the sūrah has used throughout its account of the community: belief and a conscious awareness of God. The second term describes a protective attentiveness, the disposition of a person who acts as though he is aware of being seen, and the Book uses it as the practical counterpart of belief.

Placing the two together is characteristic of the Book's ethics. Belief without awareness produces the conduct described in the preceding verses, and awareness without belief has no object. The sentence presents them as a single condition, and the reward is attached to the pair. The Book's descriptions of the successful often pair these two qualities, and its warnings to the believers describe the loss of either one as the beginning of decline. The sentence in this verse states the pair as the condition on which the better reward hung.

**Better Than What Was Purchased**

The word rendered better echoes the language of trade used at the end of the previous verse, where the community's practices were described as a purchase that cost them the Hereafter. This sentence sets the divine reward against that purchase, and the comparison is between something acquired through a transaction and something given freely by God.

The Book makes the same comparison in other passages where it contrasts what people obtain by their own arrangements with what God grants. The sūrah's argument throughout this section is that the community had been offered the better option and had chosen the worse, and the verse states the difference in the form of a lament.

**The Repeated Exclamation**

The sentence ends with a phrase that has appeared several times in this passage: if only they knew. The expression is not a comment on the community's intelligence, since the preceding verses state plainly that the community understood what it was doing and knew that those who dealt in such practices had no share in the Hereafter.

What the phrase marks is the gap between information and estimation. The community knew the facts and had not priced them correctly, and the Book's repeated exclamation draws attention to that failure of valuation rather than to a failure of knowledge. The Book's use of the phrase in this passage is a measure of how much the community lost, and the loss is described in terms of estimation rather than of ability. What the sentence laments is not a shortage of information but a failure to treat what was known as decisive.

**What the Verse Offers to Its Own Readers**

The sentence is addressed to a community that had made its choice, and the Book presents the alternative for the benefit of others. A reader who has followed the passage from the covenant through the calf and the magic has been shown a sequence of decisions and their costs, and this verse supplies the summary: faith and awareness would have produced a better return.

The sūrah's later instructions to the believers restate the same conditions in positive form, commanding the prayer and the alms and promising that whatever good a person sends ahead he will find with God. The two passages belong together, since one describes what the community lost and the other describes how it can be obtained.

**The Short Verse as a Pivot**

The sentence is brief and stands between the long account of the community's practices and the instruction that follows for the believers. That placement gives it the role of a hinge. The passage's account of what was rejected is complete, and the sūrah moves next to the conduct of the Muslim community, beginning with a command about how its members address the Prophet ﷺ.

Read in that sequence, the verse carries a practical edge. The community's loss is presented not as a subject for speculation about its fate but as a warning to the people now receiving revelation, and the conditions it names are the ones the sūrah will keep repeating as the chapter continues.

**What the Community Could Have Had**

The reward the verse mentions is left undescribed, and the omission is deliberate. The Book's habit in such sentences is to make the comparison the point rather than to inventory the alternative, and the reader is left to weigh what was purchased against what was available. The sūrah describes the Garden at length elsewhere in the chapter, and the details it supplies there fill in what this sentence leaves open.

What the verse provides is the structure of the decision: a community chose one course when another was on offer, and the difference between them was not a matter of degree. The next verses turn to the believers with instructions about prayer and charity, and the placement makes the passage's purpose plain. The account of what was lost stands beside the commands that describe how it can still be obtained.

**A Note on the Book's Method**

The verse does not explain why the community chose as it did, and it adds no threat to the regret it expresses. The Book repeatedly presents its warnings in this register, leaving the reader to infer the consequence from the comparison on offer.

The effect is a passage that argues by showing rather than by accusing, and the reader who has followed the account is expected to reach the conclusion without being told. The next verses continue in a different key, turning from the record of a community that lost its way to instructions for the community that has accepted the message. The chapter's warnings are consistently framed this way, leaving the hearer to work out the cost of a course of action from the comparison the verse presents. The method expects an attentive reader, and the sūrah's later commands assume the same attention.
