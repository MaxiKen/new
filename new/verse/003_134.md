## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:134

> ˹They are˺ those who donate in prosperity and adversity, control their anger, and pardon others. And Allah loves the good-doers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Portrait of the People Described**

The verse continues the description of the people for whom the garden is prepared, and it gives three practices: giving in ease and hardship, restraining anger, and pardoning others. It ends by stating that God loves those who do good.

The three items cover the three situations in which a person's character is tested. The first concerns wealth, the second concerns provocation, and the third concerns power over someone who has done harm. Each of them is a place where most people fail in a predictable way, and the verse names the practice that constitutes success in each.

**Giving When It Is Easy and When It Is Hard**

The phrase about ease and hardship has been explained in two ways. Al-Ṭabarī read the first term as describing a time of plenty, so that the verse praises the person who gives both when he has much and when he has little. Al-Rāzī read the pair as describing the giver's feelings, so that the person gives whether the giving pleases him or costs him something.

The two readings cover the same ground. The Book elsewhere praises those who give out of what they value and warns against giving only what is worthless. The verse under discussion names the two conditions under which giving is easy to abandon, and it places the practice of the people described beyond both of them.

**Restraining Anger, and the Word for It**

The word used here for anger describes the internal movement of resentment, and one commentator distinguished it from the term that describes anger expressed in words or actions: the first is the feeling, and the second is the feeling made visible.

The distinction matters for what the verse praises. The people described are not said to be free of the feeling; they are said to hold it back. The Book's standard for conduct is that a person governs what he does with what he feels, and the verse places that government among the marks of those for whom the garden has been prepared.

**A Report About Strength**

The Prophet's definition of strength takes up the same theme. A narration from Abū Hurayrah reports him saying that the strong person is not the one who overpowers others, but the one who controls himself while in a state of anger (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6114, Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2609).

The report gives the verse's instruction the weight of a redefinition. A community that measured strength by the ability to dominate is told that the quality it admires belongs to the person who governs himself, and the Book's praise of those who restrain their anger is the same valuation stated in the language of the Hereafter, where the reward is described as a filling of the heart with security and faith.

**Pardoning Those Within One's Power**

The last of the three practices is the pardoning of others, and the commentators noted that the term applies most naturally to those over whom a person has authority. It is easy to forgive someone who cannot be called to account; the verse praises the forgiveness that is offered by someone who could have taken his due.

The Book states the same ethic in other passages. One describes the righteous as those who, when they are angered, forgive (42:37). Another states that whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation will find his reward with God (42:40). A third commands the believers to respond to evil with what is best, and observes that the person with whom one is in a feud may then become a close friend (41:34).


The verse closes with a sentence about God's love for those who do good, and the Book attaches that love to particular qualities in different passages. Here it is attached to the combination the verse has just described.

The statement is the highest form of praise the Book offers, and its placement at the end of a list of difficult practices is deliberate. The community is told that the behaviour described is not merely advisable but beloved, and the sentence supplies the motivation that the preceding items require.

**The Character of a Community Prepared for the Garden**

Read together with the previous verse, this sentence supplies the content of the phrase about those who are mindful of God. The people for whom the garden is prepared are those whose dealings with money, with provocation, and with power have a particular shape.

That is how the sūrah defines its terms. Rather than leaving the quality of mindfulness as an abstraction, it shows the conduct of the people who possess it, and the next verse completes the portrait by describing how they handle their own failures.

**Three Tests Named in a Single Verse**

The practices listed in this verse correspond to three situations in which a person's character is exposed. Money reveals whether a person's generosity depends on his comfort; provocation reveals whether his conduct depends on his temper; and power over someone who has wronged him reveals whether his sense of justice can be tempered by mercy.

The Book groups them because they belong together. A community in which resources are shared in hard times, tempers are governed, and offences are pardoned is a community that can survive its internal strains, and the sūrah has been concerned with those strains since its commands about unity. The description of the people prepared for the garden is therefore a description of a functioning community as much as of individuals, and the sentence about God's love for those who do good closes the list by placing the whole of it under approval.
