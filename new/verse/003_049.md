## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:49

> and ˹make him˺ a messenger to the Children of Israel ˹to proclaim,˺ ‘I have come to you with a sign from your Lord: I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah’s Will. I will heal the blind and the leper and raise the dead to life— by Allah’s Will. And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses. Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Messenger With a Named Audience**

The announcement now appoints the child to a mission and specifies the people it concerns: he is made a messenger to the Children of Israel. The Book states the same limit when it reports him speaking to that nation and identifying himself as God's messenger sent to them (61:6), a verse that goes on to say he announced a messenger coming after him.

The restriction is worth comparing with the mission given to the Prophet of this sūrah, which the Book describes as addressed to all of humanity (34:28), and with the declaration that he is God's messenger to all people without exception (7:158). The Qur'an does not blur the difference between the two commissions; it states each in its own terms and leaves both standing, and the reader is meant to weigh what each messenger was sent to do.

**The Bird Made From Clay**

The list of signs begins with a piece of work in clay. The account describes him shaping a form and breathing into it, after which it becomes a living bird, and the Book recalls the whole scene in a reminder given to Jesus in the Hereafter, among the favours God bestowed on him and his mother: how he moulded a bird from clay, breathed into it, and it became a living creature by God's permission (5:110).

The detail is preserved nowhere in the four Gospels, and it appears in the sūrah as one of the signs by which a prophet addresses a people who demanded evidence. The Book's presentation of it is not that Jesus had innate creative power. The same passage of the sūrah of the Table repeats the qualifying clause after each of the three great signs, so that the reader hears the limits of the act as often as its wonder.

**"By Allah's Will" — the Clause Attached to the Miracles**

The phrase occurs twice in this verse, once after the bird and once after healing and resurrection, and it is repeated in the reminder at 5:110 with the divine voice saying "by My Will" at each healing and each raising. The pattern is too consistent to be incidental.

The clause does the central work of the sūrah's teaching about Jesus. A sign performed by permission belongs to the one who permits it, and the Book's insistence on the clause at the very moments a reader might conclude otherwise is a deliberate safeguard. It also matches the way the Book describes the Prophet's own miracles elsewhere: works beyond human power occur at the command of God, and the servant who performs them remains a servant.

**Healing the Blind and the Leper**

Two healings are named, and the terms used matter. The first describes a person blind in a way that most commentators took to mean blind from birth, since the Book uses the standard word for blindness elsewhere and a specific form here. The second describes the leper, a sufferer of a disease that produced severe disfigurement and nerve damage, was widely regarded as contagious, and remained incurable by medicine until the modern era.

The Prophet's teaching addressed the fear such illnesses produced in his own society: he rejected the idea that disease transmits itself independently of God's will, and he instructed people in the same report to keep away from a leper as one would flee from a lion (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5707). The instruction captures the reality of the time. Healing a person in that condition, and touching him, was a sign that no physician of the age could offer, and the sūrah places it among the proofs of a prophet's mission.

**Raising the Dead**

The third sign is the greatest in the sequence, and it is named in a single clause. The Book repeats it in the reminder of favours: how he brought the dead to life, by God's permission (5:110). The claim is accepted in the sūrah as a fact about his mission, and the same claim is made in the Gospel accounts, so that a reader of either tradition meets it as familiar ground.

What the sūrah does with it is the same thing it does with the bird from clay: it states the act and attaches the permission. The Book has confronted the argument that such deeds prove divinity by the blunt method of repeating the qualifier, and it has already applied that method to the greater act of creation itself, where the one who says to a thing "Be" is God and the thing that comes to be is a creature.

**Telling Them What They Eat and Store in Their Houses**

The last sign is domestic and quiet, and it is the one that most requires explanation. He informs his people what they consume and what they keep stored at home. Commentators offered several circumstances: as a child he would tell his playmates what their parents were doing or keeping; on another account the report concerned a time when people were forbidden to hoard food; and a third reading connects it to the table sent down from heaven in the sūrah of the Table, where the people were told to eat of the provision but not to store it, and were then taken to task for doing so.

The explanations differ, and they agree on the point of the sign. What is being described is knowledge of hidden private matters, given to the messenger as evidence that his knowledge does not come from the ordinary channels. A prophet who can tell you what is in your own house is making a claim about the source of what he knows, and the claim is the same as the one made by every revelation in the Book.

**"A Sign for You If You Truly Believe"**

The verse closes with a condition, and the condition is the sūrah's recurring theme. The signs are called a sign for you, if you believe; the benefit of evidence depends on the disposition of the person receiving it.

The Book states that principle repeatedly. Signs and warners are of no benefit to those who refuse to believe (10:101), and the Prophet is told that if their rejection is unbearable he should consider that God could have guided them all without any of it, since signs are not what opens a heart (6:35). The list of miracles in this verse is therefore offered to a specific audience, and the Book is honest about what it can and cannot do. Evidence settles a case for a mind that has not already decided, and the miracles of a prophet are addressed to people who are willing to look at them.
