## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:111

> In their stories there is truly a lesson for people of reason. This message cannot be a fabrication, rather ˹it is˺ a confirmation of previous revelation, a detailed explanation of all things, a guide, and a mercy for people of faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"In Their Stories"**

The plural is broader than the chapter. The commentators understand the reference as the stories of the messengers mentioned throughout the Qur'an, of which Joseph's is one and the longest. The verse therefore closes this sūrah by placing it inside a collection, and the collection is what the claim rests on. A single narrative might be dismissed as an invention; a body of narratives sharing a structure — a messenger, a people, delay, despair, help, a selective deliverance — is a different kind of object. Verse 110 has just described that shared structure, and verse 111 draws the conclusion from it in the same breath. Drawing the conclusion in the same breath is characteristic of this ending. No separate argument is mounted for the value of narrative; the stories are named, the account is described as neither invented nor fabricated, and the purpose is stated, all inside a single verse. The sūrah's final claim rests on the form it has actually used for a hundred verses rather than on a defence of that form.

**"A Lesson for People of Reason"**

The noun denotes an admonition, a lesson, something that moves a person from one state to another by way of an example. Those addressed are people of intellect — a phrase the Qur'an uses for the audience capable of drawing an inference from what is presented to them. The specification is important after verse 103, which stated that most people will not believe however keen the messenger is. The lesson exists and is available; the verse names the capacity required to take it. The chapter's own story supplies the exercise: a dream reported in verse 4 and fulfilled in verse 100, with a hundred verses of arrangement between them, is a lesson of exactly this kind.

**"It Is Not a Fabricated Account"**

The denial answers the charge the Qur'an records elsewhere, that its narratives are legends written down and recited. The verse's argument is not that fabrication is impossible but that this account cannot be one, and the ground is given in the next clause: it confirms what came before. A fabricated story would diverge from the earlier revelations it purports to continue; this one corroborates them while supplying detail they do not contain. Verse 102 made the related claim from the other direction — the recipient was not present at the events and had no access to them — and verse 111 makes it from the side of the text's relation to prior scripture. The second ground complements the first. Verse 102 argued from the messenger's lack of access — he was not present at the council and could not have known what was said there; this verse argues from the text's agreement with what came before it. Absence of a source and presence of a match are different kinds of evidence, and the closing verses deploy both.

**"A Confirmation of What Came Before"**

The chapter has repeatedly run alongside the biblical account and diverged from it in specific places: no accusation of spying before the demand for a brother, no fraction taken from the harvest during the years of plenty, no fifth of the produce, no harshness attributed to Joseph at the first meeting, no disbelief on Jacob's part when told his son lives. The confirmations and the corrections belong to the same claim. A text that simply repeated prior accounts would add nothing; a text that contradicted them in their essentials would not confirm them. What verse 111 describes is a third relation, and the hundred preceding verses are its demonstration. That relation is one of agreement rather than dependence. The chapter is neither summarising an earlier scripture nor disputing with one; it reports a story whose details it claims to have received, and it claims that the report confirms what was revealed before it. The demonstration is the narrative itself, which the reader has just completed and can compare against anything else he has been told.

**"A Detailed Explanation of All Things"**

The phrase describes a function rather than a quantity. The Qur'an uses the same description of itself elsewhere and applies a comparable formula to the Torah given to Moses — a guidance, a mercy and an elaboration. The commentators connect it to the way the text sets matters out in detail: rulings, narratives, arguments, and the patterns that connect them. Verse 111's use of the phrase at the end of a narrative sūrah is notable, since what has just been detailed is a story rather than a legal code. The chapter's implicit claim is that a narrative set out with this degree of specificity — a fifteen-year calendar, a legal procedure, a family's moral history — is itself part of the elaboration.

**"A Guidance and a Mercy for People Who Believe"**

The final two terms are joined, and the joining has a condition attached. The guidance and the mercy belong to a people who believe, which does not mean the text withholds itself from others but that its effect is realised in them. Verse 104 described the same message as a reminder to the whole world, with no restriction; verse 111 describes its guidance and mercy as belonging to believers. The chapter holds both claims without reconciling them, and the same structure appears in verse 103: most people will not believe, and the message is addressed to everyone. Availability is universal; benefit is conditional. The condition is what makes the verse an address rather than a summary. A statement about a text describes its contents; a statement about belief describes the reader's position relative to them. The chapter ends by telling its audience that the hundred verses behind this one are capable of guiding and of showing mercy, and that the capability is realised on the reader's side.

**The Chapter's Verdict on Itself**

Verse 111 is the only place in the sūrah where the text evaluates its own content directly. Everything before it is narrative, address or diagnosis. The verdict has four parts: the stories contain a lesson, the account is not invented, it confirms prior revelation and elaborates all things, and it is guidance and mercy for believers. The four parts answer the four questions the closing section raised — where this came from (12:102), why most people refuse it (12:103), what is being asked in return (12:104), and what it is for (12:111). The chapter ends by stating its own purpose in a single verse, after a hundred verses of letting a story carry the argument. Stating the purpose at the end reverses the ordinary order of an argument, and the reversal is the point. A reader told in advance what the story meant would have read it as an illustration; a reader told afterwards has already lived through it, and the statement arrives as a description of something that has happened to him. The sūrah closes by naming what it has just done.

**From the Opening Verses to the Closing One**

The sūrah began with disconnected letters, an oath by a clear Book, and a declaration that it was sent down as an Arabic recitation so that people might understand (12:1–3). It ends with a lesson, a denial of fabrication, a confirmation, an elaboration, a guidance and a mercy. The frame is complete and the middle is a single continuous narrative with no interruption except the doctrinal verses placed inside it. The chapter's claim about itself is therefore not argued separately from its content; the content is the argument. A reader who has followed the story from the dream in verse 4 to the prostration in verse 100 has already been given what verse 111 describes, and the final verse names it.
