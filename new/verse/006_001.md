## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:1

> All praise is for Allah Who created the heavens and the earth and made darkness and light. Yet the disbelievers set up equals to their Lord ˹in worship˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Praise Opens a Case It Does Not Yet Argue**

Praise is given to God before the dispute is named, and that placement is already an argument. The sūrah opens with the formula *al-ḥamdu li-llāh*, and four other sūrahs open the same way, each introducing the claim it will spend itself defending: the Lord of all worlds (1:2), the One Who revealed the Book to His servant (18:1), the Owner of all that is in the heavens and the earth (34:1), and the Originator who made angels messengers with wings (35:1). Praise in these openings is not decoration set above an argument; it establishes jurisdiction. Whoever made the heavens holds the title deed to them, and no court higher than the Maker exists.

The Makkan audience was being asked to weigh a claim about worship against the claims of their own ancestors, their neighbours, and their trade. This verse refuses to begin at that level. It raises the question of ownership first, because a dispute about who deserves worship can only be settled by knowing who made the worshippers.

**The Widest Frame and the Nearest Evidence**

Creation of the heavens and the earth is the largest extent the eye can carry; darkness and light are the smallest daily cycle nobody can escape. Placing them together in one sentence stretches the listener from the edge of the sky to the switch of the lamp. The alternation of night and day is listed among the signs for people of understanding (2:164), and the day and night are said to overlap in rapid succession under their Lord's command (7:54). The point is not the scale of the universe but the intimacy of it: the same will that set the sky in place also decides when the room goes dark.

Nothing in this verse is beyond the audience's own experience. They travelled by the stars, woke with the light, and feared the night. A claim over worship is being pressed on people who had already received, every twenty-four hours, the evidence that something outside themselves controls the switch.

**Darkness in the Plural, Light in the Singular**

Arabic says *ẓulumāt* for darkness, a plural, and *nūr* for light, a singular. The Book keeps both forms: believers are brought out of darkness into light, and those who follow false gods are led out of light into darkness (2:257); guidance brings people out of darkness into light by God's will (5:16); and the question is put plainly, can darkness and light be equal (13:16)? God is named the Light of the heavens and the earth (24:35), and never the darkness of anything.

The grammar carries a claim about error and truth. Falsehoods multiply — one for the tribe, one for the harvest, one for the road — while truth remains one thing that does not need a local version. Makkan idolatry was not a single doctrine but a heap of practices, each with its own shrine and season. The verse's plural tells the reader how many ways out of the light exist, and its singular tells him how many ways back.

**A Charge They Had Already Answered Against Themselves**

The verse closes on the disbelievers setting up equals to their Lord, and the Book treats that charge as self-inflicted. Ask them who created the heavens and the earth and they answer, "Allah" (29:61); the same answer is given in 31:25, and in 43:9 they credit the Almighty, All-Knowing. Commentators report that many Makkans acknowledged a supreme Creator while keeping their idols as intermediaries, which is why the argument keeps returning to creation: the confession is already on their lips, and the practice contradicts it.

An equal shares the rank, not necessarily the workshop. The Quraysh did not claim their stones made the rain; they claimed the rain needed a recommendation. The verse answers that borrowed authority with its own premise: the One who made both the rain and the light did not appoint a partner to receive the request.

**The Sūrah Reported to Have Come Down Whole**

Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by al-Ṭabarānī in *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr* (12/215) and by al-Ḥākim (2/314), said the entire sūrah was revealed in Makkah at night, accompanied by seventy thousand angels raising their voices in glorification; the version in al-Bayhaqī (1/365) says the angels filled the horizon. The chains have been disputed — several variations are weak, and some scholars judge the report ḥasan — while the unity of the sūrah's argument is accepted by most who study its sequence.

That report is worth the pause because it says something about the verse in front of us. The opening is not a fragment that later grew a context; it stands at the head of one continuous case, argued from creation to worship, from Abraham to the dietary rules, without a change of subject.

**al-Ṭabarī Heard an Echo of Genesis**

Early commentators noticed that this opening resembles the first lines of the Torah, where God creates the heavens and the earth and then separates light from darkness (Genesis 1:1–5). Al-Ṭabarī records the observation and leaves it as an observation. The resemblance is real and unsurprising in a city that traded with Jewish and Christian neighbours, and the Qur'an addresses what its audience already knows rather than pretending the knowledge is new.

The difference matters more than the echo. Genesis tells how the world was ordered; this verse tells a people who read the stars who owns them. The first line of the sūrah is therefore not a lesson in cosmology but a deed of title, and everything the sūrah says afterwards about cattle, harvests, and offerings is an argument about who may be thanked for them.
