## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:40

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Imagine if you were overwhelmed by Allah’s torment or the Hour—would you call upon any other than Allah ˹for help˺? ˹Answer me˺ if your claims are true!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Uses the Audience's Own Experience**

The verse takes the Makkans to a situation every traveller in Arabia knew: a moment of overwhelming danger. Facing the punishment of God or the arrival of the Hour, the question asks whether they would call on anything besides Allah. It is not a question about their theology. It is a question about their conduct in the one situation where conduct becomes involuntary, and the answer is demanded with an oath-like challenge: answer me, if your claims are true.

The Book uses this form repeatedly, and it is one of the sharpest arguments in the Makkan sūrahs because it requires no new information from the audience. They are not being asked to accept a premise; they are being asked to remember what they did.

**The Sea That Made Them Monotheists**

The Book records the answer in several places. When they are touched with hardship at sea, they forget every deity they normally invoke, except Him, and when He brings them safely to shore, they turn away (17:67). When a gale overwhelms a ship and the passengers assume they are doomed, they cry out to Allah alone in sincere devotion, promising gratitude in exchange for rescue (10:22). Waves like mountains produce the same result: they call on Him alone, and once delivered, only some remain grateful (31:32). The same pattern is reported in the sūrah of the Spider, where after rescue they associate others with Him once again (29:65).

The sūrah of al-Anʿām itself has already asked the question in a similar form: who rescues you from the darkest times on land and at sea, the One you call upon in humility, openly and secretly, saying that if He rescues you, you will be grateful (6:63). The answer supplied there is that only Allah rescues from this and any other distress, and yet they associate others with Him (6:64).

**Why Emergency Produces Sincerity**

The phenomenon the verse relies on has a simple explanation. A person in a storm has run out of intermediaries. The stone in its niche cannot still the wind, the tribal patron cannot reach the ship, and the star cannot alter the course of the water. What remains is the One the Makkans had never denied as the Creator, and the instinct that reaches for Him under those conditions is the plainest evidence that the deeper knowledge was never actually lost.

That is why the verse can be called an argument from the audience's own nature. The Book elsewhere notes that even the idolaters, when asked who created the heavens and the earth, answer that Allah did (31:25; 43:9). The crisis on the water simply dismantles the layer of practice that had been built over that confession.

**"If Your Claims Are True"**

The challenge at the end of the verse places the burden of proof on the people making the claims. The Makkans claimed that their deities had standing and could be approached for help. The verse puts the claim into the only test the claimants had never had to face, and asks them to supply the evidence from their own experience.

There is a pointed restraint in the demand. The Book does not ask them to abandon the idols on the basis of a revelation they did not accept. It asks them to notice what they themselves do when revelation is the last thing on their mind, and to draw the conclusion from the one moment when their practice and their belief were forced to agree.

**Torment or the Hour: Two Ways for the Test to Arrive**

The verse names the two forms the reckoning could take: a punishment in this world, or the Hour itself. The Book treats both as legitimate possibilities for its audience, and it describes the first as having already happened to earlier nations while the second remains fixed for everyone. The next verse in this sūrah addresses the same subject from the side of the wrongdoers and their fate when it comes.

For the sūrah's argument the pairing matters, because it removes the escape of a distant deadline. A person who reasoned that the judgment was far away still had to reckon with the possibility of a tribulation in the middle of his own life, and the Book tells its audience elsewhere that they might be taken while travelling on a road or asleep in a house.

**What the Verse Establishes for the Argument Ahead**

Once this question is asked, the sūrah has closed the gap between the audience's beliefs and their conduct. Whatever a man may say about the idols when he is safe and surrounded by his clan, the verse has secured his admission that in the moment of real need he would call on Allah alone. Every later argument in the sūrah about the absurdity of associating partners is built on that confession.

The Book therefore treats the rest of the discussion as unnecessary. If the One who is called in the storm is the One who answers, then the question of who deserves worship has already been answered by the worshippers themselves, and the next sentence simply states the conclusion they have been avoiding.

There is a further implication the audience would have felt. A deity who is not called upon in the emergency is not performing the function of a god at all, whatever his shrine receives during the seasons of pilgrimage. The verse does not dispute the idols' existence; it points out their total absence from the one moment in which the word god has any content.
