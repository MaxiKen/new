## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:51

> And ˹remember˺ when We appointed forty nights for Moses, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Appointment and an Absence**

The verse places two events in a single sentence: a meeting arranged between God and Moses, and the conduct of the community while their leader was away. The two are joined by the word rendered *then*, which makes the second event follow the first in time and stand against it in character. The reader gets no explanation of the interval; the Book allows the contrast to do the work.

The structure is characteristic of this section, where the favours and the failures of the community are arranged next to each other. Deliverance from Pharaoh and the crossing of the sea have just been recited; the appointment on the mountain is a further honour granted to the people through their prophet; and the sentence ends with what the people did with the time it gave them.

**The Forty Nights**

The Book states the duration of the appointment in one number and explains the detail elsewhere: *“We appointed for Moses thirty nights then added another ten— completing his Lord's term of forty nights.”* (7:142). The passage also records what Moses did before he left, placing his brother in charge of the people with an instruction to act rightly and not to follow the corruptors.

The thirty plus ten tells the reader that the period was extended, and the sūrah of al-Baqarah compresses the whole into the final total. What matters for the argument here is that the period was set by God and known to Moses. The community was not abandoned in the dark about their affairs; they had a leader who had been summoned, a deputy who had been appointed, and a stated duration to wait.

**What They Made While He Was Away**

The Qur'an describes the object in detail in another sūrah, and the description aims at its absurdity: *“In the absence of Moses, his people made from their ˹golden˺ jewellery an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound. Did they not see that it could neither speak to them nor guide them to the ˹Right˺ Path? Still they took it as a god and were wrongdoers.”* (7:148). The Book's account names the material, which was the wealth they had been given, and the sound, which was the full extent of its powers.

The verses in al-Baqarah do not repeat these details, and the omission is part of the sūrah's manner in this section: it states the act and lets the reader who knows the fuller account supply the picture. What the shortened version preserves is the sequence — appointment, absence, worship of a thing made by hand — and the judgment it deserves.

**"Acting Wrongfully"**

The closing phrase uses the same word the sūrah has applied to Adam's transgression and to Iblīs's refusal in different forms. The Book's vocabulary for wrong in this chapter is consistent: it describes the person who places a thing where it does not belong, and it applies the term to the one who damages himself as readily as to the one who damages others.

Here the wrong is unmistakable. A people who had been rescued from a regime that claimed divine status had turned, within weeks of their rescue, to an object they had manufactured. The Book does not present this as a lapse in information. The command against association with God had been given, the signs had been public, and the man who had led them out of Egypt was still on the mountain that had been split open before their eyes.

**The Test Hidden in a Leader's Absence**

The episode establishes a pattern the sūrah uses for the rest of its address to this community. A leader is removed for a while, and the community's conduct in the interval reveals what their commitment rested on. The Book elsewhere reports Moses' own words on returning and Aaron's answer about being unable to control the people, and it treats the failure as a test of the community rather than of its prophet.

That reading is consistent with the sūrah's earlier account of Adam, where the test was also a relatively small prohibition under conditions of abundance. The Qur'an tests by removing supports rather than by applying pressure directly, and the absence of Moses was the removal of a support. What the calf-worship shows is that the faith of the crowd had been anchored to a man rather than to the Lord who sent him.

**What the Book Makes of the Episode**

The sūrah returns to this failure later in the chapter when it recounts the community's own words about it, and it uses the incident as one item in a long list of violations. The Book's purpose in repeating it is not to humiliate an ancient people but to make the record complete, because the request being made to their descendants in Madinah rests on the whole of it.

Placed where it is, the verse also prepares the reader for what follows. The sūrah has just described the rescue at the sea, and it now reports the first major failure in the community's history as a free people. The verses that follow will state the pardon extended to them, which means the passage is moving in its familiar rhythm: gift, failure, mercy, and a fresh invitation.

**Jewellery From Egypt and the Speed of the Decline**

The Book notes where the material for the idol came from: gold ornaments, which in the fuller account were the jewellery the community had carried out of Egypt (7:148). What was given as the spoil of their rescue was melted down to make the thing that replaced their rescuer. The detail is not incidental; it is the Book's way of showing how quickly a gift can be turned against its giver.

The interval matters as well. The appointment lasted forty nights, which is a short period even by ordinary human measure. Within it, a people who had watched the sea open took up worship of an object they had made and could hear making a lowing sound. The sūrah does not explain the psychology of the change, and it does not need to. The sequence is stated, and the reader is left with the contrast between what they had seen and what they did.
