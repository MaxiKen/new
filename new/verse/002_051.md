## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:51

> And ˹remember˺ when We appointed forty nights for Moses, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Appointment of Forty Nights**

The recital's second entry opens with a divine appointment: *wāʿadnā Mūsā arbaʿīna laylah* — We set a term for Moses of forty nights. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf files the same appointment with its arithmetic exposed: *"We appointed for Moses thirty nights then added another ten— completing his Lord's term of forty nights. Moses commanded his brother Aaron, 'Take my place among my people, do what is right, and do not follow the way of the corruptors'"* (7:142). The term was the miqāt — the tryst at the mountain for the giving of the Torah — and its length carries the Qur'an's numerology of completion: forty is the number at which a thing ripens, the span of formation before reception. The nights are named rather than the days because Arabic reckoning runs by the moon — the night precedes and gives its name to the day — and because the vigil's interior is a night-work: Moses fasting, watching, waiting on revelation. The appointment's structure matters for what follows: a prophet called away for a fixed term, a deputy installed with instructions, and a people left in the interval. Every element of that arrangement becomes material in the crisis the same verse reports, and the verse reports both halves in one breath — the appointment *and* the calf — so the reader holds the gift and the failure together.

**Ittakhadhtum: The Calf Was Taken**

The verb of the sin is *ittakhadhtum* — you took, adopted, took up. The translation renders it *worshipped*, and the rendering is right about the act, but the Arabic keeps the act's grammar of acquisition: idolatry here is not something that happened to the people but something they *took*, the way one takes a position, a tool, or a side. The taking was manufactured. Sūrah Ṭā Hā stages the scene: *"Then he moulded for them an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound. They said, 'This is your god and the god of Moses, but Moses forgot ˹where it was˺!'"* (20:88). The ḥadīth of the Qur'an's telling is in the details — the calf was moulded, not found; it lowed by artifice, a sound engineered into a hollow body; and the proclamation wrapped the new god in the old religion's language, claiming it for Moses himself. The people did not abandon their vocabulary; they re-pointed it. This is the anatomy of the taken idol: it arrives wearing continuity, announced in the idiom of the faith it replaces, and its adoption feels to the adopters like fidelity rather than defection — *Moses forgot*, they said, and thought themselves the better keepers of his god.

**The Sin of the Interval**

The temporal clause is the verse's quiet center: *in his absence* — the calf was taken while Moses was away, inside the forty nights, in the interval between the prophet's departure and his return. The people had everything they needed for the interval: a deputy installed by the prophet himself, explicit instructions against the corruptors' way (7:142), a fixed term — the appointment was announced, not open-ended. The failure was therefore not of information but of duration: a faith that held under the prophet's visible mediation did not hold under his announced absence. The verse exposes the difference between believing in Allah and believing in a prophet's presence — the second collapses at the first miqāt, and the first is what the covenant of verse 40 actually contracted for. The interval is also where the Sāmirī worked: the Qur'an's fuller accounts show him gathering, melting, shaping — craftsmanship performed in public over time. The calf was not a sudden panic but a project, built unhindered across days of the prophet's absence by a people who watched it take shape and said nothing until it lowed.

**Ẓālimīn: The Wrong Landed on the Wrongdoers**

The verse closes its accusation with a circumstantial participle — *acting wrongfully*, *wa-antum ẓālimūn*. Ẓulm is the placement of a thing outside its proper place: the word for injustice, for tyranny, and for the mislocation at the heart of sin. Applied to shirk it is the Qur'an's standing diagnosis — shirk is named the great ẓulm (31:13) precisely because it puts worship where worship does not belong. The participle's form is equally precise: *you* were wrongdoers — the sentence's subject and its wronged party coincide. The calf did not injure Pharaoh's memory, the Egyptians, or the desert; the taking of it injured the takers, severing them from the favor that had just defined them as a delivered people. Sūrah al-Baqarah returns to the same event with the wrongness filed in the heart's own hydraulics: *"Moses came to you with clear proofs, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully"* (2:92) — the charge restated almost word for word later in the sūrah — and then, in the verse after that, the interior mechanism: *the love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief* (2:93), a people made to drink the idol as one drinks water, the calf's love soaking the heart through the disbelief that opened it. The act was public; the ẓulm was capillary.

**The Charge Refiled on the Descendants**

Verse 51 addresses the wilderness generation as *you* — and the sūrah will file the identical charge against the Madinan descendants in the same second person (2:92), which is the inheritance logic of verse 49 operating in the negative direction: the favors flow down the line, and so do the liabilities of the family record. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the descendant generation's version of the pattern plainly: the People of the Book who demanded a revelation in writing from heaven are reminded that their forebears demanded greater — make Allah visible to us — and that they *"took the calf for worship after receiving clear signs"* (4:153). The verse's own structure anticipates the refiling: clear proofs precede the taking in every account (the sea still wet behind them, the manna still arriving), which makes the calf not a failure of evidence but a failure of the interval. And the refiling answers the objection every descendant raises about ancestral crime — that no soul bears another's burden — by identifying what actually descends: not guilt but pattern. The same heart-drink is offered in every generation; verse 51 is read by Madinans as a mirror because their own interval — a prophet's absence by death, a community's custody of his deposit — runs the same test in slower time.

**What the Interval Tests**

The forty nights are therefore a fixture of the Qur'an's moral pedagogy, and verse 51 installs it: the interval tests what the presence cannot. Under a prophet's eye, obedience and awe are indistinguishable; under his announced absence, only what was actually faith remains standing. The verse pairs with verse 45's method clause — patience and prayer are exactly the equipment of intervals, the disciplines that hold a person on the line when the line's author is not visibly walking it — and the calf is what patience and prayer were not called in to prevent. The Qur'an's communities after this one live wholly inside such an interval: the final Prophet ﷺ has died, the miqāt of history runs on, and the lowing shapes are moulded in public by craftsmen who claim continuity with him. Verse 51's value to that condition is diagnostic before it is hortatory: it tells the waiting community where the failure will present — not in the open denial of verse 41, not in the sale of verse 41's signs, but in the taken thing, adopted during the wait, dressed in the vocabulary of the faith, and loved before the prophet returns. The audit of verse 48 hangs over the interval; the pardon of the sūrah's next movement meets it — and between the two stands this verse, the interval's own case law.
