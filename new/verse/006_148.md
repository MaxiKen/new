## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:148

> The polytheists will argue, “Had it been Allah’s Will, neither we nor our forefathers would have associated others with Him ˹in worship˺ or made anything unlawful.” Likewise, those before them rejected the truth until they tasted Our punishment. Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Do you have any knowledge that you can produce for us? Surely you follow nothing but ˹false˺ assumptions and you do nothing but lie.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument of the Polytheists**

The polytheists will argue, Had it been Allah's Will, neither we nor our forefathers would have associated others with Him in worship or made anything unlawful. The verse records the audience's appeal to the divine will as an excuse for their conduct, and the appeal is one the sūrah has already anticipated. The sūrah stated that had God willed, the idolaters would not have associated partners with Him (6:107), and it made the same statement about the killing of children (6:137); the verse in front of us shows the audience turning that statement into a defence.

The Book records the same argument from other communities. The sūrah of the Bee states that the polytheists said that had God willed, neither they nor their ancestors would have worshipped anything besides Him, nor would they have forbidden anything (16:35); the sūrah of the Ornament records their claim that if the Most Compassionate had willed they would never have worshipped the idols (43:20); and the sūrah of the Cow records the answer of those who were invited to follow the revelation, that they would follow only what their forefathers followed (2:170). The argument appears in each passage as the last resort of a people who have no evidence.

**The Precedent of the Earlier Communities**

Likewise, those before them rejected the truth until they tasted Our punishment. The sentence places the audience's argument in the history of the communities that preceded them, and it states that the same plea was made before the punishment arrived. The sūrah has described the destruction of the earlier generations in several passages, and the sentence in front of us connects the argument to the fate that followed it.

The Book's accounts of the destroyed communities include the excuses they offered before their destruction. The sūrah of the Poets records the denials of the peoples of Noah, Hūd, and Ṣāliḥ, and the sūrah of the Heights records the arguments of the communities that rejected their messengers. What the verse in front of us states is that the plea of the audience in Makkah belonged to a pattern that had already been tested.

**The Demand for Knowledge**

Ask them, Do you have any knowledge that you can produce for us? The question turns the appeal back on the people who made it, and it asks them to produce something that would support their claim. The Book's word for the knowledge in question covers any evidence or revelation that would establish that God had willed their practices in the sense they claimed.

The demand is the same one the sūrah made when it challenged the audience's dietary rules and required them to state the basis of their claim (6:143–144). The method is consistent: the audience is asked for the foundation of its position, and the absence of any foundation is then stated.

**The Charge of Assumption and Falsehood**

Surely you follow nothing but false assumptions and you do nothing but lie. The sentence states the result of the challenge, and it uses the same vocabulary the sūrah has applied to the audience throughout. The verse about the majority said that they follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie (6:116), and the sūrah of Yūnus states that most of them follow nothing but assumption and that assumption cannot replace the truth (10:36).

The two charges correspond to the two parts of the audience's conduct. The assumptions are the beliefs they inherited and never examined, and the lies are the claims they made about God on the basis of those beliefs, including the claim that He had forbidden certain things and willed their practices.

**What the Verse Says About the Appeal to God's Will**

The verse does not deny that God's will stands behind everything that occurs. The sūrah has stated the same fact about the idolaters' association of partners and about the killing of children, and the verse in front of us quotes the audience stating it. What the Book rejects is the use of that fact to remove responsibility, and the answer it gives is that the audience has no knowledge that would support the inference they draw from it.

The Book's resolution of the matter appears in the verse that follows, where God is said to have the most conclusive argument and to have been able to impose guidance on everyone had He willed (6:149). The divine will and human accountability are both affirmed, and the appeal that would use one to cancel the other is answered by the statement that God's case is complete and that the messengers were sent so that no one would have an excuse (4:165).

**What the Verse Says About Inherited Religion**

The audience's argument rested on the practices of their forefathers, and the sentence records the connection explicitly. The Book's answer to inherited practice appears in the sūrah of the Cow, where the people who are invited to follow the revelation reply that they will follow what their forefathers followed, and the sūrah asks whether they would do so even if their forefathers understood nothing and were not guided (2:170).

The verse in front of us applies that answer to the audience's appeal to God's will. The practices were inherited, the claim that God had willed them was the audience's own inference, and the sūrah's charge is that the inference rested on nothing but assumption.

**The Transition to the Next Verse**

The next verse gives the Prophet the declaration that God has the most conclusive argument and that He could have imposed guidance upon all people had He willed (6:149). The sentence completes the answer to the audience's appeal, and it states the ground on which the argument between the messenger and his people is settled.

The passage that follows continues with instructions about the path of God and the prohibitions that the sūrah lists in its closing verses. The verse in front of us supplies the audience's final argument and the Book's answer to it, and the reader is left with the knowledge that the assumption on which the argument rested had no foundation.
