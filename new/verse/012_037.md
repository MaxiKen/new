## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:37

> Joseph replied, “I can even tell you what kind of meal you will be served before you receive it. This ˹knowledge˺ is from what my Lord has taught me. I have shunned the faith of a people who disbelieve in Allah and deny the Hereafter.

**Expanded Commentary**

**He Answers a Larger Question Than the One Asked**

Two men have asked for an interpretation of two dreams. Joseph's reply begins with a claim about knowledge in general, and the interpretation itself is not delivered until verse 41, four verses later. The delay is not evasion. He is establishing the standing of the thing he is about to give them, because an interpretation delivered by a slave with no credentials would be received as guesswork, and guesswork about a man's fate is worse than silence. The method is deliberate and it is his method throughout: state the ground of the knowledge, then supply the knowledge. The same order will appear when he addresses the king's dream, where the answer is preceded by a plan for the country rather than followed by one (12:47–49).

**"Before It Reaches You" Is the Test He Proposes**

The clause describes something verifiable in advance: whatever provision is brought to them, he will tell them what it is before it arrives. Two readings of the sentence have been transmitted. The narrower takes it literally — food served in the prison, announced beforehand — and treats the announcement as a standing miracle of foreknowledge that the two men can check daily. The broader takes *food* as a figure for whatever comes to them, including the outcome of their dreams, so that the claim is: I will inform you of the interpretation of your matter before the matter itself arrives. The commentators hold both, and al-Jalālayn's gloss favours the second. The first reading has an advantage the second lacks: it is falsifiable within hours, and a claim that can be tested the same day is a claim worth making to two sceptics in a cell.

**"This Is From What My Lord Has Taught Me"**

The attribution is immediate and unqualified. Nothing in the sentence suggests natural aptitude, long study or inherited technique. The knowledge is *mimmā ʿallamanī rabbī*, from what my Lord taught me — a portion of a teaching whose source is named. That connects this verse to two earlier statements: his father's promise that God would teach him the interpretation of dreams (12:6), and the narrator's declaration that God established him in Egypt precisely so that He might teach him it (12:21). The prison is therefore not an interruption of that education but its setting. Joseph's insistence on the source also does practical work in the room: a slave claiming foreknowledge invites a charge of sorcery, and locating the knowledge in God's teaching is the only defence available to him.

**A Confession of Faith Inserted Into a Request for a Service**

The third sentence changes register entirely. The two men asked for something from him; he tells them what he believes. The digression is the verse's most striking feature and the commentators explain it in two directions. On one reading, God taught him these things because he abandoned the creed of an unbelieving people, so the mention of faith is the ground of the knowledge and belongs in the sentence. On the other, Joseph used the occasion of their request to call them to the oneness of God, treating the interpretation as an opportunity rather than a transaction. Both readings agree on the priority: the religious claim comes before the favour. A man who has been imprisoned by that very society uses his first audience in it to repudiate its religion.

**"I Have Left the Creed of a People" Is a Public Renunciation**

The verb *faraktu* — I have abandoned, forsaken — is in the perfect tense and describes a completed break. What he has left is *millat qawmin lā yuʾminūna bi-Llāh*, the creed of a people who do not believe in God. The reference is to the religion of Egypt, the state cult of the household he served and the court that imprisoned him. Note what is absent: no name, no accusation, no mention of the ʿAzīz, his wife, the guests or the decision of verse 35. Joseph renounces a creed without denouncing any person, and he does it in a prison where his hearers belong to the king's own household. The renunciation costs him nothing he still has and risks everything he might yet regain, which is why the sūrah records it before recording the interpretation.

**Two Denials Named Together**

The people whose creed he has left are described by two negatives: they do not believe in God, and they are deniers of the Hereafter. The pairing is the Qur'an's standard account of unbelief, and the order matters. Denial of the last day is presented not as a separate error but as a consequence of the first, since a person who acknowledges a Lord is expected to acknowledge an accounting. The two denials also define the two things Joseph's own conduct has depended on. He refused an offer with immediate rewards and immediate risks because he believed in a God who sees and a judgement that follows (12:23–24). He accepted prison because he preferred a later outcome to a present one (12:33). The creed he renounces is the exact inverse of the creed that carried him through the last thirteen verses, and the verse asks two prisoners to notice the difference.

The distinction Joseph is drawing has a practical edge in an ancient setting. Knowledge of the future was a professional industry in Egypt and elsewhere, and the ordinary suppliers of it were diviners, astrologers and soothsayers whose authority rested on technique and secrecy. A prisoner who announces beforehand what food will arrive is making exactly the kind of claim such people made, and the line between prophecy and divination is not self-evident to an audience. Joseph draws it by naming the teacher. What separates his foreknowledge from theirs is not accuracy but source: the diviner's claim rests on his own art, while his rests on a revelation he received and did not produce. The verse therefore performs two acts at once — it establishes a credential and it refuses a profession, and the refusal is what makes the credential acceptable to a believer.
