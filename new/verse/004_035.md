## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:35

> If you anticipate a split between them, appoint a mediator from his family and another from hers. If they desire reconciliation, Allah will restore harmony between them. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware.

**Expanded Commentary**

**When a Split Is Feared, Bring the Families In**

The verse begins with *wa in khiftum shiqāqa baynihimā* — if you anticipate a split between them. *Shiqāq* — a split, a rift, a pulling apart where each side goes to its own *shiqq* — edge, side — describes a marriage where harmony has cracked and the two are veering apart. The subject *khiftum* — you fear, you anticipate — was read as addressed to the community, the rulers, or the families who become aware, not only to the couple themselves, because the couple inside the rift may not see or want mediation. The command *fa abʿathū ḥakaman min ahlihi wa ḥakaman min ahlihā* — appoint a mediator from his family and another from hers — brings kin into the room for a reason the sūrah has been building. The family knows the history, the temperaments, the pressures, and the wealth that has been handled as a trust (4:2–6, 4:29). Two mediators — *ḥakaman* — one from each side — balance concern, so that neither spouse fears the other’s family will dominate. The sūrah has already shown that family is both a tie of mercy — *arḥām* sharing the root of *raḥmah* (4:1) — and a structure for justice — heirs and witnesses and shares. Here that structure becomes a shelter for the marriage itself, before the split becomes final.

**Two Mediators, One Purpose**

The choice of one from his family and one from hers was explained as wisdom about knowledge and trust. A mediator from his family can speak to him plainly without the defensiveness a stranger would trigger; a mediator from her family can do the same for her. Both can hear what outsiders would not be told and can verify what each claims about dowry, maintenance, and conduct. The pair are called *ḥakam* — arbiters, judges — but their role in this verse was read as reconciliatory first: to listen, to advise, to remind of the firm commitment — *mīthāq ghalīẓ* (4:21) — and of the command to live with women fairly (4:19) and to speak equitably (4:9). The sūrah’s prior gradation for ill-conduct — advise, forsake in bed, discipline gently, and then cease when amendment appears (4:34) — already assumed that correction is attempted inside the home; this verse assumes that where the rift persists, help from outside the home is better than hardening inside it. The two mediators therefore embody the sūrah’s larger method: bring the womb-tie back into the household that is at risk, because the same tie that was to be honoured at creation (4:1) can now be the tie that repairs.

**If They Desire Reconciliation, Allah Will Restore Harmony**

The promise *in yurīdā iṣlāḥan yuwaffiqi Allāhu baynahumā* — if they desire reconciliation, Allah will restore harmony between them — places success on two conditions and one divine act. The pronoun *in yurīdā* was read as the two spouses desiring, or as the two mediators desiring, or as all four desiring together, but the core is the same: where the will for *iṣlāḥ* — mending, reconciliation — is sincere, *tawfīq* — the bringing together, the enabling — comes from God. *Yuwaffiq* carries the sense of making two things meet at the right point, like a seam that holds. The sūrah has already promised that for those who repent and mend, relief follows (4:16), and that God wills to turn toward you and lighten your burdens (4:26–28). This verse is that lightening in the domain of marriage: where people want mending, God brings the fit. That does not guarantee that every mediation ends in staying together; the verse that follows will say that Allah witnesses all, and the law will allow separation with fairness where staying would be harm. But it guarantees that where the desire for repair is real, the help to repair will be given. The community that appoints the two mediators is therefore not only managing a dispute; it is creating a space where divine help can arrive, because God helps the hearts that have turned toward mending.

**All-Knowing, All-Aware Over What Is Hidden**

The closing *inna Allāha kāna ʿalīman khabīrā* — surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware — fits a verse about private conflict. *ʿAlīm* knows the history each spouse brings — the words said in the home, the wealth spent or withheld, the dowry given or pressured (4:19–21). *Khabīr* — All-Aware, intimately informed — knows the subtle motives the mediators cannot fully see — whether the desire for reconciliation is sincere or a tactic, whether the claim of ill-conduct masks a wish to reclaim a gift, whether fear of God or fear of family opinion is speaking. That pairing deters two abuses at once: the couple cannot deceive the mediators without God seeing, and the mediators cannot favour one side without God knowing. The sūrah has used similar closings to temper power — Most High, All-Great over caretaking (4:34), Witness over pledges (4:33), All-Knowing, All-Wise over shares (4:11, 4:26). Here awareness tempers both spouses and families, reminding all that the arbitration table is also watched, and that the success of its work — *yuwaffiqi Allāhu* — depends on a sincerity that only God can fully weigh. Honesty before that watch is therefore the first step of any settlement.

**Mediation Before Dissolution**

Placed after the caretaking and discipline gradation (4:34) and before the comprehensive ethic of kindness that will follow (4:36), this verse positions mediation as the sūrah’s preferred way to handle a feared split. It does not rush to dissolution, nor does it urge the couple to endure harm without help; it brings the families in early, when *khiftum* — fear — first appears, so that the rift does not harden into enmity. That early entry reflects the Medinan community’s new kinship, where migrants and hosts were already bound by pledge — give them their share when you have pledged (4:33) — and where inheritance and maintenance had been made precise to reduce quarrels. The mediators from each side extend that kinship into the home. By appointing both, the verse also protects the woman’s voice where custom had once inherited her against her will (4:19); she is given an arbiter from her own family who knows her right to dowry and to kind companionship. A split that might have become a contest of power becomes a conversation between two families whose interests are aligned toward *iṣlāḥ* if that remains possible. Where it is not, the same families will ensure that separation, if it comes, is done with witnesses and with *“sufficient is Allah as Reckoner”* (4:6) still true.

**The Path That Desires Mending**

The verse’s conditional promise — if they desire reconciliation, Allah will bring harmony — teaches that the outcome rests on desire as much as on procedure. Two mediators can meet, but if both spouses desire dominance rather than mending, no *tawfīq* will hold. Conversely, a couple that desires mending will find that desire itself becomes the invitation for divine help, even where means are small. That pattern matches the sūrah’s opening will to make things clear and turn toward you (4:26) and to lighten because you are weak (4:28); the law provides the mediators, but God provides the joining. The sūrah will soon broaden that ethic outward — worship God, be kind to parents, relatives, orphans, poor, neighbours near and distant, companions, travellers, and those in your possession (4:36) — showing that mending between two spouses is a single case of a larger repair the community is asked to undertake. A community that learns to appoint fairly, to desire sincerely, and to trust the All-Knowing, All-Aware to bring the seam together will have learned more than how to save a marriage; it will have learned how to hold itself together.
