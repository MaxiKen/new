## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:268

> The Devil threatens you with ˹the prospect of˺ poverty and bids you to the shameful deed ˹of stinginess˺, while Allah promises you forgiveness and ˹great˺ bounties from Him. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Voices at the Purse**

The verse stages the giver's interior as a contest of rhetorics, and the staging is symmetrical to the point of architecture. Satan *threatens* — yuʿidukum, promises-in-the-mode-of-menace — with poverty; God *promises* — yaʿidukum — forgiveness and bounty. The same verb-form serves both speakers, and the verse's genius is the reversal it exposes: the threat comes from the one who has nothing to give, and the promise comes from the One who owns the treasuries. The commentators' reading of the mechanism is precise: Satan makes the soul dread want, and the dread produces the withholding; God sets before the soul the remembrance of forgiveness and of generosity, and the remembrance produces the open hand. Ibn Kathīr states the verse's whole function in the section: it explains, at the interior level, the behavior the preceding verses legislated against — the dreg-selection of 2:267, the dust-film of 2:264, the reminders of 2:262 — all of which are the poverty-threat's downstream products. The section's ethics of giving had reached the point where external command was not enough; this verse opens the room where the commands are obeyed or sabotaged.

**The Threat of Poverty**

Al-Rāzī's analysis of the threat is the tradition's fullest map of the miser's descent, and it runs as a cascade. It begins with a distortion of the standard: ideally the person gives from the best of what he has, but the soul under the first touch of the threat wants to part only with the dregs — the closed-eyes item of the previous verse. From dreg-giving the descent continues: the fear of losing anything at all grows, and the giving stops — the charity is withheld entirely. Then the miserliness infects the rest of the soul, which arranges itself around keeping: holding what it desires, releasing nothing of value, the retention becoming the self's organizing principle. The final stage is the inversion of agency — passivity toward Satan's whispering becomes activity in sinning, the soul that stopped giving starts taking, until it ceases doing good at all and commits every manner of evil. The cascade's first step is the verse's whole warning: the poverty-threat does not arrive as a command to evil but as a piece of apparently prudent arithmetic — keep this, for the future is uncertain — and the arithmetic, obeyed repeatedly, becomes the character the last verse described as disbelieving dust on stone.

**The Shameful Deed**

The threat's second product is named in the verse's own translation-gloss: Satan *bids you to the shameful deed — of stinginess*. Faḥshāʾ is the Qur'an's word for the indecency, the excess that decency cannot look at — the word used for sexual transgression and for everything the moral sense finds grotesque. Its application here to miserliness is the verse's most radical naming, and the commentators hold it deliberately: the stinginess is not a weakness or a preference but an indecency, the soul's exposure of what should stay covered — the appetite that keeps while another's need stands at the door. The pairing with the poverty-threat defines the two movements of the same hand: the threat works on the future (you will want, later), and the faḥshāʾ names the present act (you are keeping, now) — fear as the motive, indecency as the deed. The tradition's moral vocabulary rarely escalates avarice this high, and the escalation is the section's doing: after the parables of the rock, the garden, and the burnt orchard, the verse can name the withholding what the parables showed it to be — the fire's fuel, the dust's source, the closed-eyes selection's engine.

**The Whisper and Its Counterpart**

The ḥadīth the exegetes cite at this verse gives the contest its interior acoustics. Satan has an instigation in the son of Adam, the Prophet said, and the angel has an instigation: as for Satan's, it is the promise of evil and the calling of the truth a lie; as for the angel's, it is the promise of good and the confirmation of the truth. Whoever finds the second in himself, let him know it is from God and let him praise God; and whoever finds the first, let him take refuge from Satan. The report supplies the verse's missing epistemology: the two voices are not equally audible, and the tradition gives their discrimination a test — the direction of the promise. The voice that promises good and confirms truth is the angel's register; the voice that promises evil (the poverty to come) while falsifying truth (the provision that is God's, the reward that is certain) is the satanic one. The verse's structure is now legible as the test applied: Satan threatens with a lie about the future and commands an indecency in the present; God promises the two truths — the forgiveness that covers the past and the bounty that answers the feared future — and the hearer's task is the ḥadīth's: recognize the register, praise or take refuge.

**Forgiveness and Bounty**

God's two promises are matched to the threat's two wounds. The poverty-threat wounds the future — you will lack — and bounty, faḍl, answers it: the provision's source is wider than the keeping can ever be, and the verse's own section has been parabling the width (the seven-hundredfold grain, the doubled garden, the mountain-nurtured date). The second wound is subtler and the commentators draw it from the section's context: the soul considering its giving-history — the dregs selected, the reminders spoken, the closed-eyes gifts — is wounded by its own record, and a promise of bounty alone would not answer the shame that keeps the hand shut. Forgiveness answers it: the past's spoiled giving is covered, and the hand may open without first solving its history. The two promises together are the verse's therapy of generosity — the future secured and the past absolved — and their pairing explains why the poverty-threat wins so often: it offers the miser a false solution to both wounds at once, keeping as security and keeping as erasure, and the verse exposes the double fraud.

**All-Bountiful, All-Knowing**

The closing pair repeats the grain-parable's names (2:261) and completes the verse's argument with them. Wāsiʿ — the Wide, the Bountiful — is the refutation of the poverty-threat in the form of a name: the One promising bounty is the One whose width the threat denies, and the verse asks its hearer to weigh a whisper against a name. ʿAlīm — the All-Knowing — governs the contest's hidden arena: which voice the heart is currently answering, whether the hand at the purse is moved by dread or by promise, whether the faḥshāʾ of keeping is being rehearsed in calculation nobody sees. The pairing also answers the ḥadīth's discrimination-test at its root: the human being cannot always tell the registers apart in himself — the prudent thought and the whisper sound alike from inside — and the All-Knowing is the verse's assurance that the ambiguity is not final: the Knower hears both voices and knows which one the soul is obeying. The section's spending-legislation thus stands between the two names it keeps returning to: the Wide, whose bounty outruns every threat of want, and the Knowing, whose audit makes the interior contest a matter of record — and the verse leaves the hand where the contest began, free to close or open, with the threat named, the promise stated, and both speakers identified.
