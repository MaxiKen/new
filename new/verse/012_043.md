## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:43

> And ˹one day˺ the King said, “I dreamt of seven fat cows eaten up by seven skinny ones; and seven green ears of grain and ˹seven˺ others dry. O chiefs! Tell me the meaning of my dream if you can interpret dreams.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Ruler Called King, Never Pharaoh**

The sūrah names the ruler of Egypt *al-malik*, the king, and does so consistently across verses 43, 50, 54, 72 and 76. It never calls him Pharaoh. The contrast with the Moses narrative is exact: the ruler who confronts Moses is always *Firʿawn*, and the word appears nowhere in this chapter. The observation is old and has been pressed into service by modern scholars who argue that the Joseph story is set before the period of the native dynasties, during the rule of the Hyksos, whose foreign kings did not use the pharaonic title. That historical identification remains a proposal rather than a settled finding. What is not a proposal is the Qur'an's own consistency. Two Egyptian courts are described in the Book and they are given two different vocabularies, a distinction a borrower from a single source would have had no reason to preserve.

**Seven and Seven, Stated Twice**

The dream has two series and they share a number. Seven fat cows devoured by seven lean ones; seven green ears of grain alongside seven dry. The repetition of seven across two different images is what makes the dream interpretable rather than merely strange, and it is what the king reports first and most precisely. The parallelism also suggests that the two series signify the same thing in two registers — livestock and crops, the two bases of an agricultural economy. Joseph's answer in verses 47 to 49 treats them as one calendar rather than two, giving seven years of plenty, seven of scarcity and a year of relief, which is fifteen years in total. The arithmetic is the key the dream hands to its interpreter.

**"Eaten Up By" Is the Image That Carries the Meaning**

The lean cows do not merely follow the fat ones; they consume them. The verb describes devouring, and the second series repeats it in a different medium — the dry ears standing beside the green. Consumption is the concept the dream is built around: surplus destroyed by scarcity, not simply succeeded by it. That distinction is what makes the dream a matter of policy rather than an omen. If scarcity merely followed plenty, no action would be required beyond endurance. Because scarcity devours what plenty has produced, the only defence is to change the way plenty is handled, which is precisely the instruction Joseph will give. The king's dream contains its own solution in the choice of verb. Consumption is the governing idea of the vision. The fat years do not merely precede the lean ones; they are swallowed by them, and the swallowing is what turns storage from prudence into necessity. Joseph's later programme, with its instruction to leave grain in the ear and to eat little, answers that verb directly, which is why his reading can move from description to policy in a single speech.

**He Asks the Men Around Him**

*Yā ayyuhā al-malaʾ* — O chiefs, O notables, the inner circle of the court. The word denotes the assembly of leading men, and the Qur'an uses it for the ruling councils of other nations as well. The king does not consult priests in this verse, does not send for specialists and does not treat the matter as private. He puts the dream to the people who run the country with him, in a form that makes it a public question. The setting matters for what follows, because the failure of this group is a failure of the state's own intellectual establishment, and the man who answers them will come from a prison rather than from a palace. The plural address matters. A king consulting one adviser would be described differently; this verse shows him putting the question to a body of men, which is how a court actually works. Their failure is therefore collective and institutional, and it opens the gap that Joseph's arrival fills — though he is not sought out to fill it, and nobody at that moment knows his name.

**"If You Can Interpret Dreams" Names a Recognised Skill**

The verb is from the root *ʿ-b-r*, to cross over, and the commentators explain its application here: interpretation is the crossing from the symbolic form a dream presents to the reality it signifies. The skill was a profession, and the king's conditional — if you are people who interpret visions — presupposes that such people exist and that his court should include them. The word for visions in this verse is *ruʾyā*, the term the tradition reserves for the true dream, as distinct from *ḥulm*, the distressing or meaningless one. The king's own description of what he saw uses the vocabulary of a genuine vision, which is what makes the answer in the next verse a dismissal rather than a failure. The conditional also tells us that dream interpretation was a recognised craft, with practitioners and a reputation, something a court could ask for by name. Set against that, the reply which follows is not an admission of ignorance but a professional verdict, and it is the verdict of the establishment: confused dreams, of no determinate meaning, and we are not the people to settle them.

**The Dream Is Treated as a Question About the Country**

Nothing in the king's response suggests he thinks the vision concerns his personal fate. He does not ask what will happen to him, whether he will die, or whether his reign is threatened. He asks for the meaning, and the meaning turns out to be agricultural: seven years of grain and seven of famine. A dream about cows and ears of corn is a dream about the food supply, and the king's instinct to convene his council rather than his household shows that he understood its register immediately. This is why the answer Joseph eventually gives is a plan for storage rather than a prediction about the king, and why a prisoner's interpretation can end with him running the granaries of the country. Read that way, the king's response also becomes intelligible. A dream about his own fate would have called for comfort or for warning; a dream about seven years of national harvest calls for an administrator. The office he goes on to create follows from the reading he has just accepted, not from any favour he intends to do a stranger.

**The Chapter Reproduces the Dream in Full for a Reason**

Every number and every image is given, so that a reader can attempt the interpretation before it is supplied. Seven, seven, fat, lean, green, dry, consumption, consumption. The elements are few, symmetrical and concrete, and the solution — a cycle of agricultural years with a famine in the middle — is available to anyone who takes the images seriously. The sūrah's decision to state the dream at length rather than summarise it turns the reader into a participant. When Joseph's answer arrives in verse 47, it does not feel like privileged information delivered from nowhere. It feels like the correct reading of a text the audience has already been given, which is exactly how the Qur'an wants its own accounts to be received.
