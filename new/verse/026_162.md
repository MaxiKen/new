## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:162

> I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lot Establishes the Basis for Hearing Him**

Lot describes himself as a trustworthy messenger. The declaration comes after he has asked his people to fear Allah, so it explains why his warning deserves a serious hearing. He is not offering a private preference about the community's behavior. He is conveying a command entrusted to him by God.

Trustworthiness includes truth in speech, fidelity to the message, and freedom from a hidden plan to profit at the people's expense. The community knows Lot as one of its own, which gives it the chance to compare his claim with the character it has observed. It cannot dismiss the warning by inventing a motive without evidence.


Lot is both trustworthy and a messenger. The first quality concerns his integrity; the second identifies the source and scope of his duty. He is not merely a respected moral critic, and he is not a ruler claiming power over the people. He serves as an envoy of the Lord.

This distinction protects the community from two errors. It should not reject the message because Lot is human, and it should not elevate Lot into a divine figure. A true messenger directs worship and obedience to God. His personal reliability matters because it shows that he will not distort the Lord's command for his own benefit.

**A Messenger From Among the People Can Be Known**

Lot's closeness to the community is part of the moral situation. The people have seen his conduct, family life, and manner of dealing with others. They cannot say that the messenger appeared without a history. If they disagree, they must address the message rather than pretend that Lot's character is unknowable.

Familiarity, however, can be used in the opposite way. People may say that they know Lot too well to accept a special calling from him. They mistake ordinary human familiarity for evidence against revelation. A messenger's shared humanity makes his example accessible; it does not cancel the trust he carries.


A messenger who wants popularity would avoid the subject that threatens the community's pride. Lot does not do that. He speaks about conduct the people have normalized and asks them to fear God. His courage is not a desire to humiliate them. It is the cost of being faithful to a warning that could save them.

The trustworthy person tells the truth even when silence would be easier. He does not soften the command until it loses its meaning, and he does not sharpen it with insults to satisfy anger. Lot's position shows that moral clarity and compassion can inhabit the same speech.

**The Messenger Does Not Own the Message**

A false religious leader may treat a message as personal property, demanding loyalty to himself and using revelation to build an empire. Lot's wording resists that pattern. He is a messenger, which means that the truth precedes him and continues beyond him. The people must follow God, not Lot's ego.

This also means that Lot cannot bargain away the difficult part of the warning. He is not free to edit divine guidance because his audience is powerful or because rejection is painful. Trustworthiness is measured by fidelity to the Lord, not by the comfort his words create.


Lot remains a human being with fear, grief, and limited control over the people. Trustworthiness does not mean possessing every power or knowing every hidden event. It means conveying what God has given him and acting honestly within his duty.

Quran 18:110 presents the same principle for the Prophet Muhammad: human nature and revelation are not opposites. A messenger's humanity shows dependence on God, while the revelation he receives gives him an authority no personal invention could supply. Lot's people must distinguish the messenger's human form from the divine source of his call.

**A Trustworthy Warning Protects the Hearer**

The people may regard Lot's message as an intrusion into their freedom. Yet the warning is protective because it identifies a practice that has become morally destructive and asks them to return to God. Silence would leave them confident while they move toward judgment.

Trustworthy counsel often feels severe to the person who wants approval. Its value lies in the danger it reveals, not in its ability to preserve a pleasant mood. Lot's concern for his people gives the warning its human warmth; God's command gives it its binding force.


Lot has identified his role clearly. If the people reject him, they cannot claim that they misunderstood whether he was speaking for himself or for the Lord. The declaration makes their decision accountable. They may dislike the message, but they know the basis on which it is presented.

For the reader, the verse offers a standard for receiving moral guidance. Look for truthfulness, fidelity, humility, and independence from personal gain. A trustworthy messenger does not invite worship of his personality. He points beyond himself to the Lord who sent him and asks the hearer to obey God rather than desire.

The messenger's trustworthiness also protects the vulnerable from being made responsible for the powerful person's appetite. A leader who uses religion to excuse exploitation is not carrying the trust of God. Lot's warning places every person under one moral authority and refuses to let social influence decide who may be harmed.

For this reason, a trustworthy messenger can be both gentle and unyielding. He knows the hearer's weakness, but he does not rename wrongdoing as harmlessness. He offers a way back through repentance and obedience, while keeping the revealed boundary clear enough that no one can mistake compassion for approval. This clarity is part of the trust placed in him: he neither hides the command nor uses it as an excuse for cruelty.
