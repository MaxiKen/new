## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13

> And ˹so˺ my heart will be broken and my tongue will be tied. So send Aaron along ˹as a messenger˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mission Presses on Heart and Speech**

Moses describes two connected obstacles. His breast will become constricted, and his tongue will not move freely. The first image concerns inward capacity: anxiety narrows the space in which a person can think, bear pressure, and remain composed. The second concerns outward delivery: a message may be true, yet the speaker may struggle to express it under threat. The verse treats both conditions as relevant to service. A messenger is not a disembodied voice; fear can touch his body, emotions, and speech.

The translation's broken heart captures the force of the image, while the Arabic points to a chest made tight. Commentators differed over whether Moses had a physical speech difficulty, a psychological inhibition, or both. The Qur'an does not settle the medical question, and the verse does not require a diagnosis. Its clear point is that Moses anticipates a real impediment to carrying out a grave public duty. He asks for help instead of hiding the impediment behind an image of perfection.

**A Tongue Can Be Tied by More Than Anatomy**

Speech becomes difficult when a person is frightened, but Moses' concern may also recall the long-standing difficulty mentioned in 20:27. Some reports and commentators imagined a physical cause; others understood the language as a prayer for inward ease and persuasive clarity. The stronger common ground is more useful than an invented certainty: Moses feared that his speech would fail to serve the message as it should.

That reading preserves the dignity of people who speak with a stammer, injury, accent, or anxiety. A limitation in expression does not disqualify a person from truth or service. At the same time, the verse does not romanticize the difficulty. Moses seeks a means by which the audience can hear the command clearly. Faith does not require pretending that communication barriers do not matter. It turns those barriers into a reason for wise support rather than shame.

**Aaron Is Requested as a Messenger, Not a Substitute for Moses**

Moses asks that Aaron be sent along. He is not seeking escape from the call; he is seeking a companion who can help carry it. Other passages identify Aaron as his brother and describe a shared mission. The pairing allows different strengths to serve one purpose. Moses remains the servant who was first commanded, while Aaron becomes a divinely appointed support rather than a replacement created by human preference.

This distinction matters because people often treat dependence as failure. The verse presents it as part of prophetic method. A difficult message may require more than one voice, and a speaker may need someone whose speech is easier for the audience to follow. The request is not a demand for personal comfort detached from duty. It is a practical petition made for the sake of making obedience more effective.

**The Heart and Tongue Must Work Together**

A clear tongue without a sound heart can produce manipulation. A sincere heart without usable speech can leave the truth hidden inside the servant. Moses names both because the mission requires inward courage and outward communication. His heart must remain open enough to endure rejection, and his tongue must be released enough to state the command. Divine service is not measured by one faculty alone.

This twofold language also shows why the Qur'an often describes spiritual understanding through the heart. The heart is not merely the site of emotion; it is the center of moral perception and resolve. If it is constricted by fear, the tongue may lose its freedom. Support for the tongue therefore also supports the whole person. Moses asks for help at the point where inner distress threatens to become an outward barrier.

**The Request Is a Form of Sound Planning**

A person can misuse reliance on God as an excuse to ignore means. Moses does the opposite. He knows that God sends him, yet he asks for the companion and skill needed for the work. The request shows that trust and planning belong together. One may believe in divine help while still identifying the weakness that should be addressed through another person, preparation, or a change of method.

The story also prevents a narrow idea of leadership. The strongest leader is not always the one who speaks alone. Sometimes leadership means recognizing which partner will make a shared task clearer and more just. Moses' request honors Aaron's capacity while remaining accountable to the mission. It turns brotherhood into public service rather than private attachment.

**A Limitation Does Not Cancel a Commission**

Moses' fear and speech difficulty appear before the signs and victories of the story. That order is important. God does not wait for the servant to become invulnerable before calling him. The commission comes to a human being who knows where he is weak. Help is then granted within the call, not after Moses has proven that he needs none.

Anyone facing a moral duty can recognize the pattern. Admit the place where pressure narrows the heart or ties the tongue. Ask for the right assistance. Keep the purpose of the request tied to truth rather than self-display. Moses' words do not weaken the mission; they prepare the conditions in which he and Aaron can carry it faithfully.

**Support Makes a Servant More Responsible, Not Less**

When Aaron is granted, Moses does not become less accountable for the message. Partnership removes one obstacle so that responsibility can be fulfilled more clearly. This is a useful distinction in every form of service. Assistance should not become a way to hand away one's duty; it should make it possible to perform that duty with greater care.

Aaron's role also shows that a weakness can become the occasion for a community of service. Moses' tongue may be constrained, but the mission is not constrained to one tongue. God provides a companion whose presence turns a private limitation into a shared strength. The answer honors both the seriousness of the obstacle and the possibility of overcoming it without shame.
