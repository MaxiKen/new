## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:14

> Indeed, Allah will admit those who believe and do good into Gardens, under which rivers flow. Surely Allah does what He wills.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise That Answers the Failure**

Verses 11 to 13 described three figures: the man on the brink, the man calling on what cannot help, and the man whose patron is worse than nothing. Verse 14 answers all three with a single sentence about a different person.

The structure is one the Qur'an uses constantly and this sūrah uses structurally: the failed case and the successful case are placed side by side so that the difference between them is visible without commentary. The believers are not praised in detail here. They are set next to the man on the brink, and the reader supplies the contrast. What separated them was not the events — both were tested — but where each was standing when the test arrived.

The sequence also answers the specific losses. The man of verse 11 lost this world and the next; the believers here gain the next in the most concrete available image. The man of verse 13 had a fictional patron; these people have a real one who admits them.

**"Those Who Believe and Do Good"**

The formula is the Qur'an's standard abbreviation for the whole of the religion, and it is worth resisting the habit of reading past it. Two conditions, always joined, never separated in this construction.

*Āmanū* — they believed, they made themselves secure in a trust. *Wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti* — and they did the deeds that are sound, that work, that hold up. The pairing matters in both directions. Belief without works is the condition of the man in verse 11, who said the words and was toppled by the first trial. Works without belief would be a moral life with no orientation, which the Qur'an does not recognize as a category.

The first occurrence of the promise in the Qur'an's arrangement uses the identical phrasing and the identical reward: *“Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow”* (2:25). The stability of the formula across the whole text is the point of it. Salvation is not described differently for different groups; the same two conditions and the same promise are on offer to everyone.

**"Gardens Under Which Rivers Flow"**

The reward is *jannātun tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — gardens beneath which rivers run. The preposition is *taḥta*, beneath: the water is described as flowing below the garden, irrigating from underneath, at the roots.

For an audience whose wealth was measured in wells and whose fears were measured in drought, the image needs no explanation. A garden watered from below cannot fail, because it does not depend on rain arriving. It is the exact inversion of the field in verse 5, which lies dead and brown until water comes down on it from outside. The paradise promised here is the field that never needs to wait.

The word *jannah* itself means a garden, a concealed place, something walled and planted. What is promised is not a state of consciousness and not a set of delights listed in the abstract. It is a place, described by the one feature of a place that matters most to a desert people: it is watered, permanently, from underneath.

**"God Does What He Wills"**

The verse closes with *inna Allāha yafʿalu mā yurīd* — God does whatever He intends.

Placed after a promise, the clause is not a threat and not a hedge. A promise is only as good as the power and freedom of the one making it, and this clause states that the Promiser is unimpeded. No one can block His decision, no rival can overrule it, and no shortage can prevent it. The garden is as good as the power behind it.

The clause is also the sūrah's quiet answer to the patron of verse 13. An evil *mawlā* is one who cannot deliver; this verse describes the One whose will is not obstructed. The two verses are three apart and they are talking about the same transaction.

**The Sūrah's Alternating Rhythm**

It is worth stepping back to see where this verse falls in the sūrah's architecture. The passage has been alternating: warning (vv. 1–4), argument (vv. 5–7), the opponents (vv. 8–10), the failures (vv. 11–13), and now the promise. Verse 23 will repeat the promise with more detail, verse 50 will repeat it again, and verse 56 will restate it at the judgment.

The alternation is a method of instruction, not a sign of repetition fatigue. Every warning is followed by what the warning is for, and every promise is preceded by what the promise is rescue from. A reader who takes either half alone gets a distorted religion: warning without promise produces despair, promise without warning produces presumption. The sūrah refuses to let a reader have either one in isolation, which is why the brink-dweller of verse 11 and the garden-dweller of verse 14 are separated by only two verses.

**What "Admit" Presumes**

The verb is *yudkhilu* — He will cause them to enter. Entry is caused, and the cause is not them.

That is the final thing the verse says about the people it describes. They believed and they did sound deeds, and still the language of the reward is that they were brought in. The two conditions are real and required, and the entry is a favour. Holding both at once is the plain sense of the sentence, and it is the settlement of the question that has run through these verses since the man on the brink decided his works entitled him to a smooth life.
