## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:54

> When the believers in Our revelations come to you, say, “Peace be upon you! Your Lord has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. Whoever among you commits evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repents afterwards and mends their ways, then Allah is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Greeting That States a Doctrine**

The verse instructs the Prophet to greet the believers with the words of peace, and then to follow the greeting with the reason it can be said: their Lord has taken upon Himself to be merciful. The greeting is the one the Book records as the welcome of the people of Paradise (7:46; 14:23; 16:32; 19:62), and it is also the form of address used between the prophets and their guests, as when Abraham's visitors greeted him with peace (15:52; 51:25).

Putting the two together makes the instruction more than a courtesy. A person entering the Prophet's gathering is met with a claim about God's own commitment, and the claim is stated in the same breath as the welcome. The message the community carries about God is the first thing a believer receives when he approaches.

**The Reason for the Instruction After the Previous Passage**

The verse follows immediately on the treatment of the poor believers and the demand that they be kept at a distance. Commentators connect the two, noting that the instruction may have arisen from a specific request by ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and others that the Prophet accommodate the notables, and others relate it to a separate incident in which believers confessed serious sins to the Prophet and received this verse as the answer.

Either way, the placement matters. A community that had just been told not to sort itself by status is now told how to receive anyone who comes to it: with peace and with the announcement of mercy. The instruction restores the dignity of the person coming in, and it places the assessment of his record with God rather than with the gathering.

**The Mercy God Prescribed for Himself**

The sentence repeats a claim the sūrah made earlier, where God is described as having taken upon Himself to be merciful (6:12), and it is the only attribute the Book says God prescribed for Himself. The earlier verse stated the fact in the context of a warning about the gathering; this verse states it in the context of forgiveness for a person who has sinned and repented, which shows the doctrine at work.

The Book keeps the two contexts together in other passages, where mercy is described as encompassing all things while punishment falls on whoever God wills (7:156). The verse in front of us gives the practical reason for the self-imposed obligation. A community of human beings will contain people who fail, and the arrangement had to include a route back.

**"Commits Evil Ignorantly or Recklessly"**

The phrase describes the ordinary way in which people do wrong: not as a deliberate campaign against God but as a lapse, a failure of attention, or a decision made without weighing what it means. The Book uses the same description where it speaks of those who commit evil out of ignorance and then repent (4:17–18), and where it says that God accepts the repentance of the one who does wrong and then turns back.

That wording keeps the door wide while it keeps the requirement firm. The ignorance does not excuse the act, and it does not remove the need for repentance, but it puts the act inside the category of the foreseeable rather than the unforgivable. What the verse will not do is remove the conditions attached to the promise.

**Two Conditions and Their Order**

Whoever repents afterwards and mends their ways is the phrase that closes the verse's conditions, and the two halves are not redundant. Repentance is a turning from the act, and amendment is a turning toward a different practice, and the Book regularly requires both. Elsewhere the same sequence appears in the sūrah of the Cow, where the instruction to the believers is followed by the announcement that God is All-Forgiving and Most Merciful toward those who repent and do right.

The order matters because regret alone can be a passing emotion. The Book treats the change of conduct as the evidence that the turning was real, and it does not ask the person returning to prove himself to the community first. The two names that close the verse, All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, state what he will find when he does return.

**The Report About God's Right Over His Servants**

The tafsīr literature connects this passage with a hadith in which the Prophet asked his Companions whether they knew the right of God over the servants, and answered that it is that they worship Him and do not associate partners with Him, and then asked whether they knew the right of the servants over God if they do that, and answered that He will not punish them. The report is quoted in the commentaries on this verse to show the reciprocal nature of what God has bound Himself to.

Read with the verse, the report explains the phrase about the Lord taking mercy upon Himself. The obligation is not something God owed anyone by nature; it is a commitment He announced, and the Book treats it as the ground on which a person may approach him with hope rather than despair.

**What the Believer Receives on Arrival**

The verse gives the community its welcome and its first doctrine in a single instruction. The person who comes is greeted with peace and told that his Lord has bound Himself to mercy and that the way back is open to whoever takes it. The sūrah then continues its argument with the disbelievers, but it does so having first described what happens at the door of the believing community, which is where the rest of the message has to be lived.
