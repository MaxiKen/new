## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:270

> Whatever charities you give or vows you make are surely known to Allah. And the wrongdoers will have no helpers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Instruments Named**

The verse names two of the believer's transactions with God and places both under one knowledge: *whatever charities you give or vows you make*. The nafaqah is the section's standing subject — the spending legislated from the grain-parable onward, given freely in response to need. The nadhr, the vow, is its sibling instrument and appears here for the first time in the section: the binding promise by which a person obligates himself to an act of worship or charity he was not otherwise obligated to perform — the vow of the fast, the pilgrimage, the specified alms, classically conditioned on a hoped-for event: if God restores the sick child, such-and-such is owed. The two instruments cover the field of voluntary giving in both directions of initiative: the nafaqah begins in the moment's need and the giver's freedom; the nadhr begins in a prior speech and binds the future moment. The verse's pairing is jurisprudence compressed — whatever moves through either channel, in whatever amount, under whatever condition — is known; and the knowing is the section's recurring answer to the invisibility of most giving.

**Known Means Accounted**

The commentators give the verse's knowledge-clause its two operative meanings. Al-Ṭabarī: God knows it means He keeps the account of it — the record-entry that guarantees the reward no audience witnessed and no recipient can reciprocate. Al-Rāzī: the knowledge extends to the intention behind the act, great or small — the audit reaching past the amount to the interior the whole section has been legislating. The two readings are the two halves of the section's custody-theology: the reward of the un-reminding givers stands *with their Lord* (2:262) because the Lord's knowledge is the account itself, and the annulment of the reminder-givers is total (2:264) because the same knowledge recorded the spoiling speech. The vow receives the same treatment at its own pressure-point: the nadhr is speech about future action, performed before God as witness, and the verse's knowledge-clause is its enforcement mechanism — the promiser who forgets, minimizes, or quietly abandons his vow meets here the fact that the promise was heard where it was made. The jurists' subsequent legislation of vow-fulfillment — the conditions of validity, the obligation of performance, the expiations of failure — all presuppose the verse's clause: a vow is a real transaction because its recipient really knows it.

**The Vow's Theology**

The nadhr's place in the tradition is more delicate than its mention here suggests, and the verse's neutral-to-positive registration is worth marking. The prophetic reports about vows run in two directions: the ḥadīth collections carry the prohibition of the conditional bargain-vow — the vow does not bring about anything not already decreed, and it extracts from the miser what the miser's generosity would not — alongside the Qur'anic praise of vow-fulfillment, the sūrah of Man's righteous who fulfill their vows (76:7) and the Day whose evil is spreading. The verse here belongs to the second register: the vow is known to Allah — recorded, accounted, and in the section's logic, another form of the giving whose custody is divine. The commentators' synthesis reads the two registers as governing two different hearts: the vow that attempts to purchase a decree is the miser's instrument and avails nothing; the vow that binds the self to gratitude — Maryam's mother dedicating what is in her womb, the recovered sickbed's promised fast — is the speech of a heart already given, and its fulfillment is among the acts this verse assures are known. Between the two stands the verse's single clause, declining to sort the hearts because the Knower sorts them.

**The Wrongdoers and Their Helpers**

The verse's second sentence turns from the instruments to the persons who fail them: *the wrongdoers will have no helpers*. The commentators identify the ẓālimūn here with precision — al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī reading them as those who give for hypocritical reasons, the 2:264 figures whose charity was dust on stone — and extend the identification to the vow's own failure-modes: the promiser who does not perform, the giver who annuls, the person whose transactions with God were performed for witnesses other than God. The helperlessness is the Day's, and it is the section's eschatology stated in its social form: the wrongdoer's helpers — the audience whose approval he bought, the recipients whose gratitude he collected, the patrons whose causes his gifts advertised — are all persons whose help operates inside the world's three currencies, and the Day refuses all three (2:254): no bargaining, no friendship, no intercession. The clause thus gathers the section's warnings into one image of abandonment: the giver who spent for the eyes of men arrives where the men cannot see, and the vow-breaker who promised before God arrives before the God he promised — with the record the verse's first clause kept, and without a single helper the second clause declines to imagine.

**The Verse as the Section's Ledger-Line**

Short, austere, and positioned at the section's center of gravity, this verse functions as the ledger-line the surrounding parables and rankings write into. It comes immediately after the wisdom-verse — the gift that reads the transactions — and immediately before the ranking of secret over public giving: the knowledge-clause here is what makes the secrecy-ranking of the next verse possible, since a charity hidden from every creature is fully visible where the account is kept, and a charity displayed is fully discounted to what its display collected. The vow-mention extends the section beyond gift-economics into promise-economics, and the helperlessness-clause extends its eschatology from the burnt garden's owner to everyone whose dealings with God were mediated by other eyes. Read in the tradition's own terms, the verse is the section's statement of God's bookkeeping: every nafaqah entered, every nadhr registered, every intention inspected, every wrongdoer's file closed without endorsement. The two sentences leave nothing to negotiate — the instruments are known, the helpers are absent — and between them the verse hands the reader the only position the section ever offered: give as one whose gift is read by its Recipient, promise as one whose promise is held by its Witness, and let the helpers go, since the Day they would have helped in has already refused them.
