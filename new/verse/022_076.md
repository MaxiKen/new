## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:76

> He knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What "Before Them and Behind Them" Covers**

*Yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum* — He knows what is before them and what is behind them.

The phrase is a Qur'anic idiom for total knowledge, and the two directions are chosen to be exhaustive rather than merely paired. What is in front of a person is what he can see and is moving toward; what is behind him is what he has left and cannot see. Between them there is nothing a person has that is not covered.

The commentators differed over the application. Al-Rāzī took *what is before them* as the Hereafter and *what is behind them* as the present world, which fits the idiom's use elsewhere for the angelic knowledge of a person's future and past. Read that way, the clause states that God knows both halves of every creature's timeline.

Either reading gives the same result, and the sūrah wants the result rather than the mechanism: nothing about these messengers, or about anyone else, is outside the knowing.

**Why the Clause Follows the Selection of Messengers**

The pronoun refers most immediately to the messengers of verse 75.

That placement is pointed. God chooses messengers, and He knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them — which is to say that the selection was not made in ignorance of what each of them would face. The rejections, the expulsions, the years in which nothing appeared to happen: all of it was known before the selection was made.

For the Prophet hearing this, that is the substance of the verse. His mission is not an experiment that has gone unexpectedly badly. The One who chose him knew what he was walking into, and the choice stands.

**The Sūrah's Attribute Pattern**

Verses 75 and 76 are part of a closing sequence in which the sūrah names the divine attributes in rapid succession: Hearing and Seeing (v. 75), knowing all and the return (v. 76).

The pattern is worth noticing because it is how the sūrah ends. After seventy-six verses of argument, legislation, history and parable, the chapter closes by stating what God is rather than by adding another ruling. The attributes are the ground of everything that came before them.

**"To God All Matters Are Returned"**

*Wa-ilā Allāhi turjaʿu al-umūr* — and to God all matters are returned.

The verb is passive and the subject is *al-umūr*: affairs, matters, the whole business of human life in all its detail. Not people only — people return to Him — but the matters themselves: the disputes, the plans, the injuries, the unfinished work.

That breadth is the verse's comfort and its warning. Every unresolved thing is going somewhere, and the somewhere is a knowing agent rather than an archive.

The same formula appears at the end of several sūrahs and in the middle of others, always in this form: *and to God all matters are returned*. The repetition across the Qur'an is what allows the reader to fill in the sense — the return is for judgment, and the judgment is by the One who knew the matter from both ends.

**The Verse as a Bridge**

Verses 74 to 76 form a unit: the failure to measure God, the right of selection, and the knowledge and return that make the selection meaningful.

They also prepare the sūrah's close. The two final verses are commands — bow and prostrate (v. 77), and strive for God as He deserves (v. 78) — and they are addressed to a community that has just been told who is addressing it. Knowledge of what is before and behind, and the return of all matters, are the premises on which a command of that weight can rest.

**Knowledge and Return Belong Together**

The verse joins two clauses that are often separated, and the joining is the sūrah's final theological statement.

Comprehensive knowledge on its own would be surveillance. A return of all matters without knowledge would be an emptying. Held together they describe a judgment: everything a person has done is known, and everything a person has done is going to be answered for.

That is the premise the sūrah's closing commands rest on. Prayer, alms and holding fast (v. 78) are not undertakings a person would take up if his affairs were simply going to dissolve. They are the behaviour of someone who expects his matters to arrive somewhere and to be examined when they do.

**The Clause Answers the Sūrah's Hardest Question**

Every sūrah that promises a judgment owes its reader an answer to one objection: why is the judgment taking so long?

This sūrah has taken the question more seriously than most. It devoted three verses to the demand that the punishment be hurried (22:47), answered that the measures of time are incommensurable, and then documented the pattern in the ruins of nine communities (22:42–48). Verse 76 is the last word on it. All matters are returned, and the return has a destination that is not in question.

What is in question is only the interval, and the sūrah has been consistent about what the interval is for: the invitation, the testing, and the record that is being written while both proceed.

**Why the Sūrah Ends This Way Rather Than With a Threat**

The sūrah opened with the most severe threat in the Qur'an's repertoire — an earthquake at the Hour, mothers dropping children. It closes with attributes and commands.

The movement is deliberate. A reader who has followed the argument from verse 1 to verse 76 does not need another warning; he needs to know what is being asked of him, and by whom. Verses 77 and 78 supply the first; verses 74 to 76 have just supplied the second.
