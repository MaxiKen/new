## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:100

> Then he raised his parents to the throne, and they all fell down in prostration to Joseph, who then said, “O my dear father! This is the interpretation of my old dream. My Lord has made it come true. He was truly kind to me when He freed me from prison, and brought you all from the desert after Satan had ignited rivalry between me and my siblings. Indeed my Lord is subtle in fulfilling what He wills. Surely He ˹alone˺ is the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Raised to the Throne**

Joseph seats his parents on the throne. The commentators gloss the action as elevating them to a place of honour — the royal seat itself, according to one account — and the gesture is the chapter's clearest statement of what the elevation of verse 56 amounts to in practice. A man who has settled wherever he pleased in Egypt gives the highest seat in the room to his father and mother. The verse does not describe the throne's material or the setting, and it does not record any objection from the Egyptian court. What it records is an ordering of honour, performed in public, in which the ruler of the country's stores places two arrivals from the desert above himself. The publicity is the point. A minister who raised two arrivals from the desert to his own seat in front of his court was making a statement about where his authority came from, and the chapter records the statement without glossing it. Joseph honours his parents in the only register Egypt recognises, and he does so at the cost of his own precedence.

**The Prostration and Its Meaning**

They all fell down in prostration to Joseph. The commentators identify the party as Joseph's parents and his eleven brothers, corresponding to the sun, the moon and the eleven stars of the dream in verse 4. They also explain the gesture: prostration toward a person in that period was a form of greeting and honour, comparable to standing for someone out of respect or kissing the hand, and was not an act of worship. The distinction matters for the verse's theology and for its law. What is described is a ceremonial acknowledgment of a person God has elevated, performed by a family that has just been fed, healed and forgiven by him, and it is the physical fulfilment of a vision reported in the chapter's second narrative verse. The fulfilment also answers the warning that opened the story. Jacob told his son not to report the dream to his brothers lest they plot against him, and they plotted; ninety-six verses later the same brothers are prostrating, and the plotting is over. The verse closes the sūrah's longest thread with a gesture and says nothing whatever about the irony.

**"This Is the Interpretation of My Old Dream"**

Joseph supplies the interpretation himself, and the word he uses is *taʾwīl* — the same term his father used when warning him not to relate the vision (12:6), the term the narrator used for what God would teach him (12:21), the term both prisoners asked for (12:36), and the term the court declared itself unable to supply (12:44). The chapter's central technical word is used throughout for the meaning of a dream as an event that will occur. Here it reaches its final instance: the interpretation of verse 4 is not a statement but a scene, in which eleven brothers and two parents bow to the boy who dreamt. Joseph names the connection himself, which is the only time in the sūrah that an interpretation is identified by the person who received it.

**"He Was Kind to Me When He Brought Me Out of Prison"**

The summary of thirty years mentions the prison and omits the well. The commentators explain the omission: Joseph had already told his brothers that there was no blame on them that day (12:92), and to name the well now would be to reopen a matter he had closed. The choice of the prison as the point of deliverance is also accurate in another sense. It was the prison that produced the two dreams, the cupbearer's memory, the king's summons and the office. What God brought him out of, in the sequence that led to this throne, was the cell — and the verse attributes the kindness to that event rather than to the injury that preceded it. The choice of reference is striking and not accidental. Joseph's summary of God's kindness passes over the well, the sale and the accusation, and even the elevation to office, to settle on the moment of release. What he remembers is being taken out of a place where he had been forgotten, and the verse lets that memory stand as the representative instance of favour in his life.

**"After Satan Had Ignited Rivalry Between Me and My Siblings"**

The explanation of the brothers' conduct is given to a third party. Joseph does not say they plotted, envied, sold him or lied. He says Satan stirred up hostility between him and his brothers, and the phrasing includes himself in the relation — *between me and my brothers* — rather than placing the fault entirely on one side. The commentators describe the move as tantamount to making an excuse for them, consistent with the mitigation he offered in verse 89 when he said they acted in ignorance. The chapter's account of the crime is thus stated three times in three registers: as an act of the brothers' souls made to seem fair to them (12:18), as an act done in ignorance (12:89), and as an enmity stirred up by Satan (12:100).

**"My Lord Is Subtle in What He Wills"**

The word denotes subtlety, refinement, the reaching of an end by means that are not visible in the means. The commentators explain that the manner in which God carries out His will is subtle, and the verse is the chapter's own summary of its plot. A dream is disclosed, a plot is made, a well is chosen, a caravan passes, a market prices a child at twenty dirhams, a household buys him, a woman desires him, a prison receives him, two servants dream, a king dreams, a cupbearer forgets and remembers, a famine is forecast, a cup is planted, a law is supplied by the accused. None of these looks like the fulfilment of verse 4 while it is happening. The word *subtle* is Joseph's name for the arrangement.

**"The All-Knowing, the All-Wise"**

The verse closes with the two names, and they close the story in the same terms in which Jacob held it during its worst point. Verse 83 ended with the same pairing, spoken by a blind father who had just rejected his sons' report and hoped for the return of three children. Verse 100 ends with the pairing spoken by a son seated in front of that father, with the family assembled and the dream fulfilled. The chapter's last theological statement is therefore identical to its darkest one, and the identity is the argument: the names that made sense as hope in Canaan make sense as fact in Egypt, and nothing about them has changed except what the speaker can now see. That is the verse's final claim about the story. Every element of it — the dream, the well, the shirt, the prison, the famine, the cup — was already arranged when it happened, and the names invoked here describe the arrangement rather than a response to it. Joseph's recognition adds nothing to the events; it changes his description of them.
