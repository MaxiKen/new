## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:43

> Why did they not humble themselves when We made them suffer? Instead, their hearts were hardened, and Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Expects No Answer**

The verse asks why the people did not humble themselves, and the question is not seeking information. It is the form the Book uses when a response was available, expected, and refused. The suffering had been sent with a declared purpose, and the purpose was not achieved, so the failure belongs to the people who lived through the affliction and used it for nothing.

The Book records the same reproach in the sūrah of the Believers, where God says that the people were seized with torment and yet never humbled themselves to their Lord, nor appealed to Him submissively (23:76). The complaint is not that they lacked a reason to turn; it is that the reasons kept arriving and nothing in them moved.

**Hardening as a Response to the Wrong Kind of Relief**

Hardness of heart is described in the Book as a condition that can develop in anyone who receives a great deal of something without being changed by it. The sūrah of Iron warns the believers not to become like those who were given the Scripture before them, who were spoiled for so long that their hearts became hard (57:16). The children of Israel are said to have hardened after the covenant was broken (5:13), and their hearts are compared to rock, or harder than rock (2:74).

The process described is gradual rather than sudden. A person who prays during a crisis and then forgets the prayer afterwards has not returned to his previous state; he has added to it the habit of not being changed by what he has seen. Each cycle leaves the heart less responsive, and the verse reports the result as a fact about the people rather than a punishment imposed on them.

**How Misdeeds Come to Look Beautiful**

The second half of the verse names the mechanism by which the hardening is rewarded: Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them. The phrase recurs in the Book with remarkable frequency, and it is used for the entire range of conduct that a society persuades itself is normal. Satan made their deeds appealing to them and hindered them from the way (27:24); he made their deeds appealing to them in the battle of Badr and then abandoned them (8:48); evil associates were set on the wrongdoers and made their past and future misdeeds appealing to them (41:25).

The same phrase is used of Pharaoh, whose evil deeds were made so appealing that he was hindered from the right way (40:37), and of the pagans who were made to see the killing of their own children as acceptable (6:137). In each case the misdeed is not denied. It is renamed, decorated, and made into a practice with reasons attached.

**The Partnership Between the People and Their Tempter**

The verse names Satan as the agent, and it does not thereby excuse the people, since the question in the first half was directed at them. The Book treats the relationship as one in which both parties are active: Satan tempts (15:39), and the person who follows him has chosen to follow. The Book also states that the delusion is a general tendency of human beings who prefer the immediate (2:212), where the life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers, and where desires of every kind have been made attractive to people (3:14).

That combination is what gives the verse its weight. The people wanted what they were being shown, and they wanted the reasons that made it acceptable. The tempter supplied the vocabulary; the appetite was already in place.

**Hardening as a Consequence of What Was Refused**

Commentators read this verse together with the passages about failure to respond to earlier warnings. The sūrah of the Heights describes the sequence directly: hardship, then a change to prosperity, until the people flourished and argued that their forefathers had seen the same pattern, and then they were seized by surprise while they were unaware (7:95). The hardening in the verse is thus a stage in a process that begins with a warning received and set aside, and it is the stage in which the warning loses its power to disturb.

That reading also explains why the verse uses the past tense for something the audience could watch happening around them. The Makkans had been living with the message for years and were still untouched by it, and the description in the verse is the diagnosis of their present condition presented as history.

**What the Verse Leaves the Reader Holding**

The sentence ends without comfort, and it does so deliberately, because the point is the closing of a door. A community that is suffering has the option of humbling itself, and the verse reports that these communities took the other option and that the option became progressively harder to take. The verses that follow describe what happened to them, and the description is the answer to the question the sūrah has just asked.

A reader living in the middle of his own life can still do what these people did not. That is the function of the verse in the sūrah's argument, and it is why the next sentence, which reports the sudden seizure of a people at the height of their confidence, is placed immediately after it.

The Book gives a detailed example of the hardening in the story of Pharaoh's people, and the detail shows how a community talks itself out of a lesson. When famine and crop failure struck them (7:130), their response was to divide the events by their own convenience: in prosperity they said that what they had was what they deserved, and in adversity they blamed it on Moses and those with him, while the Book notes that all of it was destined by Allah and that most of them did not know (7:131). The test produced an accountant's argument rather than humility, which is precisely the outcome this verse reports in general terms.
