## Sūrah an-Naml 27:40

> But the one who had knowledge of the Scripture said, “I can bring it to you in the blink of an eye.” So when Solomon saw it placed before him, he exclaimed, “This is by the grace of my Lord to test me whether I am grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful, it is only for their own good. But whoever is ungrateful, surely my Lord is Self-Sufficient, Most Generous.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Described Only by What He Knows**

The second volunteer is identified by no name, no species and no rank. He is *alladhī ʿindahu ʿilmun min al-kitāb* — the one who had knowledge from the Book. Everything the Qur'an tells us about him is contained in that phrase.

The contrast with the previous speaker is built into the description. The *ʿifrīt* was named by his kind and vouched for himself; this one is named by what he has access to and says nothing about himself at all. His entire speech is five words in Arabic: a claim about time, with no assurance of strength or reliability attached. The Qur'an's refusal to identify him further is a deliberate withholding, since the narrative could easily have supplied a name. What matters for the argument is the category, and the category is knowledge of a Book.

**Who Was He?**

The commentators filled the gap in three different ways, and the disagreement is instructive. Al-Ṭabarī takes him to be a man, and the name usually supplied is Āṣif ibn Barkhiyā, said to have known a name of God by which supplications are answered. Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have identified him as Khiḍr, the figure who later becomes Mūsā's teacher. Al-Rāzī records and prefers a third possibility: that the speaker was Sulaymān himself, addressing the *ʿifrīt*.

Al-Rāzī's grounds are contextual and they are the strongest of the three. A prophet would be the person in the room most knowledgeable about the Book. And the gratitude that immediately follows — *this is by the grace of my Lord to test me* — reads most naturally as the words of the one who performed the act rather than of one who watched someone else perform it. Against this, the verse's own grammar introduces the knower and Sulaymān as apparently distinct, with Sulaymān *seeing* the throne set before him. None of the three identifications can be established, and the first can be traced to its source. The name Āṣif ibn Barkhiyā enters the commentaries through Ibn Isḥāq, the biographer, who reports him as a companion of Sulaymān — which places it in the same body of transmitted narrative material as the other Israelite detail in this story, not in the ḥadīth corpus. The Khiḍr identification is reported from Ibn Masʿūd and is likewise an opinion rather than a prophetic report. The Qur'an gives no name, and the disagreement among the commentators is itself the evidence that none of them had one.

**Which Book, and What the Knowledge Did**

*ʿIlmun min al-kitāb* is a portion of knowledge from the Book, and the partitive again matters — not the whole of it, a share. Al-Rāzī canvasses what the Book might be: the Preserved Tablet described in *“in a Preserved Tablet.”* (85:22), the scriptures of the prophets generally, or some revelation particular to Sulaymān, though what that would be he does not specify.

The mechanism is left equally open. Al-Ṭabarī's transmitted accounts have the man invoking a divine name, and some reports have the throne rising out of the ground rather than travelling. What the text establishes is only the relation: knowledge from a revealed source produced an effect that a mountain-sized jinn could not match for speed. That is a claim about where power actually comes from, and it disposes of the reading in which this scene is a magic contest. Nothing here is a technique anyone could acquire by practice. Both volunteers act by permission; the difference is that one of them acts through what God has disclosed.

**"Before Your Glance Returns to You"**

The phrase for the promised speed is *qabla an yartadda ilayka ṭarfuk*, literally before your gaze comes back to you. It means immediately, but the image is worth unpacking because the commentators drew different pictures from it.

Al-Ṭabarī reports one reading in which the sense is: in less time than it would take a man within your sight to walk over to you. Another takes it as the time required for your eye to travel out to the limit of its range. A third abandons the visual measure entirely and has the throne spring up from the ground where Sulaymān sat. The three readings agree on instantaneity and differ on the metaphor. What all of them preserve is that the unit of measurement is an act of seeing, which is apt for a chapter whose recurring subject is what people look at and what they fail to notice. The *ʿifrīt* measured his offer in the duration of a council session. This one measures it in the movement of an eye.

**The Throne Arrives and the King Suspects Himself**

Then comes the reaction, and it is the most important thing in the verse. Sulaymān sees the throne in front of him and does not celebrate. He calls it a grace from his Lord given to test him — *li-yabluwanī a-ashkuru am akfur* — whether I will be thankful or ungrateful.

Al-Ṭabarī's gloss is that Sulaymān understood such astonishing blessings to carry a trial of sincerity inside them. That is the insight, and it inverts the ordinary reading of good fortune. Most people examine hardship for a divine purpose and accept success without inspection. He does the reverse. Having just watched a throne cross a continent at his word, his first thought is that this is the more dangerous of the two conditions, because nothing about it feels like a test. The same recognition shaped his prayer after the ant, where he asked to be marshalled into gratitude rather than assuming he would feel it. A man who has already identified his own weak point does not need a second warning.

**Gratitude Benefits Nobody but the Grateful**

The closing sentences state the theology exactly. Whoever gives thanks gives thanks for his own soul; whoever is ungrateful, my Lord is *ghaniyy karīm* — Self-Sufficient and Generous.

Al-Ṭabarī reads the mention of self-sufficiency as emphasising that God needs nothing from human gratitude and is neither helped by its presence nor harmed by its absence. That is the point of naming the attribute here rather than, say, the Avenger. God does not collect thanks; the transaction is entirely one-directional, and what gratitude does is change the person performing it. Pairing self-sufficiency with generosity makes the argument complete: He does not need your thanks, and He gives anyway. That pairing also answers the fear the test might otherwise produce. A king who has just realised his blessing is a trial might reasonably expect the blessing to be withdrawn if he fails. The last word of the verse says the giver is generous, which is not the attribute of one waiting to repossess. The throne now in Sulaymān's hall is prepared for its owner's arrival in the verse that follows.
