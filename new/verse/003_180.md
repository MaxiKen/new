## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:180

> And do not let those who ˹greedily˺ withhold Allah’s bounties think it is good for them—in fact, it is bad for them! They will be leashed ˹by their necks˺ on the Day of Judgment with whatever ˹wealth˺ they used to withhold. And Allah is the ˹sole˺ inheritor of the heavens and the earth. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning to the Miserly**

The verse tells those who are miserly with what God has given them not to suppose that their holding back is good for them, and it states that it is evil for them. The sentence deals with a calculation that people make about their property, and it reverses the calculation's conclusion.

The sūrah has dealt with property at several points: the command to spend, the descriptions of those who give in ease and hardship, and the earlier passage about defrauding the community of what was held in common. The verse before the reader belongs to that sequence, and it addresses the withholding that the community's critics practiced.

**The Bounty Recalled**

The verse describes what the people withhold as what God has given them from His bounty, and the phrase places the property in question within a gift. The word for bounty has appeared in the sūrah in the descriptions of the martyrs and of the men who returned from the pursuit, and its use here is the same.

The placement matters for the warning's logic. What the miserly person holds is not his own achievement but something received, and the sentence treats the withholding as a response to generosity rather than as the prudent management of what one has. The Book's instructions about wealth are consistently grounded in this understanding.

**Material Wealth and Spiritual Knowledge**

The commentators read the verse as covering both material wealth and the knowledge that a person is given, and al-Ṭabarī applied it specifically to the People of the Book who refused to teach what they had received. The verse's wording about the bounty of God supports the wider application.

The two kinds of withholding share a structure. In each case a person keeps something that was given for a purpose beyond his own comfort, and in each case the holding is presented in the Book as a loss to the holder rather than as a gain. The passage about the earlier community's scholars is the clearest instance the sūrah's first readers would have recognised. The sūrah's readers are expected to examine their own case along both lines.

**The Collaring on the Day of Resurrection**

The verse states that on the Day of Resurrection those who were miserly will be collared by what they withheld, and the image is the same one the sūrah used in its passage about the defrauder who carries what he took. The tradition records an account attributed to the Prophet in which a person's treasure appears on that day as a serpent that follows him and wraps itself around him, saying that it is his wealth and his treasure.

The Book's imagery in these passages gives property a body that accompanies its owner. What a person kept and refused to part with becomes the thing that binds him, and the sentence is designed to make the miser see the object of his attachment in its final form.

**The Book's Other Warnings About Miserliness**

The Book returns to the subject in several passages. One describes those who are stingy and encourage others to be stingy, and it reminds them that God is self-sufficient. Another tells the believers that whoever withholds gives up only his own advantage, since God is self-sufficient while people are in need.

The sūrah of the Repentance announces a painful punishment for those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in the way of God. All of these passages describe the same error and the same outcome, and the verse before the reader is the sūrah's own statement of the warning. The repetition indicates how persistent the error is in human communities, since each generation is addressed in the same terms.

**The Contrast With Spending**

The Book places the miserly against the image of those who spend, and the contrast is drawn with the same emphasis. One passage compares what is spent in God's cause to a grain that produces seven ears, each containing a hundred grains, and it states that God multiplies the reward as He wills. Another describes those who give of their wealth to purify themselves.

The two pictures stand at the opposite ends of the Book's instruction about property. What the miser believes he is preserving is described as a serpent on his neck, and what the giver parts with is described as a seed that becomes a harvest, and the sūrah's readers are expected to make the comparison.


The verse states that to God belongs the inheritance of the heavens and the earth, and the commentators explained the phrase with care. God is in possession of everything always, and the inheritance is spoken of because all other owners eventually cease while He remains.

The reading makes the sentence a comment on the futility of hoarding, since what a person holds is held for a short time by an arrangement that he did not create and cannot maintain. What a person holds is held for a short time by an arrangement that he did not create and cannot maintain, and the Book's phrasing reminds the reader of the end of every ownership.

**The Closing Statement About Awareness**

The verse ends with the statement that God is aware of everything the community does, and the sentence closes the passage as it has closed several others in this sūrah. The awareness covers the hidden calculations as well as the visible acts.

The community that recites the verse is told that its treatment of what it has been given is known in full, and the sentence makes the accounting of the Day of Resurrection a matter of certainty rather than of speculation. The sūrah's later passages continue to deal with the community's property and its obligations, and the warning in this verse sets the terms for everything that follows.
