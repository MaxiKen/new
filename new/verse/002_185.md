## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:185

> Ramaḍân is the month in which the Quran was revealed as a guide for humanity with clear proofs of guidance and the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. So whoever is present this month, let them fast. But whoever is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. Allah intends ease for you, not hardship, so that you may complete the prescribed period and proclaim the greatness of Allah for guiding you, and perhaps you will be grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Month Defined by Its Book**

The verse names the month and defines it — not by its fast but by its revelation. Ramadan is the month in which the Qur'an was sent down, as guidance for humanity, with the clear signs and the criterion. The priority of this definition is the verse's first lesson: the month is sacred because of what entered the world through it, and the fast is the human response to that entry, not its cause. The exegetes described the descent in two stages, following the Qur'an's own accounts elsewhere: the Book was sent down in its entirety to the guarded tablet in heaven — the preserved original of 85:22 — and thence to the lowest heaven of this world, from which the angel Gabriel brought it to the Prophet passage by passage, occasion by occasion, across twenty-three years. The month's connection to that descent is fixed by the Night of Power within it — the night better than a thousand months, on which the sending down occurred (97:1–2) — so that every Ramadan reenacts, in its fast and its recitation, the month the Book arrived. The believers do not merely fast in Ramadan; they fast in the anniversary of revelation, and the traditional month-long recitation of the whole Qur'an in the night prayers keeps the definition audible.

**Guidance, Clear Signs, Criterion**

The three descriptors the verse gives the Book map the ways a revelation guides. *Hudan* — guidance — is direction itself, the road named. *Bayyināt* — the clear signs, the manifest proofs — are the evidence along the road, the verses and demonstrations that make the direction recognizable as true rather than merely asserted. *Furqān* — the criterion, the separator — is the instrument of judgment, the standard by which right is distinguished from wrong, case by case. The same triad describes revelation's function throughout the Qur'an, and its presence here explains why the month of the Book is kept by the body: a criterion is not only read but practiced, and the fast is the practice in which the separation between the lawful and the forbidden is felt hourly — the morsel permitted at sunset and forbidden at noon is furqān made edible. The verse thus ties the month's two contents together: the Book that separates truth from falsehood, and the fast that trains the will in every smaller separation, so that the community holding both learns the criterion with its mind in the recitation and with its hunger in the day.

**Whoever Is Present, Let Him Fast**

The command that follows is the legislation's completion: whoever of you is present in the month, let him fast it. The wording — present, witnessing the month — was read against the exemptions of the previous verse, and the reading fixed the law's shape. Al-Qurṭubī records the decisive consequence: this command abrogated the option, reported in the early community, of feeding a poor person instead of fasting; from this verse forward the fast is the duty of everyone present and able, and the feeding remains only for those who cannot. The exemptions are then repeated in the same breath — the sick, the traveler, the same number of other days — and the repetition is legally significant: the concession survives the tightening. The month-fast is now universal in obligation and particular in application, binding on the present and the well, lifted for the journeying and the ill, with the deferral arithmetic unchanged. The jurists' debates over the clause's details — how sick, how far a journey, whether the traveler fasts if able — are the verse's working-out in the schools, with opinions differing as they do; but the structure is the verse's own and admitted by all: presence in the month triggers the fast, incapacity suspends it, and the suspended days are kept as a debt to the same law.

**Ease Intended, Not Hardship**

The reason clause is among the most-quoted sentences in Islamic legal literature: *God desires ease for you, and does not desire hardship for you*. The clause is not decoration; it is a hermeneutic key the jurists applied across the law. The exemptions of illness and journey are instances of the desired ease — the same Lawgiver who commanded the fast removed it from the bodies it would harm, and the removal is as much His will as the command. The principle generalizes: no hardship is laid upon you in the religion (22:78), and the Prophet's standing instruction to his envoys was to make things easy and not difficult, to give glad tidings and not drive people away. The verse thereby installs mercy inside the legislation's logic — the fast is severe and the severity is bounded by the same hand, so the believer receives both the discipline and its limits as one gift. This is why the exemptions are not experienced as loopholes in the tradition but as evidence: the concession proves the Legislator's intent, and the intent explains why the obligated fast is undertaken joyfully rather than resentfully. A law whose author has declared that He desires ease is a law whose every harshness carries its own measured scope.

**Completing the Number and Proclaiming the Greatness**

The verse's final movement gives the month its ending: complete the number, and magnify God for having guided you. The completion was read two ways, both operative. The first: complete the count of the fast's days — whether the month arrives at twenty-nine or thirty, its number is completed by the sighting that ends it, and the makeup days of the exempted complete their number in turn. The second: complete the obligations the month carries, so that nothing of the prescribed period is left unperformed. Then the magnification — *takbīr* — which the tradition located at the month's close in the chanting of *Allāhu akbar* on the way to the festival prayer, the community proclaiming God's greatness aloud after thirty days of proclaiming it silently, by hunger. The clause states the reason for the proclamation: *for having guided you* — the takbīr is gratitude for the guidance, which is the same word the verse used for the Book itself. The month thus ends as it began, in the vocabulary of guidance: the Book sent down as guidance, the fasters magnifying God for having guided, the whole legislation framed as one course in being led. And the last word opens beyond it — that you may be thankful — placing the completed fast on the same footing as every blessing the sūrah has legislated gratitude for, the month's hunger resolving into the community's thanks.
