## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:7

> The disbelievers say ˹mockingly to one another˺, “Shall we show you a man who claims that when you have been utterly disintegrated you will be raised as a new creation?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mockery Staged as an Invitation**

The sentence is built as an offer: *hal nadullukum* — shall we point you to, shall we direct you to. In ordinary Arabic this is the phrasing of a guide showing the way to something valuable, and the Qur'an uses it that way elsewhere. Here the speakers borrow the form of helpful direction and fill it with ridicule. That inversion is the whole rhetorical strategy of the verse. They are not arguing against resurrection; they are producing the man who teaches it as a curiosity to be looked at, the way one might point out an eccentric in the market. Notice also that they are speaking to each other, not to him. The Prophet is referred to in the third person — *a man* — while the audience being recruited is the crowd. Contempt that addresses its target directly at least concedes that the target is worth addressing. Contempt that talks about him in front of him is denying him standing in the conversation altogether, and the Qur'an preserves the exact grammar of that denial.

**"A Man" — The Deliberate Withholding of a Name**

They do not say Muḥammad, and they do not say the one who recites. They say *rajulun*, a man, with the indefinite ending that in Arabic can carry dismissal: some man, a certain man. Two things are being accomplished at once. The indefinite reduces a known figure from their own tribe to an anonymous specimen, and it quietly denies the category he claims. He is not a messenger, not a warner, just a man — which is precisely the objection their predecessors raised against earlier prophets in almost the same words: *"We see you only as a human being like ourselves"* (11:27). The Qur'an's answer to that objection runs throughout the Book and is not given here. What this verse records instead is the social mechanics of dismissal: strip the name, supply an indefinite noun, and the audience has been told how to hear whatever comes next before a word of content has been spoken.

**The Word They Chose for Decay**

The strongest word in their sentence is *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq* — torn apart with a complete tearing, rendered here as *utterly disintegrated*. The root *m-z-q* means to rip fabric, and the form used is intensive, then reinforced by its own cognate noun, a construction Arabic reserves for maximum emphasis. They are not describing death politely. They are describing a corpse pulled to shreds and scattered until no piece is identifiable. Their choice of vocabulary is the argument. Other Qur'anic passages report the same objection in milder terms — bones and dust (17:49), decayed bones (36:78), dust alone (50:3) — but these speakers go further, selecting the most violent image available so that the claim of reassembly sounds maximally absurd. The verse also contains a small irony they did not intend. The same verb returns later in this very sūrah, when the people of Sheba are *scattered utterly* (34:19) as a punishment. The word they use to mock the power to reassemble is the word describing what that power does to a civilisation.

**The Objection Is About Identity, Not Just Physics**

What exactly did they find impossible? Not the existence of divine power in general — they conceded a creator. The difficulty is the one their wording exposes: if a person has been torn into unidentifiable pieces, what would it even mean to bring *him* back rather than something new? Their phrase *khalqin jadīd*, a new creation, contains the problem. A new creation is new; the old one is gone. The Qur'an meets this objection at several points by refusing the premise that scattering equals erasure, which is the work the earlier verses of this sūrah have already done — the Record that holds everything down to an atom's weight (34:3), and the knowledge of everything that goes into the earth (34:2). Yā-Sīn answers the same complaint by pointing at the first making: *"They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being"* (36:79). The reply turns on knowledge as much as on power, which is why this sūrah spent its opening verses establishing knowledge.

**"New Creation" Is Their Phrase, and the Qur'an Keeps It**

The expression *khalq jadīd* recurs across the Book, and almost always in the mouths of deniers. Al-Isrāʾ: *"When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?"* (17:49). Al-Raʿd reports the question and comments on the questioners: *"When we are reduced to dust, will we really be raised as a new creation?"* (13:5). Al-Muʾminūn puts it as a chief's sarcasm to his people (23:35). What is striking is that the Qur'an does not replace the term with one of its own. It adopts the deniers' phrase and affirms it — yes, a new creation, exactly as you say, and the newness is not the difficulty you take it to be. Sabaʾ has already shown the mechanism in its second verse: things go into the earth and things come out of it, continuously, and nobody watching a field regards the emergence as a violation of anything. The objection depends on treating one instance of a familiar pattern as uniquely impossible.

**Why the Mockery Is Quoted at Length Instead of Summarised**

The Qur'an could have said that they denied the resurrection, and moved on. Instead it hands them the microphone and lets the sentence run its full length, complete with the stagey invitation, the anonymous *a man*, and the shredding verb. There is a reason for the method. Ridicule works by tone rather than content, and tone cannot be refuted by paraphrase — it has to be reproduced and then allowed to sit in the open where its emptiness shows. Read cold, the sentence contains no argument at all: it names a claim, describes it vividly, and expects laughter to do the rest. By preserving it verbatim, the sūrah lets its audience notice that. The technique recurs through this chapter, which quotes its opponents more often than most — their denial of the Hour (34:3), their boast about wealth (34:35), their charge of sorcery (34:43). Their sentence is not yet finished; it runs on into the next verse, where the mockery finally produces two accusations.
