## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:18

> Now We have set you ˹O Prophet˺ on the ˹clear˺ Way of faith. So follow it, and do not follow the desires of those who do not know ˹the truth˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Means a Road Before It Means a Law**

The word rendered here as the Way of faith is sharīʿah, and this is the only place the noun occurs in the Qur'an. Its earlier sense is concrete: a track leading to water, the kind of path that a traveller in the Arabian desert needed in order to survive. That image is not lost in the verse. The Prophet is described as being placed on a road, and the road leads to something that keeps a person alive. Later Muslim usage made the word the standard term for the revealed law as a whole, which is a natural development of the image but which can obscure what the verse is doing. Here the emphasis falls on the path itself, on its clarity, and on the water at the end of it rather than on the body of rulings that later generations would derive.

**Placed on the Road, Not Asked to Build It**

The verb is decisive: We have set you on it. The road is not the Prophet's invention, and the verse does not present him as a thinker who worked out a religious system and then recommended it. God placed him on an existing path, the same path travelled by the messengers before him, and the word for placement suggests deliberate appointment rather than accidental arrival. The same construction appears elsewhere when the Book speaks of God placing a people in a land or placing an authority on the earth. A person placed somewhere has been given a position rather than a project, and the work that follows is the work of remaining there. That is the picture the verse paints of prophethood: not authorship of a way but firm footing on one.

**Command That Follows the Gift**

The imperative comes second: so follow it. A road is no use to someone who stands still at its head, and the verse is explicit that the gift of being guided does not remove the obligation to walk. The Book treats this combination consistently. Guidance is granted, and the guided person still has to act on what he was shown; the two are never presented as alternatives. The command also sets the direction of travel for everything else in the passage. If the road is given, then the disputes of the previous verse look different, because a community with a clear path in front of it has no need to argue about which way to face. The verse addresses the Prophet directly and, through him, everyone who accepts that the path is there.

**The Desires of Those Who Do Not Know**

The prohibition is stated in the plural: do not follow the desires of those who do not know. The Arabic word for desires carries the sense of inclinations that pull a person downward, and the Qur'an uses it for the appetites that shape a mind before evidence can reach it. The people in view are not described as enemies or criminals. They are described as people without knowledge, which makes the warning sharper rather than softer, because an argument can be answered while an appetite cannot. The Book returns to this command repeatedly, telling the Prophet that he will never satisfy the People of the Book until he follows their religion (2:120), that each community has been given its own code and way (5:48), that he should not follow the desires of the ignorant (42:15), and that he should bow to no one's demand for compromise (68:8–9). The danger is not only intellectual. It is social, and it arrives as an invitation to be reasonable.

**A Reported Offer and a Standing Warning**

Commentators report that the verse was revealed after leaders of the Quraysh approached the Prophet with an offer: they would follow him if he returned to the religion of his forefathers, whom they maintained had been better men than he was. The report is reported rather than established, but it matches the shape of the command, which answers an offer rather than a philosophical position. The pressure described is not the pressure to accept an argument; it is the pressure to stop making trouble, to trim the message for the sake of agreement, to grant the old ways the respect they had always been given. Every generation of believers faces some version of that offer, and the verse's answer is a flat refusal, expressed as a command about following rather than as an argument about who is right.

**"Those Who Do Not Know" — a Description, Not an Insult**

The verse does not say that its opponents are stupid or that their position has been refuted. It says that they do not know, which in the Qur'an's vocabulary is a factual description of a person whose judgments are being formed by something other than the evidence. The same Book says elsewhere that assumption cannot replace the truth, and it distinguishes carefully between those who are unaware and those who have been shown and have refused. The distinction preserves the possibility of argument in both directions: a person who does not know can come to know, and the verse's command to follow the path does not forbid explaining it. What it forbids is letting the desires of the uninformed set the agenda for the one who has been placed on the road.

**One Road, Many Codes, One Destination**

The Prophet is not the first traveller on the path. The Qur'an says that God placed him on a clear way just as he had placed the messengers before him, and it also says that each community was given a code of law and a way of life (5:48), which means the different codes were never meant to be melted into a single legal system by human negotiation. What they share is the destination, and what distinguishes them is the guidance granted to each people in its own time. This is why the verse can command following without commanding isolation. The Prophet is told to walk the road assigned to him and to refuse the desires of those who do not know, at a time when the roads of the People of the Book were still in use and their scholars were still respected. A road is followed, not negotiated, and no traveller improves his position by turning back.
