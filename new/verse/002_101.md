## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:101

> Now, when a messenger from Allah has come to them— confirming their own Scriptures—some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah behind their backs as if they did not know.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger Who Confirmed**

The verse restates the arrival with its decisive credential attached: *a messenger from Allah has come to them — confirming their own Scriptures*. The confirmation-relationship is the recital's standing answer to the provenance objection — the new Book testifying to the old, the arriving truth vindicating the truth already held — and the verse positions it as the scene's aggravating circumstance. This was not a foreign claimant demanding surrender of an inheritance; it was the inheritance's own witness arriving with the inheritance's own marks upon him. Verse 89 filed the recognition at the arrival; verse 99 named the signs' clarity; verse 101 combines them into the setup for the crime it is about to describe: everything needed for acceptance was present, present in the community's own idiom, certified by the community's own Book. The sentence's *now* — the temporal particle marking the completed setup — functions like a courtroom's *and then*: the conditions are established, the evidence is entered, and the verse's second half records what the people of the evidence did with it. The recital's method throughout this passage has been to remove every excuse before filing every charge; this verse is the method in single-sentence form — credential first, conduct second, with no room between them for a defense.

**Behind the Backs: The Body-Language of Abandonment**

The central image is physical and exact: *they cast the Book of Allah behind their backs*. The Arabic idiom — نبذ وراء الظهر, to throw behind the back — describes not loss but disposal: the deliberate motion of a hand putting something away in the direction the eyes do not look. The image's precision is in its direction. A book dropped is an accident; a book shelved is respect without use; a book cast behind the back is refusal performed with the whole body — the object discarded to the region of non-attention, where it cannot be seen, consulted, or consulted-by-others. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān uses the same idiom for the same crime at its origin: *"Allah took the covenant of those who were given the Scripture to make it known to people and not hide it, yet they cast it behind their backs and traded it for a fleeting gain"* (3:187) — and the pairing of the two verses gives the image its full anatomy: the Book was held in the hands, covenanted into custody, and the custody ended in a backward throw. The verse's grammar keeps the actors inside the Book's own community: *some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah* — the possessors disposing of their possession, the crime's peculiar shape. Outsiders cannot cast behind their backs a Book they never carried; the throw requires custody, which is why this verse indicts a betrayal available only to inheritors.

**Which Book Was Cast?**

The exegetes divide on the identity of the cast Book, and the division is fine-grained enough to matter. The phrase *the Book of Allah* could denote the Qur'an — the messenger's own revelation, rejected at his arrival. But the stronger reading, which al-Ṭabarī and the majority follow, takes it as the Torah, and the verse's own wording carries the argument: the subject is *those who were given the Book*, and what they cast is what they were given — their Scripture, specifically its testimony confirming this messenger, thrown behind the back at the moment the testimony became consequential. The two readings converge in practice, since casting the Torah's confirmation is casting the Qur'an's claim in the same motion; but the Torah-reading sharpens the indictment by locating the betrayal inside the community's most sacred possession. A parallel division runs over the subject: some exegetes restrict *those who were given the Book* to the learned — the scholars whose custody made the casting criminal in the full sense — while others (al-Qushayrī, al-Rāzī) extend it to all who take the Torah as their book and disregard what it says. The extended reading fits the verse's social anatomy better: the scholars cast first, at verse 75's station of informed distortion, and the casting became the community's posture when the majority followed the scholars' direction rather than the Book's. Both the object and the subject of the crime were disputed precisely because both were consequential — the Book's identity fixes which custody was betrayed, and the casters' identity fixes how far the betrayal reached.

**As If They Did Not Know**

The verse's final clause is its psychology: *as if they did not know*. The knowing is conceded by the sentence's whole structure — the confirmation was *their own Scriptures'*, the signs were clear (99), the recognition had occurred (89) — and the clause describes the performance of its opposite. *Ka-annahum lā yaʿlamūn*: the manner of the ignorant adopted by the informed, the posture of innocence worn over a record of comprehension. The recital has catalogued this performance at every station: the distortion *after understanding* (75), the private counsel that managed what public confession would concede (76), the covering laid over blessings received (90). The *as if* is the catalog's common form — a similitude maintained in public while the dissimilitude is known in private — and its cost is the verse's quiet statement: the performed ignorance must be performed continuously, against the performer's own memory, which makes it labor rather than lapse. The clause also fixes the legal character of the casting. Ignorance, actual and invincible, excuses; simulated ignorance aggravates, because the simulation is itself a second decision — the decision to make the first denial unanswerable. The verse indicts both decisions in three words, and the recital's audience keeps the diagnostic: wherever a community's conduct requires its members to behave as if they did not know what the community demonstrably knows, the *as if* is doing work, and the work is the casting of a Book behind backs that once carried it.

**The Anatomy of Abandonment**

What the verse describes is the mechanism by which a religious community abandons its own revelation without ever formally rejecting it, and the mechanism's subtlety is its danger. No book is burned in this verse; no council declares the Torah void; the confession of verse 76's meeting-room still says *we believe*. The abandonment happens in the direction of attention: the Book remains honored in statement while its operative testimony is thrown where consultation cannot reach it. The distinction the verse installs — between a Book held and a Book heeded — is the recital's permanent instrument for every custodian community. The Torah's confirmation of the coming messenger was in the Torah, known, located, quoted in private among the learned; the casting consisted in arranging public life so that this content never functioned. The verse's transfer to its hearing community is immediate and uncomfortable in the way all the recital's transfers are. Every Scripture-keeping people casts something behind its back: the ruling that costs, the testimony that would decide an argument the community has already settled otherwise, the verse that reads the community's own conduct. The casting is rarely announced and never feels like rejection — it feels like emphasis, like context, like the natural quiet of a text no longer quoted. The verse names it for what it is: the Book of Allah, in the custody of the people of the Book, behind their backs, in the posture of those who do not know — and the naming is the mercy, because an abandonment that can be named can still be undone, by turning the body around and picking the Book back up.
