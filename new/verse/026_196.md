## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:196

> And it has indeed been ˹foretold˺ in the Scriptures of those before.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Revelation Stands in a Line of Earlier Guidance**

The verse places the Quran within the history of revelation. It is not presented as a message with no relation to the scriptures given before it. The bracketed word foretold captures an interpretive sense: the earlier scriptures contained a description, anticipation, or testimony connected with what has now been revealed.

Continuity does not mean that every community preserved or interpreted its scripture in the same way. It means that Allah's guidance has a recognizable direction. The call to worship Him, reject corruption, and prepare for judgment is not an invention of one generation. The Quran confirms that prophetic truth has a history before its final Arabic expression.

**Two Readings Explain What Was Foretold**

Exegetes have read the pronoun in two main ways. Some understand it to refer to the Quran, meaning that its truth or description was present in earlier scriptures. Others understand the reference to concern the Prophet, whose coming and qualities were known through earlier revelation. The first reading follows the immediate subject of the verse, the revealed message. The second gains support from passages such as 7:157, which speaks of the Messenger's description in the Torah and the Gospel.

Neither reading turns the earlier scriptures into a replacement for the Quran. The stronger conclusion is that the verse affirms continuity at the level of divine guidance while allowing both the book and its messenger to be part of what earlier revelation anticipated. The Quran's own claim in 2:97 that it confirms what came before supports this connected reading.

**Foretelling Is Evidence, Not a Shortcut Around Understanding**

A prior testimony can strengthen recognition, especially for people who know the earlier scriptures. It does not remove the need to examine the message now being recited. A person cannot claim faith merely because a prediction is asserted; he must ask what has been foretold, how the description fits, and whether the new message carries the marks of truth.

This protects the verse from being used as a vague appeal to authority. The Quran points to a real relation with earlier revelation, while its arguments, moral commands, and linguistic clarity remain before the hearer. Continuity gives the message a place in sacred history, and present evidence gives the audience a responsibility in the present.

**The New Revelation Does Not Break the Old Moral Law**

The people of Shuʿayb were commanded to fear Allah and protect property; the Quran's later audience is called to the same basic submission. The outward circumstances differ, but the Lord is one and moral corruption remains corruption. A revelation that comes in a new language can still renew an old truth.

The verse therefore teaches the Prophet's opponents to examine the claim without pretending that it appeared from nowhere. Allah has sent guidance before, and He is sending guidance again. A person who recognizes the family resemblance of revelation should not let tribal rivalry or unfamiliar delivery conceal the continuity of the call.

The claim about earlier scriptures also protects the Quran from being treated as a detached moral philosophy. It speaks within a story of prophets, communities, warning, and response. The earlier books are not mentioned merely to provide an impressive pedigree. They form part of the evidence that Allah has repeatedly addressed human beings and required them to recognize His authority in their own circumstances.

Continuity should not be confused with a demand that every later reader know every earlier text in detail. The deniers are challenged because some of them had access to people who possessed that knowledge. A person without that background is still addressed by the Quran's own proof and guidance. The verse gives the knowledgeable an added responsibility, while it does not make salvation dependent on belonging to a particular scholarly community.

The two readings of what was foretold can also be held without forcing a contradiction. Earlier revelation may contain signs of the coming messenger and descriptions that prepare people to recognize the Quran he brings. The messenger and the message are joined in the prophetic mission. A scripture is not delivered by itself; it comes through a trustworthy human bearer who calls people to the Lord.

This continuity also explains why the Quran can correct corruption without presenting worship as a new invention. The people of Shuʿayb were not free to say that justice was irrelevant because their ancestors had different customs. Earlier prophets had already called communities to Allah, and the final revelation renews that call in a clear Arabic form. The moral law is not made true by novelty.

The verse finally asks the reader to approach unfamiliar revelation with fairness. Do not reject a claim merely because its form is new to you. Ask whether it fits the truth Allah has sent before, whether it calls to worship and justice, and whether its evidence withstands examination. Continuity is a bridge, not a substitute for belief, but it is a bridge the deniers were not entitled to ignore.

The verse also corrects a common picture of revelation as a series of unrelated religions competing for ownership of God. The prophets differ in language and circumstance, but the Lord who sends them is one. Their communities are asked to recognize the same basic claims: worship Allah, reject false authority, deal justly, and expect an account after death.

Earlier scripture can therefore function as a witness without making every historical community flawless. People may preserve texts, forget parts of a message, or argue over interpretation. None of those human conditions cancels the fact that Allah has sent guidance before. The Quran appeals to the original source and truthful knowledge, not to every later behavior performed in the name of scripture.

The word foretold also keeps expectation alive. A prophecy or description is not useful only after fulfillment; it prepares a person to recognize what arrives. The knowledgeable among a prior community could compare the new revelation with what they had learned. Those who refused were not lacking every possible path to recognition. They chose to close a path that earlier guidance had opened.

For the reader, continuity creates both confidence and duty. Confidence, because the Quran's call belongs to the long history of divine mercy. Duty, because a person who recognizes that history should not praise earlier revelation while resisting the guidance that confirms its source. The Lord's message is continuous enough to be recognized and fresh enough to demand a present response.
