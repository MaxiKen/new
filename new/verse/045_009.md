## Sūrah al-Jāthiyah 45:9

> And whenever they learn anything of Our revelations, they make a mockery of it. It is they who will suffer a humiliating punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Whenever" Describes a Practice**

The verse opens with a form that means each time, on every occasion, with no limit set. It is not reporting an incident but a habit: the man hears something from the revelations, and the effect is predictable. That construction matters for the reader's understanding of the people in view, because it rules out the excuse of a single bad day or a momentary lapse into cruelty. A practice has roots, and the practice described here is the second half of the portrait the sūrah has been building — the liar of v. 7, the arrogant listener of v. 8, and now the mocker who has made derision his ordinary way of handling what he hears. The Book's habit is to show the interior condition and then the behaviour it produces. Mockery is the behaviour.

**Any Fragment Will Do**

The verse says they learn anything of the revelations — a part, not the whole. That detail is accurate about how contempt usually works. A person need not master a text to jeer at it; a clause heard in passing, a verse repeated in the market, a rule that seems inconvenient taken on its own, will do. What the Qur'an criticises here is not the study of parts but the use of parts as a substitute for the whole. Someone who takes one sentence away from its context and laughs at it has not refuted anything; he has announced the size of the effort he is willing to make. The same defect shows up in the person who knows the Book only as performance, as a sound he has heard and never as a claim he must answer. Surface knowledge is not a lesser form of knowledge here. It is the raw material of the mockery.

**Two Kinds of Sign, One Kind of Contempt**

This sūrah has spent six verses teaching that the word for signs covers two domains: the created order and the recited text. Contempt can be aimed at either. A person mocks the signs in creation when he treats the sky, his own body, and the animals as accidents and refuses to grant that they point anywhere; a person mocks the revealed signs when he laughs at the sentences of the Book itself. The two insults have different flavours — one runs through ingratitude, the other through denial — but they come from the same refusal, which is the refusal to be addressed by anything. This is why the verse bundles them under one verb. The man who jokes about rain and the man who jokes about revelation have both decided that nothing out there has a claim on them, and the sūrah treats the decision, not the joke, as the offence.

**Humiliation Trails the Proud**

The punishment is called humiliating, and the choice of word answers the crime in the language of honour. The men in view wanted standing; status was the thing their mockery protected. The Book has already named God "the Almighty" in the second verse of the chapter, and the word used here for the punishment they receive comes from a root meaning low, base, and brought down. The Qur'an sets up this contrast repeatedly: what the proud protect is their rank, and what they are given instead is public dishonour. The reckoning is not a private disappointment but a humiliation, seen and acknowledged. In the world of the first hearers, where a family's standing was the currency of daily life, no sentence could have cut deeper, and the Book states it as a certainty rather than a threat.

**Mockery Needs an Audience, and Finds One**

Laughter is social. A man mocking the Qur'an in private would barely be a mocker of anything; the act exists in front of others, and it recruits. The verse uses a plural, "they", and its punishment is collective too, which fits the way derision works: one man's joke lowers the cost of the next man's doubt. This is why the Qur'an treats public mockery of revelation as a category of its own, distinct from private disbelief. The private doubter can still be argued with, and the Book argues with him at length. The mocker has already turned the question into a performance, and a performance has no interest in being corrected; what it needs is a crowd, and the crowd it gathers is the court in which it will later stand. The sūrah keeps the two cases apart because their remedies are different, and the second has none offered.

**The Verdict Keeps Its Shape Across the Sūrah**

This is the first time the sūrah states the penalty for mockery, and it will state it three more times before the chapter ends: what they ridiculed closes in on them, the Fire becomes their home, and their own deeds unfold before them. The repetition is not redundancy. It builds a pattern in which the punishment is never arbitrary and never invented at the end of the story — it is the thing the man himself chose to treat as nothing, returned to him as everything. The "humiliating punishment" of this verse is the first turn of that screw, and the word for it stays fixed through the verses that follow. What the mocker cannot keep is his pose, because the audience he gathered will see him in the end as the one position he never expected to occupy.

**The Two Audiences of Every Recitation**

A recitation always has two audiences, and the sūrah keeps them apart with care. One consists of the people standing in front of the reciter, who may mock, argue, or walk away; the other consists of the one whose words are being recited, who is present in the transaction whether or not anyone acknowledges him. The mocker believes he is performing for the first audience and that the second is a fiction, which is why the verse's sentence comes from the side he had written off. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same mistake in plainer terms, saying that a record is kept by honoured scribes who know what is done (82:11–12), and that no soul is left without a vigilant recorder (86:4). If the account is being taken by the very party being mocked, then contempt is not a cheap gesture at all. It is a statement made in front of the court, entered into evidence, and answered in the verses that follow.
