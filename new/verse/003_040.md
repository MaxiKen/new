## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:40

> Zachariah exclaimed, “My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?” He replied, “So will it be. Allah does what He wills.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Astonished Question, Not a Denial**

Zachariah's response to the announcement is a question, and the Book reports it without any note of censure. He states two facts — his own advanced age, his wife's barrenness — and asks how the thing announced can happen. This is the same response recorded in fuller form in the sūrah of Maryam, where he adds an appeal to God's earlier creation of him from nothing (19:8–9).

The distinction between a question and a rejection matters in this Book, and it is drawn by the text itself. When Abraham asks to be shown how God gives life to the dead, he is answered without rebuke and even told what to do to have his heart reassured (2:260). When Gabriel announces a birth to Sarah, the astonishment is met with the gentle reproof that the decree of God should not surprise anyone: *“Are you astonished by Allah’s decree?”* (11:73). Zachariah's question belongs to that family of exchanges: a believer asking how, not whether.

**The Two Obstacles He Names**

The two facts Zachariah mentions are the two total obstacles, and naming both is the point of the sentence. Advanced age rules out the ordinary contribution of the father, and barrenness rules out the ordinary contribution of the mother. Between them they leave the situation with no natural cause available at all.

Had he mentioned only one, a reader might imagine a partial explanation, a late marriage, or a delayed conception. The Book has him state the case at its hardest, and the answer he receives does not soften the facts or correct his observation. What follows instead is a statement about how God works, which suggests that the obstacles are not being denied but being classified.

**The Same Astonishment in Other Households**

The sūrah of Hūd narrates a nearly identical scene. The messengers bring Sarah news of a child, and she laughs and exclaims at the impossibility, saying that she is an old woman and her husband an old man, and describing the news as astonishing (11:72). The reply carries the same correction as the answer given to Zachariah, and it is followed by a blessing on the household (11:73). The sūrah of the Scatterers reports the same woman coming forward with a cry, holding her face, and asking how a barren old woman could bear a child, with the answer that her Lord has decreed it and is All-Wise, All-Knowing (51:29–30).

Three households, three astonished believers, one answer. The repetition is how the Book teaches: a reader who meets the same exchange in different stories learns the standing rule for impossible news — the objection is heard, and the power of the one announcing it is what settles the matter.

**"So Will It Be" — the Answer Given**

The reply is a phrase and a principle. *So will it be*, followed by the statement that God does what He wills. Nothing is said about the mechanics of the conception, nothing about the age of the parents, nothing about how the announced mission will be carried out.

The Book's formula for creation appears at exactly this point in several places: God says to a thing *Be*, and it is (2:117; 36:82; 40:68). The same formula is used in this sūrah when Mary asks how she can have a child with no man having touched her, and the answer there adds the extra clause that when God decrees a matter He simply tells it to be (3:47). Zachariah's reply and Mary's reply are constructed from the same material, which is why the two announcements belong together in the sūrah's structure.

**"Allah Does What He Wills" — the Doctrine in One Clause**

The clause at the end of the answer deserves its own attention because it is the ground of everything the sūrah says about prayer. God is not constrained by the order He has established; the order is His instrument, and He acts within it or beyond it as He wills. A statement of the same kind closes the account of creation's submission in another sūrah: whatever is in the heavens and the earth bows to Him, and whoever He disgraces none can honour, for He does what He wills (22:18).

The clause also protects the reader from a conclusion that this narrative might otherwise invite. If God could override the ordinary, then a person could demand the extraordinary as a right. The sentence says the opposite. The will is His, the choice of when to act outside the ordinary is His, and the petitioner's part is to ask and to wait, which is what Zachariah did.

**Why No Rebuke Was Given**

The Book rebukes some questions and answers others, and the difference is instructive. The people who asked Moses to show them God plainly, or who asked why a messenger was not an angel, were answered with refusal and warning because their questions were a pretext for rejection. Zachariah's question is the question of a servant whose whole life has been spent in the service of the sanctuary, standing there when the announcement came.

The sūrah therefore leaves the exchange as a model of honest prayer. A believer may say to God that the situation looks impossible, and the Book records the saying without disapproval, because the one who is addressed is the one who decrees, and complaining about impossibility to the one who decrees it is a form of conversation rather than a form of doubt.

**What the Exchange Produces**

The question and the answer together leave a doctrine in the text: the impossible is not a category that applies to God, and the natural order is a habit of His rather than a limit on Him. Readers of the sūrah who face their own closed situations are meant to carry that sentence with them.

The next request in the narrative asks for a visible sign that the promise will hold.
