## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:12

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, "To whom belongs everything in the heavens and the earth?" Say, "To Allah!" He has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. He will certainly gather ˹all of˺ you together for the Day of Judgment— about which there is no doubt. But those who have ruined themselves will never believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Puts the Answer in Their Mouths**

The verse has the Prophet ask and then answer on the audience's behalf, and it is a device the Book uses because the audience's own position supplies the premise. Ask them to whom the earth and those on it belong (23:84), and the exchange proceeds until they concede the point (23:89). Ask who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon, and they will certainly say, "Allah" (29:61). The same concession is recorded in 31:25 and 43:9.

What the question extracts is not new information but an admission. The Quraysh were not being asked to learn that God owns creation; they were being asked to notice that they already said so, in the marketplace, without drawing the conclusion.

**Ownership First, Judgment Second**

Once everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to God, the right to gather and judge is His without further argument. No third party holds a share in the audience, no other authority has jurisdiction over the verdict, and no one can appeal to a higher court because no higher court exists. The verse moves from ownership to assembly in one breath, and the sequence is logical rather than abrupt.

This is the sūrah's standard method. Creation is established in the first verses, accountability is drawn from it, and the idols are shown to be tenants with no title. The gathering of all people is simply the day when the owner of the property takes inventory of it.

**The Obligation God Placed on Himself**

Between the ownership clause and the announcement of the gathering sits a sentence that changes the tone of the whole verse: God has taken upon Himself to be Merciful. The Book expands it — Your Lord has taken upon Himself to be Merciful, forgiving those who sin in ignorance and then repent (6:54) — and elsewhere speaks of mercy that encompasses everything (7:156). No other attribute in the Qur'an is described as prescribed for God by God.

That construction carries a subtle point. Mercy is not simply a mood that arises in God when creatures deserve it; it is a commitment He announced, recorded, and bound Himself to honor. If mercy were merely temperamental, the respite the Makkans were enjoying would be unpredictable. Because it is a self-imposed obligation, delay has a reason and a limit.

**Why Mercy Is Mentioned Before the Gathering**

The order of the verse explains the world its hearers were living in. Mercy comes first, so punishment is not rushed; the gathering comes afterwards, so it is not cancelled. That is precisely the arrangement the Makkans were misreading. They took the long delay as proof that no accounting would ever be held, when the Book describes the same delay as the product of a promise God made to Himself.

Two errors grow in that interval: the wicked conclude that nothing is watched, and the impatient believer wonders why nothing is answered. The verse answers both by holding the two clauses together. The mercy is real and it is why the guilty are still walking around; the assembly is equally real and it is why they should not enjoy the walk too much.

**The Day About Which There Is No Doubt**

The Book attaches that formula to the Hour repeatedly: the Hour is coming and there is no doubt about it (40:59; 22:7); God will gather all people on the Day of Judgment, about which there is no doubt, and no word is more truthful than His (4:87). The same pairing of the gathering with an oath of certainty appears when God gives life, causes death, and then gathers everyone for a Day on which no one doubts (45:26).

The phrase is a statement about the reliability of the speaker rather than about a probability estimate. Doubt about the Hour is therefore not intellectual caution; it is a refusal of testimony. The verse treats it as the last item in a series of denials that have already been described in the sūrah — of the signs, of the messengers, and of the meeting with their Lord.

**When the Denial Was Put in Plain Words**

The Book preserves the flat version of the Makkan position, and the reply to it uses an oath. They say the Hour will never come to them; they are told to the contrary by the Lord of the unseen, that it will certainly come, and that not even an atom's weight in the heavens or the earth escapes Him, nor anything smaller or larger, but it is written in a perfect Record (34:3). The denial in that passage is not a request for evidence. It is an announcement that the subject is closed.

Set beside that report, the present verse reads as an exercise in patience. It hands the questioner a confession about creation, adds a clause about mercy, and then states the gathering as a fact about God's word. The three statements are arranged so that a person who accepts either of the first two has already granted the third, and a person who rejects the third must explain what he thinks happens to the property God owns.

**Those Who Ruined Themselves Cannot Believe**

The verse ends where the sūrah began, with a portrait of ruin. Those who have lost their own souls will not believe, and the same sentence returns later in the sūrah with the same finality (6:20). The word order is worth sitting with: the ruin is named first, the unbelief second. The Book presents unbelief here as a symptom of damage rather than a neutral opinion held by an otherwise healthy person.

That diagnosis matches the conduct described in the preceding verses. A people who asked for a sign and then called it sorcery, who requested an angel and would have dismissed him in human form, have not been deprived of evidence. They have spent something in themselves that evidence is received with, and the verse reports the loss as a fact about the losers rather than as a punishment imposed on them.
