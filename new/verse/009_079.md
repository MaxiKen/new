## Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:79

> ˹There are˺ those who slander ˹some of˺ the believers for donating liberally and mock others for giving only the little they can afford. Allah will throw their mockery back at them, and they will suffer a painful punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mockery Attacks Both Abundance and Scarcity**

The mockers criticize two opposite donors. They slander the person who gives generously and ridicule the person who can afford only a little. Their target is not the amount itself. Their target is the act of giving, because generosity threatens the selfish order they prefer. If a person gives much, they accuse him of showing off; if he gives little, they call the gift worthless.

This double attack reveals a standard designed to condemn everyone. It cannot be satisfied because its purpose is not evaluation but humiliation. The believer should recognize this tactic in public life. A malicious critic may use any result as proof against the person he already wants to discredit. The answer is not to stop doing good. It is to keep the intention directed to Allah and to use sound criteria when evaluating the work.

**A Large Gift Is Not Automatically Proud**

A generous donation can come from wealth, gratitude, and a sincere desire to relieve need. It can also be mixed with a desire for recognition. Human beings should not claim certainty about the unseen motive merely because the amount is large. They can protect the fund from vanity and still honor the possibility of sincere sacrifice.

The donor should examine himself. Does he prefer that people know, or would he still give if his name were hidden? If public acknowledgment serves accountability, it may be lawful; if it becomes the purpose, the gift is in danger. The correct response to suspicion is neither self-display nor paralysis. Give responsibly, purify the motive, and leave the final acceptance to Allah.

**A Small Gift Can Carry a Large Sacrifice**

The person who gives little may be giving a large share of what he owns. Amount alone cannot measure sacrifice. A small contribution from limited means may represent a night's labor, a family's careful budget, or the choice to share food that was already scarce. Allah knows the proportion between the gift and the giver's capacity.

This does not make every small donation automatically superior. It teaches the community not to despise modest giving. A poor person should not be shamed into giving beyond ability, and a wealthy person should not use a small public gift to avoid a larger obligation. Each servant asks what Allah has placed in his hand and gives with sincerity and wisdom.

**Mockery Destroys the Social Purpose of Charity**

Charity joins people across differences in wealth. Mockery breaks that bond by making the wealthy afraid to give publicly and the poor ashamed to give at all. The mocker wants both groups isolated: one anxious about being accused, the other convinced that his effort has no value. The result is less generosity and more resentment.

A healthy community protects donors from vanity without humiliating them and protects modest donors from contempt without romanticizing poverty. It teaches accurate accounting, privacy where appropriate, and gratitude for every sincere contribution. Correction should improve the giving, not make giving itself socially dangerous.

**Allah Returns the Mockery to Its Source**

The statement that Allah throws their mockery back at them describes a moral reversal. The people who thought they were superior become the ones exposed as foolish. Their laughter cannot diminish the gift, but it does diminish their own character. They reveal that they prefer contempt to service.

The believer should not imitate this reversal by mocking the mockers for pleasure. He can expose the wrong and protect the giver without feeding the same spirit. Allah's judgment is enough. The servant's task is to keep his own heart free from the desire to humiliate those who humiliate others.

**Sincerity Is Not Measured by the Crowd**

The verse frees the donor from public judgment. People may misunderstand a generous act or overlook a modest one, but Allah sees the means, the intention, and the need. This allows the believer to give without building his worship around approval.

He should also receive criticism intelligently. If a donation is wasteful or misdirected, he should correct it. If the criticism is merely slander, he should not surrender the good act to the slanderer's standard. The final measure is neither the largest amount nor the loudest laugh. It is whether the servant gave what he could, where Allah allowed, with a heart that sought Allah rather than an audience.

This verse also asks communities to protect the dignity of effort. Not every donor can give the same amount, and not every large gift should control the direction of the work. Transparent criteria, private encouragement, and gratitude without flattery can keep charity healthy. The giver with little should not be made into a symbol for others to admire while his actual need is ignored. The giver with much should not be praised so excessively that the gift becomes a title. Allah looks at the heart and the sacrifice, while people should look at need, lawful purpose, and responsible use.

The believer can answer this mockery by making charity more truthful, not more theatrical. He gives according to ability, checks that the need is real, and avoids turning another person's poverty into a public lesson. The community can honor generosity without creating a market for praise. It can invite the wealthy to give more without treating modest donors as ornaments. Allah's response to the mockers is enough; the servant does not need to reproduce their cruelty. He protects the small gift, disciplines the large gift, and leaves acceptance to the One who knows what each hand sacrificed.
