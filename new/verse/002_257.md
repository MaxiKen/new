## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:257

> Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light. As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guardian Named**

Walī is a word with a range of duties inside it: ally, protector, the one who manages the affairs of another. In the Qur'an it is used for kinship, for patrons, and for God, and the jurists distinguished walāya (the bond that carries protection) from wikāla (the delegation of a task). When the sentence says that God is the walī of those who believe, it is claiming for Him what the tribes of Arabia paid their patrons to provide: defence, management, and a place to belong.

The Qur'an warns the community about the opposite mistaken allegiance in 3:28 and 4:88–90, where the taking of disbelievers as awliyāʾ is discussed; the same root carries the concept of loyalty that a city of mixed alliances could not afford to keep vague. The sentence answers an ordinary question from the community's early years: whose protection does a believer actually live under?

**From Darkness into Light**

The second clause gives the guardian's work: He "brings them out of the darkness into the light." The plural is used because the Qur'an's idiom prefers it — the variety of errors is many, and the truth is one; the image recurs in 5:16, 14:1, 33:43, 57:9, and 65:11, where leaving the darkness is always God's act done through revelation. The light is named with the definite article because there is one.

Al-Ṭabarī reads light and darkness here as guidance and error. Ibn Kathīr adds the observation the image invites: darkness is not one thing but a condition in which direction is impossible, and light is similarly not a doctrine but the ability to see where one is going. The verse describes a change of condition rather than an instruction set.

**The Idols as Guardians**

The sentence then draws its negative pole: "As for those who disbelieve, their guardians are false gods (ṭāghūt) who lead them out of light and into darkness." The symmetry is deliberate and is one of the few places in the Book where the movement runs backwards in the same image. The things they trust cannot bear the weight, and the path bends downward.

The commentators note that the ṭāghūt here is plural in meaning even where the form is singular: every object of reliance that takes God's place — an idol, a chieftain, a market, a habit — can serve as the patron of a life. Al-Rāzī observes that the sentence denies the disbelievers even a false benefit: their guardians do not merely fail to help, they actively lead.

**The Residents of the Fire**

"The residents of the Fire; they will abide therein forever." The Qur'an states the permanence in the present tense, a device the grammarians call the certain future. The word khālidūn is used of the people of the Garden as well, and the verse's symmetry keeps the two destinies paired in the reader's mind, as v. 39 and v. 81 of the same sūrah do.

The sentence is not simply a threat appended to a pastoral image. In the passage's argument, the destination is the outcome of the direction: a life that has been conducted on the path away from the light arrives where that road ends.

**The Pastoral Image Underneath**

Both halves of the verse describe people being led. The Qur'an's anthropology is not that of the self-made man; the question it presses is who is managing the affair. In 2:286 the community is taught to say "You are our Master (mawlānā)" — the same vocabulary from the other side of the relationship. The verse in this place is the affirmation that the community has chosen its guardian deliberately.

**The Motif Through the Book**

The image of this verse is one of the Book's most persistent. Al-Nūr calls God the Light of the heavens and the earth (24:35); al-Māʾidah says the Book guides by it "those who follow His good pleasure to the ways of peace, bringing them out of darkness into light" (5:16); Ibrāhīm's mission is described with the same words (14:1), and the prayer of the angels for the believers in 33:43 and 57:9 repeats them. Al-Ṭabarī concludes from the consistency that the singular "light" of this verse is the guidance itself, and that the plural "darknesses" of the Book's idiom corresponds to the many ways of losing it.

The community's allegiances were the practical question underneath. Madinah's population included Jewish clans, pagan tribes, and the emigrants, and the Book addresses the matter in 3:28 (a warning against taking disbelievers as patrons over believers), 4:88–90 (the hesitation of the hypocrites in the affair), and 60:8–9, which distinguishes those who fight the community from those who do not. Al-Qurṭubī gathers these under walāya: the bond that carries protection must be given to God first, and the ordinary dealings of neighbourhood and trade remain open to everyone. The verse's contrast is therefore a statement about ultimate reliance, not a prohibition of civility, and the sūrah's later verses on marriage, oaths, and debt assume exactly that openness.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse is a statement about patronage, movement, and destination, in that order: God as walī, the movement from darkness to light, the counterpart movement of those who take false patrons, and the two ends. It supplies the creedal frame for everything the sūrah says about obedience, and the following verses introduce the disputant who thought his kingship made him a rival of God — the next portrait of a life being led by the wrong patron.
