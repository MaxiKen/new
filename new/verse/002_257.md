## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:257

> Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light. As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Walī**

The verse opens with a single relational noun carrying the weight of everything that follows: God is the walī of those who believe — guardian, protector, ally, friend, patron; the word holds all of them at once, and the tradition's glosses move across the range. A walī is the one who stands nearest with power to help: the guardian of an orphan, the protector of a client, the ally of a confederation, the friend whose friendship has consequences. Assigned as the believer's walī, the name gathers the sūrah's whole theology of care — the All-Sustaining of the preceding verse, the wakeful preservation that does not tire — into a personal relation: the God who keeps the cosmos keeps the believer, and keeps him as a walī keeps his charge, near, responsible, and effective. The symmetry the verse then builds is exact and inverted: as the believers have God, the disbelievers have their awliyāʾ too — the ṭāghūt just renounced in the handhold-verse — and the two guardianships produce two opposite migrations.

**Out of the Darknesses, Into the Light**

The movement ascribed to God's guardianship is an extraction: *He brings them out of darkness and into light*. The Arabic's asymmetry is the image's first teaching, and the commentators mark it: the darknesses are plural — ẓulumāt — while the light is singular, nūr. Error, in the Qur'an's idiom, is many; truth is one. The plural darknesses are the unbelief's varieties — the idols, the doubts, the appetites, the inherited errors — and each extraction is particular: this believer out of these darknesses. The singular light is the destination's unity: one faith, one direction, one God. The symbolism runs through the whole Qur'an, and this verse is its most explicit statement of movement — elsewhere the sūrah of the Table names the Book *a light by which Allah guides* (5:16), the sūrah of Light builds the symbol into its greatest verse — *Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth* (24:35) — and the extraction-formula recurs: out of the darknesses into the light at 14:1, 33:43, 57:9, 65:11. Al-Ṭabarī records the majority gloss plainly: light is faith and guidance, darkness is denial and error — and the bringing-out is the work of the walī, not the achievement of the brought.

**The Reverse Migration**

The verse's second sentence is its most disturbing symmetry: the disbelievers' guardians *lead them out of light and into darkness*. The migration is real and it runs backward — and its starting point is the verse's quiet claim about unbelief: it begins in light. The ṭāghūt-guardianship does not operate on people who never saw; it takes people who did — the clarity established in the preceding verse, the rushd distinguished from ghayy, the handhold within reach — and leads them out of it. The commentators read the reversal as the work of false patronage: whatever is followed in rebellion against God performs, for its followers, the inverse of God's walāyah — it has the shape of guardianship (devotion given, direction taken, trust extended) and the function of undoing. The plural returns in the destination as in the cause: out of the one light, into the darknesses — the single truth abandoned, the many errors entered, each idol and appetite its own chamber. The symmetry denies any neutral ground: every person is migrating, under one guardianship or the other, and the verse's two sentences are the two itineraries.

**Guardianship as Pedagogy**

The bringing-out and the leading-out share a structure the exegetes draw out: both are gradual, both are relational, and both are performed on the person rather than by him. Light and darkness in this idiom are not information but states — a person is in them, and getting out requires a hand. The believer's extraction is the history of revelation itself: the Book sent *that you may bring people out of the darknesses into the light, by their Lord's permission* (14:1) — the messenger's mission defined as the same bringing-out the verse ascribes to God, since the walī's work runs through the means the walī appoints. The disbelievers' leading-out has its ministers too: the callers to the ṭāghūt, the merchants of Syria in the occasion-narrations of the preceding verse, the inherited customs of Yathrib's vowed children. The verse's pastoral instruction is to audit one's guardianships — to ask of every attachment that shapes the heart's direction whether it is extracting toward the one light or conducting toward the many darknesses — because the migration is underway either way, and nobody travels unaccompanied.

**Residents of the Fire**

The closing sentence states the second itinerary's end: *It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.* The residence-language is the Qur'an's term of permanence — ahl, inhabitants, the Fire as a dwelling rather than an event — and its placement here completes the verse's symmetry one final time: the guardianship determines the migration, the migration determines the residence. The commentators connect the permanence to the guardianship's nature: the ṭāghūt followed in this life becomes, in the idiom of the reversal, the company kept in the next — the darknesses entered one by one close into the single dark of the Fire, and the extraction refused becomes the confinement chosen. The verse's own logic supplies the final contrast with the preceding āyah: the handhold-verse ended with the hearing and knowing God over every grasp; this verse ends with the residence over every letting-go. Between the two endings stands the whole of the sūrah's account of faith — a hold and a being-held, or a leading-out and a being-led, with no third road through the darknesses.

**The Verse in Its Sequence**

Positioned between the no-compulsion verse and the debate of Abraham, this āyah is the sequence's hinge: the handhold-verse declared faith uncoercible because clarity has been established; this verse explains what happens to the clarity under the two guardianships — received as extraction or reversed as seduction. The Abraham-narratives that follow will exhibit both migrations in person: the king who is led out of what he knew into dumbfoundment, the passers-by and the prophets brought out of question into certainty. And the verse supplies the frame the sūrah's coming legislation will assume: the spending-parables, the giving-without-injury, the charity by night and day — all of it is the extracted person's conduct, the behavior of someone whose walī has brought him into the light and who walks, in the light's idiom, as one being kept. The residents of the Fire are named once, at the verse's end, and the sūrah returns immediately to the living work of the illuminated: the debates, the resurrections, and the grain that multiplies.
