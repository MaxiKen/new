## Sūrah al-Taḥrīm 66:8

> O believers! Turn to Allah in sincere repentance, so your Lord may absolve you of your sins and admit you into Gardens, under which rivers flow, on the Day Allah will not disgrace the Prophet or the believers with him. Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right. They will say, “Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us. ˹For˺ You are truly Most Capable of everything.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repentance as Turning Rather Than Regretting**

The verb *tūbū* means to turn back, and the direction is specified: *“Turn to Allah”* (66:8). This is the second time the chapter has called for repentance — the two wives were offered it in the fourth verse, and now the whole community is. What the word describes is a change of orientation rather than a feeling. A person who is sorry has an emotion; a person who repents has changed the way he is facing.

The distinction is practical. Remorse is easy to generate, pleasant in its own way, and compatible with repeating the act next month; the Qur'an's term requires a reversal of course. This is also why the sūrah puts the call here, immediately after quoting what is said to those who arrive with excuses. The alternative to making an excuse later is turning now, and the chapter has just demonstrated that the first option will not be available.

**What Makes Repentance "Sincere"**

The qualifier *naṣūḥ* has been explained by the commentators in terms of durability. Sincere repentance is not a turning away from the sin that leaves the door open behind it, but one accompanied by genuine contrition and a settled resolve never to return to what was repented of. The word's root carries the sense of purity and of sincere counsel, so that the repentance is honest advice a person gives himself and acts on.

Placed in this chapter, the adjective is doing corrective work. The sūrah has been dealing with failures of exactly the kind that produce insincere repentance — a broken confidence followed by a denial, an alliance maintained after the correction, an oath sworn to settle a quarrel rather than to solve it. Against that background, the demand for *naṣūḥ* asks for a repentance that is not another item of household diplomacy. The verse then attaches *ʿasā*, it may be, to the outcome: *“so your Lord may absolve you”* (66:8). The word conveys hope, and hope from God is certainty; what it removes is any sense that repentance is a transaction which obliges Him.

**Absolution and the Company It Keeps**

*“absolve you of your sins and admit you into Gardens, under which rivers flow”* (66:8) joins the two promises the Qur'an habitually pairs. The covering of sins is linked in different verses to different things — to reverence, as in the previous sūrah's *“He will absolve them of their sins and reward them immensely”* (65:5) and in *“If you are mindful of Allah... absolve you of your sins, and forgive you”* (8:29); to faith and good work, as in *“As for those who believe and do good, We will certainly absolve them of their sins”* (29:7); and to avoiding the gravest wrongs, as in *“If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds”* (4:31).

The pattern across these verses is that absolution is never free-standing. It is always attached to something a person does, and the something varies with the context in which the promise is made. Here it is attached to a turning, which is the appropriate condition for a chapter about people whose hearts had tilted. What is being covered over, moreover, is not merely punishment but the record itself — the Arabic image is of concealment, a fault put out of sight. For a community that had just watched a private conversation in the Prophet's house become public revelation, a promise that God covers things over is not a small one.

**The Day the Prophet Is Not Disgraced**

The timing clause is unexpected: *“on the Day Allah will not disgrace the Prophet or the believers with him”* (66:8). A verse about personal repentance suddenly names the Prophet's honour as the marker of the occasion. The connection runs back through the chapter. Its opening verses recorded a correction, a broken confidence and a household in difficulty, and the enemies of the community had material there to work with. The sūrah closes that account by fixing the day on which nothing of the kind will be possible.

The believers are included in the same clause — the Prophet *and those who believe with him* — which links their standing to his. This is one of the Qur'an's recurring moves: the community's fate is described in the company of its Messenger rather than separately. For an audience that had just been warned about the Fire, about excuses, and about hearts leaning off true, the promise of a day without disgrace addresses a fear distinct from punishment. Many people fear exposure more than pain.

**Light That Runs Ahead and a Prayer to Complete It**

*“Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right”* (66:8) describes the believers moving with their own illumination. The fuller scene appears elsewhere: *“On that Day you will see believing men and women with their light shining ahead of them and on their right”* (57:12), and the verses following it show the hypocrites calling out to borrow some of that light and being told to go back and look for it where light is acquired (57:13). Light on that Day is not distributed at the gate; it is carried in from the life before.

That context explains the prayer. *“Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us”* (66:8) is understood by the commentators as spoken when the believers see the light of the hypocrites put out around them, and the sight makes them ask for their own to be completed rather than merely maintained. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī read the supplication differently and more hopefully: God does complete their light, and they go on calling to Him anyway, seeking nearness. Both readings fit the words, and both say something about the state described. People walking in their own light are still asking for more of it, and still asking for forgiveness — which is, for a chapter that began with an oath sworn to keep peace in a household, an unusually precise picture of what a settled soul looks like.
