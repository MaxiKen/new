## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:87

> Indeed, We gave Moses the Book and sent after him successive messengers. And We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. Why is it that every time a messenger comes to you ˹Israelites˺ with something you do not like, you become arrogant, rejecting some and killing others?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chain of Revelation**

The verse changes register. After the account of broken covenants and traded inheritances, the sūrah reminds its hearers of the provision that made all of that possible: God did not leave the community without guidance. He gave Moses the Book, and He sent messengers after him in succession, so that the history being described is a history of continuous divine attention rather than of abandonment.

The word rendered successive is literal in the Arabic traditions of reading: the prophets came one after another, replacing one another as the carriers of a single message. The Qur'an states the same idea when it describes the messengers as following in one another's footsteps, and the sūrah's audience is asked to see its own refusal against that background of repeated provision. The word translated as successive carries the sense of each messenger following in the footsteps of the one before, a picture the Book uses elsewhere for the unity of the message. Its presence here reminds the hearer that the refusal was made against a pattern rather than during an interval of silence.

**Moses and the Book**

The verse names Moses first because the community's own foundation rests on him. The Book given to him is described elsewhere in the sūrah as guidance and light for the people, and the Qur'an insists repeatedly that the revelation to Muhammad ﷺ confirms it rather than replaces or contradicts it.

That emphasis runs through the whole passage. The charge against this community is not that it lacked a messenger or a scripture. It is that it stood in the middle of an unbroken line of revelation, was reminded of it constantly, and still treated each new messenger as an inconvenience to be removed. The insistence on continuity explains why Moses is named first. He stands for the moment the community received a written law, and every later messenger was measured against that gift. The complaint in this passage is that the people who honoured the earlier revelation treated its continuation as an intrusion.

**Jesus, the Clear Proofs, and the Holy Spirit**

The verse then names Jesus, son of Mary, and attaches two things to him: clear proofs and the support of the holy spirit. The proofs in the Book's own account include healing, raising the dead, and the signs the people witnessed, and the holy spirit is explained by the commentators in several ways, the most widely reported being the angel Gabriel.

The Qur'an uses the phrase elsewhere in an account of the annunciation, where the spirit is sent to Mary in the form of a man, and it describes support given to Jesus by the spirit in several passages. The point the verse makes with the phrase is support given directly by God to a messenger, and readers have noted that the same expression is used of the Prophet's own support during the migration.

**Arrogance as the Root of the Charge**

The question in the verse identifies the motive before it identifies the offence. Every time a messenger brought something the community did not like, it grew arrogant, and the arrogance produced two results in sequence: rejecting some messengers and killing others. The Book's diagnosis is that the problem was never the evidence; it was the requirement to accept a message that cut against the community's preferences.

That analysis is why the passage has been building through covenants and breached undertakings. A refusal driven by appetite rather than by argument cannot be answered with more evidence, and the sūrah will say as much when it tells its readers that signs do not persuade people who have already decided. The verb for arrogance here is the same one the Book used earlier of a figure who refused a command out of pride, and it is a deliberately consistent vocabulary.

**Rejecting Some and Killing Others**

The two verbs are joined, and they form a descending scale. The community's refusal began with rejection — declining to accept a messenger, disputing his claim, waiting for him to fail. It progressed to violence, and the Book states elsewhere that this people killed a series of prophets whose only fault was to bring commands that were unwelcome.

The charge is repeated later in the sūrah when the community is asked why it killed the prophets in earlier times if it truly believes in what was sent down to it (2:91). The repetition is not redundancy. The Book is establishing that this conduct was a pattern rather than an incident, and the word *every time* in this verse makes that explicit. The pair of charges returns when the sūrah reports the answer this community gives to the Prophet ﷺ, which includes the same two offences against the messengers who came before. The Book presents the whole history as one continuous attitude rather than as a series of unrelated incidents.

**The Question That Closes the Verse**

The sentence ends with a question, and it is put as though the reader could answer it. Why does a messenger's arrival with unwelcome news produce violence rather than reflection? The question is not answered in the verse, because the Book's answer is spread across the whole passage: because a community had attached its identity to a set of claims that revelation kept challenging.

The Arabic construction of the question, addressed directly to the Israelites, carries the same force the sūrah has used with the same community elsewhere. It presumes that the hearer knows what drove the conduct and simply does not want to say it aloud, and the sūrah leaves the presumption standing. The question is put to people who could have answered it from their own history, and the silence it produces is the argument. The Book uses this form sparingly, only where the answer is already known, and here it appears twice within two verses, which marks how thoroughly the record is expected to speak for itself.

**A Warning Encoded for Later Readers**

The verse names a specific community, and the pattern it describes is not specific to them. Any community that receives revelation, builds an institution around it, and then finds a later messenger inconvenient faces the same choice between adjusting and refusing. That is why the sūrah's later addresses to the Muslims use the same verbs, and why the passage as a whole can be read as a warning about what a religious community becomes when its standing matters more to it than the message it holds. The sūrah does not leave the application implicit. Later in the same passage it addresses the Muslims in the second person about their own obligations, and the chapter's closing exhortations return to gratitude and steadfastness. What this community's history teaches is meant to be used by the community now receiving the revelation.

**Why a Message and Its Messenger Are Not the Same**

The verse identifies the error that makes a community's refusal possible: it separates the message from the messenger and treats the arrival of a prophet as an event to be managed. The proofs had been given, the confirming scripture was in their hands, and yet each new messenger was weighed by what he asked people to give up. The Book uses the same terms for the reaction to Muhammad ﷺ, and it describes this community's ancestors reacting in exactly that way to Jesus and to the prophets before him. What the sūrah wants its hearers to notice is that this pattern needs no new motive each time. One preference, held consistently, produces the same result whenever a messenger arrives, and the question in the verse is designed to make that repetition visible.

**An Unbroken Line of Provision**

The verse's opening clause is easy to pass over, and it carries the passage's argument. Revelation did not arrive once and then stop; messenger followed messenger, each confirming the one before, and no generation of this community was left without guidance. Against that record, the charge in the question at the end of the verse lands differently. A refusal by people who had never heard a prophet would be a failure of opportunity; a refusal by people standing in the middle of a continuous line of provision is a failure of response. The sūrah states the provision before it states the offence precisely so that its hearers weigh the two together, and the same order governs the sūrah's later addresses to the Muslims about the revelation they have received.
