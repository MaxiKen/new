## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:87

> Indeed, We gave Moses the Book and sent after him successive messengers. And We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. Why is it that every time a messenger comes to you ˹Israelites˺ with something you do not like, you become arrogant, rejecting some and killing others?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prosecution's Exhibit: A Chain Without Gaps**

The recital shifts from the community's conduct to its credentials, and the shift is prosecutorial. Before charging the pattern of rejection, the verse establishes that rejection had no excuse of deprivation: *We gave Moses the Book and sent after him successive messengers*. The Book is the Torah, the foundation; the successive messengers — the exegetes name David, Solomon, Elijah, John, and the rest of the prophetic line recorded in the Torah and Gospel — came after it, each preaching the Oneness of God within the law of Moses rather than founding a new religion (al-Rāzī's note), a continuous renewal of the same charter the covenant-verses quoted. The exhibit's force is in the word *successive*: revelation to this community was not a single ancient event but a standing supply, messenger after messenger across the centuries, each re-serving the covenant on the generation that inherited it. The structure answers the defense the verse anticipates — that the law grew distant, that the founding age was unrepeatable — by documenting the provision against distance: when the Book's generation passed, Allah sent its renewers. A community so provisioned cannot plead that faith was hard to maintain for lack of guidance, and the question the verse ends with is posed on that established ground: with all this given, what happened when the next messenger came?

**Clear Proofs and the Holy Spirit**

The chain's final named link receives the verse's fullest description: *We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit*. The clear proofs — *bayyināt*, the things that make plain, that demonstrate — are read by the exegetes as the Gospel itself (al-Ṭabarī), or the miracles: healing the sick, raising the dead, quickening the shape of a bird (3:49), the announcements from the unseen — or both together (al-Rāzī). The holy spirit's identification runs in three streams: usually the Archangel Gabriel (Ibn Kathīr); or the name by which Jesus quickened the dead; or the Gospel — a reading al-Rāzī notes sits awkwardly with Sūrah al-Māʾidah's juxtaposition of Gospel and spirit as distinct gifts (5:110), where Allah recounts strengthening Jesus *with the holy spirit* alongside the Book and the signs. What the readings share, al-Rāzī observes, is the sense of life-giving: bodily quickening or the nourishment of heart and intelligence — the spirit as the divine instrument by which dead things live. The term's reach runs back to the first creation: the spirit breathed into Adam when Allah proportioned him (15:29), the same gift the sūrah's own verse of the Throne will name among what Allah keeps as His own (2:255's *the spirit, of His command*). Jesus, in this verse, is thus documented with the maximum equipment a messenger carries — Book, proofs, spirit-support — and the documentation is deliberate: the coming question will ask what a community did with a prophet furnished like this, and the furnishing removes every plea of ambiguity.

**“With Something You Do Not Like”**

The verse's question names the trigger of the pattern, and the trigger is the verse's diagnosis of rejection's real mechanism: *every time a messenger comes to you with something you do not like*. Not something false, not something unproven, not something contradicted by their own Book — something disliked. The criterion the question exposes is appetite: the messenger's truth is measured not against revelation but against desire, and the measurement happens before examination, which is why the equipment listed in the verse's first half never gets its hearing. The exegetes connect the phrase to the substance of the dislike — the commands that fell against the community's wishes, the rulings that cost what appetite wanted kept — and the Qur'an's parallel filing in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn runs the same pattern across the prophetic ages. The dislike is not incidental to the rejection but generative of it: what begins as distaste becomes denial when the denial protects the distaste, and the verse's next words file the escalation.

**Arrogance, Rejection, Killing**

The escalation is three steps and the verse compresses them into one clause: *you become arrogant, rejecting some and killing others*. Arrogance — *istakbartum* — is the interior movement, the self enlarged against the messenger until his message cannot be received without the self shrinking; the sūrah filed the same word for Iblīs's refusal, and its appearance here puts the community's rejection in that genealogy: the disliked commandment meets not argument but selfhood. Rejection follows as the arrogance's verbal form — some messengers denied, called liars, their proofs explained away. And killing others — the escalation's end and the pattern's confession: the messenger whose truth cannot be refuted is removed, because a dead witness testifies no more. The three steps are not a list of separate crimes but one motion at three intensities, and its direction is fixed: appetite denied, then denounced, then murdered. The verse's question form — *why is it that every time?* — presses the pattern's regularity: this was not an incident but a policy across generations, every time, the machinery of verse 75's distortion and verse 85's rationing now shown at its extreme output. The community addressed had built its identity on the prophets it received; the recital files the identity's other half — the prophets it killed — and asks the two halves to meet.

**What the Passage Says Inwardly**

The Sufi commentators read the verse's address as doubled, and the doubling is its transfer to every later audience: what the passage says outwardly to the Jews it communicates inwardly to the Muslims (al-Kāshānī's note). God keeps sending the community its renewers — saints and sages who mark right from wrong and point the way — and these luminaries get called liars, opposed by accusers whose passions are in command. The inward reading does not soften the verse but extends its jurisdiction: the pattern of *something you do not like* runs wherever guidance meets appetite, and the killing of messengers has its reduced forms — the sage's honor assassinated, the reformer's name blackened, the unwelcome ruling buried by the committee that received it — each step the same arrogance at lower intensity. And the warning is prophetic in the literal sense: the Prophet ﷺ told his Companions, *"You will follow the ways of those nations who were before you, span by span and cubit by cubit... even if they entered a hole of a mastigure, you would follow them"* — and when they asked whether he meant the Jews and the Christians, he said, *"Whom else?"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7320). The long file of Israel's record exists so that the second community recognizes its own reflexes in it. The verse's question is therefore not museum-piece polemic but a standing interrogation: when the last piece of guidance you received cost you something you liked, what did you become — examiner, rejecter, or something worse?

**The Question That Closes the File**

The verse's grammar deserves its final note: the question is posed in the second person, to the community as a single person across its generations — *comes to you, you become arrogant* — the same present-tense address that folded the covenant's ratification into every descendant (84). The whole passage's architecture now stands complete on its evidence side: the charter quoted (83), the bond witnessed (84), the contradiction caught live (85), the trade named (86), the provision documented (87). Against a supply of guidance this continuous and a covenant this explicit, the pattern of rejection loses every explanation except the one the question supplies — the disliked thing, the enlarged self, the escalating hand. And the exhibit's last item, Jesus furnished with proofs and spirit, leaves the question standing at its maximum force: what does a community do with guidance it cannot claim to have lacked? The recital proceeds to quote the accused's own defense and weigh it — but the weighing can wait, because the question has already landed. The verse itself needs no verdict appended; its question is the verdict, and it remains audible to every generation that receives a messenger it does not like — which is to say, to every generation, whenever the Book says what the heart has already decided against.
