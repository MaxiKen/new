## Sūrah an-Naml 27:38

> Solomon asked, “O chiefs! Which of you can bring me her throne before they come to me in ˹full˺ submission?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Request With No Stated Reason**

Sulaymān turns to his own council and asks for something strange: the queen's throne, brought to him, before she arrives. He gives no explanation. The verse reports the request and moves directly to the volunteers.

Al-Ṭabarī records two proposals for the motive. One is that the throne was to be a reproof — a demonstration that what she took to be the seat of her power could be lifted out from under her. The other is that it would serve as a proof of prophethood, something she could see and recognise and be unable to explain. The second reading is better supported by what actually happens, since the throne is not used to shame her but is altered and shown to her as a test of recognition. Neither reading is in the text. What the text supplies is the timing — before they come — which tells us the object was wanted for the meeting rather than for its own sake.

**The Timing Clause Assumes an Outcome**

The most informative words in the verse are *qabla an ya'tūnī muslimīn* — before they come to me submitting. Sulaymān speaks as though the arrival is settled. No message has reached him announcing her journey; the last communication in either direction was his threat to the envoy.

Commentators split over what the participle means here, and the split matters. Al-Qurṭubī reports the reading that it means in obedience to Sulaymān, a political surrender. Al-Ṭabarī takes it as Muslims in the religious sense, submitted to the one God. The narrative's ending favours al-Ṭabarī: she arrives and declares faith in the Lord of all worlds, and no political settlement is ever mentioned. Reading the clause that way makes Sulaymān's confidence more remarkable rather than less. He is preparing a demonstration for a woman he expects to arrive as a believer, before she has left her capital, and the preparation is designed to help her get there.

**When Was This Said?**

A chronological question divides the commentators. Al-Qurṭubī reports the majority view that these words came after the gift was refused and the envoys sent back — the placement the sūrah's own order suggests. Others hold that the request was made much earlier, when the hoopoe first brought news of the queen and her throne, following directly on Sulaymān's statement that he would see whether the bird spoke truly.

The minority view has a real argument behind it: the hoopoe's report mentioned the throne specifically, and producing it would verify the report conclusively. But the verse's own timing clause works against that reading. The request is framed by an expected arrival, and no arrival was in prospect when the bird first spoke. The Qur'an's narrative order is not always chronological, and the commentators were right to raise the question, yet here the sequence on the page is also the sequence that makes the sentence intelligible. The majority position is the stronger one.

**A King Asks Instead of Commanding**

The form of the request repays attention. He does not order the throne brought; he asks which of his chiefs can do it. *Ayyukum ya'tīnī bi-ʿarshihā* — which of you will bring me her throne.

This is the same man who commands jinn and marshals armies in ranks, and he opens a demand for the impossible by inviting volunteers. The effect is to make the response a matter of capability rather than obedience, and the two answers that follow are both offers rather than compliance. Each volunteer states what he can do and how fast, which is information Sulaymān would not have obtained by issuing an order. There is a continuity here with his conduct throughout the chapter: he inspected rather than assumed, verified rather than accepted, and now asks rather than commands. A ruler who wants to know what his people are actually capable of has to leave them room to tell him.

**The Throne Was the Right Object to Choose**

Of everything in Sheba, Sulaymān asks for the throne. The choice is precise. The hoopoe had named three things about that kingdom — a woman ruling, full provision, and a magnificent throne — and the throne is the one that stands for the other two.

A throne in the ancient world was not furniture. It was the physical assertion of a right to rule, made deliberately large and costly so that it would settle questions of rank before a word was spoken, and hers had impressed a bird flying over a wealthy kingdom. Removing it accomplishes something no message could. It demonstrates, without violence and without a single casualty, that the foundation of her authority is not beyond reach — and it does so before she arrives, so that the demonstration is available to her as evidence rather than as a humiliation after the fact. Whatever Sulaymān intended by the request, the object selected was the one that carried the argument. Who answered him, and what they offered, comes next.

**The Same Council, a Different Question**

Sulaymān addresses *al-mala'*, his chiefs, using the identical term the Qur'an applied to Sheba's council four verses earlier. Two courts, the same word, and the chapter places their deliberations side by side within a few lines of each other.

The parallel invites comparison and the comparison is not simple. Her council was asked what should be done and answered with a capability report. His is asked who can do a specific thing and answers with two competing offers. Hers deferred the decision upward; his competes to execute one. Neither court is presented as a failure, and both function — which is worth saying, because the chapter could easily have made the idolaters' council incompetent and did not. What differs is the question put to them. She faced an open strategic choice; he faced a task. The chapter has been careful throughout to let its comparisons rest on what people actually do with the situations in front of them.
