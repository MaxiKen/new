## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:137

> Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Behind "Wonts"**

The verse tells the community that patterns of conduct have passed away before it and directs people to travel and consider how those who denied the truth ended. The word rendered here as wonts is the plural of a noun that covers an established practice, a way of doing things that has been set up and followed.

The Book uses the term for the practices of earlier peoples and for the way God has dealt with them, and the word therefore carries both halves of the lesson at once. There are habits that people establish generation after generation, and there is a pattern in how those habits end. The verse asks its listeners to look at both.

**The Other Peoples Mentioned in the Book**

The Book refers to the practices of previous communities in a wide range of passages, and it does so when it wants a general principle rather than a single episode. It also refers to the way God has acted toward deniers of earlier times as a settled pattern rather than as an isolated intervention.

The consistency of the language matters for the argument of this verse. The Book is claiming that history is not a random sequence of events in which the powerful win and the weak lose. It is claiming a regularity, and it invites its listeners to examine the record and test the claim for themselves.

**A Command to Journey and Consider**

The instruction to travel the earth is part of the Book's method of teaching through observation. The listener is not asked to accept a report about the past but to look at what the past has left behind and to reason from the evidence.

The end that the travelers are told to consider is the outcome of denial, and the Book returns to it in many passages. It notes that the deniers of earlier generations were often the wealthiest and most powerful of their time, and it draws the conclusion that their position did not preserve them. The ruins and the abandoned sites the first audience knew were the visible form of that conclusion.

**What "the End" Means**

The word used for the end of those peoples is not a simple marker of time. It carries the sense of a result, an outcome, and the effect an act has on the person who performs it.

The Book uses the term with that weight when it tells people to consider what happened to particular kinds of people: those who were warned, those who denied, those who worked corruption. The lesson is not merely that certain events came last in a sequence but that conduct produces a consequence, and that the consequence can be seen in the record. Reading history this way turns it into instruction, which is what the verse intends.

**The Peoples the First Audience Knew**

The commentators identified the peoples the earliest listeners would have known best, including ʿĀd and Thamūd, whose stories the Book relates at length. Their territories and remains were within reach or within memory of the Arabian audience.

Al-Ṭabarī drew the general lesson the verse supports: the wealth and power these peoples had accumulated did not stand between them and their fate. The Book tells the stories of these communities repeatedly because they are close enough in time and place to function as evidence rather than as distant legend, and this verse turns the familiarity into an argument.

**Hearing About the Past and Seeing It**

Some commentators observed that a person can acquire the knowledge this verse commands without making the journey, simply by learning what happened to those peoples, while adding that hearing is weaker than seeing. The Book's instruction still prefers the direct encounter when it is available.

The observation fits the Book's teaching elsewhere about the difference between hearing a matter and witnessing it. The command to travel therefore has a purpose beyond information: the traveler who stands among ruins where a prosperous people once lived receives the lesson in a form that argument alone rarely produces, and the sūrah's audience was surrounded by such sites.

**The Later Meaning of Sunnah**

The word in this verse is the same word that Muslims later used for the practice of the Prophet, the body of his sayings and actions transmitted in the reports, and the class of recommended conduct that follows his example in matters that are not obligatory.

That usage grew out of the meaning present in the verse: an established way that people follow. The Prophet's practice became the normative form of that idea for the community, and the word's history shows how the sūrah's vocabulary entered the everyday language of the religion. The verse itself speaks of many wonts, good and bad, and the tradition later invested the term with the highest of them all.

**A Report About Establishing a Practice**

A report from Jarīr ibn ʿAbdullāh records the Prophet's statement that whoever establishes a good practice in Islam has its reward and the reward of those who act on it after him, without anything being taken from their reward, and that whoever establishes an evil practice carries its burden and the burden of those who act on it after him (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017).

The report explains what is at stake in the word the verse uses. Practices outlive the people who begin them, so the account of a person who sets a pattern is not closed when his own life ends. The verse's command to consider the wonts of earlier peoples and the report's language about establishing practices belong to the same idea.

**The Verse's Place After the Battle**

Some commentators connected this verse with Uḥud, reading it as a statement that the enemies of the believers would meet a fate like that of the enemies of earlier messengers. The community that had just lost seventy of its men was being told to look at the wider pattern.

The consolation in the verse is not that the loss did not matter. It is that the community's story belongs to a sequence that has been running through history, in which denial eventually reaches its end. The instruction to travel and look gives that claim its evidence, and the verses that follow state the conclusion in the community's own situation: the revelation itself is an exposition, a guidance, and an exhortation.
