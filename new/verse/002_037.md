## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:37

> Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Words Were Given, Not Invented**

The Arabic verb in this sentence describes receiving something, and its subject is Adam. He did not compose a defence or negotiate a settlement; he was given words, and the Book presents the gift as the first step in his recovery. The initiative in this verse belongs to God from beginning to end: the words are supplied, and the acceptance of the repentance is stated as the consequence.

That order matters for what the verse teaches. A guilty person's most urgent need is not a strategy but a vocabulary, and the Qur'an presents repentance as something a human being learns rather than something he improvises. The same pattern recurs throughout the Book, where the servants of God speak in sentences supplied by revelation, and the sūrah's own text is the largest instance of it.

**What the Words Actually Were**

Commentators identified the received words in several ways. The most direct identification is the plea recorded in al-Aʿrāf: *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers.”* (7:23). Another report holds that God taught Adam and Eve the rites of pilgrimage, describing the pilgrimage itself as the words. A third transmits a longer prayer of confession and appeal, beginning with the declaration of God's unity and ending with a request for forgiveness.

Al-Rāzī listed the options and left the question open, which is the posture he takes on several details of this story. What the readings share is the content of the sentence: an admission placed on the speaker, an appeal addressed to God, and no attempt at a justification. Whether the exact wording is the plea of 7:23 or a longer formula, the verse in al-Baqarah tells the reader that a confession was given and that it was accepted.

**The Verb That Runs in Two Directions**

Arabic uses one root for the human act of turning back and the divine act of turning toward, and the two are distinguished only by the preposition attached to the verb. The noun *tawbah* therefore means a return, and the Book's usage presents repentance as a movement in two directions at once: the servant comes back to God, and God turns toward the servant.

The name that closes this verse belongs to the same root in its intensive form, and the Book applies it to God repeatedly, including in the promise of acceptance to anyone who turns. The vocabulary is worth noticing because it removes the idea of repentance as a transaction with a reluctant party. The Book's God is described as the one who responds to the turning, and the verse states the acceptance in the same sentence as the confession.

**The Two Names at the End**

The verse ends with a pair of names, and the pairing answers two needs the situation creates. The Accepter of Repentance addresses the question of whether the confession will be received; the Most Merciful addresses the question of what happens to the wrong that was committed. Both names are attached to an event that occurred in the very first generation of human history, before any law had been revealed and any community existed.

The sūrah will use these names again, and the Book uses them in contexts where human failure has been severe. The same mercy is offered to people who have gone far past the boundary: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The first instance of forgiveness in the Book establishes the pattern that the rest of the Book will follow.

**Repentance Established as a Standing Law**

Nothing in this verse suggests that Adam's case was an exception, and the Book never treats it that way. The sentence is narrated as the beginning of a permanent arrangement between human beings and their Maker, and the sūrah's own later passages apply the same remedy to the community addressed by the revelation. A mistake, an admission, and a return: this is the cycle the Book presents as normal rather than extraordinary.

The contrast with the previous scene is deliberate, though the Book leaves the reader to observe it. Iblīs refused a command he knew, defended himself with an argument about merit, and is never described as asking for pardon. Adam crossed a line, was given words, and used them. Two failures are narrated in sequence, and the difference between them lies entirely in what the offender did after the offence.

**A Fall That Ends in a Pardon Rather Than a Sentence**

The verse closes a unit within the sūrah. The appointment, the demonstration, the prohibition, the deception, the descent, and now the pardon: the sequence is coherent because it ends with the remedy in place before the human story on earth begins. Adam goes down to the earth forgiven, and the terms of his residence have already been set.

That is the reading the Book supports throughout, where the mercy of God is described as preceding and surrounding human weakness without excusing it. The verse does not tell us that Adam escaped consequences or that the offence was trivial. It tells us that the relationship was restored, and it names the two attributes by which the restoration was possible.

**Pardon Given Before the Earth Was Reached**

The forgiveness recorded in this verse takes place while the couple are still in the Garden, before the descent commanded in the following verse. Adam goes down with the offence already settled, and the sūrah's account gives the reader the full sequence in order: command, failure, confession, pardon, and only then the change of residence.

That sequence is the reason the Book does not carry a doctrine of inherited guilt. What descends to the earth is a forgiven man and a commission, not a debt owed by his children. Comparatively, the Book gives human beings the same remedy that Adam used, and it addresses later communities with the invitation to turn back on the same terms. The verse also supplies the vocabulary for it, since the two names at its end describe the acts of accepting a return and showing mercy, and both were demonstrated before the first law was given.
