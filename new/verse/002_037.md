## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:37

> Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Words Received, Not Composed**

The verse's pivot is a single verb: *fa-talaqqā Ādamu min rabbihi kalimāt* — Adam *received* words from his Lord. The reception is from a direction the reader must not miss: the words of repentance were authored on the divine side first and handed down, so that the sinner's first true act of return was itself a gift. A person cannot repent with his own resources, the verse teaches, because the fall has damaged precisely the resources repentance needs; so Allah supplies the sentence. The commentators identify the words variously, and the identifications form a small library of the tradition. The dominant reading takes them as the confession preserved in al-Aʿrāf: *"Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers"* (7:23). Others report Adam's plea in three questions — Did You not make me a vicegerent? Did You not breathe into me of Your spirit? Does Your compassion not outstrip Your wrath? — with the divine "yes" to each as the received words. Others again say the words were the rites of the pilgrimage taught to him, or a formula of tawḥīd and self-accusation recorded by al-Rāzī: *There is no god but You; glory be to You and praise; I have wronged myself, so forgive me*. The plurality is itself informative: the tradition agrees on the mechanism — repentance is taught before it is offered — while leaving the syllables open.

**One Verb, Two Directions**

Arabic compresses the whole theology of repentance into a single root. *Tāba* means to turn or return, and the same verb serves both parties in the transaction: the servant turns back to Allah, and Allah turns back to the servant. English needs two words — repent and relent, or repent and accept — because the actors differ; Arabic needs one verb and a preposition, because the motion is one. The verse displays both uses in sequence: Allah *turned* to Adam — *fa-tāba ʿalayhi*, He relented toward him, accepted his turning — and the intensive divine name *al-Tawwāb* at the verse's close names the One for whom this turning is a repeated, habitual act. The Biblical parallels the commentators note — passages where a kind of relenting is predicated of God — mark a shared Semitic intuition; the Qur'an's formulation is precise about direction: *ʿalā*, upon him — the divine turning descends onto the human one, covering it. Repentance, in this grammar, is a single movement with two ends, and the end that starts it is the one above.

**The Acceptance Runs Ahead of the Return**

Sequence the clause carefully: Adam received the words, *then* He turned to him. The acceptance is attached to the receiving, not to a completed program of amendment — no penance is interposed, no waiting period, no expiation enumerated. The verse's two closing names ratify the speed: *al-Tawwāb*, the Accepter of Repentance in the intensive form — the repeatedly-turning, the One whose nature is to accept — and *al-Raḥīm*, the Most Merciful, the particular mercy of al-Fātiḥah's third verse now at work at the lowest point of human history. The pairing will recur through this very sūrah at every hinge where a failing people is offered a way back, and its first appearance in the Qur'an's narrative of humanity fixes the register: the divine response to confessed failure is not reluctant tolerance but named, titled mercy. The verse is, in effect, the charter of verse 36's leasehold — the earth is survivable because the Accepter of Repentance is in office.

**Adam Confuted Mūsā: The Verified Reading of the Fall**

The ḥadīth corpus supplies the scene's most famous epilogue, verified in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī: Adam and Mūsā argued before their Lord. Mūsā opened the case — *O Adam, you are our father; you disappointed us and turned us out of Paradise.* Adam answered with a question about Mūsā's own honors: Allah favored you with His direct speech and wrote the Torah for you with His own hand — *do you blame me for a matter Allah had decreed for me forty years before He created me?* And the Prophet ﷺ concluded, three times over: *so Adam confuted Mūsā* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6614). The tradition's logic requires care, because it is often misused. Adam's appeal to decree is not a sinner's excuse offered before repentance — that appeal the Qur'an forbids — but a forgiven man's statement about a closed case: the matter had passed through words, through acceptance, through the turning of verse 37, and after pardon the only true description left is the decree. The ḥadīth thus reads verse 37 forward: once repentance is accepted, the fall belongs to Allah's prior knowledge and to mercy's record, and blame is no longer the correct grammar.

**Two Sinners, Two Grammars**

Set beside verse 34, this verse completes a diptych the sūrah has been building. Two beings sinned in the same narrative week. Iblīs sinned from self-magnification and answered the question with a comparison — *I am better than he is* — wrapping the refusal in an argument. Adam sinned from deception and appetite and answered with received words — *we have wronged ourselves*. The divergence is total: one sin explains itself, the other confesses itself; one produces a syllogism, the other a prayer. The Qur'an thereby defines the two available postures after any failure. The way of Iblīs is the way of justification — the sin re-described as a position, the position defended by rank, the rank computed by origin. The way of Adam is the way of reception — nothing to argue, only words to take and a turning to make. Every reader stands between the two verses with the choice between the two grammars, and the sūrah's long history of Israel that begins twenty verses later is, in one reading, the record of a community oscillating between them.

**The Door the Sūrah Will Keep Reopening**

*Al-Tawwāb* makes its first appearance here, and it does not make its last: the name returns in this sūrah at the legislation of the calf's aftermath (2:54), in Abraham's prayer for the city (2:128), over the pardon of the three who stayed behind in a later campaign (9:118 in the wider Book), and wherever a breach is followed by a way back. Its placement at the head of human history is architectural — repentance is installed as constitutional before a single law is given, so that the law will arrive to creatures already provisioned with its remedy. The verse also answers, retroactively, the angels' fear of verse 30. The creature who sheds blood and corrupts is real; but so is the creature who receives words and turns, and the second description is the one Allah chose to seal with two of His names. The fall narrative closes here — forgiven, installed, briefed — and the next verse re-reads the descent-decree to the whole species as a covenant with terms.
