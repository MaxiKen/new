## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:37

> Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Words Given After Words Broken**

“Then Adam was inspired with words of prayer by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance” (2:37). Fa-talaqqā Ādam min Rabbihi kalimāt — Adam received words from his Lord. The verb talaqqā suggests reception as gift: what was broken by a slip is repaired by taught speech. The previous scene ended with descent and enmity (2:36); this one opens a return before the return is deepened. The first human error is followed by the first divine teaching of tawbah.

The sequence matters. The khalīfah taught names in heaven (2:31) now receives words on earth. Naming ordered creation; these words order the relation after failure. Both are taught: knowledge and remorse are alike from above.

**What Were the Words? — Held Lightly**

The source lists several views for kalimāt without ranking one as binding:

- The Qur’anic supplication “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we shall be among the losers” (7:23), understood as the shared prayer of Adam and his wife.
- A dialogic litany reported in tafsīr where Adam asks, “Did You not make me vicegerent? Did You not breathe Your spirit into me? Does Your mercy not outstrip Your wrath?” and the Lord answers “Yes” to each — the exchange itself being the words received.
- Words for the rites of pilgrimage, or a concise formula — “There is no god but You, glory be to You, praise be to You; You know evil, I have wronged myself; forgive me, best of forgivers” — with variants exchanging mercy and repentance for forgiveness.

These reports differ in detail and should be rated UNVERIFIED in exact wording unless traced to a Sahih chain; their shared core is that the words were tawbah — a turning phrased as confession, praise, and plea. The Qur’an deliberately leaves the phrasing open here, keeping the emphasis on the Giver rather than the syllables: Adam received words *from his Lord*.

**Fa-tāba ʿAlayhi — He Turned Toward Him**

“so He accepted his repentance” — fa-tāba ʿalayhi. The Arabic root tāba means to turn. When the servant turns, it is repentance; when the Lord turns, it is relenting — a return of mercy to the returner. The same root gathers both sides. Al-Tawwāb, the intensive form, is rendered here as “Accepter of Repentance,” underscoring repetition: the One who accepts again and again, not once. The title recurs in contexts that stress repeated return (4:17–18; 4:147).

The comparison noted in the source to Biblical idiom — where God is said to “repent” or relent (Jonah 3:9; Jeremiah 15:6; Genesis 6:6) — helps hear the root’s breadth: to turn is not to change essence but to turn in relation — servant toward Lord, Lord toward servant with mercy after mercy.

**“Surely He Is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful” — Two Names That Seal**

The verse seals with al-Tawwāb al-Raḥīm — the Accepter of Repentance, the Most Merciful (2:37). The pairing teaches how return succeeds: tawbah is met not with mere remission but with raḥmah. Mercy is not only pardon of fault but restoration of nearness. Adam is not left forgiven yet distant; he is forgiven and still commissioned. The later descent of guidance promised in the next verse (2:38) grows from this mercy: a forgiven humanity is not abandoned on earth but instructed upon it.

**Why Tawbah Comes Before Guidance Descends**

The order — slip (2:36), then words and relenting (2:37), then “descend all of you… guidance will come” (2:38) — shows that earthly life begins under mercy, not under lingering curse. Adam lands not as condemned archetype but as taught penitent. The descent remains consequence, yet the first earthly act credited to him is not labor or lawgiving but receiving and being returned to. The sūrah thus frames all subsequent law as mercy after mercy, not as probation after rejection.

**Not Inheritable Guilt, but Teachable Return**

The source’s reminder that Islam does not affirm original sin finds its proof here. Guilt is not bequeathed. What is bequeathed is teachability: a way back phrased in words, and a Lord named as ever-returning. Every child of Adam inherits the capacity to be taught a sentence that his Lord accepts. The fall does not make children guilty; the words make them redeemable.

**Living the Verse Today**

- Seek words when willpower fails. The verse models dependence on given speech. When remorse has no phrasing, ask for phrasing: the Qur’anic “we have wronged ourselves” (7:23) is itself the requested gift.
- Keep the two names together. To seek tawbah without raḥmah expects forgiveness yet fears nearness; to expect raḥmah without tawbah expects nearness without turning. The verse joins them.
- Teach the words. If Adam was taught them, then households and circles that teach a sincere formula of turning continue the same grace: they distribute what was first distributed to Adam.

This verse does not name a hidden sacrament or locate tawbah in a place. Reports that the words were specifically the manāsik of ḥajj or a long queued catechism are tafsīr expansions to be held as UNVERIFIED when lacking authenticated prophetic wording. What is firm is the gift: words from the Lord, a turning accepted, and a sealing with the names ever-accepting and ever-merciful (2:37).
