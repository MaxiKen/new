## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:18

> Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge. He is the Maintainer of justice. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Testimony, Stated in Court Form**

The verse is built like a legal document. It names a testifier, gives the content of the testimony, and then adds two further witnesses: the angels and the people of knowledge. What is being testified to is not an opinion about God but a fact about Him — that there is no deity except Him — and the fact is presented as something that can be established by witnesses.

That rhetorical shape is itself an argument. In ordinary religious language, the unity of God is a doctrine to be believed. Here it is a matter on which testimony has been given, by God first and by two classes of created witnesses after Him. The reader is not being asked to accept an assertion; he is being shown a sworn statement and told who signed it.

**The Three Witnesses and the Order Between Them**

God's witness comes first, and it needs no corroboration: He testifies about Himself, which the Book elsewhere describes as a sufficiency — *“And Allah ˹alone˺ is sufficient as a Witness.”* (4:166). The angels come next because they are the created beings whose knowledge of God is unclouded by appetite or interest. The people of knowledge come last, and their inclusion is the verse's real surprise.

A human being qualifies as a witness to the unity of God not by lineage, authority, or office, but by knowledge. That single word opens the testimony to anyone who studies the evidence in the world and in revelation and reaches the conclusion the verse states, and it also sets the standard against which a scholar's claim is measured. The `ulamāʾ` of this verse are not the custodians of a privilege; they are the ones whose learning leads them to bear witness.

**"Maintainer of Justice": the Phrase in the Middle**

Between the two halves of the testimony sits a sentence about God's own standing: *He is the Maintainer of justice*. The participle *qāʾim* conveys standing, upholding, and keeping something in place, and *qisṭ* is equity — the giving to each what is due. God is not described as merely just in His rulings. He is described as the one who keeps justice standing in existence.

This is why the verse can move directly from a doctrine about God to a fact about the world. Justice in the Book is not a matter of chance good behaviour among people; it is a structure that God maintains, which is why injustice in the human record is always temporary and always accounted for. The attribute also explains why the two names at the end of the verse are power and wisdom: justice requires both the capacity to enforce it and the knowledge of what is actually due.

**Testifying to God and Testifying in Court**

The Book repeatedly takes the vocabulary of this verse into the public sphere. Believers are commanded to become witnesses in the same sense: *“O believers! Stand firm for Allah and bear true testimony. Do not let the hatred of a people lead you to injustice.”* (5:8), and the command is sharpened to include the hardest cases: *“Stand firm for justice as witnesses for Allah even if it is against yourselves, your parents, or close relatives.”* (4:135).

The connection is not decorative. A person who has understood that God's unity is a witnessed fact has accepted a world in which facts are established by testimony and not by power, and the habits of that acceptance travel with him into the courtroom and the market. The Qur'an treats perjury as a religious crime rather than a procedural one, which is consistent with a Book that argues from the reliability of witnesses.

**The Sentence That Opens and Closes the Verse**

The declaration *there is no god except Him* appears twice in this verse, once as the content of the testimony and once attached to the names Almighty and All-Wise. The repetition is a frame, and it mirrors the structure of the sūrah itself: the same words opened it at 3:2, and they will return at intervals as the argument develops.

A frame does work that a single statement cannot. It tells the reader that everything between the two declarations belongs inside them, that the intervening material about books, messengers, desires, and arguments is a set of consequences of the fact stated first and restated last. In a sūrah that will address communities with competing scriptures, that frame is a claim about jurisdiction: no dispute arising in this sūrah stands outside the testimony of verse 18.

**What a Person Does With a Fact Like This**

A testimony produces obligation in a way that a preference does not. A person may argue about taste, but a witness who has given evidence on a matter of fact can only be believed or contradicted. The verse leaves no third option, and the angels and the learned are named precisely so that the reader cannot claim he stands alone with an opinion.

The response the Book expects is not enthusiasm but uprightness. If there is one God, then the question of how a person's life is ordered is no longer a matter of private inclination, and the testimony becomes the standard against which conduct is judged.

The three witnesses also settle a question about certainty that readers of this sūrah are likely to be carrying. The verse does not present the unity of God as a doctrine that reasonable people may or may not reach; it presents it as a fact that has been attested by God, by the angels who know Him directly, and by the learned who have examined what He made. A reader who doubts is not being asked to trust a rumour from a distant age. He is being placed in front of a record of testimony and asked what he intends to do with it. The next verse names what accepting that testimony is called, and how the communities that had received earlier books handled the same question.
