## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:51

> If only you could see when they will be horrified with no escape ˹on Judgment Day˺! And they will be seized from a nearby place.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Left Deliberately Unfinished**

*Wa law tarā idh fazīʿū* — and if you could see when they are terrified. As in the earlier verse that opened the same way (34:31), the conditional has no stated consequence. Arabic allows the apodosis to be omitted when the speaker judges that any completion would diminish the effect, and the Qur'an uses the device precisely at moments it wants the hearer to supply. You would see something, and what you would then feel is not named. The technique is more effective here than in its first appearance because the sūrah is now in its closing movement and the reader has been given all the materials. Note also the tense: *fazīʿū* is a perfect verb describing a future event, which is the Qur'an's habitual way of reporting the Day as something already accomplished rather than pending.

**Terror Without Exit**

*Fa-lā fawta* — and there is no escaping. The noun *fawt* means the slipping away of something, a missing or a passing beyond reach; the same root gives the verb used when an opportunity is lost. So the phrase does not merely say they will not escape. It says the category of escape does not apply — nothing can slip past, nothing gets away. The sūrah has been building toward this for fifty verses by closing exits one at a time: no hiding from the record that catches an atom's weight (34:3), no intercessor with standing (34:23), no protection in wealth (34:37), no help from one party to another (34:42). This verse states the cumulative result in three words. Terror in ordinary life is bearable partly because the mind is calculating a way out even while afraid. This is terror with the calculating removed.

**Seized From a Nearby Place**

*Wa ukhidhū min makānin qarīb* — and they are taken from a close place. The phrase is short and has attracted a range of readings, all of which sharpen the same point. Some take *a close place* as the grave, since the dead are seized directly from where they lie the moment they are raised, with no interval and no journey. Others read it as the earth itself, close beneath them. Others hear it as an idiom for immediacy: taken from right where they are, without pursuit, without needing to be found. The readings converge because they all describe the collapse of distance. The men in question had spent their lives operating at a comfortable remove from the thing they denied, and the seizure happens at arm's length.

**The Word "Near" Used Against Them**

There is a deliberate cruelty in the vocabulary that a reader of the Arabic cannot miss. The previous verse ended by naming God *Qarīb*, Ever Near, as the reassurance underwriting the Prophet's dependence on revelation (34:50). This verse uses the same root for the place from which the deniers are seized: *qarīb*, near. One word, two applications. Nearness is the ground of hope for the one who calls and the ground of terror for the one who does not, and the sūrah sets the two uses a single verse apart so that the contrast cannot be missed. The same proximity that makes a prayer answerable makes an arrest instantaneous. Nothing in the divine attribute has changed between the two verses; only the relationship of the person to it.

**Why Terror Rather Than Punishment**

The verse describes a psychological state and not a physical one. No fire is mentioned, no shackle, no sentence — only fright, the absence of escape, and the seizure. This is characteristic of how the Qur'an handles the opening moment of the Day, and it is more effective than a description of torment would be. Punishment can be imagined at a distance and discounted; the sensation of having no exit cannot be discounted by anyone who has ever felt a version of it. The sūrah has also earned this moment by recording, at length, the confidence these people expressed while the question was still open: the Hour will never come to us (34:3), we will never be punished (34:35). Terror is the exact reversal of a settled certainty, which is why it is the emotion named.

**Who Is Being Addressed by "If Only You Could See"**

The verb *tarā* is singular, so the address is to one person — the Prophet — rather than to the assembly. This detail changes the verse's function. It is not a warning shouted at the deniers about what awaits them; it is a scene shown privately to a man who has spent the chapter being mocked, accused of fabrication and called insane. What he is offered is not revenge but information: the argument he has been losing in public has an ending, and the ending is not in doubt. The Qur'an does this for him repeatedly in the Meccan period, and the sūrah has done it once already with the same construction at the quarrel of leaders and followers (34:31). Both scenes are given to the Prophet rather than to his opponents, which is worth noticing. Those who would most benefit from seeing the Day are precisely the ones who cannot be shown it, because being shown it would end the test the sūrah described in its twenty-first verse.

**The Structure of the Chapter's Closing Scene**

This verse opens the last movement of Sabaʾ, four verses that run without a break to the end of the chapter. The arrangement is chronological: seizure here, the belated declaration of faith next, the reason it cannot be accepted after that, and the barrier that closes the account. What the sequence does is answer, one final time, the question the opponents put in verse 29 — when will this promise come to pass? They wanted a date and were told only that the appointment was fixed and unmovable (34:30). Now the chapter describes the appointment being kept, in the present tense, with the audience still holding the delay they thought was safety. The next verse gives them their first and last words at the hearing.
