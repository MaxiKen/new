## Sūrah al-Zukhruf 43:1

> **Ḥâ-Mĩm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Letters That Are Not a Sentence**

The sūrah opens with two names of Arabic letters, ḥāʾ and mīm, spoken as letters and not as a word. Nothing is predicated of them. They do not form a verb, a noun, or a phrase with a dictionary meaning, and the reciter pauses on them as one pauses on a full sentence. This alone tells a listener that the speech about to follow is not ordinary speech. Twenty-nine sūrahs open with such letters, and in almost every case the very next clause speaks of the Book, its clarity, or its descent. Al-Zukhruf follows the pattern exactly: the letters come first, and the oath by the clear Book comes second. The sequence is the argument. The same sounds that every Arab child learns to name are the raw material of a text that no Arab poet could match. Before a single claim is made about God, prophets, or the Hereafter, the Qurʾān places its own building blocks in front of the audience and lets them feel the distance between the alphabet they know and the composition they are about to hear.

**Fourth of the Seven Ḥawāmīm**

This is the fourth of seven consecutive sūrahs that begin with these two letters: Ghāfir (40), Fuṣṣilat (41), al-Shūrā (42), al-Zukhruf (43), al-Dukhān (44), al-Jāthiyah (45), and al-Aḥqāf (46). The tradition calls them the ḥawāmīm, the plural of ḥā-mīm, and treats them as a family. All seven are Makkan, all seven are concerned with the authority of revelation against the objections of the Quraysh, and all seven turn the letters into a doorway to a statement about the Book. Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have called them the ornament of the Qurʾān, a phrase that fits the present sūrah in particular, since it takes its name from gold ornament (zukhruf) in verse 35. Reading the seven as a set changes how the opening is heard. A listener in Makkah who had already heard Ghāfir, Fuṣṣilat, and al-Shūrā would recognise the two letters as a signal: another chapter in the same case, argued against the same audience, on the same central question of whether this recitation is from God.

**What the Letters Mean: Three Positions and Their Grounds**

The exegetes divide over the meaning of these letters into three broad camps, and each camp has a reason. The first, associated with Abū Bakr, ʿAlī, and later al-Shaʿbī and Sufyān al-Thawrī, holds that the letters are a secret of God, a part of the mutashābih whose meaning He kept to Himself. Their ground is silence: the Prophet ﷺ never explained them, and the Companions who heard them did not ask, which suggests they were not meant to be decoded. The second camp, represented by reports from Ibn ʿAbbās and developed by al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī, treats the letters as a challenge. The Qurʾān is made of the same letters the Arabs use every day; produce something like it if you can. Their ground is the position of the letters, which almost always precede a mention of the Book. The third camp reads them as abbreviations, for example ḥā-mīm as a shortening of al-Raḥmān, or of a divine name beginning with each letter. Its ground is a handful of early reports, but the reports conflict with each other and no single expansion commands agreement. The second position is the strongest, because it is the only one that explains the consistent placement of the letters before a statement about revelation, and because it does not require knowledge the text withholds.

**Sound Before Sense**

There is a further reason the letters work as a challenge rather than as a code. Arabic has twenty-eight letters, and the letters that open the sūrahs, when duplicates are removed, number fourteen: exactly half the alphabet. They include representatives of every phonetic class the grammarians recognised: the whispered and the voiced, the emphatic and the soft, the letters of the throat and the letters of the lips. Ḥāʾ is a letter of the deep throat; mīm is a letter of the closed lips. The pair spans the whole length of the vocal tract. Whether or not that was noticed in Makkah, the effect on a listener is physical. The reciter draws out the long vowels, and the audience hears the medium of the message laid bare before the message itself arrives. This is why the letters are recited as part of the text and counted as a verse here, and why they are not a preface that can be skipped. The Qurʾān's claim is not only that its ideas are true but that its speech is inimitable, and the letters are the first evidence offered.

**A Verse in Its Own Right**

The Kūfan school of verse-counting, which the standard Egyptian text follows, counts Ḥā-Mīm as a complete verse in all seven ḥawāmīm. The Baṣran and Madinan schools do not count it separately but join it to what follows. The disagreement is technical, but it has a consequence for how the sūrah is read aloud. Counting the letters as their own verse forces a stop after them, and the stop creates the effect described above: the alphabet stands alone for a moment. Joining them to the oath makes the letters a kind of vocative that leads straight into the swearing. Both readings are ancient and both are permitted. The Kūfan count also produces the total of eighty-nine verses for this sūrah, which is the number used throughout this commentary. What the two counting traditions share is more important than what divides them. Neither treats the letters as decoration. They are part of the revealed text, recited in prayer, protected by the same rules of pronunciation as any other verse, and they carry the reader directly into the oath that follows.
