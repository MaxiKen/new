## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:259

> Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins. He wondered, "How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?" So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life. Allah asked, "How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?" He replied, "Perhaps a day or part of a day." Allah said, "No! You have remained here for a hundred years! Just look at your food and drink— they have not spoiled. ˹But now˺ look at ˹the remains of˺ your donkey! And ˹so˺ We have made you into a sign for humanity. And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!" When this was made clear to him, he declared, "˹Now˺ I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything."

**Expanded Commentary**

**The City on Its Roofs**

The second scene of the life-and-death sequence shifts from a debate to a solitary ride: a passer-by comes on a city *in ruins* — the Arabic picture is precise, the roofs collapsed and the walls fallen in on top of them, and the word khāwiyah carrying, the commentators note, the double sense of fallen and emptied. The ruin is total in the way ruins of great places are: not a village abandoned but a civilization folded in on itself. The passer-by's question is the verse's hinge, and its tone has been debated from the first: *How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?* Read as doubt, it is the hardest question faith faces — not whether God can raise the dead in general but whether God will restore what history has actually destroyed, the particular city, the particular loss. Read as wonder, it is the mind standing at the edge of the conceivable. The verse answers the question the only way the Qur'an ever answers such questions — not with a proposition but with an experience: the questioner is made into the evidence.

**Who Passed By**

The tradition offers identities and the commentary records them all: Ezra, most commonly — the ʿUzayr of the narrations, connected to Jerusalem's destruction and restoration; Ezekiel, whose biblical vision of the valley of dry bones supplies a parallel the exegetes themselves note; even Khiḍr, the unnamed servant of the cave-sūrah. Al-Rāzī preserves the fourth reading that dissolves the question: not a prophet or saint at all but a disbeliever, shown the resurrection's reality directly. The Qur'anic text's own reticence — *the one who passed by* — is why the translators' notes and the classical renderings hedge the pronouns: the story's identity-interest is minimal and its demonstration-interest total. Whoever asked the question, the answer was the same hundred years of death, and whoever he was, he came back with the confession the verse ends on. The anonymity functions like the king's in the preceding verse: the debate's antagonist is unnamed because his title is the point, and the rider is unnamed because his question is the point — every reader who has looked at a ruin and wondered how God could restore it has, in the verse's grammar, already passed by the city.

**A Day or Part of a Day**

The resurrection's first datum is the sleeper's own estimate: *How long have you remained?* — *Perhaps a day or part of a day.* The question-and-answer pair is the Qur'an's standing instrument for measuring the dead's experience of time, and it appears at the two other great awakenings: the sleepers of the cave, roused, ask each other *How long have you remained asleep?* and answer themselves *perhaps a day, or part of a day* (18:19); and the resurrected soul, questioned on the Day — *How many years did you remain on earth?* — will answer with the same collapsed arithmetic (23:112). The three passages state one doctrine of death's duration: from inside, the interval is nothing — a day, a part of a day — however the outside counts it. The correction that follows is therefore not about the man's honesty but about the two clocks: *No! You have remained here for a hundred years!* The century has passed over him and not through him; his estimate is what a hundred years of death feels like, and the verse preserves both numbers because both are true — the calendar's and the sleeper's.

**The Food and the Donkey**

Two proofs are staged for the awakened man, and their contrast is the scene's construction. First the untouched: *look at your food and drink — they have not spoiled*. The hundred years have passed beside his provisions without touching them — the commentators note the food's freshness as the miracle's quiet half, time arrested around the sleeper's own belongings. Then the dismantled: *look at your donkey* — and the donkey is bones. The verse's longest imperative unfolds the reconstruction as a spectacle: *look at the bones, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh* — the assembly watched bone by bone, the clothing watched layer by layer, the rider watching his mount reassemble. The exegetes debate whether the bones-clothing clause describes the donkey's revival or the man's own reconstitution, al-Rāzī recording both; on either reading the demonstration is the same — resurrection shown not as announcement but as procedure, the steps visible, the agent named. The man who asked how God could restore a collapsed city is given a collapsed body to watch rebuilt, at a scale where every movement of the rebuilding can be seen.

**A Sign for Humanity**

The scene's purpose-clause is attached to the spectacle: *We have made you into a sign for humanity*. The commentators test the clause against an obvious inference — that being made a sign means being made a prophet — and refuse it with a counterexample from the Qur'an itself: Pharaoh too was made a sign (10:92), the drowning king preserved as a lesson, and he is the opposite of a prophet. The sign-status is about the person's evidentiary function, not his rank: this man's century of death and his minute of waking are now public property, a datum the community can carry. And the tradition softens the question behind his original wonder: if the rider is a prophet, his how-could-Allah is not pernicious doubt but the same spirit as Abraham's request in the verse that follows — *show me how You give life to the dead* — the question of one who believes asking to see. On that reading the two scenes are one movement: the passing rider and the patriarch both ask the how of resurrection, and both are answered with demonstrations — one with a century of his own death, the other with four birds and a call.

**I Know That Allah Is Able**

The confession that closes the verse is the scene's whole yield: *Now I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything*. The commentators mark the shift precisely — the verse's first sentence held a question about possibility (*how could Allah*), and the last holds knowledge of power (*I know that Allah has power over all things*). The distance between them was not an argument but a hundred years, a jar of unsoured drink, and a donkey reassembled under its owner's eyes. The knowledge named is not abstract omnipotence but omnipotence verified in the particular case the questioner had doubted: this city, this body, this ruin — the restoration whose how was unimaginable has been performed on the questioner himself. The verse's instruction, read with its neighbor, completes the pair the sūrah set out to teach: the king argued about life and death and was confounded by the sun; the rider wondered about life and death and was answered by his own bones. The first would not be guided — wrongdoing closes the reception; the second asked and was shown — and his showing ends where every demonstration in the Qur'an ends, with the knower naming what he now knows: God is, over everything, able.
