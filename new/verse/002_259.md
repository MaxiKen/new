## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:259

> Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins. He wondered, “How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?” So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life. Allah asked, “How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?” He replied, “Perhaps a day or part of a day.” Allah said, “No! You have remained here for a hundred years! Just look at your food and drink— they have not spoiled. ˹But now˺ look at ˹the remains of˺ your donkey! And ˹so˺ We have made you into a sign for humanity. And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!” When this was made clear to him, he declared, “˹Now˺ I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Asked**

The sentence opens with another account and describes a man who passed by a city whose buildings had fallen upon one another, and the community's teachers read the scene as the image of a settlement from which every sign of life had been removed. The ruins prompt the question that the sentence records, whether God can restore a place that has been destroyed.

The Book places the account after the dispute of the ruler with Abraham, so that the two passages treat the same subject from two directions. One argument concerned the power to give life, and the account here supplies the demonstration of that power in a form that the observer can see.

The wording describes the city as fallen upon its roofs, and the commentators explained the phrase as the statement that the walls had collapsed onto the structures beneath them. The scene is complete before the question is asked, so that the doubt and the answer stand within a single passage.

**The Death and the Raising**

The sentence states that God caused the man to die for a hundred years and then raised him, and the community's scholars read the account as the answer to the question in the form of an event rather than an argument. The man who asked how the dead could be restored was himself brought through death and back to life.

The teachers noted that the question was not treated as an offence. The commentators compared the passage with the request of Abraham in the following verse, and they concluded that a question asked in order to understand is answered with the help that the person requires.

The period of a hundred years also served the design of the account, since the city and its people had passed away while the questioner remained within the death that he had asked about. His return to life therefore answered the question about the city as well as the question about himself.

**The Food and the Drink**

The sentence records the instruction to look at the food and the drink, and the statement that they had not spoiled. The commentators noted the detail, since the provisions that the man had with him were preserved through the century in which his body lay without life.

The teachers read the clause as the evidence placed before the man's own eyes. The resurrection that he had questioned was not presented to him in the abstract but in the objects that he could examine, so that the demonstration rested on what he found beside him.

The community's scholars also connected the detail with the account's larger purpose, since the preservation of the provisions is a lesser act of the same power that restores the body. The sentence orders the man to look, and the look supplies the knowledge that the question had sought.

**The Donkey and the Bones**

The sentence then directs the man to look at his donkey and describes the bones that God set up and clothed with flesh. The commentators discussed the reading of the clause, and some held that the reference is to the animal's body while others read the phrase as the description of something else in the scene.

The community's teachers preserved the positions they received and noted the point on which they agreed, that the display of the animal's restoration served the purpose of the whole account. The man who had doubted the raising of the dead was shown the process in the creature that had carried him.

The instruction to look at the bones follows the instruction to look at the provisions, and the two commands present the small evidence and the great evidence together. The sentence thus leads the questioner through the objects of his own journey to the conclusion the account intends.

**The Identifications**

The commentators discussed the identity of the man, and the reports name figures from the accounts of the earlier communities, including Ezra and Ezekiel, while others mention the servant of God who appears in another sūrah. A further position holds that the man was not a prophet or a saint but a person to whom the demonstration was given.

The community's scholars noted that the Book does not name him, and they treated the identifications as material from the traditions of the earlier peoples. The lesson was drawn from the account whatever the name of the person who passed by the ruins.

The wording of the sentence, with its address to a single man, supported the reading that the account concerns an individual whom God instructed. The teachers used the passage when they spoke about the resurrection because it presents the matter as a demonstration rather than as a claim.

**The Sign for Mankind**

The sentence states that the event was done in order to make the man a sign for people, and the community's scholars read the clause as the statement about the purpose of the account. The man who received the demonstration became the evidence for those who came after him.

The Book uses the same word for the signs that the prophets were given, and the teachers gathered the passages on the subject. The account therefore belongs to the material that the sūrah presents for the instruction of its community, and the sentence's placement among the verses about the resurrection serves that end.

The closing sentence records the man's statement that he now knows that God has power over all things, and the community's teachers read the confession as the fruit of the demonstration. The question that opened the account is answered by the knowledge that closes it, so that the passage moves from the ruins to the certainty of the man who stood among them.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The sentence establishes the account of the man and the ruined city, the death and the raising that he experienced, the preservation of his provisions, the restoration of the animal, the purpose of the event as a sign for people, and the knowledge that the man declared at its end. The teachers used the verse when they set out the evidence for the resurrection, and the community's preachers repeated the account because it presents the doctrine through the experience of a single person. The passage then turns to the request of Abraham, so that the two accounts of the raising of the dead stand together in the sūrah.
