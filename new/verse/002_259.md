## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:259

> Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins. He wondered, “How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?” So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life. Allah asked, “How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?” He replied, “Perhaps a day or part of a day.” Allah said, “No! You have remained here for a hundred years! Just look at your food and drink— they have not spoiled. ˹But now˺ look at ˹the remains of˺ your donkey! And ˹so˺ We have made you into a sign for humanity. And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!” When this was made clear to him, he declared, “˹Now˺ I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A City Fallen on Its Roofs**

The verse opens with a second observation: a man passing by a town "khāwiyah ʿalā ʿurūshihā." Khāwiyah carries both ruin and emptiness — the roofs have come down onto the walls, and nothing stands. The word choice appears in the poetry of the pre-Islamic Arabs for abandoned campsites, and the verse uses it to present a place from which every sign of life has gone.

The commentators name the man variously: Ezra (ʿUzayr), Ezekiel, or al-Khiḍr, with some holding he was no prophet at all but a man shown the demonstration. The Qur'an leaves the identity open, and the scholars of the community recorded the positions without coming to agreement. The lesson hangs on the question he asks, not on his identity.

**The Question and the Answer**

The question asks how a place in that condition could ever be restored, and it concerns possibility, and it is answered by an event rather than by reasoning, since the account states that the man was made to die for a century and then brought back to life. The man who asked how resurrection could work becomes the demonstration of it.

Al-Rāzī reads the sequence as part of the sūrah's method of teaching by sign. The question was not treated as an offence, and the commentators compared it with Abraham's "My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead" in the following verse. In both cases the request comes from a desire for the evidence of the eyes rather than a wager of the mind.

**The Food That Did Not Spoil**

"Look at your food and your drink — they have not spoiled." The instruction has the quality of a laboratory demonstration: God could have raised the man in a different place, but the verse puts him back beside his own provisions, unspoiled after a century, so the miracle is something he can inspect with his hands.

The commentators noted further that the preservation of the food answers a subsidiary question the man had not asked: whether the identity of a body survives the decomposition of the grave. His provisions had done so. The verse then moves to the more difficult case.

**The Bones and the Flesh**

"Look at your donkey" — and the verse describes the bones set up and clothed with flesh. The scholars discussed the referent of the phrase; most read it as the animal's restoration, some as the man's own body. The donkey is mentioned because it was the property whose loss he could measure.

The Qur'an elsewhere describes the first clothing of bones with flesh as the creator's own work (23:14), and the verse's vivid language here serves the visual argument. When the man sees it, the account records his verdict that he now knows God has power over everything

**A Sign for Mankind**

One clause explains the purpose of the whole event: "that We may make you a sign for mankind." The same word, āya, is used for the verses of the Book and for the wonders shown to the earlier communities. The Qur'an uses Pharaoh with the same expression in 10:92 — "so that you may be a sign" — where the sign is negative, a warning rather than a proof.

The community's scholars drew the point that the sign is intended to circulate: it happened to one man so that many could hear the report. The sūrah's use of narrative follows this logic throughout, and the verse's coda — the man's own confession — is the form the report takes.

**The Question Asked Three Times**

The question "how long have you tarried?" occurs in three places in the Book, and the community's commentators read them together. The sleepers of the cave give the same estimate when they wake (18:19), and in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn the resurrected reply in the same terms, having lost the ability to measure the interval (23:112–114). The three accounts agree that the span of the grave is experienced as almost nothing.

Al-Ṭabarī draws from the sequence the point that the demonstration was for the man's own certainty; the raising of the donkey was an argument addressed to his eyes, since his question had been about the possibility of the deed. Some of the early authorities also read the verse as a statement that the man was made a prophet, while others pointed to 10:92, where Pharaoh is called a sign for those who come after — a warning rather than a proof. The disagreement turns on the function of the sign, not on its reality, and the confession at the verse's end — "I know that God has power over all things" — is the fruit the account intends.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse establishes the demonstration of resurrection by means of a witness who lived it: ruin, the question, death, the hundred years, the unspoiled provisions, the reconstituted animal, the sign, and the confession. Al-Ṭabarī records that the phrase "and look at the bones, how We set them up" was read by some of the companions with a different subject, and the variation shows how closely the early community attended to the scene. The passage then turns to Abraham's request, so that the reader meets two kinds of question in succession: the doubter's and the prophet's.
