## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:15

> Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mockery Returned to Its Owners**

The sentence takes the activity described in the previous verse and answers it in kind. The Arabic verb expresses a deliberate response rather than an accident of history: the mockery does not simply rebound on its own, it is sent back by the one who was not fooled. Sūrah 9 states the same promise in the same words for a different group of mockers: *“Allah will throw their mockery back at them, and they will suffer a painful punishment.”* (9:79).

The Book repeats the phrase because the situation repeats. Whenever a community receives a revelation, there are people who treat the whole business as a performance to be managed with a smirk, and the revelation consistently tells them that the smirk is being recorded by the one who will settle accounts. The answer is not a rebuke in kind by the believers. The believers are not told to answer derision with derision; the response is reserved for God.

**When God Is Named as the One Who Mocks**

Attributing mockery to God raises an obvious difficulty, and the commentators addressed it directly. One line of explanation treats the statement as the law of recompense: the mocking returns upon the mocker as a consequence of his own act, so that God is named as the one who brings it about rather than as someone who enjoys the activity. Another line treats what God does as a state rather than a mood — the disgrace into which the mocker settles, long drawn out, which is the same thing the verse says of them in the second half.

Neither reading turns God into a participant in the world's mockery. Both treat the divine response as the precise application of a correspondence that runs through the Book: as a person treats the truth, so the truth treats him. The verse's vocabulary is chosen to make the symmetry exact, and the symmetry is the sentence.

**The Law of Correspondence in the Book**

The Qur'an states this law repeatedly, and always in the language of the opponent's own behaviour. Of the hypocrites who plotted while pretending to pray: *“Surely the hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but He outwits them.”* (4:142). Of those who planned against a prophet: *“And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺, but Allah also planned —and Allah is the best of planners.”* (3:54). Of a company that plotted in secret: *“And ˹so˺ they made a plan, but We too made a plan, while they were unaware.”* (27:50).

Each sentence takes a verb that describes the plotters' activity and applies it to God with a difference. The human verb names a finite, contingent attempt; the divine counterpart names an outcome no human being can arrange or foresee. The mockery in this verse follows the same pattern. What returns to the mocker is not an insult. It is a state of affairs he cannot manage, arriving on a schedule he does not control.

**Wandering Blindly, and the Vocabulary of Drift**

The second half of the verse names the condition into which these people settle: left to wander in their excess. The Arabic verb *yaʿmahūn* comes from a root denoting blindness and confusion, and it describes a person who moves without being able to orient himself. The Qur'an uses the same vocabulary for the same phenomenon elsewhere. One passage states the rule in general terms: *“We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance.”* (6:110).

The Book's narrative of refusal regularly ends not with a blow but with a stated inability to find the way. The point of the vocabulary is that the punishment begins inside the person's own perception. He is not stopped by a wall; he is released into a landscape where every path looks usable and none arrives, and nothing he does in that landscape will correct his sense of direction.

**Ṭughyān: Going Beyond the Limit**

The word rendered defiance, *ṭughyān*, describes the crossing of a boundary, and the Book attaches it to individuals and to regimes that overran the limits set for them. Pharaoh is named with it as a warning delivered by Moses: *“Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all limits˺.”* (20:24). The sūrah that will contain this verse's portrait is still in its opening pages, and the word chosen for the hypocrite is the word the Qur'an uses for tyrants.

The choice ties the small deception to a large category. What the men in the two rooms were doing looked like social management rather than tyranny, but the Book classifies it as trespass, because the boundary they crossed was the boundary of honesty about the revelation and their own position toward it. The verse then places them in a condition where the trespass itself becomes their horizon.

**Punishment That Adds Nothing to the Crime**

The verse describes a penalty that can be mistaken for a lack of one. The men are not struck; they are let alone, with their own directionlessness as their company. The Book has already stated a similar sentence over the people whose hearts were sealed, and it states the principle again in this verse: the answer to a chosen blindness is deepened blindness, and the increase of the condition is the punishment.

Read with the mockery that provoked it, the sentence is exact. The mocker measured the faith of others by nothing and treated it as an amusement. He is now measured by nothing in return, and left to wander in a space where no measurement can correct him. The verse gives no timetable, and it does not say how long the wandering lasts. It says only where the road is heading, and leaves the men described here to consider it.
