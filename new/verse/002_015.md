## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:15

> Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mockery Returns to Its Sender**

The answer to the private room is one verb: *yastahziʾu bihim* — Allah mocks them, in the same measure and the same mode as their *innamā nastahziʾ*, "we were only mocking." The Qur'an attributes mockery to Allah, and the attribution troubled exegetes enough to generate two reconciliations. One reading holds that the disgrace the disbelievers bring upon themselves is the mockery in substance — recompense arriving in the deed's own shape, not an act of derision originating in God. The other, al-Rāzī's, treats the prolonged ignominy in which Allah leaves them as tantamount to mockery: a scheme permitted to run its full course until the schemer is exposed. Weighed against the Qur'an's wider idiom — plotting answered by plotting, deception answered by outwitting — both readings describe one law: the moral universe returns acts in kind. The joke of verse 14 was told at the believers' expense; this verse announces that its punchline was reserved, from the first syllable, for the tellers.

**The Law With a Second Witness**

The formula is not local to al-Baqarah, which is what makes it a law rather than an incident. Sūrah al-Tawbah records the same exchange over alms: *"˹There are˺ those who slander ˹some of˺ the believers for donating liberally and mock others for giving only the little they can afford. Allah will throw their mockery back at them, and they will suffer a painful punishment"* (9:79). Two settings — Madinan hypocrisy in the sūrah's opening, Madinan hypocrisy around the charity drives years later — one identical verdict. The recurrence shows mockery being treated as a category with a fixed consequence, not a sin singled out for local color. It also refines the category: in al-Tawbah the object of the joke is generosity measured against the giver's means, the sneer of the ledger-keeper at sincerity. What is mocked in both sūrahs is never the powerful; it is faithfulness in its unimpressive forms. The returned mockery therefore has a target list, and the list is compiled by the mocker himself.

**Ṭughyān: The Overflow That Becomes a Vehicle**

"Wandering blindly in their defiance" ends on *ṭughyān* — defiance, rebellion, transgression — a noun from *ṭaghā*, to pass beyond a limit, the way water passes beyond a bank. The word family carries its hydrology into the theology: transgression is an overflow, a quantity exceeding the vessel that contained it. The Qur'an's exemplar is Pharaoh, and the command to confront him states the charge in the word itself: *"Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺"* (20:24). The hypocrites of this passage have their own overflow: a public creed they do not believe, a private loyalty they do not confess, and mockery poured over the gap between the two. Ṭughyān names the condition of living past one's own limits — beyond sincerity, beyond consistency, beyond the point where the performance can be sustained. What the verse says of them is that they are left *in* it: the overflow becomes the medium they now move through.

**Yaʿmahūn: Staggering Inside the Sentence**

The closing verb, *yaʿmahūn*, has no clean English equivalent, which is why translations reach for "wander blindly" or "continue blindly." It describes the staggering of a person whose vision has gone — movement without orientation, effort without direction, the walk of one who circles. The Qur'an keeps the word as a fixed formula and deploys it across settings: *"Whoever Allah allows to stray, none can guide, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance"* (7:186), with further instances in al-Anʿām, Yūnus, and al-Muʾminūn. The pairing with *ṭughyān* in each case is the point: the wandering is not punishment imposed on an innocent activity but the interior experience of the transgression itself. To live past your limits is to lose the horizon; the rebel's reward is vertigo. The hypocrites mocked believers who walked a straight path toward a known destination; the returned mockery leaves the mockers in motion and the mockers' destination unknown to the mockers.

**The Heaviest Judgment Is Withdrawal**

Read carefully, the verse's verb structure makes Allah's action a leaving: *wa-yadharuhum* — He leaves them, abandons them to the wandering. Nothing is struck; no bolt falls. The mockery returned is not God performing a joke on them but God withdrawing the hand that steadies, and letting the joke complete its own circuit. This is the Qur'an's recurring economy of judgment — the sinner's greatest danger is not divine intervention but divine permission, the respite that reads as success while it accumulates ruin. The withdrawal answers the mockery with exact justice: they treated faith as a game played before an inattentive audience; they are given what their creed assumed — an audience that has stopped intervening. And the verse locates the duration inside the leaving: *yamadduhum*, He extends them, stretches the rope while the staggering continues. The mockers of verse 14 asked, in effect, to be taken unseriously. Verse 15 grants the request literally.

**Justice Paid in the Same Currency**

The exchange between the two verses is a closed circuit, and its symmetry is the theological claim. They said: we were only mocking. The answer: Allah will throw their mockery back at them — the same noun, the same activity, redirected. The moral order this assumes is not mechanical but articulate: it knows the currency of each deed and pays in it. The liar is undone by a discovered lie; the deceiver becomes the deceived; the mocker inherits the joke. That is why the verse offers the hypocrites no new information about Allah — only a mirror with a verdict attached. For the reader outside the scene, the circuit carries a second charge: every mockery spoken at sincerity, generosity, or prayer is registered as a loan, and the repayment schedule is not published in advance. The sūrah's opening movement ends here, its taxonomy complete, and turns next from what the hypocrites are to what they have done with what they had — a transaction stated in the vocabulary of the market.
