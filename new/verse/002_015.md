## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:15

> Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Can Mockery Belong to God?**

The verse attributes mockery to God, and the attribution has been handled in two ways by the commentators. The first holds that God does not originate mockery; these people bring it on themselves, and what looks like mockery from the divine side is the recoil of their own act. The second holds that the disgrace and ignominy in which God leaves them, sustained over a long stretch of time, is *as if* mockery — the language describing the effect rather than the character of the actor.

The first is the safer theology and has the better support, since the Quran states the recoil without any attribution problem at all elsewhere: *Allah will throw their mockery back at them, and they will suffer a painful punishment* (9:79). The second is the better reading of this sentence, because the subject of the verb here is plainly God. Both agree on the outcome, which is what matters: the mockery does not land where it was aimed.

**A Penalty Shaped to the Crime**

The Quran's punishments regularly take the shape of the offence, and this is a clean instance. They laughed at the believers in private; they will be laughed at in public. They treated the truth as something to be managed rather than obeyed; they will be managed by it.

The correspondence is not vindictiveness but intelligibility. A penalty that matched nothing would be arbitrary violence; a penalty that matches the act is the act itself returned to its author. The sūrah is describing what the mockery *is*, once the performance is over — a person standing in a position he constructed for someone else.

There is a particular cruelty in the shape of it, and the Quran does not soften it. Mockery is a weapon that only works from a position of safety: the mocker needs to be sure he is not the one exposed. What is returned here is precisely the loss of that safety. The person who laughed in private is himself laughed at in public, and the only thing that has changed is the room.

**Left to Wander**

The second half is the harder clause: God leaves them to continue *wandering blindly in their defiance* (2:15). The verb carries the sense of extending or lengthening — giving more of something, letting a thing run out. What is being extended is not a punishment imposed from outside but the thing they were already doing.

The Quran uses the same combination of ideas wherever it describes this state: *Whoever Allah allows to stray, none can guide, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance* (7:186); *We leave those who do not expect to meet Us to wander blindly in their defiance* (10:11); *˹Even˺ if We had mercy on them and removed their affliction, they would still persist in their transgression, wandering blindly* (23:75). In every case the wandering belongs to the people and the leaving belongs to God. The two verbs are doing different work, and the verse needs both.

**Ṭughyān: Crossing a Line**

The word rendered *defiance* is *ṭughyān*, from a root meaning to exceed a limit. It is the Quran's word for transgression understood as overflow — water going over a bank — rather than as simple disobedience, and it is the term used for the archetypal rebel: *Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺* (20:24; 79:17).

That choice of word places the hypocrites in surprising company. Their offence is not that they rejected a creed; it is that they crossed a boundary and kept going. Ṭughyān describes movement beyond a marked limit, and the limit here was set by what they had been told and had seen. They are not ignorant. They are people who went past what they knew.

**Why Continuance Is the Sentence**

The logic of this verse is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly. Being allowed to continue is presented as a punishment, which means that what these people are doing is, in itself, destructive. If the activity were neutral, more of it would be neutral. The sūrah's claim is that the activity is ruining them, and therefore that the worst thing that could happen to them is to be given more of it without interruption.

This is the same thought the Quran states elsewhere as a general rule: *evil plotting only backfires on those who plot* (35:43). The plot does not need to be intercepted. It needs only to be left alone long enough to complete itself.

The sūrah makes the same point about the opposite case a few verses later, and the symmetry is worth noticing: those who believe are given *more* of what they are doing as well. Guidance increases where guidance is (19:76), as rebellion increases where rebellion is. The mechanism is one and the same; only the direction differs, and the direction was chosen by the person, not by God.

**What "Leaving" Means**

The verb also implies withdrawal. God is not described here as seizing, striking, or obstructing; He is described as not stopping. The image is of a restraint lifted rather than a hand laid on, and it explains why the condition is stable — nothing external is holding it in place. The person is doing exactly what he wants, and what he wants is what is killing him.

For anyone reading, the practical conclusion is that the opposite of punishment is not automatic safety. A life going badly wrong and meeting no obstacle is not evidence that God is pleased. On this verse's account it is the most frightening state there is.
