## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:26

> And ˹remember˺ when We assigned to Abraham the site of the House, ˹saying,˺ “Do not associate anything with Me ˹in worship˺ and purify My House for those who circle ˹the Ka’bah˺, stand ˹in prayer˺, and bow and prostrate themselves.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Site Given, Not Chosen**

*Wa-idh bawwaʾnā li-Ibrāhīma makāna al-bayti* — and when We settled Abraham at the place of the House. The verb *bawwaʾa* means to give someone a place to alight, to install him somewhere and make it habitable for him.

The significance of the verb is that the location is not Abraham's discovery. He did not survey the valley and choose a site; he was placed at one. The sūrah that has already said that the Sacred Mosque was appointed for all people (22:25) now says that its founder was assigned its position, and the two together remove any human claim of ownership over it. The man who built it did not select it, and the people who inherited the custodianship did not build it.

The instruction that follows is therefore addressed to a custodian rather than to a proprietor, which is why the first command is not about construction at all.

**The Site Existed Before the Building Did**

The verse says that Abraham was settled at the *place* of the House — *makāna al-bayti* — not at the House itself. The building came later, and the Qur'an records its construction elsewhere in a phrase that makes the sequence explicit: *“And ˹remember˺ when Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael, ˹both praying,˺ ‘Our Lord! Accept ˹this˺ from us’”* (2:127).

The distinction matters for how the sanctuary is understood. What was assigned to Abraham was a location, and the location was assigned before a single stone was laid. The holiness of the place is therefore not a property of the structure, and it is not something a builder conferred on it. It survived the building's destruction and rebuilding more than once in the centuries that followed.

The verse that names the House as the first established for humanity puts the same point positively: *“Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people”* (3:96).

**The First Command Is Not About Building**

*An lā tushrik bī shayʾan* — that you associate nothing with Me. Before any stone is laid, before any rite is specified, the founder is told to keep the worship of the place pure.

The priority is instructive for reading the rest of the passage. The House is not being established as a shrine that will then need to be defended against corruption; it is being established as a place whose entire purpose is the exclusion of rivals, and everything else — the circuit, the standing, the sacrifice — is secondary to that. When the sūrah speaks a few verses later about shunning the filth of idols (22:30), it is returning to this first instruction rather than adding a new one.

The parallel passage in Sūrah al-Baqarah records the same commission in the same order, with the purification commanded first and the worshippers named: *“We made the House a place of return and a sanctuary for the people, ˹saying,˺ ‘Take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer.’ And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, those who meditate in it, and those who bow and prostrate themselves”* (2:125).

**"Purify My House"**

*Wa-ṭahhir baytī* — and purify My House. The possessive is emphatic in the Arabic: the House is Mine.

The command to purify implies that the House can become unclean, and the commentators specified what the uncleanness is: idolatry first, and with it the doubts and innovations that attach themselves to a holy place. Purification is not a one-time act performed at the founding; it is the standing work of anyone who holds the place in trust.

The verse is also, read historically, a prophecy. The Kaʿbah that Abraham purified was filled with idols by the time of the Prophet ﷺ — three hundred and sixty of them by some accounts — and the command to purify was addressed to a man who had been dead for two and a half millennia. The sūrah is telling its audience that the restoration they are witnessing is the original commission being carried out again.

**Three Postures, and What They Cover**

*Li-l-ṭāʾifīna wa-l-qāʾimīna wa-l-rukkaʿi al-sujūd* — for those who circle, those who stand, and those who bow and prostrate.

Three groups are named, and between them they cover the two great forms of Islamic worship. The circling is the distinctive rite of the pilgrimage, the movement around the House that no other religion shares. The standing, bowing and prostrating are the components of the canonical prayer.

Putting the two in one sentence does something theologically important: it places the pilgrimage rite and the daily prayer on the same footing, as worship addressed to the same One, and it places both under the same condition — that the House be purified and no partner associated with God. A circumambulation performed among idols is not the rite this verse commissions.

The last pair, *al-rukkaʿ al-sujūd*, are also the words used of the whole creation in verse 18 of this sūrah. Everything in the heavens and the earth prostrates; the people who come to this House do it deliberately.

**Whose House It Remains**

The verse's possessive has a consequence worth stating directly. If the House is God's, then no one has the authority to determine who may enter it — which is precisely the principle the previous verse asserted when it declared the resident and the visitor equal in it.

The two verses are a unit. Verse 25 denies the custodians a property right in the precinct; verse 26 explains why, by showing that the founder himself was given a site and told to prepare it for anyone who would come and worship in the three named postures. Ownership never entered the transaction, at either end of the history.
