## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:33

> Joseph prayed, “My Lord! I would rather be in jail than do what they invite me to. And if You do not turn their cunning away from me, I might yield to them and fall into ignorance.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**He Answers a Room by Addressing God**

The demand came from a woman, backed by her guests, with a threat attached. Joseph's reply is not to any of them. *Rabbi* — my Lord — opens the sentence and the rest of it is spoken upward. There is no argument with the threat, no negotiation about conditions, no appeal to the guests' judgement, no reference to the finding of his innocence two verses earlier. He bypasses the entire room. The move is not evasion; it is an accurate assessment of the situation. Nothing said in that hall could have changed what was being demanded, and the only party capable of changing it was not present in it. The chapter records the prayer in full, which makes the verse a model of where to direct a request when every human avenue has been closed by people who control them.

**Prison Preferred, Not Welcomed**

*Prison is dearer to me than what they invite me to.* The construction is a comparison between two evils, and the word for dearer is the ordinary term for preference. He does not say prison is good, does not present himself as eager for suffering, and does not claim to be beyond temptation. He states which of two available outcomes he would rather have. That restraint is what makes the verse usable. A hero who welcomes the cell is beyond imitation; a man who ranks a cell above a sin is not. The commentators draw the theological consequence: the preference places the next life above the fleeting pleasure of this one, since compliance would have bought comfort here and ruin there, while prison buys discomfort here and preservation there.

**"What They Invite Me To" Is Deliberately Plural**

The pronoun is theirs, not hers. Joseph describes the pressure as coming from a group, which matches the scene the previous verse constructed: a woman issuing an order in front of guests who, on the transmitted account, added their own urging. The plural changes the character of the test. A single seducer can be refused; a room that agrees cannot be argued with, because there is no one left to appeal to. The plural also anticipates the second half of the prayer, where the same group is named again as the holders of a cunning that must be turned away. Joseph has correctly identified that he is facing a collective operation rather than a personal weakness, and his prayer is framed accordingly.

**The Second Half Is the Real Content of the Prayer**

*And if You do not turn their cunning away from me, I might yield to them.* Nothing in the first half of the verse is remarkable; refusing a sin is the minimum requirement of faith. The second half is what distinguishes this prayer, because it disclaims the capacity to refuse. Joseph does not say he will resist. He says that without divine intervention he will incline toward them and become one of the ignorant. A prophet asked to commit indecency in front of a hostile room does not announce his strength; he reports his dependence. The verse thereby contradicts the expectation most readers bring to it. The moral of the scene is not that Joseph was strong enough. It is that he knew he was not, and asked.

**"Fall Into Ignorance" Uses Sin's Own Vocabulary**

The word he chooses for what he fears becoming is *jāhilīn*, the ignorant — the plural of the term the Qur'an uses for those who act foolishly or sinfully. The idiom is established elsewhere in the Book: repentance is promised to those *“who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after”* (4:17), where the ignorance named is not absence of information but the condition of a person acting against what he knows. Joseph has just been vindicated as a truthful man and has heard the household admit that his accuser sinned. He knows precisely what is being proposed. What he fears is the state in which knowledge stops governing conduct, and he names it accurately. The prayer is therefore not a request for information but for the maintenance of a disposition.

**A Prayer Answered in the Next Verse**

The structure of the exchange is exact. Joseph asks that God turn their cunning away; verse 34 reports that his Lord answered him and turned their cunning away, using the same verb and the same object. The general law behind the exchange is stated elsewhere without ambiguity: *“Your Lord has proclaimed, ‘Call upon Me, I will respond to you’”* (40:60). What is unusual here is the form the response takes. The cunning is not turned away by removing the demand, by softening the woman's heart or by opening a door. It is turned away by making prison acceptable to him, which is a change in the man rather than in his circumstances. The sūrah treats that as a genuine answer, and in doing so it defines divine assistance in a way the reader is unlikely to have expected: help arrived as an increased capacity to accept a worse outcome.

The shape of the prayer is worth comparing with another from the same family of stories. Moses, after the blow that killed a man, says three things in one sentence: *“My Lord! I have definitely wronged my soul, so forgive me.”* (28:16), and the verse reports the forgiveness in the clause that follows. Joseph's prayer is equally compact but has a different object. Moses asks for a past act to be erased; Joseph asks for a future act to be prevented. Neither prayer contains a preamble, an explanation of circumstances or a list of merits, and both are answered within a verse or two. The chapter's model of supplication is therefore minimal in form and specific in content: one named danger, one request for intervention, no elaboration. That economy is what makes the prayer usable. A reader can hold it in mind and repeat it under pressure, which is presumably why the Qur'an reproduces it in full rather than summarising it.
