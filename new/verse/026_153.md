## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:153

> They replied, “You are simply bewitched!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The People Attack the Messenger Instead of the Message**

Thamûd answers Ṣāliḥ's call with an accusation: he is bewitched. The charge moves attention away from the truth of what he has said and toward a label attached to his person. If the messenger can be dismissed as mentally or spiritually affected, the people no longer have to examine the command to fear Allah or the evidence placed before them.

This is a familiar defensive move. When a warning threatens a settled identity, the hearer may question the speaker's condition rather than the substance of the warning. The accusation does not show that Ṣāliḥ is false. It shows that the people are searching for a way to avoid the responsibility created by his message.

**The Accusation Can Carry Two Senses**

The Arabic wording allows a discussion about what Thamûd means by the charge. It may refer to a person affected by sorcery, or it may carry a sense connected with a human creature who eats and lives ordinary bodily life. The first reading attacks Ṣāliḥ's reliability; the second treats his humanity as evidence that he cannot be a messenger.

Both readings reveal the same refusal. The people do not ask whether the call to worship God is true. They seek a defect in the messenger that will make obedience unnecessary. The ambiguity does not weaken the verse. It shows how rejection can use either superstition or a narrow view of humanity to protect itself.


Thamûd may think that a messenger should be unlike the people he addresses. The Qur'an repeatedly corrects that expectation. Quran 25:7 records critics objecting that a messenger eats food and walks through marketplaces, while Quran 18:110 presents the messenger as human while distinguishing him through revelation.

Humanity is not proof against prophethood. It is what makes the messenger an example that people can understand and imitate. He experiences hunger, fear, work, and social pressure, yet he remains faithful to God's command. His ordinary needs do not erase the extraordinary trust placed in him.

**Bewitchment Is a Way to Avoid Evidence**

If Ṣāliḥ were merely confused, Thamûd could pity him and move on. But the accusation does not account for the clarity of his call, the steadiness of his character, or the sign that God will place before them. It is a verdict pronounced before the people have answered any of those matters.

A fair critic identifies a contradiction or offers evidence against a claim. A label such as bewitched can function as a substitute for both. It closes the conversation by declaring that the speaker is disqualified in advance. Ṣāliḥ's opponents use the accusation to keep their own assumptions unexamined.


Calling a messenger bewitched can reassure a community that it is still normal and powerful. If Ṣāliḥ is defective, Thamûd need not admit that its own customs are corrupt. The label preserves the people's self-image by placing the problem entirely in the person who warns them.

This is why public accusations can be more dangerous than private doubt. They recruit the crowd into a judgment and make honest reconsideration look disloyal. A person who suspects that Ṣāliḥ speaks truth may silence that suspicion to avoid being associated with the insulted messenger.


A human messenger can be rejected for being too ordinary, while a divine sign can be rejected for not satisfying the people's preferred form. The standard keeps moving because the real issue is not evidence but submission. Thamûd wants a reason to remain in control of the conditions for belief.

True humility begins differently. It asks whether God has actually sent guidance and then accepts that guidance in the form He chooses. The servant does not dictate that revelation arrive through an angel, a ruler, or a spectacle designed to flatter him. He listens to the messenger God sends.

**The Accusation Cannot Erase Ṣāliḥ's Brotherly Relation**

Ṣāliḥ has spoken as their brother and called them to fear Allah. The people know that he shares their life, yet they treat that closeness as something to overcome with a slur. Their familiarity should have supplied a basis for trust, but pride turns knowledge of the messenger into a reason to belittle him.

A community may remember a person's ordinary past and use it to deny his present calling. That reasoning assumes that God cannot choose someone known to the people. The Qur'anic pattern rejects the assumption. Familiarity can test whether a society follows truth or only honors distant figures.


A name placed on a messenger does not answer his argument. Thamûd still has to face the call to worship God, the demand for taqwa, and the question of how it uses its gifts. The accusation does not touch those matters. It only tries to make the speaker disappear behind a word.

The reader should therefore learn to separate diagnosis from dismissal. Sometimes a person can be genuinely ill or deceived, but such a claim requires evidence and careful judgment. It cannot be assumed whenever a truthful warning is uncomfortable. Ṣāliḥ's opponents provide no proof; they provide a shield for their refusal.

The charge also reveals how quickly a community can make an insult sound like a conclusion. Repeated often enough, the label may become part of public memory and obscure the messenger's actual words. Truthful judgment requires returning to the claim itself rather than accepting the crowd's description of the person who made it.


The people speak with confidence, but confidence is not the same as knowledge. Their accusation may make the crowd feel secure, yet it leaves the central question unanswered. What does God require of them, and is Ṣāliḥ conveying it faithfully?

A believer protects the heart from this form of certainty by remaining willing to examine. Do not make the messenger's humanity, unfamiliarity, or social position a substitute for testing the message. Thamûd's word “bewitched” is short, but it displays a long refusal to let truth judge the people who hear it.
