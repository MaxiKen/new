## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:240

> Those of you who die leaving widows should bequeath for them a year's maintenance without forcing them out. But if they choose to leave, you are not accountable for what they reasonably decide for themselves. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Year Bequeathed**

The widow's verse returns — this time from the side of the estate rather than the calendar. The four-months-and-ten term (2:234) fixed how long the widow waits; this verse legislates where she waits and on whose purse: the dying husband is to make provision for his wife — maintenance for a year, and residence in his house *without forcing them out*. The bequest-form is explicit: the maintenance is something he is to arrange, a charge laid on his estate ahead of its division, and the expulsion-ban is its physical half — the year's maintenance is meaningless if the house it is spent in can be closed to her. The commentators read the two halves as one protection: sustenance and shelter, ordered together, owed by the dead man's wealth to the woman his death leaves standing in it. The verse extends the code's pattern — provision scaled and owed at every dissolution — to the dissolution no one contracts: death itself.

**The Abrogation Question**

The year and the four-months-and-ten stand in obvious tension, and the exegetes record the classical solutions. One position holds this verse abrogated by 2:234 — the waiting-term of four months and ten days replacing the year's provision, a reading supported by the verse-order oddity that the abrogating verse appears before the abrogated one, which the tradition explains by the revelation's sequence differing from the compiled arrangement. A second position finds the abrogation in the inheritance legislation of the sūrah of Women — the widow's fixed share, *your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless* (4:12), supersedes the year's bequest. A third position, recorded by al-Qurṭubī and argued on the no-contradiction principle, dissolves the tension without abrogation: 2:234 governs the waiting period — the term of remarriage-eligibility — while this verse governs maintenance and residence, two different questions with two different durations.

**The Harmonizing Reading**

The third position deserves its full statement, because it makes the two verses a single coherent institution. On this reading the widow's waiting period is four months and ten days, fixed and unextendable; her right to remain in her late husband's house with maintenance from his estate runs up to a year; and the two rules interlock rather than conflict. If she remarries before the year ends, the maintenance stops — her new husband assumes her support — and there is no blame on the heirs for ceasing it. If she does not remarry, she may complete the year in the house, maintained, before the estate's full division closes around her. The reading preserves every clause of both verses: the term's precision, the year's generosity, the expulsion-ban's force, and the heirs' exoneration the moment her circumstances change. The jurists who preferred abrogation answered that a year's charge on the estate conflicts with the heirs' fixed shares; the harmonizers answered that the bequest precedes the shares — *after the fulfilment of bequests and debts* (4:12) — and the year's provision is exactly such a bequest.

**If They Choose to Leave**

The verse's second half returns the widow's agency, in wording that deliberately echoes the earlier widow-verse: *But if they choose to leave, you are not accountable for what they reasonably decide for themselves*. The non-accountability clause — the same formula as 2:234's — covers the community, the heirs, and the authorities: her decision to leave the house before the year ends, to forgo the remaining maintenance, to relocate or remarry on honorable terms, is hers and carries no blame on anyone who permitted it. The clause completes the protection's shape. The year's provision is a right, not a tether: she may take it or leave it, and leaving it is not forfeiture of honor. The exegetes connect this to the pre-Islamic practice the code was replacing — in which the widow was neither maintained nor free, held in the dead husband's house under his heirs' control — and the verse's architecture answers both halves of that abuse: maintenance is commanded, and exit is unblamed.

**Bequest, Share, and the Estate's Order**

The verse sits inside the Qur'an's wider legislation of the estate, and its mechanics repay that context. A bequest (waṣiyyah) in Qur'anic law is discharged before the fixed shares are computed — the estate's first charges are debts and bequests, and only the remainder divides among the heirs (4:12). The widow's year, on the harmonizing reading, rides in that first rank: not a share competing with the quarter or the eighth the same verse assigns her, but a charge ahead of them. The jurists debated whether the year's provision survives the later inheritance-legislation's arrival precisely because the two operate at different stages of the estate's arithmetic — and the debate produced the position al-Qurṭubī records, that the widow takes her fixed share and, on some readings, was additionally housed for the waiting-term's duration from the estate. The verse's practical legacy in the schools is thus layered: everywhere it informs the widow's housing-right during her waiting, and in one school's history it legislated a full maintained year.

**Almighty, All-Wise**

The closing name-pair is the code's legislative signature — the same pair that closed the orphan-intermingling ruling (2:220) — and it fits this verse's peculiar difficulty. ʿAzīz, the Mighty, answers the verse's enforcement problem: the widow's protection runs against the heirs, the people with the estate in their hands and every incentive to shorten her year, and a rule for the benefit of the weak against the strong needs the Mighty behind it. Ḥakīm, the Wise, answers the verse's construction: the interlocking of term and year, of bequest and share, of commanded maintenance and unblamed exit, is drafting of a precision the harmonizing reading exists to display. The pair also covers the abrogation-debate itself — whether the year stands or yields, the disposition is wise, since God's legislation adjusts across revelation the way a single wise legislator adjusts across a case's circumstances. The widow ends the verse as she began it: provided for, unexpelled, free to go, and held — whichever reading of the year survives — under the two names the code reserves for its hardest protections.
