## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:152

> remember Me; I will remember you. And thank Me, and never be ungrateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command of Remembrance**

The verse opens with a command in the first person, which is uncommon in the sūrah and is used by the Book at points where the relation between God and the servant is being stated directly. The sentence asks the community to remember God and promises a corresponding remembrance in return.

The commentators understood the pairing to indicate that the relation is reciprocal. God's remembrance of a person is not the recollection that overcomes forgetfulness; it is His turning toward him with favour and mercy. What the verse describes is a bond in which each side's action corresponds to the other, and the sentence presents the command as an offer as much as an obligation.

The Book uses the imperative of remembrance in several settings, including the prayer, the pilgrimage, and the times after the completion of a ritual. The command here is unqualified, and the commentators took it to cover every situation in which a person is able to turn his attention to God. The reports also describe the Prophet ﷺ fulfilling the command throughout the day, and the community’s practice has preserved those occasions as the times of fixed and spontaneous remembrance.

**The Reports About the Promise**

The reports preserved about this sentence explain both sides of the relation. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have conveyed God's statement that whoever remembers Him within himself will be remembered by God within Himself, and that whoever remembers God in a gathering will be remembered in a gathering better than it. The community's scholars used these reports to explain the promise attached to the command.

The reports also describe the conditions of the remembrance. Some mention the state of ritual purity and the direction of the person's heart, and others warn against loud calling that resembles the conduct of a marketplace. The community's practice has taken the command to cover the remembrance offered with the tongue, in the heart, and in the acts of worship that are performed at fixed times.

**Remembrance as a Discipline**

The Book's commands to remember God appear throughout its chapters, and they are connected with prayer, with the pilgrimage, and with the ordinary conduct of a person's day. The sūrah returns to the subject later when it instructs the community to remember God during the days appointed for the pilgrimage and when it describes the believers who remember God standing, sitting, and lying down.

The discipline is presented in the Book as a means by which the servant's attention is kept on the relation that the passage has established. A community that has received a direction of prayer, a messenger, and a revelation is told to keep those gifts present in its awareness, and the sentence in this verse makes that maintenance a matter of command.

The discipline has a social dimension as well, since the Book describes gatherings in which God is remembered and attaches a reward to those who take part. The reports preserved about the promise in this verse describe a person remembered in a gathering better than the one he attended, and the community’s scholars used them to encourage public remembrance alongside private devotion. The sentence therefore concerns the community’s shared life, and not only the devotions of individuals.

**Gratitude and Its Opposite**

The second half of the verse commands gratitude and forbids its opposite, and the commentators noted that the point being denied is the denial of the blessings themselves, since a complete denial would be unbelief. Gratitude in the Book's usage covers the acknowledgment of a blessing in the heart, the praise of God with the tongue, and the use of the blessing in what is good.

The sūrah has described the community's blessings in the preceding verse, and the command to be grateful follows directly. What the reader is told is that the proper response to a messenger from among the people, to the recitation, the purification, the teaching, and the knowledge that was missing is not merely acceptance but a gratitude expressed in action.

The Book treats ingratitude as a failure that follows blessing closely, and it warns the community against the condition of the earlier people who received provision and then forgot the One who provided it. The command in this sentence is a caution drawn from the passage that has gone before, since the sūrah has just narrated the history of a community that was given much and held on to little. What the reader is offered here is the safeguard: to keep the blessing in view and to name its giver.

**The Structure of the Book's Command**

Both commands in the sentence are given in the first person, so that the reader hears the words as addressed to him by God rather than as a report about Him. The Book uses this directness at points of high significance, and the sūrah's placement of the verse after the account of the community's formation makes the command a statement about its ongoing life.

The command also carries a warning that has been noticed by the commentators, who pointed out that the community's blessings would not secure it any more than the earlier community's blessings had secured them. The refrain about the community that has passed away has already been sounded in the passage, and the verse's instruction to remember and be grateful places the community's own outcome in its own hands.

**How the Verse Functions in the Passage**

The sentence stands among the verses that follow the direction of prayer and the description of the messenger's mission, and it turns the reader's attention from the controversies described earlier to the practices that sustain the community. The next verse continues in the same form by declaring God's signs, and the passage then takes up the subject of being tested with fear, hunger, and loss. The command to remember and to be grateful is placed at the point where the community is being prepared to meet difficulty, and the assurance attached to it is the community's provision for that time.

The sentence comes after the description of the messenger’s work and before the passage about trials, and its position gives the community the two practices that carry it through both. What the community has been given is stated in the previous verse; what it is to do with the gift is stated here; and what will test it is described in the verses that follow. The reader who keeps the sequence in view understands that remembrance and gratitude are not ornaments added to the religion but the response that the gifts of the passage require.
