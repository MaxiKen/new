## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:109

> I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah Removes the Suspicion of Personal Payment**

Noah states that he asks no reward for the message. The sentence answers a common suspicion about religious calls: perhaps the messenger is using belief to obtain money, rank, control, or social honor. Noah rejects that motive directly. The message is not a service he is selling to the people. It is a trust he is delivering for God.

The refusal of payment does not mean that the message has no value. It means that its value cannot be measured by a worldly fee. A messenger is not a merchant who withholds guidance until the audience pays. He calls because God has commanded him, and the people are asked to respond to truth rather than to purchase access to it.

**The Lord of All Worlds Is Noah's Reward**

Noah does not say that he receives nothing. He says that his reward lies with the Lord of all worlds. The distinction matters. A messenger may endure rejection, ridicule, and danger without receiving immediate human compensation because his true reward is held by God. The absence of worldly payment does not make the mission futile.

The title Lord of all worlds also places the reward beyond one community's control. Noah's people cannot decide whether his service has value by withholding gifts or praise. God sees the work, the intention, and the cost. A messenger who seeks reward from God is free from dependence on the audience's approval.

**Freedom From Payment Protects the Message**

If Noah demanded payment from each hearer, the people could treat the call as a private bargain. Those who paid might imagine they had purchased favor, while those who could not pay might be excluded. By refusing a reward, Noah shows that guidance is not a privilege reserved for the wealthy or powerful.

The refusal also protects the messenger from adjusting the message to increase revenue. A paid performer may be tempted to say what keeps the audience satisfied. Noah's reward being with God frees him to warn people even when the warning threatens their preferences. His livelihood is not presented as the measure of truth.

In 11:29, Noah likewise declares that he does not ask the people for payment and that he will not dismiss the believers. The parallel shows that financial independence and social fairness belong together. A messenger who seeks no fee has no reason to reserve God's message for a wealthy class or to remove poor believers for the comfort of elites.

This makes the economic statement part of the moral argument. Noah's call is for all who can respond to God. The people cannot demand that he reshape the community around their status. The message is not a product of the chiefs' preferences, and its acceptance is not restricted by their generosity.

Noah's reward is with God, which places it beyond immediate sight. The people may judge him unsuccessful because he does not gain wealth or majority support. The verse rejects that measure. A messenger's work can be faithful even when the public response is hostile and the worldly return is absent.

This principle helps anyone who serves truth without quick recognition. Teaching, care, counsel, and reform may produce no visible reward for a long time. If the work is done for God, its value does not depend on applause. The servant should still act with skill and justice, but he need not turn the audience into a source of personal validation.

Noah's refusal concerns the message he conveys. It does not create a rule that every worker must serve without lawful compensation or that communities may exploit teachers and caregivers. Work has value, and justice requires that people be paid for labor when payment is due. Noah's point is that prophetic guidance is not a commodity whose price grants spiritual access.

This distinction keeps the verse from being misused. A messenger's independence from payment protects revelation from being bought. It does not authorize people to deny fair rights to those who perform ordinary work. The principle is about the purity of the call and the direction of reward.

**The Messenger's Reward Is Not Social Victory**

Noah's reward being with God also frees him from measuring success by the collapse of his opponents or the size of his following. His task is to convey the message faithfully. The people may reject him, but their rejection does not turn his obedience into failure. God alone evaluates the service and gives its lasting result.

This posture prevents triumphalism. A caller who seeks only to defeat opponents may begin to enjoy humiliation more than guidance. Noah's reward is with God, so the central desire remains that the people respond to truth. The messenger warns firmly, but he does not turn the message into a personal contest.

People often act for money, status, gratitude, or fear of criticism. These motives can shape religious work as well as worldly work. Noah's sentence asks the servant to examine what ultimately sustains him. If every human reward disappeared, would the duty still be worth doing for God?

The question does not demand indifference to every earthly benefit. It demands that earthly benefits remain secondary. A teacher may appreciate thanks, and a community may support a worker, but the heart must not make the support its god. Noah points to the Lord of all worlds as the only reward that gives the prophetic task its true worth.

**The Message Is a Trust, Not a Transaction**

A transaction ends when the price is paid. Noah's message creates a continuing relation of worship, obedience, and accountability to God. No payment can purchase exemption from that relation, and no poverty can make a person ineligible for it. The reward language reveals the difference between divine guidance and a human service sold for advantage.

Noah therefore stands before his people as a trustworthy messenger whose motive is clear. He wants no worldly fee. He seeks God's reward and calls the people to respond to their Lord. The integrity of the message is protected because its bearer does not make the audience's money or praise the measure of his obedience.
