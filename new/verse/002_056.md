## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:56

> Then We brought you back to life after your death, so that perhaps you would be grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Resurrection Filed in History**

Eleven words in the translation carry an event the Qur'an nowhere softens: after the stroke, the struck were raised. *Thumma baʿathnākum min baʿdi mawtikum* — then We brought you back to life after your death. The death is stated as death, not swoon or near-death; the raising is stated as Allah's act in the first person, with no instrument, no prophet's prayer narrated, no mechanism supplied. The verse is one of the small number of places where resurrection — the raising of the dead to life — happens inside history, to a named community, as a reported event rather than a promised one. Its position in the recital gives it argumentative weight: it follows immediately the demand to see Allah and the stroke that answered it, so the sequence reads as demand, judgment, reversal — the same community that tried to force disclosure receives the disclosure's most extreme form instead: not the sight of Allah but the experience of death and its undoing. The verse states the reversal flatly because its flatness is the point. A resurrection that required elaboration would be a story; a resurrection filed in one clause, between a stroke and a purpose, is a precedent.

**Baʿatha: The Verb That Raises and Sends**

The verb *baʿatha* does double duty in Arabic, and the double duty generated a minority reading of this verse. The root means to rouse, to raise up, to set in motion — and in Qur'anic usage it carries both the raising of the dead and the sending of prophets: Allah *baʿatha* messengers among peoples (2:213 among many), and Allah *baʿatha* the dead at the Last Day. One reading takes this verse accordingly: the raising after the stroke was the sending of prophets among the Israelites — their revival understood as institutional, the community resuscitated through renewed guidance rather than resuscitated in its bodies. The majority reading takes the plain sense: bodily resurrection, because the verse's own premise is a literal death (*after your death*) and a literal stroke, and because the Arabic idiom of the resurrection from the grave uses this same verb. The minority reading has one thing in its favor — the word's documented range, familiar to Muslims from the celebration of the Prophet's ﷺ commissioning, ʿĪd al-Mabʿath, named from this root. But it has to read *your death* metaphorically to work, and the previous verse supplies no metaphor: the thunderbolt struck, and the people watched. The plain reading is stronger, and it is the one the recital's logic requires.

**The Proof of the Last Day, Demonstrated Early**

The verse's resurrection is doing eschatological work in a historical file, and the sūrah states the connection itself. Its opening argument for the Last Day runs on exactly this grammar: *"You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned"* (2:28). The four-step sequence — lifelessness, life, death, life again — is the sūrah's proof that return is not novel for Allah but habitual: the God who ran the sequence once on a species runs it again on a community at the sea's edge, and will run it finally on everyone. Verse 56 is thus the middle filing of the sūrah's resurrection argument: 2:28 states the rule, 2:56 shows the rule executed on witnesses, and the rule's final execution is the Day of verse 48. For the recital's addressees the demonstration has an edge — a community that demanded to *see* Allah received instead the one sight that proves the unseen's power over their own bodies: death reversed on themselves. The demand of verse 55 failed; the evidence that actually arrived was stronger than what was demanded, because it was lived rather than viewed.

**Gratitude as the Purpose of Return**

The purpose clause — *so that perhaps you would be grateful* — is the recital's second filing of the same formula: verse 52's pardon ended with *so perhaps you would be grateful*, and verse 53's gift of the Scripture will end with its own *laʿalla*, that perhaps you would be guided. The particle states the divine intent behind the act without guaranteeing its landing: the raising makes gratitude possible, not automatic. The clause also identifies what resurrection is *for* in this recital. The raised are not raised as trophies of power — the verse does not end with "so that you would know that I am able," though the knowledge is implied — but as debtors restored to the capacity of thanks. A dead man cannot be grateful; a forgiven calf-taker cannot serve; a struck-down community cannot keep a covenant. Every reversal in the recital reopens the same closed circuit between gift and acknowledgment, and gratitude is the circuit's name. The verse thereby binds the resurrection to the sūrah's economy of verse 47: favors are remembered or lost, and the greatest favor — life returned from death — is filed here with its purpose attached, so that no later reader can receive the story as spectacle without receiving its claim.

**A People Who Have Seen Their Own Death**

The recital's coming accusations all acquire their weight from this verse. The community addressed in the second person — *We brought you back* — is a community whose members died and returned, and whose descendants inherit the file. Whatever this people later does in the wilderness, it does as people who have been on the far side of death and came back with the covenant still standing: a position no other nation in the Qur'an occupies. The severity of the recital's later charges — refusing the city, trading the manna for onions, breaking the Sabbath — is calibrated against this fact, and the verse is why the charges stick. But the same fact runs in the other direction as comfort, and the Qur'an intends both. A people raised after a stroke are a people whose worst moment was reversed: the demand that deserved permanence received instead a second life, which publishes the sūrah's standing rule that Allah's dealing with covenant-breakers runs through reversal before it runs through ruin. For the individual reader the verse installs the same logic: the death of a soul — in sin, in despair, in the stroke of its own demands — is not outside the range of the verb *baʿatha*. The One who filed this resurrection has the form on record.

**Reversal Is Allah's Standing Method**

Verse 56 does not stand alone in the Qur'an, and its companions fix its genre. Later in this same sūrah, a man passing a ruined city asks how Allah could bring it back to life, and is answered by being killed for a century and raised — *"So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life"* (2:259) — with his food unfresh and his donkey's bones reclothed in flesh as the evidence laid out around him. Abraham asks to be shown how the dead are given life, and is walked through four birds scattered on hilltops and called back in haste (2:260). ʿĪsā is given the raising of the dead as a sign of his mission, *by Allah's Will* (3:49). The pattern across the filings is constant: reversal is demonstrated to a questioner, a passerby, a prophet, a community — always to witnesses, always with the same verb-family, always as proof of the general rule rather than as exception to it. Verse 56 is the filing addressed to a whole people at once. Its placement here — before the manna, before the city, before the rock — makes reversal the foundation of the wilderness narrative: everything the recital gives this community afterward is given to people who have already been dead and returned, which is the sūrah's way of saying that every favor in the file arrives on top of the first one.
