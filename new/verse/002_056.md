## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:56

> Then We brought you back to life after your death, so that perhaps you would be grateful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Revival Reported as an Act of Kindness**

The sentence gives the community a second life, and it states the purpose in the same breath: so that you would be grateful. Nothing in the wording presents the restoration as a reward and nothing describes the community's condition between the two events. The Book states the fact, states the reason, and moves on, which is the register it has used throughout this section of the sūrah.

The verb chosen describes being raised or roused, and the Book uses it for resurrection and for the sending of messengers. Its range allows the sentence to be read two ways, and the commentators kept both in view: a literal restoration to life after the blast, or a turning of the community's condition in a way the Book elsewhere calls giving life. The plain sense of the context favours the first reading, and the second reading does not change the shape of the lesson.

**The Purpose Named: Gratitude**

The clause at the end of the verse names thankfulness rather than obedience, and that choice is consistent with the passage. The sūrah has twice attached gratitude to gifts given to this community, once after the pardon that followed the calf and once here after a resurrection. Gratitude, in this Book's usage, is the appropriate response to a gift a person could not have produced himself.

The Qur'an's general statement of the connection sharpens the point: *“And ˹remember˺ when your Lord proclaimed, ‘If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe.’”* (14:7). The verse in al-Baqarah does not threaten; it names the grace and states what it was for. The Book's usual pattern is to give the reason and then report what the recipients did with it, and this passage has already supplied several examples of the second part.

**A People Given a Second Chance**

What the verse adds to the story is time. A community struck down for a demand had its existence handed back, and it resumed the role it had been given: a people with a covenant, a book, and a place in the history of revelation. The Book's later references to this generation continue as though nothing had been interrupted, which is itself a statement about the scale of the mercy in the sentence.

That pattern recurs in the sūrah's account of Israel. The people are struck, then restored; they fail, then receive instruction; they ask for food, and food arrives with conditions. Repeated rescue is what the address to their descendants assumes, and the sūrah's reader encounters the whole sequence in order before any demand is made of him personally.

**How the Event Sits in the Argument of the Passage**

The verse follows a punishment and precedes a provision, and it functions as the hinge between them. The blast taught a community what it could not demand; the restoration returned it to a position where it could still be taught. The sūrah then immediately records the gifts that were sent: shade, bread and quails, and the meal is described as coming with an instruction to eat what is wholesome.

Read as a unit, the three verses describe a cycle the Book returns to for this community throughout the address: a failure severe enough to end the relationship, a restoration that the community did not earn, and a further provision accompanied by a requirement. Each element is stated without commentary, and each one adds to the record that the following verses will use.

**Gratitude as the Test of Restored Life**

The verse states its purpose in terms that make the restored life a test rather than a fact. A person raised from the dead has received the clearest possible demonstration of what he does not control, and the Book treats such a person as owing a specific response. The next verse reports the provision of food, so that the reader learns what the community did with the time it was given.

The Book's consistent practice is to attach gratitude to the recognition of where a gift came from. The sūrah's later verse states the general principle when it addresses the community about its history, and the same logic appears in the earlier account of Adam, where the pardon was followed by a descent into a world of guidance and provision. Every restoration in the Book arrives with further gifts attached, and each of those gifts is accompanied by the same expectation.

**The Frame the Book Places Around the Event**

The sūrah reports this episode as a memory belonging to the people being addressed, in the same way it reports the sea and the appointment. The command that opened the section was to remember the favours received, and a resurrection is one of them. That framing keeps the passage from reading as a catalogue of offences; the offences are recorded because they were answered by gifts, and the record is what the address rests on.

The reader who follows the sequence to its end reaches the following verse with the two facts held together: a community that demanded to see God, and a community that was revived and fed. The address continues to be made to the descendants of those people, and the invitation that closes the section is issued on the strength of the same history.

**A Community Given Time Rather Than a Verdict**

The revival in this verse is stated without any indication of how long the community was given, and the Book does not describe the interval between the strike and the restoration. What the record preserves is the fact and its purpose, and the space between them is left to the reader's imagination. The result is a narrative in which the punishment is a moment and the mercy is a continuation.

The sūrah's later treatment of the same people keeps that interval open. They go on to receive instruction, provisions and further correction, and none of that would have been possible without the restoration described here. The Book's habit of pairing a judgment with a following gift means that its narratives rarely end at the point of punishment, and this passage is one of the clearest examples of that method.
