## Sūrah al-Ḥajj 22:24

> for they have been guided to the best of speech, and they have been guided to the Commendable Path.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guided to Speech**

*Wa-hudū ilā al-ṭayyibi mina al-qawli* — and they were guided to the good of speech. The verb is passive and the object is speech itself. What they have been given is not only a destination but a vocabulary.

The commentators proposed several referents, and the list is worth having because the disagreement is about emphasis rather than substance. Al-Ṭabarī took the guidance as inspiration toward these things in general. Others specified the testimony of faith — *lā ilāha illa Allāh* — or the phrase *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh*, praise belongs to God, or the Qur'an itself. Still others took the good speech as what will be spoken *in* the Garden: the greeting of welcome the inhabitants receive, and the words of the angels to them.

The readings are not exclusive, and the verse's phrasing is wide enough to hold all of them. What matters is the claim they share: the people of the Garden are people whose speech was set right. They said the true thing in this life, they will hear the true thing in the next, and both are described as guidance rather than achievement.

**The Good Word Elsewhere**

*Al-ṭayyib mina al-qawl* is a phrase with a history in the Qur'an. Sūrah Ibrāhīm builds a parable on it: *“Do you not see how Allah compares a good word to a good tree? Its root is firm and its branches reach the sky”* (14:24). The good word is pictured as something with roots and height, which is to say something alive and load-bearing rather than ornamental.

The paradisal application is made explicit in the scenes of arrival. *“And those who were mindful of their Lord will be led to Paradise in ˹successive˺ groups. When they arrive at its ˹already˺ open gates, its keepers will say, ‘Peace be upon you! You have done well, so come in, to stay forever’”* (39:73). The gates are already open, and the first thing that happens is speech.

The evil word gets the matching parable, and the matching is exact: *“And the parable of an evil word is that of an evil tree, uprooted from the earth, having no stability”* (14:26). Roots and height against uprooting and collapse — the two words pictured as two trees, one load-bearing and one already fallen. Verse 24's guided speech is the first tree's fruit: the inhabitants said the firm word in this life, and the firm word is what they find on every tongue in the next.

**What the Inhabitants Say to Each Other**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden are unusual among ancient accounts of the afterlife in one respect: they specify the conversation. Not only what is eaten and worn but what is said.

The inhabitants are greeted: *“the Gardens of Eternity, which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants. And the angels will enter upon them from every gate”* (13:23). They are welcomed with a word — *“where they will be greeted with ‘Peace!’”* (14:23). The greeting is named as a divine greeting on the Day of meeting (33:44) and as a greeting from the Merciful Lord Himself (36:58). And what is absent is named as precisely as what is present: *“There they will never hear any idle talk—only ˹greetings of˺ peace”* (19:62), and *“never to hear any idle talk or lying therein”* (78:35).

The negative description is the more revealing half. A great deal of human misery is verbal — slander, lying, the noise of people competing — and the Qur'an promises its absence as a positive feature of the reward. Being guided to good speech means, among other things, being removed from bad speech permanently.

**"The Path of the Praised"**

*Wa-hudū ilā ṣirāṭi al-ḥamīd* — and they were guided to the path of the Praised.

*Al-Ḥamīd* is a divine name: the Praised, the One to whom praise belongs and who is praiseworthy in Himself. The commentators took "the path of the Praised" as the path of God, meaning the route that ends at the Garden — which is how the verse functions here, as the second half of a description of how these people got where they are.

The choice of this name over the others is not incidental. A path described as belonging to the Praised is a path whose end is praise: the praise the inhabitants will offer, and the praise they will be given in the greeting of peace. The destination is named by the sound that will fill it.

Sūrah Sabaʾ joins the same name to the same path, as what the revealed truth guides to: *“and that it guides to the Path of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy”* (34:6). *Ṣirāṭ al-ʿazīz al-ḥamīd* — the path named by its owner's attributes, might guarding it and praise filling it. Verse 24's second guidance is that arrival: the road walked in this life under the name, ending where the name is the sound of the place — praise offered, praise answered, peace spoken from every gate.

**The Verse Closes a Circle Opened in Verse 19**

Verses 19 through 24 form a single unit, and verse 24 is its last line. The punishment was described in four verses and the reward in two, and the reward's description ends not with a picture of the Garden but with an explanation of how the inhabitants got there: they were guided, to the right words and to the right road.

That ending is deliberate. If the passage had closed on silk and bracelets, the reader's last impression would be of the reward as a possession. Closing on guidance makes the last impression a relationship — these are people who were brought, twice over, to the thing they received.

It also completes the circle the sūrah opened in verse 16, where clear signs were sent down and guidance was said to be God's to give. Six verses of punishment and reward have been the illustration of that sentence.

Sūrah Yūnus states the guidance-to-Garden movement as the mechanism of arrival: *“Surely those who believe and do good, their Lord will guide them ˹to Paradise˺ through their faith, rivers will flow under their feet in the Gardens of Bliss”* (10:9). Guided *through their faith* — the belief itself the instrument of the guiding — and the rivers placed at the sentence's end as where the guided arrive. Verse 24 closes verses 19 to 24 on the same note: the silk and bracelets exhibited, then the guidance named as how the wearers got there.

**Why Guidance Is Mentioned Twice**

The verb *hudū* appears in both halves of the verse, and the repetition is the verse's argument.

Guidance, in the Qur'an's usage, is not the information a person receives once at the start. It is a continuous relation — and here it is said to extend to a person's words and to his direction, which between them cover what comes out of him and where he is going. Both are attributed to guidance rather than to his own management.

This is also the verse's quiet link back to verse 16, where the sūrah said that God guides whom He wills. Those who have arrived in the Garden are described in exactly those terms: they were guided, twice over, and the thing they were guided to was speech and a road. The disputants of verse 8 were described in the same grammar — without guidance — and the sūrah has spent the intervening verses showing where the two roads end.

The petition for guidance opens the Qur'an and never leaves the believer's day: *“Guide us along the Straight Path”* (1:6). Asked in every unit of every prayer — guidance not as a gift once received but as a relation maintained, renewed at every standing. Verse 24's doubled *hudū* is that petition answered in both its dimensions: the words set right and the direction set right, the tongue and the road, both held by the One asked.
