## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:281

> Be mindful of the Day when you will ˹all˺ be returned to Allah, then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Seal on the Economy**

The ribā-block closes as the sūrah's great legislative blocks have consistently closed — not with a new ruling but with the Day held up behind the rulings: *Be mindful of the Day when you will all be returned to Allah*. The commentators read the verse's function exactly: the passage on ribā ends with the reminder that all matters, economic ones included, will be accounted for in the Hereafter — the block's five legislative movements (the devourers' portrait, the blight-and-growth thesis, the abandonment-command, the war-and-principal settlement, the respite-and-charity invitation) all terminate in this single clause of return. The ittiqāʾ — the mindfulness, the same God-wariness that headed the command (2:278) — is now directed at a Day rather than an act, and the direction is the verse's legislation: the economy just governed is not a province life walls off from the fear of God but one of the fear's densest terrains, where every ledger-entry, every postponed term, every waived remainder is a transaction conducted in the Day's full jurisdiction. The tradition's report that this verse stands among the final revelations — cited by the exegetes among the passages said to be the last sent down before the Prophet's death — gives the seal a biographical weight: the economy's closing word is among the Book's closing words.

**Returned to Allah**

The return-clause's grammar carries the verse's first precision: *you will be returned* — the passive, the returning done to the returner. The commentators note the formula's kinship with the sūrah's own opening movement — how can you disbelieve, when you were dead and He gave you life, then causes you to die, then gives you life, then to Him you are returned (2:28) — and the kinship is the verse's argument: the return is not a journey the soul undertakes but an appointment it is brought to, the same divine passive that governed the first giving of life. The Day named is yawm — unspecified, undated, definite only in its certainty — and its content here is a single event: the return to God. Not the return to the earth, the tribe, the market, or the grave's silence but the return to the party every transaction in the block was secretly with: the Provider whose provision was spent or hoarded, the Warner whose warning arrived at the ribā-ledgers, the Lord whose principal-rule protected both creditor and debtor. The verse asks its reader to perform the block's whole economics as preparation for a meeting, and names the meeting's location as the meeting's meaning: to Him.

**Every Soul Paid in Full**

The Day's operation is stated in the verse's middle clause: *then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done*. The tawfiyah — the full paying-out, the completion of the wage — is the verse's economic verb, and its use at the Day's description is deliberate: the block that legislated debts, principals, respites, and waivers closes by describing the ultimate settlement in the same vocabulary. Every soul — kullu nafs, the individual in her singularity, the collective nouns of creditor and debtor dissolved into the unit that actually acted — receives the payment in full: nothing held back from the wage and nothing added to the bill. The commentators connect the clause to the resurrection of the devourers (2:275) as its double: the ribā-eater's convulsed rising and the soul's complete payment are the same Day seen from the two ledgers — the one whose increase was blighted arrives to find the blight total, and the one whose charity was grown arrives to find the growth counted to the last grain. What it has done — mā kasabat, what the soul earned — is the section's kasb-vocabulary applied at maximum scope: the earning includes the loans given and the increases abandoned, the respites granted and the debts waived, the principal retained without wronging and the surplus released without being wronged.

**None Will Be Wronged**

The verse's final clause is the block's own formula lifted to the Day: *and none will be wronged* — wa-hum lā yuẓlamūn. The commentators mark the echo deliberately: the same no-wronging that described the creditor's right at the settlement — you shall have your principal, neither wronging nor being wronged (2:279) — now describes every soul's position at the final audit. The parallel is the verse's quiet theorem: the settlement that the ribā-legislation imposed on the community's ledgers was a rehearsal of the Day's arithmetic, and the Day's arithmetic is the settlement's guarantee. Where the community's courts could only approximate the neither-wronging — the straitened debtor misjudged, the respite refused, the waiver uncredited — the Day performs it exactly: no soul overpaid, no soul underpaid, no increase surviving the blight, no charity lost from the growth. The commentators note the clause's double edge for the block's two parties: for the devourer who persisted, the no-wronging means the war-declaration's consequence arriving with full precision; for the repenter, the waiving creditor, the patient lender, it means the if-only-you-knew finally known — the better that was withheld from sight paid in full, to the last day of respite credited as the ḥadīth promised, to the last hidden gift found in the deposit with the Lord.

**The Individual Soul**

The verse's narrowing is its last structural gift: the block began with classes — the devourers, the believers, the creditors — and ends with the soul. Every economic identity the preceding verses assigned dissolves at the return into the single nafs that acted, and the singularization is the verse's pastoral point: no one stands at the Day as a member of a lending-house or a debtor-class, and the principal each soul carries is its own kasb. The commentators connect this narrowing to the sūrah's wider movement — the same every-soul formula runs through the Qur'an's Day-descriptions, and the sūrah's own later verses will restate it (what every soul has forwarded and left behind, 2:286's earned-and-perpetrated pair) — but here the narrowing serves the economy specifically: the ribā-block's hardest cases were always individuals inside systems, the creditor whose tribe's whole income ran on deferment-fees, the debtor whose distress the system called default. The verse hands both to the Day alone: what the system made complex, the soul's payment simplifies, and what the soul did — released or retained, waited or pressed, waived or devoured — is what the payment will be for.

**The Verse Between Two Legislations**

Positioned where it stands, this verse is also the hinge between the sūrah's two great documentary legislations: the economy-block it seals and the contract-verse that follows — the Book's longest verse, legislating the writing of debts. The commentators read the sequence as one argument: the Day-return verse establishes that every transaction is ultimately audited, and the documentation-legislation that follows builds the human audit to match it — the scribe, the witnesses, the written term, the small and great contracts recorded. The heavenly tawfiyah and the earthly kitābah are the two halves of one order: souls paid in full at the end, debts written in full at the beginning, and between them the mindfulness this verse commands — the ittiqāʾ that makes a merchant write honestly and a creditor postpone patiently, because both have read the same final clause. The ribā-block ends, and the sūrah's economic teaching continues into its documentation-form, with this verse standing at the door between them: the Day named, the return announced, the payment guaranteed complete, the wronging excluded — and the ledger of the world, in the verse's light, revealed as the draft of a document that will be settled to the last letter.
