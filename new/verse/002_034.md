## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:34

> And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused and acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Command, One Refusal**

The scene that began with the appointment of a deputy on the earth reaches its first crisis here. A command is issued to the angels, and the Book states the response in two movements: they all prostrated, and one did not. The exception is placed at the end of the sentence, so that the reader sees the unanimity broken only after he has seen it established.

The Book reports the same scene in several other sūrahs, and each telling supplies a detail the others leave out. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf gives Iblīs's reason (7:12), al-Ḥijr states the command's setting (15:29–31), and al-Kahf supplies his origin (18:50). Taken together, the accounts describe a single act of refusal narrated from several angles, which is how the Qur'an teaches the same history repeatedly rather than once.

**Refusal and Arrogance, in That Order**

Two verbs carry the sentence: he refused, and he acted arrogantly. The order runs from action to motive, so that the reader learns what he did before he learns why. The Book treats the pair as connected everywhere it tells this story, and the connection is stated as a rule of human character in a report the Prophet ﷺ gave: no one who has an atom's weight of arrogance in his heart will enter Paradise (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 91). When a companion asked about liking fine clothes and shoes, the answer distinguished appearance from the vice itself and defined arrogance as rejecting the truth and looking down on people.

That definition fits this verse exactly. Iblīs rejected a command whose rightness did not depend on his opinion of it, and he did so on the basis of a comparison between himself and another creature. The Book gives the comparison in his own words: *“I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”* (7:12).

**Who Iblīs Was**

The reports differ on his kind. One widely repeated account places him among a group called *al-ḥinn*, made from smokeless fire, and describes him as a custodian of Paradise; another attaches him to the angels. The Qur'an states his status without ambiguity in the sūrah of the Cave: *“but not Iblîs, who was one of the jinn, but he rebelled against the command of his Lord.”* (18:50). The Book's own identification settles the question the popular narrations left open, and al-Rāzī noted the same point, concluding that material describing him as an angel cannot be relied upon.

What all accounts agree on is his position at the moment of the command. He was present, he heard it, and he was capable of obeying. The verse's charge is built on that presence, which is why the Book does not describe a foreign power entering the scene. The refusal came from inside.

**Prostration to Adam, Worship of God**

The command raises a question the commentators addressed at length, since prostration in the Qur'an ordinarily belongs to God. Their answer is that the act here is a greeting and an acknowledgement of honour, not worship, and that the one being worshipped is God in both cases: Adam is the qiblah of the moment, so to speak, and the obedience is rendered to the command. The same reading covers the prostration of Joseph's family in the later sūrah, which the book records without censure.

That distinction is what makes Iblīs's answer intelligible as a theological error rather than a defence of monotheism. His words in al-Aʿrāf are about *merit* — fire against clay — not about the exclusivity of worship, and the verse here names the result of the whole exchange: he became one of the unfaithful.

**"Becoming Unfaithful": the Word's First Appearance**

The Arabic term rendered unfaithful is *kāfirūn*, and the verse places it at the end of the sentence as a verdict rather than a description of his initial state. The Book uses the word throughout for covering over what one knows, and Iblīs's refusal fits the usage precisely: he did not lack awareness of God or of the command. He had both, and he set them aside.

The Qur'an reports the scene without adding a punishment here because the sūrah is not finished with him. What the reader takes from this verse is the shape of the failure — a creature in possession of knowledge, ordered to acknowledge a peer, refusing on grounds of rank — and the sequel in the coming verses shows where that refusal led, first for Adam and then for all who inherit his position.

**The Threshold of the Human Story**

This verse closes the scene of the appointment and opens the story of the fall. The sūrah put a question in the angels' mouths, answered it by demonstration, and now records the one reaction that was neither praise nor acceptance. Everything after it in this sūrah's telling of human history depends on that reaction, because the enemy of the human being is introduced before the human being's first mistake is narrated.

The Book describes the same adversary as a declared enemy from the start: *“Would you then take him and his descendants as patrons instead of Me, although they are your enemy?”* (18:50). The warning is given before the temptation, which is the pattern the sūrah follows throughout: the danger is named first, and the failure is recorded second, so that no reader can claim the opposition was a secret.

**The Enemy Introduced Before the Temptation**

The order of the sūrah's narrative is part of its teaching. The refusal of Iblīs is reported before Adam is placed in the Garden, so the reader meets the adversary before he meets the test. Nothing that follows in the story can be blamed on a surprise, and the Book states the enmity outright in the same passage where it names his origin: Iblīs and his descendants are described as enemies of the human being who must not be taken as patrons (18:50).

That arrangement shapes how the whole account of the fall should be read. The human being was warned, the opponent was identified, and the terms of residence were set before the deception occurred. What the sūrah records is therefore not the ambush of an innocent but the failure of a warned creature, and the mercy that follows in the next verses is measured against that knowledge.
