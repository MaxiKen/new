## Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:147

> amid gardens and springs,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scene of Security Is Abundant**

The phrase completes the setting of Ṣāliḥ's question. Thamûd imagines security amid gardens and springs. Its environment is not described as empty or desperate. Green growth and flowing water create the conditions in which a people can feel settled, nourished, and confident about tomorrow.

That setting makes the warning more searching. The community is not being asked to fear because every visible support has already vanished. It is being asked to remember God while the supports are present. Gratitude during abundance is a form of worship, and forgetting the Giver in abundance can become more dangerous than acknowledging dependence in hardship.


A garden embodies cooperation among soil, water, cultivation, time, and human labor. Its beauty may appear natural after it has been established, but its life depends on conditions that no person controls completely. The sight should therefore awaken humility. People tend what they receive; they do not create the entire order on which growth depends.

Quran 16:10–11 connects rain with water for drinking, grazing, crops, and fruit. That parallel shows how the Qur'an treats cultivated abundance as a sign that invites reflection. Gardens are not merely scenery. They reveal dependence and ask whether the people will respond with thanks or with a stronger claim of self-sufficiency.

**Springs Make Provision Feel Permanent**

A spring appears to offer continuity. Water emerges again and again, so a people may begin to regard its flow as guaranteed. Yet a source that serves a community remains within God's creation and command. Its regularity is a mercy, not a contract granting the people permission to ignore the Provider.

Water also creates a moral relationship among neighbors. Those who control a spring can sustain life or use thirst to command others. Fear of Allah requires just management of what people need. A society cannot call itself secure while its comfort depends on preventing others from sharing the conditions of life.


A green landscape encourages attachment. Thamûd may look at its gardens and conclude that its way of life has been endorsed. Ṣāliḥ's question exposes that mistake. Beauty is real, but it is not a verdict that every belief or practice of the people is right.

A garden can be cultivated by honest work or maintained through exploitation. The appearance of abundance does not reveal the whole moral account. Who labored? Who received the harvest? Who was excluded from the water? The setting of security must be examined through justice as well as admiration.


Gardens and springs support families, workers, travelers, and animals. Their benefit naturally extends beyond the person who first claims them. A prosperous community honors the Giver by making provision serve life broadly rather than concentrating it only where power already exists.

This does not abolish private responsibility or ownership. It places both under a higher duty. The holder may manage a resource, but he cannot forget that God sees the needs of those affected by his decisions. Security becomes trustworthy when it includes protection for the vulnerable rather than comfort for the strongest alone.

**Abundance Can Narrow the Moral Imagination**

When a person has always seen water flowing and fields producing, he may struggle to imagine deprivation. Familiar blessing becomes invisible, while the suffering of those outside his circle becomes easy to ignore. Ṣāliḥ's mention of the landscape can therefore be heard as a demand to remember how fragile and unequal access to provision can be.

Gratitude widens the imagination. It asks the comfortable person to notice the labor and need around him, and it makes him reluctant to waste what another person lacks. The gardens and springs are not only reasons for private pleasure. They are tests of whether abundance produces compassion.

**A Garden Is Not the Hereafter**

The image of a garden can tempt a person to treat present beauty as the final reward. Yet a garden in this world grows, withers, and passes between hands. Its beauty points to God's generosity but cannot replace the lasting security that comes through obedience and mercy.

This distinction prevents two errors. The believer need not despise the world, and he must not make the world his ultimate refuge. Enjoy the garden with thanks, care for its source, and remember that the One who provides it is greater than the gift itself.


Ṣāliḥ's question asks whether Thamûd expects to remain secure in what it has. Gardens and springs show why the people might answer yes. They have visible reasons for confidence. But visible reasons are not final guarantees. A landscape can flourish for a season and still be placed under a decree that no human planning can reverse.

The verse therefore turns a pleasant scene into an examination. Are the people grateful tenants or boastful owners? Do they see a provision that calls for worship or a possession that proves they will never be punished? Their answer will be shown not by their poetry about the land but by how they respond to Ṣāliḥ's warning.


The gardens and springs are not arguments for fear detached from mercy. They are evidence that the Lord has cared for Thamûd. The correct response is to recognize the care, use the resources justly, and remain humble about their duration.

A people can be wealthy and still be spiritually safe if its wealth produces gratitude and obedience. It can also be materially comfortable while moving toward ruin if comfort silences correction. Ṣāliḥ names the setting so that Thamûd can see the choice hidden inside its abundance.

A landscape cannot speak for the people who control it. The same gardens may support generosity or exclusion, and the same springs may be managed with justice or seized as instruments of power. The blessing becomes morally meaningful through the decisions made around it. Thamûd is secure only if its abundance is placed under the Provider's command.
