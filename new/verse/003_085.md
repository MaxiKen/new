## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:85

> Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them, and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement About the Accepted Way**

The verse declares that whoever seeks a way other than Islam will not have it accepted from him. The word rendered Islam is the same term the sūrah has used since early in its argument, and its meaning in the Book is submission to God rather than membership in a community.

That distinction is not a modern accommodation, and the sūrah itself supplies the evidence for it. The word's participle is applied in the Book to Abraham and Jacob in their advice to their children, to the disciples of Jesus in their answer to him, and to the community that receives this revelation. What the verse is stating, then, is the acceptance of a stance rather than the registration of a name, and the sūrah's earlier treatment of the same word prepares the reader to hear it that way.

**The Wider Sense and the Narrower**

Commentators differed about the verse's scope, and the positions they took are worth setting out. One reading holds that after the coming of the Prophet of this sūrah no religion other than the one he brought will be accepted by God, and this reading is applied by the same authorities to other verses that make the point. A wider reading, emphasised by many classical authorities across the centuries, takes the verse's word to mean submission to God, a state that describes those who conformed to His will in every community that received a messenger.

Both readings rest on the same sentence, and the second has the support of the Book's own usage of the term. What the sūrah does not do is restrict the sentence to the name of a later community at the expense of everything it has said about Abraham, Ishmael, and the disciples, all of whom are described in the Book with the word this verse uses. The reader is best served by holding the two readings together: the way God accepts has always been submission, and it has been given its most complete and final statement in this revelation.

**Where the "In the Hereafter" Is Placed**

The verse ends by saying that the person who seeks another way will be among the losers in the Hereafter, which locates the outcome of the choice after death.

The Book places this theme at the end of several of its arguments with the communities of the Book, and the reason has to do with the nature of the dispute. The parties who disagreed about Abraham and Jesus were not disputing material advantages, at least not only those; the disagreement concerned what would be accepted from them at the end. The verse states the criterion and states the sphere in which it operates, and it leaves the world in which the argument is being conducted as it is. Nothing in the sentence tells the community to enforce the criterion, and nothing in it describes a punishment in this life. It speaks about the accounting that the whole sūrah has been describing since its opening verses.

**The Question of Other Communities**

The verse has been read by some as a denial that any religion other than the one revealed through the final Prophet can be accepted, and that reading has a long history in the tradition. The difficulty it raises, which the classical commentators noted, is that the same Book describes figures from earlier communities with the vocabulary of submission and promises them rewards.

The resolution offered by the commentators who favour the wider reading turns on the sequence of revelation rather than on the standing of the communities. What a community is answerable for depends on what it has received, and the Book makes the point in a verse about the earlier communities when it refers to those who were given a portion of the scripture before this message arrived. The verse above states the rule for the person who has heard what has come and looked for another way; it does not adjudicate the case of anyone who never encountered the question, and the Book's treatment of such people belongs to other passages.

**The Word "Seeks" and Its Implication**

The verb describes a deliberate search, not an unfortunate circumstance of birth or an inherited position. The verse concerns a person who has the option of the way God accepts and goes looking for a substitute.

That is a narrow class, and the narrowness is the point. The Book's arguments in this sūrah have been directed at people who had the message in front of them, argued about the messengers of God, and then turned away from the covenant they had accepted. The verse states the outcome for exactly that conduct, and it does so in the vocabulary of a person making a choice rather than of a person born into the wrong community. Reading it as a verdict on the world's population requires the verb to mean something its sense does not carry, and the surrounding verses continue to address the communities in front of the Prophet as parties who may still respond.

**A Criterion That Has Been Used Against Its Readers**

The verse has a further function in the sūrah's own argument, which is to answer the confidence of communities who took their standing for granted. The people addressed in this passage had a revelation, a history, and a position in the world; the sentence tells them that what will be accepted is not their standing but their submission.

Readers of the verse through the centuries have applied it in the opposite direction, which is a measure of how easily a criterion becomes a possession. The sūrah's own use of the sentence is a warning to anyone who treats the accepted way as a badge rather than as a practice, and the following verses describe what the Book says about those who leave the submission they had accepted.

**How the Verse Stands With Its Neighbours**

The verse sits between a creed that names every prophet without distinction and a passage about people who believed and then turned back. Its position states the relation between the two.

Belief in the whole line of messengers is the content of the submission; the abandonment of that belief is the seeking of another way. The verses that follow pursue the second case in detail and end with a provision for repentance, and the reader is meant to see them as part of one argument rather than as disconnected verdicts on different communities. What the sūrah provides at this point is the standard, and the next lines describe what happens to those who had met it and then left.
