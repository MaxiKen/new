## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:53

> In this way We have tested some by means of others, so those ˹disbelievers˺ may say, “Has Allah favoured these ˹poor believers˺ out of all of us?” Does Allah not best recognize the grateful?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Inequality as a Test Rather Than an Accident**

The verse states that the differences between people are arranged as a trial, and it states it in the first person: We have tested some by means of others. The Book treats disparities of wealth, strength, and status as part of the design rather than as faults in it, and it says in another place that if God had willed He could have made humanity a single community, but the will is to test people with what they have been given (5:48).

The testing runs in both directions. The wealthy are tested by their responsibility toward the needy, and the needy are tested by their trust in God and by the patience required to accept help from those who have less standing. Neither condition is a verdict on the person living in it.

**The Question the Disbelievers Ask**

Those who disbelieved look at the Prophet's followers and ask whether God has favoured these poor believers out of all of them. The question relocates the argument from truth to status, which is the move the sūrah has been dismantling since its opening verses. The Makkans had assumed that if a message came from God it would arrive among the powerful and be accepted by them first, and the composition of the Prophet's following was being used as evidence against him.

The Book records the same reasoning in the story of Noah, where the notables demanded that he drive away the believers of low standing, and he refused on the ground that he could not expel those whose account rested with God (11:29; 11:31). The assumption that spiritual worth follows social rank was old before Makkah, and the Book treats it as a recurring error.

**The Question Posed From Both Sides**

Commentators note that the question may equally be asked by the poor, looking at the wealth of the disbelievers and wondering why God continues to grant them prosperity while the believers remain poor and exposed to persecution. Under that reading the sentence covers both directions of the same bewilderment, and the verse's answer serves both groups at once.

The Book deals with the second question in several places. The disbelievers should not imagine that living longer is good for them, since they are only given more time to increase in sin (3:178), and the wealth of an enemy is not evidence about his standing with God. The answer to both versions of the question is the same: the present distribution of comfort is a field of testing rather than a table of verdicts.

**What the Grateful Are and Why the Word Is Chosen**

Does Allah not best recognize the grateful? The question at the end defines the quality that distinguishes one person from another in this arrangement, and the quality is gratitude. The Book links gratitude to perception of the giver, and it describes the person who uses his abundance to acknowledge God rather than to congratulate himself. The opposite failure is described in the same sūrah a few verses later, where people in prosperity credit themselves and in adversity blame God's messenger (7:131, in the sūrah of the Heights).

The choice of word also answers the disbelievers' question about favour. God's favour is real, and it is recognized by its fruits in the person who receives it. What the proud could not see was that the poor believers were displaying the fruit that the arrangement was meant to produce, while their own wealth was producing the confidence that kept them from the message.

**Testing by Means of One Another in Practice**

The mechanism described here shapes the daily life of every community. A person's treatment of those below him in status tests his character more reliably than his treatment of those he needs, and the Book's legislation is full of provisions for people whose standing made them easy to overlook: orphans, the poor, travellers, debtors. The sūrah itself turns next to the greeting and the promise of mercy that the Prophet is to extend to the believers who approach him.

Read that way, the verse is a portrait of the community being built rather than a comment on the society being left behind. The differences between people are permanent features of the world, and the Book's response is not to abolish them but to make the arrangements through which they are held a matter of conscious and tested conduct.

**The Answer Given to a Hard Question**

The verse does not comfort the poor by telling them their poverty is meaningless, and it does not console the rich by insisting they are secretly better off. It states that the whole arrangement is a test and that gratitude is the criterion God recognises. Everything the audience wanted to know about the meaning of rank and wealth is answered in that one word, and the answer is a question, which is the Book's preferred way of leaving the reader with something to work out for himself.

The verses that follow turn to the practical consequences of the principle in the Prophet's own circle, instructing him to greet the believers with peace and to announce the mercy his Lord has placed upon Himself. The theory of testing is immediately applied to how people are to be treated.

The sūrah names the same principle once more later in its account of the idolaters' practices, where the pagans are said to have killed their own children out of a confusion in their faith, and where the Book states that had God willed they would not have done it (6:137). The distribution of ranks, wealth, and even understanding among people is presented throughout the sūrah as an arrangement with a purpose, and the purpose is never to establish which human being matters more.
