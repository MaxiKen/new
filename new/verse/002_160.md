## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:160

> As for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known, they are the ones to whom I will turn ˹in forgiveness˺, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Reopens Every Case**

The curse of the previous verse was total — God and all who curse — and this verse begins with the word that keeps totality from meaning finality: *except*. The concealers are cursed; the concealers who turn are received. The exception is not an afterthought softening a harsh rule; it is the rule's designed horizon, placed one verse away so that no reader can encounter the curse without meeting the door beside it. The sūrah's whole treatment of sin runs on this pattern — the offense stated at full severity, the return opened at full width — and here the pattern governs the very crime the passage cares most about, the hiding of guidance. Even that is forgivable. Even the war against clarity can be ended by the one who waged it. The verse's position teaches the community how to hold its own condemnations: nothing said against a sin may be said in a way that closes repentance from it, because God Himself pronounced the curse and Himself attached the exception.

**Three Movements, Not One**

The exception is conditioned, and the conditions are three: they repent, they mend, they clarify. The first is the turn itself — *tābū*, the heart's reversal of direction. The second, *aṣlaḥū*, carries the sense of putting things right, repairing what the sin damaged: the amended life, the restored practice, the corrected habit. The third is specific to this crime and is what gives the verse its jurisprudence: *bayyanū* — they make clear, they address and declare the very truth they had concealed. Al-Ṭabarī's explanation is exact: the repentant concealers must publish what they suppressed. The three conditions map onto the sin's three dimensions — the inward turn answers the intention, the mending answers the behavior, the clarification answers the public harm. A concealment is not undone privately, because it was not committed privately: it operated on other people's knowledge, and its repair must operate there too. The verse thereby establishes a principle larger than its case: repentance must match the injury. Stolen wealth is repaid; hidden truth is announced; the returning sinner retraces the exact road of his sin, undoing it step by step.

**One Root, Two Directions**

The verse's grammar enacts its theology in a single root. "They repent" and "I will turn to them in forgiveness" translate the same verb — *tāba* — turned by its prepositions: the servant turns *to* God (*tāba ilā*), and God turns *upon* the servant with acceptance (*tāba ʿalā*). The reciprocity is in the word itself: the movement of return is one motion with two ends, and the divine end is what makes the human end possible. The sūrah showed the mechanism at humanity's first sin: *"Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance"* (2:37) — the words themselves given before the asking. The same verse names God *Tawwāb* — the Accepter of Repentance, in the intensive form that denotes repeated, habitual, overflowing acceptance: not one who accepts once, reluctantly, but one whose nature is to keep accepting. The legal verses of the sūrah of the Women frame the boundaries — repentance accepted for those who sin ignorantly and turn soon after (4:17), and the question that makes despair illogical: *"Why should Allah punish you if you are grateful and faithful?"* (4:147) — but it is this verse's intensive Name that carries the whole doctrine: the door is not merely open; the Doorkeeper is described as leaning toward it.

**The First Person of Acceptance**

The verse shifts suddenly into the divine first person: *they are the ones to whom I will turn, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful*. The surrounding passage speaks of God in the third person or the majestic plural — We revealed, We made clear; cursed by Allah. Here the acceptance is personalized: *I*. The shift is the verse's tenderness. A curse pronounced in the third person can be heard as a verdict of the system; forgiveness spoken in the first person can only be heard as the welcome of a Person. The sinner returning from concealment does not re-enter a mechanism but meets a Speaker, and the Speaker names Himself twice in the same breath — Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful — as if the return needed two guarantees rather than one. The pairing also orders the gifts: the *tawbah* accepts the turning, the *raḥmah* covers what the turning cannot repair, since no clarification fully un-rings the years a truth stayed hidden. Acceptance handles the crime; mercy handles the residue.

**Against Despair, Against Presumption**

The verse stands between two errors the tradition policed with it. Despair: the concealer who reasons that his sin was against clarity itself, that he fought God's disclosure, and that such a case admits no pardon — the verse answers that the worst category of hiding is inside the exception. Presumption: the concealer who reasons that since God is the Accepter of Repentance, his private sorrow suffices and his published lie may stand — the verse answers with the third condition, which is precisely the one presumption refuses. True repentance from concealment costs the concealer his audience's revised opinion of him; he must stand before the same people he misled and say, what I hid was this. The cost explains why the three conditions are inseparable: a turn without mending is emotion, mending without clarification is self-improvement, and only the three together are the tawbah this verse accepts. God's first-person promise is then waiting at the end of all three — *I will turn* — the intensive Accepter meeting the completed return, and mercy absorbing the rest.
