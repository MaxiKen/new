## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:160

> As for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known, they are the ones to whom I will turn ˹in forgiveness˺, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Remedy Stated**

The verse states the terms on which the offenders described in the previous sentence are restored, and it names three actions: repentance, the correction of what was done, and the disclosure of what had been concealed. The sentence then names the one who accepts the repentance, using a form that speaks in the first person.

The Book's practice of following a statement of punishment with a statement of remedy runs through the sūrah, and the reader is shown that the door is not closed by the severity of the offence. What the verse establishes is that the concealment of religious knowledge can be repaired, and it describes what the repair consists of.

**Repentance as a Turning**

The term rendered as repentance comes from a root meaning to turn, and the Book uses the same root for God's turning toward the servant, so that the two movements are named by one word with different prepositions. The community's scholars explained the pairing as the servant's turning from disobedience and God's turning toward him with acceptance.

The Book treats repentance as a transaction that involves both parties, and it describes God's acceptance of it as an act of grace rather than an obligation. The verse's use of the same root in the two halves of the sentence makes the relation visible, and the reader is shown that the movement of the servant is met by the movement of his Lord.

**Putting Things Right**

The second term describes the correction of what has gone wrong, and the commentators explained it in the context of the previous verse as the restoration of the knowledge that had been withheld. The wording covers the amendment of conduct as well as the repair of the specific harm, and the community's teachers applied it to anyone who had misled others and then wished to undo the damage.

The Book's usage gives the term a practical weight, since what is required is not only the admission of the fault but the work of repairing it. A person who concealed a truth is told to make the matter clear, and the sentence places that action alongside the repentance rather than treating it as an optional addition.

**Making the Truth Known**

The third term describes disclosure, and the commentators explained it as the statement of what had been hidden, whether that was the description of the Prophet ﷺ in the earlier scriptures or a religious truth that a person had suppressed. The requirement matches the offence exactly: what was withheld is to be produced.

The precision of the remedy is characteristic of the Book's treatment of wrongdoing, where the correction corresponds to the fault. The sūrah has described communities that dealt with their scriptures in ways that served their position, and the sentence here offers them the path by which the position can be surrendered. What is asked of the reader, should he find himself in the same condition, is the same three-part movement.

**The First Person Address**

The sentence states that God relents toward such people, and it shifts from the third person of the preceding verses to a form in which the speaker is named. The commentators noted that the shift gives the promise a personal character, and the Book uses the same device at points where a statement about forgiveness is being made.

The ending of the sentence names two attributes, describing God as the one who turns toward His servants and as the merciful, and the community's teachers read the pair as the ground of the promise. What the reader receives is not a general permission but a statement made in the first person by the One whose acceptance the repentance requires.

The shift into the first person gives the promise a weight that a report in the third person would lack, and the community’s teachers noted that the Book uses the device when a statement about God’s treatment of His servants is being made. The reader hears the sentence as spoken by the One whose acceptance he seeks, and the names at the end of the verse describe the disposition with which that acceptance is offered. The passage therefore closes on a statement of divine willingness rather than on a condition imposed on the offender.

**Repentance in the Sūrah's Earlier Passages**

The sūrah has treated repentance before, in the account of Adam receiving words from his Lord and in the prayer of Abraham and Ishmael for forgiveness. The community's teachers gathered those passages with the present verse and with the later treatment of repentance in the sūrah, and they described the Book's teaching as consistent throughout.

The repetition indicates the importance the Book attaches to the subject, and it also shows how the sūrah presents the history of the earlier communities. The people who received the Book and mishandled it are not described as beyond repair, and the remedy offered them in this verse is the same one the Book offers to everyone.

The community’s teachers gathered the passages in which the sūrah treats the subject and read them as a single teaching. Adam is described receiving words from his Lord after his lapse, and Abraham and Ishmael pray for forgiveness as they raise the foundations of the House. The Book presents repentance in these accounts as a normal part of the life of the faithful rather than as an emergency measure, and the reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening pages meets the present verse as the same teaching applied to a particular offence.

**What the Verse Adds to the Passage**

The sentence completes the statement that began with the charge of concealment, and it does so in a way that leaves the reader with a task rather than with a verdict. The Book has described the offence, named the condemnation that follows it, and then set out the conditions under which a person leaves that condition behind.

The passage that follows returns to those who disbelieve and die in their disbelief, and the contrast between the two verses shows the reader where the division lies. What the Book places before him is the choice between the concealment that ends in condemnation and the disclosure that ends in acceptance, and the verse in this place supplies the second.
