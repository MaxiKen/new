## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:160

> As for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known, they are the ones to whom I will turn ˹in forgiveness˺, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Three Steps Named in Order**

The exception stands on three acts, and al-Ṭabarī treats each as a separate condition. *Tawba* is the return, the regret that turns the offender back from the act he committed. *Iṣlāḥ* is the repair, the correction of conduct so that the return is not a mood that passes with the week. *Bayān* is the declaration, the exposure of the truth that had been covered. Ibn Kathīr states the three as regret for the past, amendment of behaviour in the present, and explanation to the people of what had been hidden from them. The order is not accidental; the third step is what distinguishes this repentance from the private regret that leaves the wrong in place.

**Why Disclosure Belongs to This Repentance**

Al-Qurṭubī draws out the reason with an analogy the jurists used in the law of sale: a man who has damaged the buyer of a defective good cannot undo the matter by regretting it in his own house, because the wrong was done to another party and lies in the other party's possession. So it is with concealed knowledge: the truth was withheld from the community, and it returns to the community only when it is spoken. Ibn Kathīr adds that the person who concealed an innovation or a false doctrine and then repents must say what he said wrongly, since the error he planted will outlive his regret unless he uproots it in speech.

**The God Who Speaks in the First Person**

The promise abandons the third person used throughout the passage. The sentence does not say that God turns to them; it places the words in His mouth, so that the reader hears the acceptance pronounced by the One who grants it. Al-Rāzī counts this among the places where the shift of speech honours the party addressed, since the repentant is answered directly by his Lord rather than informed about Him. The two names that close the sentence sustain the same tone: *al-tawwāb* is the One who returns to His servant with acceptance each time the servant returns, and *al-raḥīm* is the mercy that keeps the door wide once the return has been made.

**The Ones Who Took the Exception**

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr record that the exception found its first subjects among the People of the Book who had concealed the description of the Prophet ﷺ and then believed. ʿAbdallāh ibn Salām and those with him are named by the exegetes, men who had known the signs in their own scriptures and came to the Prophet ﷺ declaring what they had been holding back. The Book states the same rule in another place, where it promises that those who repent after their concealment and make amends will be met by God's forgiveness and mercy (3:89). The community's teachers used the two passages together in dealing with converts from the earlier religions, and with anyone who corrects a lie he once spread.

**The Joy of a Servant's Return**

Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim carries the report of a man who lost his camel in an open country and despaired of it, then found it standing over his own provisions, and said in his relief that God is his servant and he is God's lord — an utterance of confusion that the Prophet ﷺ reported to describe the intensity of the man's joy. The Prophet ﷺ stated that God's joy at the repentance of His servant exceeds the joy of that man. The sentence of this verse and the ḥadīth stand together: the verse names God as the One who returns to the returning, and the report explains why the promise is phrased as an embrace rather than a transaction.

**The Place of the Verse Between Warning and Sentence**

Read in its place, the verse is a hinge. Before it stands the curse pronounced on those who hid the signs; after it stands the sentence on those who disbelieve and die in that state; and between the two the sūrah sets the three-step remedy. Al-Saʿdī observes that the mercy is offered while the offence is still fresh in the reader's hearing, so that the severity of the preceding sentence is never allowed to stand alone. The reader is left in the position the Book intends for him in this world: knowing what he would deserve, and knowing where the door is.

**The Name al-Tawwāb Through the Book**

The name that closes the verse runs through the Book as the answer to ruined circumstances. It is the name by which the first human being was met after his fall, when words were given to him and his Lord turned toward him (2:37). It is the name carried by the sūrah that bears the title of repentance, where the three men who stayed behind from the expedition are pardoned after the earth seemed to close in on them (9:118), and where the community is told that God is the One who accepts the turning of His servants and receives their charity (9:104). Al-Ṭabarī explains the form of the word as an intensification: God turns to His servant with acceptance again and again, so that the door of this verse is not opened once for a single generation but repeatedly for every reader who walks through it.

**What the Verse Establishes**

Repentance that leaves a public wrong unrepaired is incomplete, and the repair of concealed knowledge is the restoration of it to the people who were kept from it. God's acceptance is not a concession wrested from an unwilling judge but the act of One who names Himself the Accepter of Repentance, and the mercy in His name follows the forgiveness He grants. The exception is placed in the text before any reader can claim it as his excuse, which is the way the sūrah treats hope throughout: announced early, conditioned plainly, and spoken in the first person by the One who grants it.
