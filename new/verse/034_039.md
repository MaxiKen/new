## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:39

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely ˹it is˺ my Lord ˹Who˺ gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants. And whatever you spend in charity, He will compensate ˹you˺ for it. For He is the Best Provider.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Sentence, With Two Additions**

The opening clause repeats verse 36 word for word until it reaches a phrase that was not there: *min ʿibādih*, of His servants. Then the verse continues into entirely new territory with a promise about spending. A repetition that adds two things is doing something specific, and the additions are related. The first occurrence of the formula was an answer to men boasting of wealth; it told them the distribution carries no verdict (34:36). This occurrence is addressed to people who might act on that knowledge, and the addition of *His servants* narrows the frame from humanity in general to those in a relationship of service. What follows is accordingly practical rather than corrective. Having established that no one can read the ledger, the sūrah now tells those who accept this what to do with whatever is in their hands.

**The Fear That Stops Generosity**

*Wa mā anfaqtum min shayʾin fa-huwa yukhlifuh* — whatever you spend of anything, He replaces it. The verb *akhlafa* means to put something in the place of what has gone, to supply a successor; from the same root come *khalīfah*, a successor, and *khalaf*, what comes after. The promise is therefore of replacement rather than of reward, and the distinction matters because it addresses the actual obstacle. The reason people do not give is rarely that they doubt giving is virtuous. It is that they can see the gap the gift will leave. A promise of merit in the next world does not close a present-tense gap; a promise of replacement does. The Prophet described two angels who descend every day, one saying: O Allah, compensate the one who spends, and the other: O Allah, bring ruin to the one who withholds (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1442, from Abū Hurayrah). The wording of that supplication uses the same root as this verse.

**Replacement in What Currency?**

The verse does not specify what the replacement consists of, and the silence is worth respecting rather than filling. Commentators have read it as compensation in this world, in the next, or in both, and the Qur'an's related statements support a range. Al-Baqarah describes charity as a loan to God, *"which Allah will multiply many times over"* (2:245). Al-Rūm contrasts it with lending at interest and says of the charitable that *"it is they whose reward will be multiplied"* (30:39). Al-Naḥl states the general principle with a comparison: *"Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting"* (16:96). What all these share is a refusal to treat the transfer as a loss. Whether the return arrives as money, as sufficiency, as protection from a cost that never materialises, or as something recorded and paid later, the verse's claim is that the outgoing entry is matched. A believer is therefore never in deficit from having given.

**Charity Placed Immediately After the Doctrine of Apportionment**

The two halves of this verse are joined for a reason that repays attention. If God expands and restricts provision as He wills, then two conclusions are available. The fatalistic one says that since the amounts are set, spending and withholding make no difference. The verse takes the other road: since the amounts are set by Someone who can also replace what leaves your hand, spending is safe. Belief in apportionment, on this account, is what makes generosity rational rather than what makes it pointless. The man most likely to hoard is the man who believes his balance is entirely his own achievement and entirely his own responsibility to defend. This is precisely the man the sūrah has been arguing with since the boast of wealth and children (34:35), and this verse quietly names the behavioural consequence of his theology.

**"Best of Providers" — A Title Applied to the One Being Trusted**

The closing name is *khayr al-rāziqīn*, the best of those who provide. The construction is comparative and therefore concedes that others provide too — an employer, a father, a patron, a market. What it claims is superiority within a shared category, and the ground of that superiority is supplied by the clause it follows. Other providers give and are depleted; this one replaces what is spent. The title appears elsewhere in the same practical register: Jesus asks for a table set down and calls on the best of providers (5:114); al-Jumuʿah uses it of what God has beyond the trade and amusement people rush toward (62:11). Placing it here converts the promise from an assertion into an argument. The reason spending is safe is not that generosity is magically rewarded. It is that the account it draws on belongs to a Provider whose supply does not run down.

**Two Kinds of Provision**

There is a distinction the verse's vocabulary invites, and the earlier Muslim commentators drew it explicitly. Al-Tustarī, the third-century ascetic of Basra, is reported to have said of this verse that provision is of two kinds: one is remembrance, which sustains the spirit, the intellect and the heart, as the angels live by remembrance and would perish without it; the other is what is eaten and drunk for the benefit of the body. The observation fits the verse's structure well, since a promise of replacement for what is spent makes most sense if the replacing supply is not limited to the same commodity. A man who gives money may be repaid in something that is not money and still be repaid. The sūrah now leaves the subject of provision and turns to a scene in which the objects of false worship are questioned directly.
