## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:213

> Humanity had once been one community ˹of believers before they lost faith˺. Then Allah raised prophets as deliverers of good news and as warners, and revealed to them the Scriptures in truth to judge among people regarding their disputes. And no one disputed the Scriptures except the very people who received them after clear proofs had come to them—out of jealousy. Then Allah, by His grace, has guided the believers to the truth regarding those disputes. And Allah guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Humanity Was One Community**

The verse opens the whole human story in a single clause: mankind was one community — ummah wāḥidah. The exegetes' readings map the possibilities, and al-Ṭabarī notes that the exact chronology is not what matters. Some locate the unity in the period between Adam and Noah; some understand it as a unity in disbelief and degradation, later corrected through Noah and then Abraham; others describe a unity in submission — all humanity following one religion, one community of believers before the loss. Another reading takes the oneness as Adam himself: humanity began as a single person and became many, and the Qur'an does use ummah of one man — Abraham was an ummah, rendered in the common translation as a model of excellence, devout and upright (16:120). A further reading connects the one community to the pretemporal covenant of 7:172, when all Adam's descendants testified Am I not your Lord? and answered yes — the unity of that original acknowledgment. The readings agree on the verse's structural point: the story begins in oneness, and everything that follows — prophets, books, differences — is the history of that oneness lost and its truth recovered. The sūrah of Yūnus states the same opening with the divergence explicit: mankind was nothing but one community, then they differed (10:19).

**The Implied Difference**

The verse's logic turns on what the first clause leaves unsaid, and the commentators supply it: many read an implied phrase, so the sentence runs mankind was one community — *and then they differed* — whereupon God sent the prophets. The implication is necessary to the sequence, because the sending answers the difference: prophets are sent to correct error and adjudicate division, and al-Rāzī's argument is decisive — had humanity remained united in one true religion, the sending would be superfluous. The difference that intervened is not detailed, and the verse's silence is informative: the scripture consistently declines to romanticize the unity's duration or itemize the first fall, treating the divergence as the given condition the revelation addresses. Some exegetes read the earliest history as a time without the need for religion's commands and prohibitions, with prophecy becoming necessary at a later stage; al-Rāzī reports — disapprovingly — a view attributing the first period to unaided intellect, humanity knowing the Creator and the good by reason until pride and envy crept in and division made the prophets necessary. Against such views stands the tradition's own premise: Adam was himself a prophet, and the clear proofs began with the first man. The difference, on whatever timetable, is the hinge the whole verse turns on — unity, divergence, and the sending that follows divergence like the answer follows the question.

**Bearers of Glad Tidings and Warners**

The prophets are sent with a double office the Qur'an names as a fixed pair: bashīr — bearer of glad tidings — and nadhīr — warner. The two functions are the revelation's two hands: the good news that draws, the warning that drives, the Garden announced and the Fire announced, and no prophet sent with one hand only. With them — and the phrase binds book to messenger in one descent — God sent down the Book in truth, to judge between people in what they differed about. The Book here is read at two scopes: the general name for all the revealed books (al-Shaʿbī's reading), or the Torah specifically if the verse is tied to the Jews and Christians alone (al-Ṭabarī's alternative); the comprehensive reading carries the verse's universal frame better — one Book in successive descents, each installment in truth, each sent for the same adjudicating purpose. The phrase in truth is the same formula that grounded the far-schismatics' sentence (2:176): the Book descends bi'l-ḥaqq, its content corresponding to what is, its purpose the settlement of disputes by a standard no party controls. The verse thereby states revelation's job-description: not to decorate the difference but to judge it — the Book as court, the prophets as its bearers, the glad tidings and warnings as its two verdicts.

**Only Those Given It Differed**

The verse's most searching clause reports the trial's outcome: none differed concerning the Book except those who were given it — after the clear proofs came to them — out of mutual injustice and envy. The paradox is the scripture's standing grief: the difference did not arise from the Book's absence but from its presence; the disputants were not the uninformed outsiders but the informed recipients; the proofs were clear and the division came after. The sūrah states the same sequence of the People of the Scripture: they did not dispute among themselves out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them (3:19); and the sūrah of the Clear Proof repeats it: the People of the Book became divided only after the clear proof came to them (98:4). The motive named — baghy, the envious overreaching — identifies the difference's engine: not honest interpretive struggle but rivalry, the competition of parties who want the Book's authority for themselves. The commentators draw the general lesson: there is something near-inevitable, given human nature, about difference arising after revelation arrives — the truth given to a community becomes, in envious hands, the truth disputed by that community. The verse thus completes the warning of 2:211: Israel altered the favor after receiving it; here the whole pattern is generalized — the recipients are the differers, the proofs precede the division, and the envy is the difference's parent.

**Guided to the Truth by His Leave**

The verse's penultimate clause turns from the divided to the guided: then God guided those who believe to the truth of what they differed about, by His leave. The guidance is specific rather than general — not guidance to a new truth but to the truth of the disputed matter, the settlement the Book was sent to make and the divided recipients failed to receive: the community of believers is given what the envious receivers withheld from themselves. The phrase bi-idhnih — by His leave, by His gracious permission — marks the guidance's character: it is not earned by the believers' cleverness but granted by the Guide's initiative, the same gratuity the verse's last clause confirms. The sūrah of the Table states the framework the guidance operates in: to each community God ordained a law and a way, and had He willed He would have made you one community — but He tests you with what He has given you, so compete in good works; to God you all return and He will inform you of what you differed about (5:48). The verse of al-Baqarah is that framework's present tense: the differences are real, the competition is commanded, the final information is deferred to the Return — and in the meantime, by His leave, the believers hold the truth of the disputed matter, not as possession but as granted guidance.

**To the Straight Path**

The verse closes with the destination's name: God guides whomever He wills to a straight path. The ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm is the petition of the scripture's opening chapter — guide us to the straight path — and the verse answers the petition from the divine side: the guidance asked for is the guidance God wills to give, and the path asked for is the one this verse has been describing — the oneness recovered through the Book's judgment, the difference outrun by granted truth. The closing clause gathers the whole verse into its final shape: one community, then difference, then prophets and Book sent to judge, then the recipients' envious division, then the believers guided to the disputed truth, then the straight path as the guidance's name. The sequence is the theology of revelation in miniature — the human story as unity lost, adjudication sent, adjudication corrupted by its own recipients, and guidance preserved by grace in a community that asks for it. And the clause's last word is the passage's hinge to what follows: the straight path is not abstraction but legislation — the qiblah, the fast, the hajj, the war's limits, the bequest's fairness — the sūrah's two hundred verses of straight-path detail, with the trials-verse ahead confirming that admission to the path's end runs through the testing of its walkers. The one community's story closes where every community's story reopens: at the path, under the Guide, by His leave.
