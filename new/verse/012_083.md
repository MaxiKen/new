## Sūrah Yūsuf 12:83

> He cried, “No! Your souls must have tempted you to do something ˹evil˺. So ˹I am left with nothing but˺ beautiful patience! I trust Allah will return them all to me. Surely He ˹alone˺ is the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Answer Given Twice, Decades Apart**

Jacob's reply repeats the answer he gave when the bloodied shirt was brought to him. On that occasion he said: *“No! Your souls must have tempted you to do something ˹evil˺. So ˹I can only endure with˺ beautiful patience!”* (12:18). The opening word and the first clause are identical here, and the second clause returns in a slightly altered form. The repetition is not a habit of speech but a judgement formed by experience: this is the second time his sons have returned from an errand with a story about a missing brother, and the first story was false. A father who has lived through the first account does not need new arguments to doubt the second, and the chapter supplies him with none. He answers the pattern rather than the report.

**Doubt Grounded in Precedent, Not Evidence**

Nothing in verses 81 and 82 supports Jacob's suspicion. The brothers have limited their testimony to what they saw, offered two categories of witness, invoked the exception clause of their own oath, and asked for nothing. Jacob's response engages with none of that. It states a conclusion about their souls. The verse is honest about the epistemic position: a man deceived once by the same people about the same subject cannot evaluate their second report purely on its merits, because the merits are not the only thing he is weighing. The chapter does not rebuke him for it. Nor does it vindicate him on the facts, which turn out to be as his sons described. What it shows is that a person can be right about a family's character and wrong about the event in front of him, and can hold both at once.

**"Beautiful Patience"**

The phrase *ṣabrun jamīl* appears only twice in the Qur'an, and both occurrences are Jacob's, in this chapter, in response to the loss of a son. The commentators understand it as patience without complaint to other people — endurance that does not seek relief by protesting to creatures. The adjective does not describe the patience as pleasant or easy. It describes it as unmarred, free of the element that would spoil it. The verse's second use of the term shows the same discipline being applied to a second loss, decades later, by a man whose eyes have already failed from weeping (12:84). The chapter presents that application as prophetic conduct rather than as stoicism, and verse 86 will define the practice by stating where the complaint is actually directed.

**"Perhaps God Will Return Them All to Me"**

The hope is plural and specific. The commentators identify the three whom Jacob expects back: Joseph, Benjamin, and the eldest son who has vowed to remain in Egypt (12:80). Jacob has been told nothing about Joseph except that a wolf devoured him, and nothing about the eldest except that he will not come home until permitted. His expectation that all three will return is therefore not a conclusion drawn from information he possesses. The commentators describe it as a kind of prophecy, since Joseph was not known to be alive, and they connect it to the claim he makes in verse 86 — that he knows from God what his sons do not know. The verse places in a grieving father's mouth an expectation that the remaining twenty-eight verses fulfil exactly and completely.

**Rejection, Endurance and Hope in One Sentence**

The structure of verse 83 is unusual and deserves to be read as a whole. It opens with a rejection of his sons' account, moves to a declaration of patience, and ends with hope and two divine names. Nothing connects the three movements except the person speaking them. A man who believes he has been deceived again, who has just lost a second child, and who has a third stranded in a foreign country states that God may return all of them to him. The chapter does not explain how the hope survives the grief and the distrust, or whether it is caused by either. It records all three in a single verse and leaves them side by side, which is the same treatment the following verse gives the grief on its own.

**The Answer Is Given to the Report, Not to the Sons**

Jacob's reply does not question the brothers, demand details, or ask what happened to the eldest. He does not interrogate the witnesses they offered or test the limit they placed on their testimony. He answers with a verdict and a hope, and the verdict concerns their souls rather than their evidence. The omission is informative. A father conducting an enquiry would have asked about the official, the search, the penalty and the substitution; a father conducting a grief asks none of it. Verse 83 therefore tells the reader less about what happened in Egypt than about what the report did to the man receiving it. The chapter keeps the facts in Egypt and keeps Canaan for the response to them, which is the division of material that governs verses 83 to 87.

**The Two Names Answer the Two Difficulties**

*Innahu huwa al-ʿalīm al-ḥakīm* — He is the All-Knowing, the All-Wise. The pairing is placed at the end of a verse that has raised two problems, and it answers each. Jacob does not know what happened in Egypt and cannot rely on the accounts of the people who were there; the first name states that someone does know. The detention of a second son, after the loss of a first, looks like meaningless repetition; the second name states that events are ordered toward an end. The claim is not that things will turn out well but that they are known and arranged, which is harder to hold and is what distinguishes this verse from ordinary consolation. Verse 100 will report the ordering in Joseph's own words: my Lord is subtle in what He wills.
