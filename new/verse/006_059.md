## Sūrah al-Anʿām 6:59

> With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him. And He knows what is in the land and sea. Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth or anything—green or dry—but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Place the Keys Are Named**

This is the only verse in the entire recitation that says God possesses the keys of the unseen, and the phrase gave its name to works of scholarship across the centuries, including the great commentary of Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī. The Book states elsewhere that God alone knows the unseen, as in the verse where He tells the Prophet to say that none in the heavens or the earth knows the unseen except God (27:65), and in the passage where He says that the unseen belongs to Him alone (10:20).

The word for keys describes the instruments that open what is closed, and the commentators understood the phrase as a reference to the storehouses from which everything created is brought forth. What the verse is doing in context is answering the demand the Makkans had just made. They wanted the Prophet to disclose the future and the timing of the punishment, and the answer is that the keys of that domain are held by someone else.

**The Five Keys in the Reported Tradition**

The commentaries connect the phrase with a report transmitted from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar, in which the Prophet said that the keys of the unseen are five and then recited the verse in the sūrah of Luqmān: with Allah is the knowledge of the Hour, He sends down the rain, He knows what is in the wombs, no soul knows what it will earn tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die (31:34). The report is recorded by al-Bukhārī in his Ṣaḥīḥ in the book of the rain prayer, and it is reported from Buraydah in the Musnad of Aḥmad with a chain the scholars judged sound.

ʿĀisha used the same material to correct a claim about the Prophet himself, saying that anyone who told her that Muhammad had seen the unseen was a liar, because God says that none has knowledge of the unseen but He, and then quoting the Prophet's statement about the five keys. The point of the tradition is not to restrict God's knowledge to five items but to name the matters people most wanted disclosed and to place them beyond every created claimant.

**Knowledge That Reaches the Smallest Things**

The verse then widens its scope from the hidden to the visible: He knows what is in the land and sea. The statement moves from the general to the minute, and the two examples chosen are a falling leaf and a grain buried in the dark of the earth. Neither object has any importance to a human observer, and both are beyond observation altogether, which is precisely why the Book selects them.

The same argument appears in the sūrah of Yūnus, where the Book says that not even an atom's weight is hidden from the Lord on earth or in heaven, nor anything smaller or larger than that, but that it is written in a perfect record (10:61). The sūrah of Naml makes the same point about what is hidden in the heavens and the earth being recorded in a clear book (27:75), and the sūrah of Sabāʾ states that nothing escapes the Lord's knowledge by so much as an atom's weight (34:3).

**The Argument Against Those Who Limited God's Knowledge**

This verse was used by the theologians against certain Muslim philosophers who held that God knows universals but not particulars. The claim was that a God whose knowledge reached every individual thing would be affected by change, and the response was that the Book's own wording leaves no room for the distinction. A leaf that falls, a grain in the dark, a seed in the ground: the verse names the particulars, not the categories.

The sūrah states the point in a form that makes the objection impossible to maintain, since the falling of a single leaf is the most contingent event imaginable. Whoever knows that knows a particular, and the Book says the knowledge extends to everything green or dry, which is another way of saying that nothing at all lies outside it.

**The Clear Record and What It Means**

The verse ends by saying that all of this is written in a perfect record. Commentators identified the record with the Preserved Tablet, of which the Book speaks when it describes the Qur'an as inscribed on a tablet well preserved (85:22), and some held that the expression refers to God's own knowledge rather than to a created object. The Book uses the same phrase for revealed scripture in several places, which shows how flexible the image is in its hands.

What the phrase accomplishes in this verse is to add permanence to knowledge. The leaf is not merely watched as it falls; it is recorded, and the record keeps the account of a thing that no human being ever noticed. The Book's readers were being told that the details of their own lives are kept in the same way, which is the practical consequence the sūrah draws from it a few verses later.

**What It Means for a Person's Own Account**

The verse that follows this one describes God taking the souls of people by night and knowing what they do by day, and the one after that describes the recording angels who watch over every person. Read together, the three verses establish the whole apparatus of accountability, beginning with the One who holds the keys and ending with the point at which a life is closed and its account returned.

For a reader, the consequence is that nothing is hidden which will not be produced, and nothing is trivial which will not be weighed. The verse's leaf and grain are not incidental details chosen for their poetry. They are the claim that the smallest event in the world is known and written, which makes every deed of a human being a matter of record.
