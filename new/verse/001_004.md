## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Readings That Complete Each Other**

The word has been recited in two ways since the first generations, and both go back to the Prophet. Five of the seven canonical readers — Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, Ibn ʿĀmir, and Ḥamzah — recite *malik*, "King"; ʿĀṣim and al-Kisāʾī recite *mālik*, "Owner," and it is ʿĀṣim's reading, through the transmitter Ḥafṣ, that most of the world recites today. Neither reading can be preferred to the exclusion of the other, and the commentaries argued the comparison in both directions.

One side holds that *malik* is the more intensive term, since every king is a master while not every master is a king. The other holds that *mālik* is the wider term, since ownership carries unrestricted power of disposal, which kingship does not necessarily imply. The dispute is not resolvable by the lexicon, and it does not need to be: the two words name two failures that human authority suffers and divine authority does not. Among human beings there are kings who own nothing and owners who command no one. The recitation says both words at once, in the sense that whichever a worshipper recites is confirmed by the other. In the ḥadīth qudsī that tracks the recitation verse by verse, this line draws the reply *My servant has glorified Me*, with one transmitter adding *My servant entrusted his affairs to Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). Glory and entrusting are exactly what a king who owns produces in a subject.

**Owner, Not Merely Judge**

The construction is an annexation: God is Master *of* the Day. Ownership of a day is a stranger idea than presiding over it. The verse does not say that God will judge on that Day, though He will; it says the Day belongs to Him, and everything that occurs in it is the disposal of an owner over His property.

The consequence is that no part of that Day is independent of Him — not its timing, not its procedure, not the standing of anyone in it. The Quran states the same claim as a question asked into a silence: *“Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!* (40:16). Another sūrah makes the exclusivity explicit: *˹It is˺ the Day no soul will be of ˹any˺ benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺* (82:19). Every form of leverage a person relied on in the world — wealth, lineage, influence, friendship — is property that has changed hands.

**What "Dīn" Carries**

The word translated judgment is *dīn*, and its semantic range is wider than any single English equivalent. It is used in the Quran for obedience, for custom or way of life, and most often for religion itself. It is also tied to *dayn*, a debt, the word used in the long instruction on recording loans: *When you contract a loan for a fixed period of time, commit it to writing* (2:282). The connection is not a pun. A debtor owes what was given; a creature owes what it was given. God is called *al-Dayyān*, the One who settles accounts, and the Day of *dīn* is the day the account comes due.

Read that way, the verse supplies the metaphor the rest of the Quran builds on. Existence is a loan, the term is fixed, and the day named here is the day of repayment. The Quran states the settlement in plain commercial terms elsewhere: *Today every soul will be rewarded for what it has done. No injustice Today! Surely Allah is swift in reckoning* (40:17), and *On that Day, Allah will give them their just penalty in full* (24:25). The scales are set up for exactly this transaction: *We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least. And ˹even˺ if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth* (21:47).

**The Day Every Other King Is Absent**

The claim is not abstract, and the tradition pictures it in a scene. The Prophet is reported to have said that God will take the earth in His grip and fold the heaven with His right hand, then declare, *I am the King: where are the kings of the earth?* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7382; also Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The Quran gives the same image: *when on the Day of Judgment the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand* (39:67), followed by the observation that those who heard this had not estimated God at His true worth.

The scene is what the verse is for. Human authority ends at death and is spectacularly useless afterwards, which is why the sūrah pairs the title with a day rather than with a realm. Nothing about the arrangement changes on that Day — God owns everything now, too — but the ownership that was always true becomes the only one left standing. The Quran describes the currency of this world being refused as legal tender there: *a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession* (2:254), and *a Day in which there will be no ransom or friendly connections* (14:31).

**The Last Name in a Descent**

The four opening verses can be read as a sequence, and several commentators read it as a descent. Allāh names the Essence. Al-Raḥmān names the mercy that encompasses everything. Al-Raḥīm names the mercy directed to whom He wills. Al-Mālik — or al-Malik — names the management of the created order, and it is in a sense the lowest of the four, because it requires an "other" to be real. Mercy can be conceived without a recipient; a king cannot be conceived without subjects.

That reading explains the shape of the whole sūrah. The first half descends from the Divine Principle to the created order, and the second half, from 1:5 onward, ascends from the creature back to the Principle — the worshipper speaking, asking, naming the path, and being answered. The verse on which the two halves turn is this one: the point at which God's governance of everything becomes the worshipper's own future, and therefore his own business.

**Reckoning as a Measure of the Mind**

If the account is real and the date is fixed, then the rational response is preparation, and the tradition measures intelligence by exactly that. When the Prophet was asked which of the believers is wisest, he answered: *Those who remember death often and have best prepared for it with good deeds; such are the wisest* (Sunan Ibn Mājah 4259, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī). The first part of his answer to the same question — which of the believers is best — was those with the best character, so wisdom here is not cleverness but foresight applied.

The Quran gives the same instruction in its own terms: *O believers! Be mindful of Allah and let every soul look to what ˹deeds˺ it has sent forth for tomorrow* (59:18). The "tomorrow" is the Day this verse names. A person who recites *Master of the Day of Judgment* several times a day is rehearsing a verdict, and the rehearsal is meant to change what is sent ahead.
