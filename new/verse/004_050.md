## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:50

> See how they fabricate lies against Allah—this alone is a blatant sin.

### Expanded Commentary

**Fabricating a lie against Allah**

The verse identifies self-purification claims as a form of fabrication against Allah. To say “God has declared me pure,” or to assign divine approval to a faction without evidence, is not merely boastful speech. It puts words in the mouth of the Lawgiver. Religious falsehood is especially dangerous because it recruits the hearer’s trust in God to protect the speaker’s ego.

**The difference between hope and certification**

A believer may hope for Allah’s mercy, speak of a good deed, and encourage others toward righteousness. What is prohibited is certainty about one’s own sanctity or about another person’s guaranteed standing without revelation. The early Muslims feared hypocrisy in themselves even while striving for faith; that fear was not despair but protection against complacency.

**Public consequences**

Fabricated holiness can justify abuse. A leader who declares his group pure may exclude critics, seize property, or excuse violence. A family that claims divine preference for its lineage may deny a woman her inheritance. The verse places such claims in the category of blatant sin because they corrupt both theology and social order.

The correction is to describe deeds honestly, cite revelation carefully, and leave hidden judgments to Allah. A community should honour knowledge and virtue without turning admiration into infallibility. Divine approval is sought through obedience, not manufactured through slogans.

**The responsibility of religious speech**

A fabricated attribution to God can change a family’s rights, a court’s judgment, or a community’s treatment of an outsider. Religious teachers should mark uncertainty honestly and distinguish scripture from commentary. Caution is not weakness; it is reverence for the One whose words are being represented.
