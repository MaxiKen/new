## Sūrah Sabaʾ 34:44

> ˹They say so even though˺ We had never given them any scriptures to study, nor did We ever send them a warner before you ˹O Prophet˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Objection Answered by Examining the Objector's Credentials**

The previous verse recorded three confident verdicts: he is a man with a motive, the book is a forgery, the experience is sorcery (34:43). This verse does not refute any of them directly. It asks on what basis they were issued. *Wa mā ātaynāhum min kutubin yadrusūnahā* — We gave them no books they were studying. *Wa mā arsalnā ilayhim qablaka min nadhīr* — nor did We send them any warner before you. The strategy is to examine the credentials of the court rather than the merits of the case, and it is a fair move in this instance because the verdicts were delivered with the confidence of expertise. A man who declares a text a forgery is implicitly claiming to know what a genuine revelation looks like. The verse asks where he acquired that knowledge.

**yadrusūnahā: The Verb for Sustained Study**

The word chosen for what they were not doing is precise. *Darasa* means to study a text repeatedly, to go over it until it is mastered; the same root gives *madrasah*, a school, and *dirāsah*, the systematic study of a subject. The verse does not merely say they had no scriptures — it says they had no scriptures **that they were studying**, in the continuous tense. This wording anticipates an obvious reply. The Meccans were not ignorant of the existence of earlier books; Jewish and Christian communities were known to them, their caravans passed through territories where those books were read, and individual Meccans had encountered their contents. What they lacked was a tradition of engagement with them. Having heard of a thing is not studying it, and the verse draws that line exactly where a critic's authority would have to rest.

**No Warner Before You**

The second clause concerns oral rather than written transmission. No messenger had been sent to this particular people before the Prophet, which is stated elsewhere in the Qur'an as the reason his coming mattered so much to them. This closes the other route by which a community might acquire a standard for judging revelation. They had neither a book to consult nor a living tradition of prophecy to compare against, and so possessed no basis at all — textual or inherited — for the confident classifications of the preceding verse. The Prophet is named as the first: *qablaka*, before you. That single word turns the verse from an accusation into a description of an opportunity. A people who had never received a warner were not being tested against a standard they had failed before; they were being addressed for the first time.

**The Same Argument Made Elsewhere**

The Qur'an uses this line of attack repeatedly and always against confident claims about unseen matters. Fāṭir asks: *"Or have We given them a Book—which they use as an authority?"* (35:40). Al-Ṣāffāt puts the challenge to those who assert things about the angels: *"Or do you have a compelling proof? Then bring your scripture, if what you say is true!"* (37:156–157). Al-Zukhruf and al-Aḥqāf press it in similar terms. In every case the demand is the same: produce the warrant. The consistency of this challenge across the Book indicates something about the Qur'an's own view of how religious claims should be settled. It does not ask its opponents to accept assertions on authority; it asks them to state the basis of theirs.

**The Sharpest Edge: Claims About the Unseen**

There is a particular target embedded in this verse that becomes visible when it is read against the rest of the sūrah. The Meccans had made a series of claims about unseen realities: that the Hour would never come to them (34:3), that they would never be punished (34:35), that their deities held standing with God (34:22). Those are not opinions about visible matters; they are assertions about the hidden order, and assertions of that kind can only be grounded in revelation. The verse points out that they had received none. Their confidence about the unseen therefore rests on nothing at all — which is precisely the charge they were levelling at the Prophet, who at least claimed a source. The sūrah has already used this reversal once, when it showed that even the jinn who served Solomon had no access to the unseen (34:14).

**What a People Without Either Actually Had**

It is fair to ask what the Meccans did possess, since the verse denies them two things without saying they had nothing. They had a sanctuary, a pilgrimage, an inherited cult, a rich oral poetry, and a detailed genealogical memory. None of these is a source of information about the unseen, and that is precisely the verse's point. A tradition can be ancient, dignified, socially binding and entirely silent on the questions in dispute. The Qur'an's challenge is not that their heritage was worthless but that it was the wrong kind of thing to settle the argument they were having. Ancestral practice tells you what your people did. It does not tell you whether the dead will be raised.

**A Verse That Removes an Excuse and Offers One at the Same Time**

Two readings of this verse pull in different directions, and both are legitimate. The grammar supports either, since the clause is attached to the preceding verse without a connective that would settle its function — it may explain why their verdicts were baseless, or it may explain why such a people needed a messenger at all. Read as a rebuke, it says they had no standing to judge and judged anyway, which aggravates the offence. Read as a mitigation, it describes a people who had genuinely been left without guidance for generations — which is why al-Yā-Sīn describes the Prophet's mission as being to a people *"whose forefathers were not warned, so they are heedless"*. The Qur'an holds both, and the tension is resolved by timing. Before the warner came, ignorance was their condition and not their fault. After he came, and after the revelations were recited as clear evidences, the ignorance became a choice. The verse sits exactly at that hinge, which is why it is placed immediately after their three dismissals rather than before them. The following verse turns from what they lacked to what earlier peoples possessed.
