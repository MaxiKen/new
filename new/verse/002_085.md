## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:85

> But here you are, killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin and aggression; and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you as captives, you still ransom them—though expelling them was unlawful for you. Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest? Is there any reward for those who do so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment? For Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Breach Set Out in Detail**

The verse turns from what the community promised to what the record shows, and it uses the present tense. Each clause corresponds to a term of the covenant that the previous verse recorded, so that the reader can match the violation to the undertaking. The Book is not making a general charge of hypocrisy; it is naming the exact clauses that were broken.

This is one of the longest sentences in the sūrah, and its length is part of its effect. It accumulates the offences without pausing to condemn each one, and it finishes with the question that reduces the whole account to a single issue.

**Killing, Expelling, and Aiding One Another**

The first two offences repeat the prohibitions of the previous verse, and the third explains how they became possible: the community aided one another in sin and aggression. The Book's analysis of the collapse is social rather than theological. People did not break their covenant in isolation; they organized around their factions and found partners for whatever their side wanted to do.

That clause names the mechanism by which a moral community dissolves. When loyalty is transferred from the undertaking to the group, the undertaking loses its hold, and the Book states elsewhere that cooperating in righteousness is commanded while cooperating in sin and hostility is forbidden (5:2). The verse shows what happens to a community that reads only the first half of that rule.

**Ransoming the Captives, Rejecting the Cause**

The most specific charge in the verse concerns the captives. The community that had expelled some of its own people would then pay to redeem those same people when they were taken prisoner elsewhere, and the Book presents the contradiction in a single breath. The ransom is not credited to them as generosity; it is entered as evidence that they knew the terms of their own scripture perfectly well.

Commentators connected this clause to the alliances of Madinah's tribes in the period before the coming of the Prophet ﷺ, when factional fighting between the Aws and the Khazraj drew in the Jewish tribes allied to each side. The tribes would fight one another, then ransom their captives according to a rule they all acknowledged, and the Book treats the ransoming as proof that the prohibition on killing and expulsion was never unknown to them.

**Believing Part of a Book and Rejecting the Rest**

The verse then asks the question that governs everything before it: do you believe in some of the scripture and reject the rest? The formulation identifies the real offence, which is not ignorance of the text but a selective relationship with it. A community that ransoms according to one clause while shedding blood against another has not lost its scripture; it has taken it in parts.

The Book returns to this charge at several points in the sūrah, and it treats the partial acceptance of revelation as a serious category of unbelief rather than a lesser form of belief. The criterion it applies is not the quantity of commands observed but the authority recognized, and the verse exposes a community that was prepared to obey where obedience was already in its interest.

**Disgrace in This Life and the Harshest Punishment**

The consequence is stated in two parts, and the first is temporal. Disgrace in this worldly life is the predictable result of a community whose internal wars are common knowledge, and the Book describes it as part of the punishment rather than as a coincidence. The second part is reserved for the Day of Judgment, where the Book calls the punishment harshest, a term it uses sparingly.

The pairing of the two is a feature of this sūrah's treatment of the community's history. The Book does not present the worldly consequence as the whole penalty, nor the final judgement as an abstract threat disconnected from conduct now. Both are named in the same sentence, and the ordinary reader is meant to see continuity between them.

**The Closing Attribute**

The sentence ends with a statement about God's knowledge rather than about His decree, and the sūrah has now appended this attribute to several of the charges in this passage. It removes the last possible refuge, which is the assumption that a violation committed inside a community remains a private matter.

What makes the ending effective is its placement. The verse has just described deeds that its perpetrators would have regarded as legitimate acts of tribal loyalty, and the final clause informs them that no such category exists in the accounting the Book is describing. The following verse draws the conclusion in one line about what such a community has traded away.

**A Charge Directed at a Method, Not a Lineage**

The verse names no individual and passes no sentence on a people as a whole. It describes a set of practices that a community had adopted while holding a scripture that forbade them, and it asks the members of that community how they reconcile the two. The question is addressed to conduct, which means any reader who recognizes the pattern in himself has been addressed as well, whatever scripture he carries.

**A Question That Exposes the Criterion**

The question in this verse deserves isolating from the rest of the sentence. It does not ask whether the community accepts the scripture, since that was never in dispute. It asks whether they accept some of it and reject the rest. The distinction drawn here governs everything that follows in the sūrah's treatment of this people, and it governs the sūrah's treatment of the believers too, since the same question returns in addresses to the Muslims about the parts of the revelation they find convenient and the parts they set aside. What the verse establishes is that selective acceptance is not a middle position between belief and unbelief. A person who obeys only what suits him has made his own preference the authority over the text, and the Book's term for that arrangement is not partial belief but disbelief in part.

**What the Ransom Clause Reveals**

The detail about the captives has always been the sharpest part of the charge. The community paid to redeem members of its own tribes whom its own factions had helped to enslave, and the payment showed that it knew the value of the lives it had been expelling. A rule the community kept when it suited them stood beside a rule it broke when breaking served the faction, and the Book places both facts in a single sentence. That contrast is what makes the verse a study in selective obedience rather than a list of crimes. The ransoming was not an act of mercy offered by outsiders; it was the community's own law applied to the community's own people, and it demonstrates that the rest of that law was never unknown to them.
