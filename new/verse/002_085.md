## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:85

> But here you are, killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin and aggression; and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you as captives, you still ransom them—though expelling them was unlawful for you. Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest? Is there any reward for those who do so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment? For Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Here You Are: The Contradiction on Live Feed**

The verse opens with the signature of an accusation caught in the act: *but here you are* — the present tense of a crime contemporaneous with the recitation, quoted to perpetrators who can check the report against their own week. The charge is the covenant's exact inversion: *killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes* — the two prohibitions of the witnessed bond (84), violated in the same reflexive grammar, each other's blood, your own people's homes. The historical scene the exegetes supply is Madina itself in the generations before the Prophet's ﷺ arrival. Three Jewish tribes — Banū Qaynuqāʿ, Banū al-Naḍīr, Banū Qurayẓah — lived among the two great Arab tribes, Aws and Khazraj, whose wars ran for decades. When Aws fought Khazraj, the Jewish tribes fought across both battle-lines: Qurayẓah and al-Naḍīr with one Arab ally, Qaynuqāʿ with the other — so that Jew fought Jew, killed him, drove him from his house, and took him prisoner, all in the service of alliances that made enemies of co-religionists and brothers-in-arms of idolaters. The clause *aiding one another in sin and aggression* names the alliances' moral structure: cooperation exists — energetic, loyal, costly cooperation — but its object is transgression, and the verse's phrase pairs the two nouns the Qur'an keeps joined for forbidden collaboration: sin against Allah, aggression against people.

**The Ransom Paradox**

The indictment's turn is its strangest feature: *and when those expelled come to you as captives, you still ransom them*. The same community that drove out its own now pays to free them — expel with one hand, ransom with the other, the crime and its expiation performed by the same people in the same war. The exegetes locate the verse's central question in precisely this contradiction: believing part of the Book and disbelieving part refers, on al-Ṭabarī's reading, to the clash between the expulsion — which violates the Book — and the ransom — which accords with it. The paradox is not hypocrisy of the simple kind but of an intricate kind, and its intricacy is what makes the verse's question so cutting. The ransom is genuine virtue: freeing captives stands in the Qur'an's own ethic among the highest acts — Sūrah Muḥammad legislates the treatment of war captives as release *by grace or by ransom* (47:4) — and the Prophet ﷺ made it a standing command of the community's duty: *"Give food to the hungry, pay a visit to the sick and release (set free) the one in captivity (by paying his ransom)"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5373). The verse does not fault the ransom. It faults the selectivity — a community that keeps the commandment which costs money and breaks the commandment which costs power, that honors the Book in the captive-market and tramples it on the battlefield, in the same week, with the same hands.

**“Though Expelling Them Was Unlawful for You”**

The parenthetical clause fixes the legal order of the two acts, and the order is the verse's jurisprudence in miniature: ransoming the captive is obedience; *expelling them was unlawful for you* — the expulsion had no warrant at any point, which means the ransom is not repairing a lawful act gone wrong but funding the aftermath of a crime. The community's own practice testifies against it: by ransoming the expelled, it admits the expelled were its people, owed the covenant's protection — the ransom is a signed confession that the expulsion breached a duty the ransomers themselves hold sacred. Al-Rāzī's alternative reading of the surrounding passage connects the partial belief to the prophecies of the coming Prophet, though he notes the immediate context does not require it; the expulsion-and-ransom contradiction remains the reading the verse's own wording carries. The clause's deeper function is to destroy the defense of consistency. A community that never ransomed could plead a coherent (if brutal) position; a community that ransoms has conceded the covenant's authority over captives and stands convicted of applying that authority selectively — honoring the Book where honor is cheap, suspending it where obedience would cost an alliance, a fortress, or a war. The recital's audit is exact: it does not charge them with rejecting the Scripture, but with rationing it.

**Some of the Scripture, the Rest Rejected**

The verse's central question generalizes the case into a permanent category: *do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest?* The wider sense of *kufr* the exegetes mark here is rejection and denial of specific truths rather than atheism of God — the expulsion and the alliances are hypocrisy and immorality enacted, and in the Qur'an's moral arithmetic such acts are inseparable from a measure of unbelief, because a truth one systematically disobeys is a truth one has functionally denied. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ will legislate the category in full: those who believe *in some and disbelieve in others* — the selective believers — are made disbelievers in truth by their selection (4:150–151). The mechanism the verse exposes is the treatment of revelation as a menu: the comfortable terms taken, the costly terms left, the selection itself performed by appetite rather than by the Book. And the selection is never random — it runs, in every age, along the line between commandments that cost money and commandments that cost power. The ransom costs money and was kept; the prohibition on expulsion cost tribal position and was broken. The verse's question is addressed to every community that keeps a Scripture, because every community that keeps one keeps it partly, and the partial pattern is legible the moment one asks which terms are honored and what each honored term costs.

**Disgrace Here, the Harshest Punishment There**

The sentence is doubled across the two worlds: *disgrace in this worldly life* and *the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment*. The worldly half is exposure — *khizy*, the shame of a community whose contradiction becomes public, its covenant-breaking visible to the very neighbors it made its allies; the recital itself is part of that disgrace, quoting the private arithmetic of expulsion and ransom in the open mosque for the city to hear. History ran the sentence to its letter in Madina's following decade, as tribe after tribe of the three lost its place in the city — the expulsion its own hands had normalized returning as its own fate. The otherworldly half carries the verse's distinctive superlative: not punishment simply but *the harshest* — a degree reserved here for the selective believers, and its logic reads back through the passage: one who never received the Book sins in ignorance; one who received it, ratified its covenant, witnessed it, and then rationed it has sinned against light, and the light aggravates the sentence. The closing clause returns the whole scene to the standing audit — *Allah is never unaware of what you do* (2:85) — the same sentence that closed the hardening of hearts at verse 74, framing this entire stretch of the recital: nothing in the two rooms, the battlefield, or the captive-market escapes the file, and the double punishment is filed against a crime the Judge watched completely.

**The Whole Book, or the Menu**

The verse leaves its audience with the choice its question implies, and the choice is the recital's demand on every Scripture-keeping community. Belief in the Book is not subscription to selected clauses; it is standing under all of them, including the ones that cost what the honored ones never touch. The Muslim community hearing the recitation was being vaccinated in real time: its own charter — the covenant of verse 83, the bond of verse 84 — contained the same costly terms, and the same wars, alliances, and exiles would come for it in its own generations, wearing the same justifications the verse strips bare. The ransom paradox is the verse's permanent diagnostic, and it works in both directions. Keeping the cheap commandments while breaking the expensive ones is selective belief condemned here by name; and performing the expensive ones as a cover for the cheap ones' violation — ransoming the captive the same hands expelled — is the aggravated form, because it dresses the rationing as piety. The verse's answer to both is the unrationed Book: every term in force, the alliance dissolved where the covenant forbids it, the expulsion undone where the covenant protects, the ransom paid where the covenant commands — and the whole held together by the clause that closes the audit: He is never unaware. What is kept and what is skipped is on the record, and the record's verdict is already quoted: disgrace here, the harshest punishment there, for those who believed in some of the Scripture and rejected the rest.
