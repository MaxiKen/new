## Sūrah Āli ʿImrān 3:65

> O People of the Book! Why do you argue about Abraham, while the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him? Do you not understand?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question About Abraham**

The verse addresses the People of the Book again, and it addresses them with a question about their own claim: why do you argue about Abraham? The dispute in view is a competition over which community may claim him, and the Book's response is to point at the calendar.

The argument is as simple as it is decisive. Abraham lived before the Torah was revealed to Moses and before the Gospel was revealed to Jesus. The claim that he belonged to a community defined by either book cannot be sustained, because neither book existed during his lifetime. A person cannot be a member of a community whose defining scripture arrives centuries after his death.

**The Dates That Make the Argument**

The commentators noted that the chronological point is one the disputants themselves accepted; the issue was never whether Abraham predated Moses and Jesus, but what conclusions could be drawn from the ancestry and the practice of their communities.

The Book draws the conclusion in the direction the facts run. If the two communities defined themselves by revelations that came later, then the ancestor they argue over preceded both, and the argument is exposed as an attempt to annex a figure from the past to a position he never held. The sūrah has already used a similar method with the claim about Jesus: it compared his mode of creation with Adam's, which places both in a single category. Here it uses chronology, which places the disputed ancestor outside the terms of the dispute.

**Ḥanīf: the Word for What Abraham Was**

The sūrah's account of what Abraham actually was comes in the next two verses, and the term at its centre is *ḥanīf*, a word the Book applies to a person who turns away from false worship toward the one God. In the vocabulary of the Qur'an it describes an inclination rather than a membership, and its consistent companion is the phrase "and he was not a polytheist".

The pairing appears throughout the Book's references to Abraham: the sūrah of the Cow gives the believers the creed of Abraham the upright, who was not of the idolaters (2:135); the sūrah of the Bees describes him as a model of excellence, devoted, upright, and not a polytheist (16:120); the sūrah of the Cattle gives the same formula in the Prophet's own declaration (6:161). The Book treats the term as a description of Abraham's actual religion before any community organised around a book, which is why it serves as the answer to a competition between those communities.

**A Shared Ancestor and a Shared Difficulty**

The argument in this verse cuts against both claimants at once, and the sūrah means it to. The Jews of Madinah and the Christians of the region each had a narrative of belonging that ran through Abraham, and each found in him a warrant for its own standing.

The Book's response refuses both moves by the same stroke: it denies that the ancestor can be claimed by a community whose distinguishing revelation came after him, and it does so without discarding him. Abraham remains central to the sūrah's argument — he is named among the chosen families at its beginning and will be discussed at length in the verses that follow. What the Book rejects is the use of his name as a deed of ownership, and it replaces that use with a description of what he believed. The question "whose was he?" is set aside and replaced by the question "what was he?", which the next verse takes up directly.

**The Logic of Argument From Priority**

The Book uses the priority of Abraham in several places, and the reasoning is consistently about the order in which God sent things. What came first cannot be a later community's property, and what came first sets the standard by which later claims are measured.

That order is the sūrah's answer to communities that treated the antiquity of their traditions as proof of their standing. Antiquity is not the issue; the sequence of revelation is, and in the actual sequence the monotheism of Abraham stands before the books that came after him. A reader who follows the argument can see the sūrah's own claim about the Prophet of this sūrah taking a similar shape: a community defined by a later revelation will be measured against what preceded it, not against how long it has existed.

**The Closing Question**

The verse ends with a question: do you not understand? The Book uses such endings to place responsibility for the argument on the listener rather than to supply another premise.

The point being urged is not obscure, and the question is not an insult. The sūrah has set out a chronology that its hearers accept, drawn a conclusion that follows from it, and left the hearer to recognise the implication. What the question asks for is a step a person may simply decline to take, and the Book's habit of ending an argument this way is a way of saying that the remaining obstacle is not the evidence.

The verse's use of a question rather than a statement also keeps the discussion open in a way a verdict would not. A reader who recognises himself in the question can still answer it by changing his position, and the sūrah continues to address the same communities for many verses after this one. The next verses supply the positive account of what Abraham was, and they do so in a form that lets the reader compare his own confession with the description as it is read out.

**What They Had Knowledge Of**

The first half of the question concerns matters that lay within the disputants' competence, and the commentators identified them in several ways. Al-Qurṭūbī and al-Zamakhsharī understood the reference to be their own scripture and the points on which they differed over it; al-Rāzī recorded the reading that it concerns what their laws permitted and forbade; and a further suggestion is that the dispute was about whether the new revelation agreed with or differed from the earlier books. All of the readings describe an argument conducted about material the parties possessed, which is why the verse can say that they had knowledge of it.

**The Claim About Abraham**

The second half of the question turns to Abraham, and the commentators are agreed that the disputants had no direct knowledge of him. Al-Qurṭūbī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī all make the same point: he lived long before the books they possessed, and neither community could produce a record of his practice that settled the question. The verse's argument is therefore about the difference between two kinds of dispute, and the difference is one of evidence. A disagreement about texts a community holds can be conducted by examining those texts, while a disagreement about a man who preceded the texts requires something else, and the passage that follows supplies it by stating what Abraham actually was. The sentence leaves the reader with a rule that applies beyond the immediate dispute: the soundness of an argument depends on what the arguer can know.
